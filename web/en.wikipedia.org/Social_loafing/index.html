Social loafing

Social loafing

Social loafing is the phenomenon of a person exerting less effort to achieve a goal when they work in a group than when working alone. It is considered one of the main reasons groups are sometimes less productive than the combined performance of their members working independently, and it is closely related to the Ringelmann effect and diffusion of responsibility.

History

French agricultural engineer Max Ringelmann reported in 1913 that men pulling on a rope in groups exerted less individual effort as group size increased: pairs pulled at about 93% of their individual capacity, trios at 85%, and groups of eight at roughly half. In the 1970s, Bibb Latané and colleagues replicated the effect with clapping and shouting tasks, coined the term "social loafing," and proposed social impact theory to explain it.

Causes

Research identifies several drivers: diffusion of responsibility, in which each member feels less personally accountable; reduced evaluation apprehension, since individual outputs cannot be identified; the "sucker effect," in which members reduce effort to avoid being exploited by free-riders; and low task meaningfulness or weak group cohesion. A 1993 meta-analysis by Karau and Williams (the Collective Effort Model) showed loafing is reduced when individual contributions are identifiable, the task is meaningful, and people expect their effort to be instrumental to valued outcomes.

Mitigation

Loafing is weaker in collectivist cultures, among women on average, in smaller groups, and when members are friends. Practical countermeasures include keeping groups small, setting clear individual responsibilities and standards, making contributions visible, and increasing commitment to the group goal. The opposite phenomenon—social facilitation, and the Köhler effect, in which weaker members work harder in groups—shows group settings can also raise effort under the right conditions.
