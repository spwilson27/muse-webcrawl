Primordial soup

Primordial soup is a term introduced by the Soviet biologist Alexander Oparin and taken up by J. B. S. Haldane in their independently developed hypotheses on the origin of life. In the Oparin–Haldane hypothesis, conditions on the early Earth — a reducing atmosphere of gases such as methane, ammonia, hydrogen, and water vapour, energised by lightning and ultraviolet radiation — favoured chemical reactions that produced progressively more complex organic compounds, which accumulated in the oceans to form a "hot dilute soup" from which living organisms eventually emerged.

The Miller–Urey experiment

In 1952–1953, Stanley Miller, working in Harold Urey's laboratory, tested the hypothesis by circulating a mixture of water, methane, ammonia, and hydrogen past an electric discharge simulating lightning. Within days, the apparatus produced a number of amino acids, the building blocks of proteins, demonstrating that organic molecules could form under plausible early-Earth conditions.

Modern discussion

Later geochemical work suggested the early atmosphere may have been less reducing than Oparin and Haldane assumed, which reduces the yield of such syntheses; alternative settings have since been proposed, including deep-sea hydrothermal vents and tidal pools. The "soup" concept remains a shorthand for the family of prebiotic-chemistry scenarios in which life began from non-living chemistry in a liquid environment. The English astronomer Fred Hoyle mockingly popularised the phrase in debates about life's origins.
