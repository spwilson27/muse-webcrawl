Mineralogy

Mineralogy is a subject of geology specialising in the scientific study of the chemistry, crystal structure and physical properties of minerals and mineralised artefacts. A mineral is a naturally occurring solid with a definite chemical composition and an ordered atomic arrangement, formed by geological processes. Specific studies within mineralogy include the processes of mineral origin and formation, classification of minerals, their geographical distribution, and their utilisation.

History

The systematic study of minerals began in antiquity: Theophrastus wrote On Stones, and Pliny the Elder catalogued minerals in his Natural History. The German scholar Georgius Agricola is often called the father of mineralogy for works such as De Natura Fossilium (1546) and De re metallica (1556). In the nineteenth century the development of crystallography, the polarising microscope and chemical analysis placed the field on a modern footing; X-ray diffraction, following Max von Laue's work of 1912 and the Braggs' determination of crystal structures, revealed the atomic architecture of minerals.

Branches and methods

Physical mineralogy examines properties used to identify minerals in hand specimen: hardness (measured on the Mohs scale), lustre, colour, streak, cleavage, fracture, specific gravity and crystal habit. Chemical mineralogy studies composition and its variation, including solid solution and polymorphism. Crystallography describes the symmetry and internal structure of crystals. Optical mineralogy identifies minerals in thin section under a petrographic microscope.

Modern laboratories apply X-ray diffraction, electron microprobe analysis, spectroscopy and mass spectrometry to determine structure and composition at ever smaller scales. The International Mineralogical Association (IMA), founded in 1958, oversees the naming and classification of mineral species; more than 5,900 valid species are currently recognised.

Classification

Minerals are grouped by chemical composition in the Strunz and Dana classification systems: native elements, sulfides, halides, oxides and hydroxides, carbonates, borates, sulfates, phosphates, silicates and organic minerals. The silicates, built from silicon–oxygen tetrahedra, dominate the Earth's crust and mantle, and their structural subclasses — island, pair, ring, chain, sheet and framework silicates — reflect increasing polymerisation of those tetrahedra.

Applications

Mineralogy underpins mining and resource geology, materials science, gemmology, soil science, planetary science and environmental studies, from locating ore deposits to characterising the minerals of meteorites and other planets.
