Reasonable doubt

Proof beyond reasonable doubt is the standard of evidence required to convict in most criminal trials.

Beyond a reasonable doubt is a legal standard of proof required to validate a criminal conviction in most adversarial legal systems. It is a higher standard than the balance of probabilities used in most civil cases, reflecting the gravity of criminal punishment and the presumption of innocence: the prosecution must prove the defendant's guilt to the point that no "reasonable doubt" could remain in the mind of a reasonable person that the defendant is guilty.

Meaning

There is no universally agreed definition of reasonable doubt, and courts differ over how, or whether, to explain it to juries. Formulations commonly describe it as doubt based on reason and common sense arising from the evidence or lack of evidence, or as being "firmly convinced" of guilt. Appellate courts in some jurisdictions discourage judges from attempting elaborate definitions, fearing they confuse rather than clarify. In England and Wales juries are instead told that they must be "sure" of guilt.

Origins

The standard developed in English common law in the late eighteenth century, partly from theological concerns that jurors who wrongly convicted might imperil their souls, and it crystallized in the maxim that it is better that ten guilty persons escape than that one innocent suffer. In the United States, the Supreme Court held in In re Winship (1970) that proof beyond a reasonable doubt of every element of the crime is constitutionally required by the Due Process Clause; in Sullivan v. Louisiana (1993) it held that a defective reasonable-doubt instruction cannot be treated as harmless error.

Comparison with other standards

The standard is deliberately demanding because of the liberty and stigma at stake, but it does not require absolute certainty: doubts that are fanciful, speculative or based on sympathy do not count. Civil systems use lower or different standards, including preponderance of the evidence and clear and convincing evidence.
