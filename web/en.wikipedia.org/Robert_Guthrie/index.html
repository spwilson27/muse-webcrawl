Robert Guthrie (28 June 1916 – 24 June 1995) was an American physician and microbiologist best known for developing the bacterial inhibition assay—the Guthrie test—that made mass newborn screening for phenylketonuria (PKU) practical, and for campaigning successfully for its adoption around the world.

The Guthrie test

Motivated in part by family experience—his son and a niece had developmental disabilities—Guthrie, working at the Children's Hospital of Buffalo, devised in 1961–62 a simple assay in which a few drops of an infant's blood, collected on filter paper from a heel prick, were placed on an agar plate seeded with Bacillus subtilis. Elevated blood phenylalanine in babies with PKU overcame a growth inhibitor in the agar, producing visible bacterial growth proportional to the phenylalanine level. Because the dried blood spots could be mailed and tested cheaply in batches, screening entire newborn populations became feasible.

Impact

Working with the National Association for Retarded Children and state health departments, Guthrie promoted universal screening against initial professional resistance; Massachusetts became the first state to mandate it in 1963, and within a decade the test was standard across the United States and in many other countries. Early dietary treatment of detected infants prevents the severe intellectual disability otherwise caused by PKU. The filter-paper blood spot card he introduced, still called the Guthrie card, became the platform for modern newborn screening panels testing for dozens of metabolic and genetic disorders.
