Nondisjunction

Nondisjunction is the failure of homologous chromosomes or sister chromatids to separate properly during cell division (mitosis or meiosis). This failure results in daughter cells with an abnormal number of chromosomes, a condition called aneuploidy.

Mechanism

Nondisjunction can occur during meiosis I, when homologous chromosomes fail to separate, or during meiosis II or mitosis, when sister chromatids fail to separate. The resulting gametes carry either an extra chromosome (n+1) or lack one (n−1). Fertilisation involving such a gamete produces a zygote with three copies of a chromosome (trisomy) or a single copy (monosomy).

Consequences

Most autosomal trisomies and monosomies in humans are lethal and result in miscarriage. Viable trisomies include trisomy 21 (Down syndrome), trisomy 18 (Edwards syndrome) and trisomy 13 (Patau syndrome). Sex-chromosome aneuploidies such as Klinefelter syndrome (XXY), Turner syndrome (X) and triple X syndrome are also consequences of nondisjunction and are generally less severe. The frequency of nondisjunction in human oogenesis increases markedly with maternal age. In somatic cells, mitotic nondisjunction can produce mosaicism.
