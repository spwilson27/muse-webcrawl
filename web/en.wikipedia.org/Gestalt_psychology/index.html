Gestalt psychology

Gestalt psychology, gestaltism, or configurationism is a school of psychology and a theory of perception that emphasises the processing of entire patterns and configurations, and not merely individual components. The term Gestalt is interpreted as "unified whole", "pattern", or "configuration". It emerged in Austria and Germany in the early twentieth century as a reaction against the associationist and structural schools' atomistic orientation.

Origins

The school was founded by Max Wertheimer, Wolfgang Köhler, and Kurt Koffka. Wertheimer's 1912 paper on the phi phenomenon — the perception of apparent motion from a rapid succession of still images — is often taken as the movement's founding document. The Gestaltists argued that the mind perceives objects as part of greater wholes, captured in the slogan that "the whole is other than the sum of the parts", a phrase often misattributed and paraphrased as "greater than the sum of its parts".

Gestalt laws of grouping

Gestalt psychologists identified a set of principles describing how humans naturally perceive objects as organised patterns. These include the laws of proximity, similarity, closure, continuity (good continuation), common fate, and figure-ground segregation, as well as the overarching law of Prägnanz, which holds that perception tends toward the simplest and most stable organisation available.

Legacy

Many Gestalt psychologists emigrated to the United States in the 1930s, where their ideas influenced perception research, problem-solving studies (such as Köhler's work on insight in chimpanzees), social psychology through Kurt Lewin, and later design, art education, and human–computer interaction, where the grouping principles remain standard guidance for interface layout.
