Isma'ilism

Ismaili history includes the Fatimid Caliphate of Egypt (909–1171).

Isma'ilism (Arabic: الإسماعيلية, al-Ismāʿīlīyah) is a branch or sub-division of Shia Islam. The Isma'ili get their name from their acceptance of Imam Isma'il ibn Jafar as the appointed spiritual successor (imam) to Ja'far al-Sadiq, the sixth Shia Imam, wherein they differ from the Twelver Shia, who accept Musa al-Kazim, the younger brother of Isma'il, as the true Imam.

History

Ismaili missionaries (da'is) spread the movement across the Muslim world in the 9th and 10th centuries. Its followers established the Fatimid Caliphate in North Africa and Egypt in 909, founding Cairo and al-Azhar University. Internal succession disputes later split the movement into Nizari and Musta'li branches; the Nizari state centred on Alamut in Persia survived until the Mongol invasions of the 13th century.

Branches today

The largest Ismaili community today is the Nizari Ismailis, led by the Aga Khan, numbering several million across Central and South Asia, East Africa and the West. Musta'li Ismailis include the Dawoodi, Sulaymani and Alavi Bohras, centred in India and Yemen. Ismaili thought is noted for its emphasis on esoteric interpretation (ta'wil) of scripture and a rich intellectual tradition in philosophy and the sciences.
