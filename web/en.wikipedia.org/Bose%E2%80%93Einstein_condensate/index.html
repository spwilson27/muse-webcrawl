Bose–Einstein condensate

Predicted: 1924–25 (Bose & Einstein)
First created: 1995 (Cornell, Wieman; Ketterle)
Temperature: Near absolute zero
Particles: Bosons in a single quantum state

A Bose–Einstein condensate (BEC) is a state of matter that is typically formed when a gas of bosons at very low densities is cooled to temperatures very close to absolute zero. Under such conditions, a large fraction of the bosons occupy the lowest quantum state, so microscopic quantum-mechanical phenomena become apparent at a macroscopic scale.

History

The phenomenon was predicted in 1924–25 by Satyendra Nath Bose and Albert Einstein, building on Bose's work on the statistics of photons. It was first realised experimentally in 1995 by Eric Cornell and Carl Wieman at the University of Colorado (using rubidium-87) and independently by Wolfgang Ketterle at MIT (using sodium-23); the three shared the 2001 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Properties

BECs exhibit superfluid-like behaviour, coherence across the whole sample, and extremely low temperatures often below 100 nanokelvin. They are used in precision measurement, studies of quantum simulation, atom lasers and fundamental tests of quantum mechanics.
