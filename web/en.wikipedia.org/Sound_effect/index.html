Sound effect


A Foley artist at work in a studio

A sound effect (or audio effect) is an artificially created or enhanced sound, used to emphasize the artistic or other content of films, television shows, live performance, animation, video games, music, or other media. In film and television production, a sound effect is a sound recorded and presented to make a specific storytelling or creative point without the use of dialogue or music. The term often refers to a process applied to a recording, without necessarily identifying the specific recording itself.

History

Sound effects were produced in the theatre long before recorded media — bells, whistles, thunder sheets, and coconut shells for horses' hooves. Radio drama in the 1920s and 1930s developed the craft extensively, with artists creating live effects in the studio. Jack Foley, at Universal Studios, pioneered the technique of performing effects in sync with the picture, an art that still bears his name.

Techniques

Foley effects are everyday sounds — footsteps, clothing rustle, handling props — performed and recorded in real time. Hard effects such as gunshots, explosions, and car sounds are sourced from libraries or recorded in the field. Designed effects are created by processing and combining recordings, as in Ben Burtt's work on Star Wars, where the lightsaber hum derived from a film projector motor and a television set.

Recording and libraries

Modern productions draw on vast commercial sound libraries and field recordings made with portable recorders. Sound effects editors work in digital audio workstations, layering and synchronizing effects to picture.
