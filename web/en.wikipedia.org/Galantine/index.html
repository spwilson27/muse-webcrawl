Galantine

Galantine
Type: Charcuterie / forcemeat dish
Place of origin: France
Main ingredients: Deboned stuffed meat (often poultry), aspic

A galantine is a French dish of de-boned stuffed meat, most commonly poultry or fish, that is poached and served cold, coated with aspic. Galantines are often stuffed with forcemeat and pressed into a cylindrical shape. Since deboning poultry is thought of as difficult and time-consuming, this is a rather elaborate dish, which is often lavishly decorated, hence its name, connoting a presentation at table that is galant, or urbane and sophisticated.

In the Middle Ages, the term "galauntine" or "galantyne" referred to several sauces made from powdered galangal root, usually made from bread crumbs with other ingredients, such as powdered cinnamon, strained and seasoned with salt and pepper. The dish was sometimes boiled or baked.

Preparation

A classic chicken galantine is made by skinning and deboning a whole bird while keeping the skin intact, laying it flat, and filling it with a forcemeat of minced meat, fat, eggs, cream, and seasonings — often enriched with pistachios, truffles, ham, or tongue arranged so the slices show a mosaic pattern. The skin is sewn or rolled around the filling into a sausage shape, tied in cloth, poached gently in stock, then pressed and chilled until set. It is unmoulded, glazed with aspic or a chaudfroid sauce, decorated, and served in slices as an hors d'oeuvre or buffet centrepiece.

Related dishes

The galantine belongs to the classical French charcuterie family alongside the ballotine (similar but served hot and usually roasted), terrines and pâtés en croûte, and aspic-glazed hams. Elaborate galantines were staples of 19th-century haute cuisine under chefs such as Antonin Carême and remained showpieces of the professional charcutier's repertoire.
