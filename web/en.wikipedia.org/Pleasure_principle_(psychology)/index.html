Pleasure principle (psychology)

Sigmund Freud (1856–1939)

In psychoanalysis, the pleasure principle (German: Lustprinzip) is the instinctive drive to seek pleasure and avoid pain in order to satisfy biological and psychological needs. It is one of the two principles governing mental functioning in Sigmund Freud's theory, opposed and eventually modified by the reality principle.

Freud's formulation

Freud introduced the concept in his essay "Formulations on the Two Principles of Mental Functioning" (1911) and developed it in Beyond the Pleasure Principle (1920). In Freud's structural model of the psyche, the pleasure principle governs the id, the entirely unconscious reservoir of libidinal drives present from birth. The id demands immediate gratification of hunger, thirst, sexual desire and the avoidance of discomfort, without regard for consequences or for the external world. A hungry infant hallucinating the breast is Freud's model of the id's "primary process" thinking.

As the child develops, the ego emerges and learns to postpone gratification in accordance with the reality principle, which evaluates the demands of the external world and seeks pleasure by safe, indirect routes. The pleasure principle is not abandoned but is modified and deferred.

Beyond the pleasure principle

In 1920 Freud complicated his own theory. Observing that patients compulsively repeated traumatic experiences — in dreams, in play (the famous fort-da game of his grandson) and in the transference — he concluded that some force operates "beyond the pleasure principle." He hypothesised a death drive (Todestrieb, later popularised as Thanatos) opposed to the life drive (Eros): an urge to return living matter to an inorganic state. The repetition compulsion and the death drive remain among the most contested elements of psychoanalytic theory.

Antecedents and influence

The idea has philosophical antecedents in hedonism, from Epicurus to Jeremy Bentham's felicific calculus, and in Gustav Fechner's principle of constancy, from which Freud borrowed the notion that the mental apparatus seeks to keep excitation low and stable. The pleasure principle influenced literary criticism, surrealism, and later critical theory, where Herbert Marcuse used the term "pleasure principle" — and its modification under capitalism, the "performance principle" — in Eros and Civilization (1955).
