Janus kinase

Janus kinase (JAK) is a family of intracellular tyrosine kinases that transduce signals from cytokine and growth-factor receptors lacking intrinsic kinase activity of their own. The family takes its name from the two-faced Roman god Janus because each JAK protein possesses two near-identical phosphate-transferring domains — one with genuine kinase activity and one that regulates it. JAKs are essential components of the JAK-STAT signaling pathway.

Family members

Four JAK family members are known in mammals:

  • JAK1 — associated with receptors for interferons and many interleukins; essential for normal development and immune function.
  • JAK2 — mediates signalling from erythropoietin, thrombopoietin and several other hormone-like cytokines; the activating mutation JAK2 V617F is found in the great majority of cases of polycythemia vera and in many other myeloproliferative neoplasms.
  • JAK3 — expressed mainly in haematopoietic cells and partners with the common gamma chain shared by interleukin receptors; loss-of-function mutations cause severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID).
  • TYK2 (tyrosine kinase 2) — involved in interferon and interleukin-12/23 signalling.

Structure and mechanism

JAK proteins contain seven JAK homology (JH) domains. The JH1 domain is the active kinase; JH2 is a pseudokinase that normally restrains JH1, and mutations in JH2 — such as JAK2 V617F — release that restraint and cause disease. When a cytokine binds its receptor, the receptor chains dimerise and bring their associated JAKs together; the JAKs cross-phosphorylate and activate one another, then phosphorylate tyrosine residues on the receptor that recruit STAT proteins, which in turn are phosphorylated, dimerise, enter the nucleus and switch on target genes.

Clinical significance

Because JAKs sit at the hub of immune signalling, drugs that block them — Janus kinase inhibitors such as tofacitinib, baricitinib and ruxolitinib — are used to treat rheumatoid arthritis, myelofibrosis, ulcerative colitis, atopic dermatitis and alopecia areata, among other conditions. Their use carries risks, including serious infections and increased rates of cardiovascular events and malignancy reported in comparative trials, which regulators have addressed with boxed warnings.
