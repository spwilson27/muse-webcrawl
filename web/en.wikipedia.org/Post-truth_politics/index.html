Post-truth politics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Post-truth politics is characterised by appeals to emotion disconnected from policy detail.

Post-truth politics is a political culture in which public debate is framed predominantly by appeals to emotion and personal belief, with factual rebuttals and policy details playing a diminished role. In a post-truth environment, claims are often repeated regardless of fact-checking, and political success depends less on the accuracy of statements than on whether they resonate with an audience's existing attitudes. The term "post-truth" was named Word of the Year for 2016 by Oxford Dictionaries, which defined it as relating to circumstances in which "objective facts are less influential in shaping public opinion than appeals to emotion and personal belief".

Term popularised: 2016

Related concepts: Fake news, filter bubbles, alternative facts

History of the concept

Although the phrase appeared earlier — the playwright Steve Tesich used "post-truth" in a 1992 essay, and Ralph Keyes published The Post-Truth Era in 2004 — the concept surged into prominence in 2016, the year of the United Kingdom's Brexit referendum and the election of Donald Trump as President of the United States. Commentators pointed to campaign claims that persisted after being shown false, the phrase "alternative facts" used by a Trump adviser in January 2017, and the role of social media in creating filter bubbles and echo chambers in which partisan content circulates unchecked.

Causes and debates

Scholars attribute the phenomenon variously to the fragmentation of news media, declining trust in institutions, the economics of attention on social platforms, polarisation, and long-standing techniques of propaganda and political spin. Critics of the term argue that dishonesty in politics is as old as politics itself, and that "post-truth" can be a condescending label for voters whose priorities journalists dislike. Others respond that the scale, speed, and algorithmic amplification of contemporary misinformation mark a genuine change, and point to state-sponsored disinformation campaigns as a qualitatively new factor.
