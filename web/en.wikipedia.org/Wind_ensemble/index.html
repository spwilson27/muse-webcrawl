Wind ensemble

A wind ensemble (also called a wind band, wind orchestra, or symphonic wind ensemble) is a performing ensemble consisting primarily of members of the woodwind, brass, and percussion families of instruments, along with the double bass or bass guitar. Unlike the larger concert band, the wind ensemble generally performs with one player per part and emphasises original repertoire written for winds.

History

The modern wind ensemble concept is credited to Frederick Fennell, who founded the Eastman Wind Ensemble at the Eastman School of Music in 1952. Fennell sought a flexible group modelled on the wind sections of the symphony orchestra, in which composers could specify exactly the instrumentation their works required. The idea spread rapidly through American universities and conservatoires.

Instrumentation

A typical wind ensemble includes flutes (with piccolo), oboes (with English horn), bassoons (with contrabassoon), clarinets in several sizes, saxophones, trumpets, horns, trombones, euphoniums, tubas, string bass, piano or harp as needed, and a varied percussion section. Because only one player takes each part, the ensemble produces a transparent, chamber-like sound.

Repertoire

The ensemble's core repertoire includes works by Percy Grainger, Gustav Holst, Ralph Vaughan Williams, Paul Hindemith (Symphony in B-flat), and Karel Husa (Music for Prague 1968), along with a growing body of contemporary commissions. Wind ensembles are staples of American colleges and high schools, while professional groups such as the Dallas Winds maintain the tradition outside education.
