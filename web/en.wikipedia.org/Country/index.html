Country

A country is a distinct territorial body or political entity. It may be an independent sovereign state, a recognized constituent state, or a distinct region with a degree of self-governance, unified government, or shared culture. The term is used loosely in most cases, despite attempts by geographers and international organizations to define it precisely. There are somewhat more than 200 countries in the world, depending on the criteria applied; the United Nations has 193 member states plus two observer states.

Definitions

Under the declarative theory of statehood, codified in the 1933 Montevideo Convention, a state should possess a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states. Under the constitutive theory, recognition by other states is itself what makes a state. In practice the terms country, nation, and state overlap but differ: a nation is a people sharing identity and culture, a state is a political and legal apparatus, and a nation-state combines both. Some countries, such as the United Kingdom, are themselves composed of constituent countries (England, Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland).

Recognition and lists

Entities such as Kosovo, Palestine, Taiwan, and Western Sahara are countries to some governments but not others. International bodies maintain varying lists: ISO 3166 country codes cover 249 entries, including dependencies and territories.

Etymology

The word entered English from Old French contrée, ultimately from Vulgar Latin (terra) contrata, "land lying opposite" or "spread out before one." In a secondary sense, "country" also denotes rural areas as opposed to towns and cities, as in "the countryside."
