Relief printing

Relief printing is a family of printing methods in which a printing block, plate or matrix — which has had ink applied to its non-recessed surface — is brought into contact with paper. The areas of the printing surface that carry ink stand in relief, while the areas that remain blank are cut away below the surface.

Techniques

The principal relief techniques include woodcut, one of the oldest printing methods, developed in China and used extensively in Europe from the late Middle Ages; wood engraving, worked on the end grain of a hardwood block; linocut, in which the design is carved into linoleum; metalcut; and relief etching. Movable type in letterpress printing is also a relief process, as is modern flexography, which uses flexible photopolymer plates and dominates packaging printing.

Process

The artist or craftsman cuts away the parts of the block not intended to print using knives, gouges or burins. Ink is rolled or dabbed over the raised surface with a brayer or ink ball. Paper is laid on the block and pressed, either by hand with a baren or spoon, or in a press, transferring the inked image in reverse. For multicolour prints, separate blocks may be carved for each colour, or a reduction method may progressively cut a single block between printings.

History

Relief printing on paper appeared in China by the Tang dynasty; the Diamond Sutra of 868 CE is the earliest dated printed book. In Europe, woodcut images circulated from about 1400, preceding Gutenberg's movable type. Japanese ukiyo-e artists such as Hokusai and Hiroshige raised the colour woodblock to a high art, and 20th-century artists including the German Expressionists revived the woodcut as an expressive medium.
