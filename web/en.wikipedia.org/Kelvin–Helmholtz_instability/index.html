Kelvin–Helmholtz instability

The Kelvin–Helmholtz instability (after Lord Kelvin and Hermann von Helmholtz) is a fluid instability that occurs when there is velocity shear within a single continuous fluid, or a velocity difference across the interface between two fluids. Examples include wind blowing over water, generating surface waves, and the distinctive rolling billow clouds that form when layers of air move at different speeds.

Theory

The instability can be understood through Bernoulli's principle: faster-moving fluid above an interface has lower pressure, which tends to amplify any small undulation of the surface. Gravity and surface tension act to stabilise the interface. For two layers of differing density, stability is governed by the Richardson number: when the Richardson number falls below about 1/4, the shear is strong enough for the instability to grow. The initially sinusoidal waves roll up into a row of vortices — Kelvin's "cat's-eye" pattern — which then merge and produce turbulent mixing between the layers.

Occurrences

Beyond meteorology and oceanography (where it contributes to mixing of ocean layers), Kelvin–Helmholtz instability appears in astrophysics: in the interaction of the solar wind with planetary magnetospheres, in accretion disks, in the bands of Jupiter and Saturn, and at the boundaries of interstellar clouds. It is also observed in plasma physics and in the wakes of aircraft.
