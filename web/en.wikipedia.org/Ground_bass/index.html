Ground bass

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ground bass or basso ostinato (Italian: "obstinate bass") is a musical form in which a short melodic phrase or harmonic progression in the bass is repeated over and over as the basis of a piece, while the upper voices vary above it. The technique flourished in the Baroque era, where forms built on a ground include the passacaglia, the chaconne, the folia and the romanesca.

Examples

Among the most famous grounds is the descending tetrachord of Johann Pachelbel's Canon in D, a two-bar progression repeated throughout the piece, and the bass of Henry Purcell's "When I am laid in earth" (Dido's Lament) from Dido and Aeneas, a chromatic descending pattern over which the aria unfolds. Monteverdi, Corelli, Handel and Bach all composed celebrated works over ground basses; Bach's Passacaglia in C minor, BWV 582, builds twenty variations on an eight-bar ostinato.

Later use

The principle of a repeating bass pattern under changing upper parts carried into later music: the twelve-bar blues, the "rhythm changes" of jazz, and loop-based popular music all function as modern analogues of the ground bass.
