Oil Pollution Act of 1990

The Oil Pollution Act of 1990 (OPA) is a United States federal law that streamlines and strengthens the ability of the Environmental Protection Agency to prevent and respond to catastrophic oil spills. It was passed in response to the Exxon Valdez oil spill of 1989, in which approximately 11 million US gallons of crude oil were spilled into Prince William Sound, Alaska. The Act was signed by President George H. W. Bush on 18 August 1990.

Provisions

  • Liability: Establishes that responsible parties are liable for removal costs and damages from discharges of oil into navigable waters, adjoining shorelines, and the exclusive economic zone.
  • Oil Spill Liability Trust Fund: Strengthens the federal fund, financed by a tax on oil, to pay for cleanup when responsible parties cannot or will not pay.
  • Double hulls: Requires newly built tank vessels operating in US waters to have double hulls, with a phase-out schedule for single-hulled tankers.
  • Response planning: Requires vessel and facility response plans, area contingency plans, and the development of a national response system.
  • Financial responsibility: Requires owners and operators of vessels and facilities to demonstrate the ability to pay for potential spills.

Application

OPA's provisions were central to the federal response and cost recovery after the Deepwater Horizon spill in 2010, during which BP, as a responsible party, funded cleanup and compensation claims under the Act's framework. The law amended and largely superseded the oil spill provisions of the Clean Water Act.
