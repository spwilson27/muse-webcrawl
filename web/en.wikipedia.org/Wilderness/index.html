Wilderness

Wilderness or wildland is a natural environment on Earth that has not been significantly modified by human activity, or any tract of land preserved in its natural state. Wilderness areas are considered important for ecological research, conservation, recreation, and the preservation of biodiversity, and they may also have spiritual and cultural significance.

Definitions and protection

The United States Wilderness Act of 1964 defined wilderness as "an area where the earth and its community of life are untrammeled by man, where man himself is a visitor who does not remain," and established the National Wilderness Preservation System, which now protects over 100 million acres. The International Union for Conservation of Nature classifies wilderness as protected area category Ib. Many other countries protect wildlands through national parks and nature reserves, though definitions of how free of human influence an area must be vary widely.

Values and threats

Wilderness provides ecosystem services including clean water, carbon storage, and habitat for species that cannot survive in modified landscapes. Only a fraction of the Earth's land surface remains wilderness, and studies suggest the amount shrank by about a tenth between the 1990s and 2010s, principally through conversion to agriculture and settlement. Wilderness values — solitude, self-reliant recreation, and religious or aesthetic experience — have motivated preservation movements since the 19th century, associated with figures such as Henry David Thoreau, John Muir, and Aldo Leopold.

Criticism

Historians and Indigenous scholars note that many lands designated wilderness were long inhabited and managed by Indigenous peoples, and that the ideal of an untouched wilderness can erase that history; modern conservation increasingly involves Indigenous stewardship.
