Autosomal dominant

In autosomal dominant inheritance, an affected person usually has an affected parent, and each child has a 50% chance of inheriting the trait.

Autosomal dominant is one of several ways that a genetic trait or disorder can be passed down through families. In an autosomal dominant condition, a single copy of a disease-associated variant in a gene on one of the autosomes (non-sex chromosomes, numbered 1–22) is sufficient to cause the condition. An affected person typically inherits the variant from one affected parent, and each of their children has a 50% chance of inheriting it.

Characteristics

Autosomal dominant disorders tend to appear in every generation of a pedigree, affect males and females equally, and can be transmitted by either parent. Expression can vary: penetrance describes the proportion of people with the variant who show the condition at all — Huntington's disease is almost fully penetrant by late adulthood — while variable expressivity describes differences in severity, as in neurofibromatosis type 1. Some cases arise from new (de novo) variants, so an affected child may have unaffected parents, as is common in achondroplasia.

Examples

Well-known autosomal dominant conditions include Huntington's disease, Marfan syndrome, familial hypercholesterolemia, autosomal dominant polycystic kidney disease, hereditary spherocytosis, von Willebrand disease and the BRCA1/BRCA2-associated hereditary breast and ovarian cancer syndrome. Mechanisms vary: some variants cause loss of function where one working copy is insufficient (haploinsufficiency), others produce a harmful gain of function or a dominant-negative effect.

Contrast with recessive inheritance

In autosomal recessive conditions, two copies of the variant are needed, so carriers are usually unaffected and two carrier parents have a 25% chance of an affected child in each pregnancy. Distinguishing dominant from recessive patterns from a family history is a basic task of clinical genetics and genetic counselling.
