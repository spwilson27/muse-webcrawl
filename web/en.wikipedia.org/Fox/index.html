Fox

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Foxes are small-to-medium-sized omnivorous mammals belonging to several genera of the family Canidae. They have a flattened skull; upright, triangular ears; a pointed, slightly upturned snout; and a long, bushy tail ("brush"). The red fox (Vulpes vulpes) is the largest and most widespread of the true foxes.

Classification

Twelve species belong to the monophyletic "true fox" group of genus Vulpes, including the red fox, arctic fox, fennec fox and kit fox. Other canids called foxes include the gray foxes (Urocyon), the bat-eared fox (Otocyon megalotis), and the South American foxes (Lycalopex), which are more closely related to wolves and jackals than to true foxes.

Biology and behaviour

Foxes are generally solitary hunters, feeding on rodents, rabbits, birds, insects and fruit. They are famed for their acute hearing and for the characteristic pounce, guided by Earth's magnetic field, used to catch prey hidden under snow or vegetation. Most foxes live in family groups or alone, occupying dens (earths) that may be dug themselves or taken over from other animals. The red fox has adapted successfully to suburban and urban environments across the Northern Hemisphere and Australia, where it was introduced in the nineteenth century.

Relationship with humans

Foxes appear as clever tricksters in folklore worldwide — from the European Reynard cycle to the Japanese kitsune and Korean kumiho. They have long been hunted for sport and fur, and a famous long-running experiment in Siberia, begun in 1959, selectively bred silver foxes for tameness, producing domesticated foxes with dog-like traits. Foxes can carry rabies and other diseases, and introduced red foxes are a major threat to Australian native wildlife.
