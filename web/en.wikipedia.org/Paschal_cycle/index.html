Paschal cycle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Resurrection of Christ is the centre of the Paschal cycle

The Paschal cycle, in Orthodox Christian liturgical practice, is the cycle of movable feasts and seasons built around Pascha (Easter), the Feast of Feasts. Because the date of Pascha varies from year to year according to the computus, all of the observances that depend on it — the preparatory weeks of Lent, Holy Week, and the feasts following the Resurrection — shift accordingly, in contrast to the fixed cycle of feasts tied to calendar dates.

Structure

The cycle is traditionally divided into two great parts, served by two liturgical books. The Triodion covers the preparatory period: the pre-Lenten Sundays (of the Publican and the Pharisee, the Prodigal Son, the Last Judgment, and Forgiveness Sunday), followed by the six weeks of Great Lent, Lazarus Saturday, Palm Sunday and Holy Week, culminating in Pascha itself. The Pentecostarion covers the fifty days from Pascha to Pentecost and the week beyond, including Bright Week, the Sunday feasts of the post-Resurrection appearances, the Ascension (on the fortieth day) and Pentecost (on the fiftieth).

The date of Pascha

The Orthodox Church calculates Pascha by the rule established at the First Council of Nicaea in 325: the first Sunday after the first full moon on or after the vernal equinox, computed using the Julian calendar conventions. As a result, Orthodox Pascha usually falls later than Western Easter, though in some years the dates coincide. The cycle of weekly hymnography in the eight tones (the Octoechos) also restarts its count from Pascha, so the whole liturgical year is in a sense governed by this movable cycle.

Related observances

Because the cycle determines the date of Lent, it also fixes such folk and parish customs as the pre-Lenten Cheesefare and Meatfare weeks, the services of Forgiveness Vespers, and the dates of the memorial Saturdays of the dead observed during Lent.
