Any linear electrical network containing only voltage sources, current sources, and resistances can be replaced at terminals A–B by an equivalent combination of a voltage source Vth in series with a resistance Rth.

Thévenin's theorem

Thévenin's theorem is a theorem in electrical circuit theory stating that, as far as a load connected to two terminals is concerned, any combination of voltage sources, current sources, and resistors with the two terminals is electrically equivalent to a single voltage source V in series connection with a single resistor R. For single-frequency AC systems, the theorem applies not just to resistances but also to generalised impedances.

The theorem was first derived by the German scientist Hermann von Helmholtz in 1853, then independently rediscovered in 1883 by the French telegraph engineer Léon Charles Thévenin (1857–1926), after whom it is named.

Calculating the equivalent

The equivalent voltage Vth is the open-circuit voltage at the output terminals of the original circuit. The equivalent resistance Rth can be found in two common ways:

  • Deactivate all independent sources (voltage sources short-circuited, current sources open-circuited) and compute the resistance looking back into the terminals.
  • Divide the open-circuit voltage by the short-circuit current at the terminals: Rth = Voc / Isc; this method also works when dependent sources are present.

Vth = Voc,   Rth = Voc / Isc

Relation to Norton's theorem

Thévenin's theorem is the dual of Norton's theorem, which replaces the network with a current source in parallel with a resistor. The two equivalents are interchangeable by source transformation: IN = Vth / Rth, with the same resistance value.

Limitations

The equivalent circuit behaves identically only externally; internally, power dissipation may differ. The theorem applies to linear networks; many real devices, such as power supplies, behave as Thévenin equivalents only within a limited operating range.
