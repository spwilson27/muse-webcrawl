Inca architecture

Inca architecture is the most significant pre-Columbian architecture in South America. The Incas inherited much of their architectural tradition from the Tiwanaku culture of the Lake Titicaca region and other Andean predecessors, and developed it into massive, precisely engineered stone construction adapted to the seismic conditions of the Andes.

Characteristics

Fine Inca masonry is famous for massive stones cut and fitted together so precisely, without mortar, that a knife blade cannot be inserted between them. Walls employ rectangular ashlar blocks or, in the most celebrated examples such as Sacsayhuamán above Cusco, enormous polygonal blocks fitted with interlocking joints. Walls typically slope slightly inward (battered) and doorways and niches are characteristically trapezoidal, wider at the base — features that contribute to earthquake resistance.

Buildings were generally single-story, of stone for important structures and adobe or fieldstone for ordinary ones, with steep thatched roofs of ichu grass supported on wooden beams. Settlements were planned with great plazas, kallanka (great halls), ushnu platforms, and storehouses (qollqa).

Notable sites

  • Machu Picchu — royal estate with temples, terraces, and precisely dressed stonework.
  • Sacsayhuamán — fortress-temple above Cusco with zigzag walls of megalithic blocks, some weighing over 100 tonnes.
  • Ollantaytambo, Pisac, and the Coricancha (Qorikancha) sun temple in Cusco, whose fine walls survive beneath a colonial convent.

The Inca road system (Qhapaq Ñan), some 30,000–40,000 km of roads with bridges and way stations, is an architectural and engineering achievement in its own right and is now a UNESCO World Heritage Site, as is Machu Picchu.
