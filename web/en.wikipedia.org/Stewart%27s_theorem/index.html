Stewart's theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Diagram of a triangle with a cevian to which Stewart's theorem applies.

Stewart's theorem gives a relation between the lengths of the sides of a triangle and the length of a cevian — a line segment from a vertex to the opposite side. It is named after the Scottish mathematician Matthew Stewart, who published it in 1746, though the result was probably known to Archimedes.

Statement

Let a, b, c be the side lengths of a triangle, with side a divided by a cevian of length d into segments of length m and n (with m adjacent to side c and n adjacent to side b). Then:

b²m + c²n = a(d² + mn)

Generations of students have remembered it by the mnemonic "man + dad = bomb + sick"? — more commonly rendered as "A man and his dad put a bomb in the sink": man + dad = bmb + cnc in an alternative arrangement.

Proof and applications

The theorem follows readily from the law of cosines applied to the two subtriangles, since the angles at the foot of the cevian are supplementary. Special cases include Apollonius's theorem, obtained when the cevian is a median (m = n), and the angle-bisector length formula. Stewart's theorem is a standard tool in olympiad geometry for computing cevian lengths.
