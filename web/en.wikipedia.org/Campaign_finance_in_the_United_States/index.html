Campaign finance in the United States

Campaign finance in the United States is the financing of electoral campaigns at the federal, state and local levels. At the federal level, campaign finance is regulated by Congress and administered by the Federal Election Commission (FEC), an independent federal agency. Sources of campaign funding include contributions from individuals, political action committees (PACs), parties, candidates' own money, and, for presidential candidates who opt in, public financing.

American law distinguishes between contributions given directly to candidates — which are capped — and independent expenditures made in support of or opposition to candidates, which the Supreme Court has held may not be limited as a matter of free speech under the First Amendment.

History

Early federal regulation began with the Tillman Act of 1907, which banned corporate contributions in federal elections. The Federal Corrupt Practices Act of 1910 and its 1925 revision imposed disclosure requirements, but enforcement was weak. Modern regulation dates from the Federal Election Campaign Act (FECA) of 1971, strengthened by amendments in 1974 after the Watergate scandal. FECA capped contributions, limited spending, created the FEC, and established public financing for presidential elections.

In Buckley v. Valeo (1976), the Supreme Court upheld contribution limits and disclosure as safeguards against corruption, but struck down limits on campaign spending and on candidates' use of personal funds, reasoning that spending money to disseminate political speech is protected by the First Amendment.

The BCRA and Citizens United

The Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002 (McCain–Feingold) banned "soft money" contributions to national parties and restricted "electioneering communications" — broadcast ads naming federal candidates near an election — when funded by corporations or unions.

In Citizens United v. FEC (2010), the Supreme Court held that corporations and unions may spend unlimited amounts on independent political advocacy, overturning bans dating to the mid-twentieth century. Together with the D.C. Circuit's decision in SpeechNow.org v. FEC, the ruling enabled the rise of super PACs — committees that may raise unlimited sums from individuals, corporations and unions so long as they do not contribute directly to or coordinate with candidates. In McCutcheon v. FEC (2014) the Court struck down aggregate limits on an individual's total contributions.

Types of money

  • Hard money: contributions regulated by FECA, subject to limits and disclosure, given directly to candidates or parties.
  • Soft money: funds raised outside FECA's limits for party-building activities; banned at the federal level by the BCRA.
  • Independent expenditures: spending advocating the election or defeat of a candidate, not coordinated with the campaign; unlimited after Citizens United.
  • Dark money: political spending by nonprofit organisations (such as 501(c)(4) groups) that are not required to disclose their donors.

Public financing

Presidential candidates may receive public funds from the Presidential Election Campaign Fund, financed by a voluntary checkoff on federal income-tax returns, if they agree to spending limits. Since 2008, when Barack Obama became the first major-party nominee to opt out of the general-election grant, most leading candidates have declined public financing because private fundraising yields more. Many states and cities, including New York City and Seattle, operate their own public financing or voucher programmes for local offices.

Criticism and reform proposals

Critics argue that the system gives disproportionate influence to wealthy donors and organised interests, and that unlimited outside spending undermines political equality. Defenders respond that spending is a form of speech and that regulation often entrenches incumbents. Reform proposals have included small-donor matching systems, mandatory donor disclosure for political nonprofits, and a constitutional amendment to overturn Citizens United.
