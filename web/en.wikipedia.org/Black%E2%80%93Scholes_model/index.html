Black–Scholes model

The Black–Scholes model, also known as the Black–Scholes–Merton model, is a mathematical model for pricing options contracts. Published in 1973 by Fischer Black and Myron Scholes, with foundational contributions by Robert Merton, it gives a theoretical estimate of the price of European-style options from the current stock price, strike price, time to expiry, risk-free rate, and volatility.

C = S·N(d₁) − K·e^(−rT)·N(d₂)

Assumptions

The model assumes the underlying asset follows geometric Brownian motion with constant volatility, markets are frictionless with no arbitrage, trading is continuous, and the risk-free interest rate is constant. From these assumptions a partial differential equation — the Black–Scholes equation — can be derived whose solution yields closed-form prices for call and put options.

Impact and criticism

The formula transformed financial practice, enabling the explosive growth of options exchanges from 1973 onward. Scholes and Merton received the 1997 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences (Black had died in 1995). Critics note that real markets exhibit volatility smiles, jumps, and fat tails not captured by the model, limitations implicated in discussions of the 1987 crash and the failure of Long-Term Capital Management in 1998. Practitioners nonetheless continue to use it as a standard quoting and hedging framework, with implied volatility as its key input.
