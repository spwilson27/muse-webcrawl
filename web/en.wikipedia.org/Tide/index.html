Tides are the rise and fall of sea levels caused by the combined effects of the gravitational forces exerted by the Moon and the Sun, and the rotation of the Earth. Most coastal locations experience two high tides and two low tides each lunar day (24 hours 50 minutes).

Mechanism

The Moon's tidal force creates two bulges in the oceans — beneath the Moon and on the opposite side of Earth — and as Earth rotates, coastal points pass through the bulges. The Sun also contributes: when Sun, Moon and Earth align (at new and full moon), their tides reinforce to give spring tides with the greatest range; at the quarter moons they partly cancel, giving neap tides. Local geography — funnel-shaped bays, shallow shelves and resonance, as in the Bay of Fundy — can amplify the range from under a metre to over 15 metres.

Prediction

Tides are predicted from harmonic constituents (such as M2 and S2) derived from long records of sea level. Tide tables are essential for navigation, fishing and coastal engineering. Tidal currents in constricted channels can be harnessed for power, as at the Rance Tidal Power Station in France (opened 1966) and Sihwa Lake in South Korea.

Other tides

Tides also occur in lakes (very small), in the solid Earth (earth tides of tens of centimetres), and in the atmosphere. Over geological time, tidal friction transfers angular momentum from Earth to the Moon, lengthening the day and pushing the Moon slowly outward.
