Virtue ethics
Branch: normative ethics
Key figures: Aristotle, Confucius, Aquinas, Elizabeth Anscombe, Alasdair MacIntyre
Central concepts: virtue, eudaimonia, phronesis (practical wisdom), the golden mean

Virtue ethics is a family of normative ethical theories that emphasise the character of the moral agent — their virtues and vices — rather than rules (deontology) or consequences (consequentialism) as the key element of ethical thinking. Its roots lie in Aristotle's Nicomachean Ethics and in Confucian thought.

Aristotelian framework

For Aristotle, a virtue is a settled disposition to choose the mean between excess and deficiency (courage between recklessness and cowardice), guided by phronesis, practical wisdom. Living virtuously constitutes eudaimonia — flourishing — the highest human good. Virtues are acquired by habituation, not merely taught.

Modern revival

Elizabeth Anscombe's 1958 paper "Modern Moral Philosophy" launched the contemporary revival; Alasdair MacIntyre's After Virtue (1981) and work by Philippa Foot, Rosalind Hursthouse and Martha Nussbaum developed it into a full rival to rule- and outcome-based theories. Critics press situationist psychology and ask whether virtue ethics gives enough guidance for specific dilemmas.
