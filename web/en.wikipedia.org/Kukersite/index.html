Kukersite is a light-brown marine oil shale that forms the economically most important rock of Estonia's vast oil shale deposits. Formed in a shallow Ordovician sea, it consists largely of the fossilized remains of the cyanobacterium Gloeocapsomorpha prisca, giving it an unusually high organic content and oil yield.

Geology

Kukersite occurs in the Kukruse Stage of the Ordovician in northern Estonia and across the border in Russia's Leningrad Oblast. Beds range from a few centimetres to several metres thick, interlayered with limestone. Its kerogen, dominated by algal material, yields up to roughly 300–350 litres of shale oil per tonne in laboratory assay — far above most oil shales.

Mining and use

Industrial mining began in Estonia in 1916 around Kohtla-Järve, and kukersite has since been the backbone of the Estonian energy sector: burned directly in power plants for electricity and retorted into shale oil and gas. The industry has left large ash hills and semi-coke dumps in Ida-Viru County, and its high carbon emissions have driven Estonia's gradual phase-down of oil shale power in favour of renewables.
