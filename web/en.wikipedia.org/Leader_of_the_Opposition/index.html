Leader of the Opposition

The Leader of the Opposition is a title traditionally held by the leader of the largest political party not in government in a Westminster system of parliamentary government. The holder is usually the person best positioned to become prime minister if the government falls or loses an election, and leads a "shadow cabinet" that mirrors the government ministers and scrutinises their policies.

Role

In most Westminster-style systems, the Leader of the Opposition is a formal, salaried office recognised in law or standing orders. Its functions include leading the opposition in debates, questioning the head of government (for example at Prime Minister's Questions in the United Kingdom), setting out alternative policies, and receiving confidential briefings on national security matters. In many jurisdictions the officeholder is a member of the privy council and is styled accordingly.

Selection

The Leader of the Opposition is normally the leader of the largest party in the legislature that is not part of the governing coalition. Where the identity of the largest opposition party is disputed, the speaker of the house may determine which member should be recognised. The concept has spread beyond Westminster to many parliamentary democracies, and variants exist in some presidential and semi-presidential systems, where the term may be an informal description of the leading figure in opposition.

History

The modern, formally recognised office developed in the United Kingdom during the 19th century; statutory recognition, including a salary, came with the Ministers of the Crown Act 1937. The phrase "His Majesty's Loyal Opposition" captures the constitutional idea that opposition to the government of the day is legitimate and loyal to the state.
