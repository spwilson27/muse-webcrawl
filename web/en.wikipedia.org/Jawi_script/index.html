Jawi (جاوي) is a writing system used for Malay and several other languages of Maritime Southeast Asia. It adapts the Arabic script with additional letters for sounds not found in Arabic, such as /tʃ/ (چ), /p/ (ڤ), /ɡ/ (ݢ), /ŋ/ (ڠ) and /ɲ/ (ڽ).

History

Jawi developed with the arrival of Islam in the Malay world; one of the earliest examples is the Terengganu Inscription Stone, dated to the early 14th century. For centuries Jawi was the principal script of Malay administration, correspondence, literature and Islamic scholarship across the sultanates of the region, including on Java's coasts and the Malay Peninsula.

Modern use

During the 20th century, the Latin alphabet (Rumi) largely displaced Jawi for everyday writing in Malaysia, Indonesia and Singapore. Jawi remains official in Brunei alongside Rumi, appears on Malaysian currency and signage, and is taught in Malaysian primary schools. Debates over Jawi instruction have periodically been politically sensitive in Malaysia.

Characteristics

Jawi is written right to left and, like Arabic, normally omits short vowels, though conventions exist for marking them. Letters change shape depending on their position in a word. The script is not identical to the Perso-Arabic adaptations used for Urdu or Persian, having its own letter inventory and spelling conventions standardized by bodies such as Dewan Bahasa dan Pustaka in Malaysia.
