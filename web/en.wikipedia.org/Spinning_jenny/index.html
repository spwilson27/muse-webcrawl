The spinning jenny is a multi-spindle spinning frame invented by James Hargreaves in Lancashire, England, around 1764. By allowing one worker to spin many threads at once — first eight, eventually over a hundred — it dramatically increased yarn production and was one of the key developments in the mechanisation of textile manufacturing during the early Industrial Revolution.

Invention

Tradition says Hargreaves was inspired when his daughter Jenny accidentally knocked over a spinning wheel, and he noticed the spindle continued to revolve upright — suggesting spindles could be mounted vertically and multiplied. He built the machine in secret; spinners who feared for their livelihoods broke into his house and smashed his jennies in 1768, but he patented the machine in 1770 and it spread rapidly.

Operation

Rovings are wound on bobbins and drawn through a moving carriage while a rotating wheel drives all spindles together; the operator draws out, twists and winds the threads in sequence. Jennies produced yarn fine enough for weft but weaker than water-frame yarn, so jenny yarn was often combined with frame-spun warp.

Legacy

Together with Arkwright's water frame (1769) and Crompton's spinning mule (1779), the jenny moved spinning from cottages into factories and helped launch the modern factory system.
