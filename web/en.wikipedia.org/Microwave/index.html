Microwave

Microwaves are a form of electromagnetic radiation with wavelengths ranging from about one meter to one millimeter; corresponding to frequencies between 300 MHz and 300 GHz. Different sources define different frequency ranges as microwaves; the above broad definition includes both ultra high frequency (UHF) and extremely high frequency (EHF, millimeter waves). Microwaves travel by line-of-sight and are not reflected by the ionosphere, so their practical communication range on Earth is limited to roughly the visible horizon.

Applications

Microwaves are widely used in point-to-point telecommunications, satellite communications, radar, radio astronomy, and navigation. Their best-known domestic application is the microwave oven, which heats food by dielectric heating at frequencies around 2.45 GHz. Other applications include Wi-Fi and Bluetooth wireless networking, microwave ablation in medicine, industrial heating and drying, and masers, the microwave predecessors of lasers.

History

The generation and study of microwaves grew out of 19th-century electromagnetism: James Clerk Maxwell predicted the waves in 1864, Heinrich Hertz demonstrated them in the laboratory in the 1880s, and Jagadish Chandra Bose conducted pioneering millimetre-wave experiments in the 1890s. Radar development in the 1930s and 1940s, including the cavity magnetron, made microwave technology strategically important in the Second World War.
