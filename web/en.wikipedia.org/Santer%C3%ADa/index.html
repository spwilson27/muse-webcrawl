Santería

Santería, also known as Regla de Ocha, La Regla de Ifá or Lucumí, is an Afro-Cuban religion that developed in Cuba during the Spanish colonial period. It fuses the traditional religion of the Yoruba people of West Africa — brought to the Caribbean by enslaved Africans — with elements of Roman Catholicism and Spiritism. Its practitioners, called santeros (male) and santeras (female), venerate a pantheon of spirit deities known as orishas, each associated with forces of nature and often identified with a Catholic saint.

Beliefs

Santería recognises a supreme creator, Olodumare (Olofi), who is remote from daily affairs; worship focuses on the orishas, intermediaries who govern aspects of the world and human life. Among the best known are Eleguá, opener of paths; Yemayá, mother and orisha of the sea; Oshún, of rivers and love; Changó, of thunder and fire; and Obatalá, of peace and purity. Each devotee is understood to be a child of a particular guardian orisha, determined through divination.

Central concepts include ashé, the vital spiritual energy flowing through all things, and systems of divination such as the dilogún (cowrie shells) and Ifá, practised by high priests called babalawos. Rituals involve offerings of food, flowers and — in some rites — animal sacrifice, along with drumming, song and dance in Lucumí, a liturgical Yoruba-derived language, at ceremonies where orishas may mount (possess) initiated devotees.

History and spread

The religion crystallised in nineteenth-century Cuba as enslaved Yoruba people, called Lucumí, maintained their worship under the cover of Catholic saints' cults in mutual-aid societies called cabildos. After the Cuban Revolution of 1959, migration carried Santería to Miami, New York, Puerto Rico, Venezuela and beyond, where it has grown and influenced music, art and popular culture. In the United States, the Supreme Court's 1993 decision in Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah protected its ritual animal sacrifice under the First Amendment — a landmark of religious-freedom law.
