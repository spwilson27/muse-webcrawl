Saul Alinsky

Saul David Alinsky (January 30, 1909 – June 12, 1972) was an American community activist and political theorist. His work through the Chicago-based Industrial Areas Foundation earned him the title of founder of modern community organizing. He is widely considered to have founded the "Chicago school" of community organizing, which later influenced labor, faith-based, and civic movements across the United States.

Early life

Alinsky was born in Chicago to Russian Jewish immigrants and raised in an observant household. He studied archaeology and criminal sociology at the University of Chicago, graduating in 1930, and spent two years in graduate study before leaving to work for the Illinois prison system and the Institute for Juvenile Research, where he studied the gangs of Al Capone's Chicago.

Organizing career

In 1939 Alinsky organized the Back of the Yards Neighborhood Council in the impoverished district beside Chicago's stockyards, uniting labour unions, churches, and ethnic associations into a single pressure group that won concessions from the meatpacking industry and City Hall. In 1940, backed by philanthropist Marshall Field III, he founded the Industrial Areas Foundation (IAF), which trained organizers and seeded community organizations nationwide, including The Woodlawn Organization in Chicago and FIGHT in Rochester, New York, whose campaign against Eastman Kodak over minority hiring drew national attention. Alinsky's organizing philosophy was pragmatic and confrontational: build independent people's organizations, let local leaders set the agenda, and target specific, winnable grievances while keeping conflict entertaining and pointed.

Rules for Radicals

His books Reveille for Radicals (1946) and Rules for Radicals (1971) distilled decades of practice into doctrine. The latter listed thirteen tactical rules, among them "Power is not only what you have but what the enemy thinks you have," "Ridicule is man's most potent weapon," and "Pick the target, freeze it, personalize it, and polarize it." The book opened with a notorious acknowledgment of Lucifer as "the very first radical." Alinsky died of a heart attack in 1972, shortly after the book's publication.

Legacy

Alinsky's methods have been adopted across the political spectrum, from labour and civil-rights organizers to the conservative Tea Party movement, and both Hillary Clinton (who wrote her senior thesis on him) and Barack Obama (who worked as a community organizer in Chicago within the tradition Alinsky created) have been linked to his legacy. He remains a polarizing figure, celebrated as a champion of the powerless and attacked as a cynical tactician of division.
