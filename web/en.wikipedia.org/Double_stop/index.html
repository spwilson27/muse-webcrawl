In music, a double stop is the technique of playing two notes simultaneously on a stringed instrument such as a violin, viola, cello, or double bass. To perform one, the player "stops" two strings at once by pressing them down with the fingers and draws the bow across both so that they sound together. Playing three or four strings at once is called a triple stop or quadruple stop respectively, though on instruments with a curved bridge such chords are usually arpeggiated or broken.

Technique

Double stops require the fingers to stop two adjacent strings at precisely the interval demanded, most commonly a third, fifth, sixth, octave, or tenth. Because intonation errors are doubled and clearly audible, extensive double-stop passages are a hallmark of advanced technique; violinists traditionally practise them using exercises such as those of Otakar Ševčík. A special case is the drone, in which a melody note is played against an open string sounded continuously — common in folk fiddling and in Baroque music.

In the repertoire

Double stops feature throughout the string literature: in Bach's Sonatas and Partitas for solo violin, which exploit them (with triple and quadruple stops) to imply full harmony on a single instrument; in Paganini's Caprices; in the concertos of Brahms, Sibelius, and Tchaikovsky; and in cello works from Bach's suites onward. Composers notate double stops with two noteheads on a shared stem or on opposing stems, and passages may be marked divisi instead when they are meant for separate players.
