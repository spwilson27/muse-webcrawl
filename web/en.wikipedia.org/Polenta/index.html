Polenta

TypePorridge
Place of originItaly
Main ingredientsCornmeal, water or stock, salt

Polenta is a dish of boiled cornmeal that was historically made from other grains. It may be eaten as a hot porridge or allowed to cool and solidify into a loaf that can be baked, fried, or grilled. Polenta is a staple of Northern Italian cuisine, especially in Lombardy, Piedmont, Veneto, and Friuli.

History

Before maize arrived from the Americas in the sixteenth century, similar dishes were made from millet, spelt, chestnut flour, or buckwheat. Cornmeal polenta became the food of the rural poor in northern Italy, where a monotonous polenta diet could cause pellagra, a niacin-deficiency disease. It later spread to Central Europe, the Balkans, and, through Italian emigration, to Argentina, Brazil, and Uruguay.

Preparation

Coarse cornmeal is slowly whisked into salted boiling water or stock and stirred for up to an hour until thick. It is traditionally finished with butter and cheese such as Parmesan or Taleggio. Cooled polenta sets firm and can be sliced and fried or grilled, often served with mushrooms, ragù, or braised meats.

Varieties

Yellow cornmeal is most common, but white polenta (from white maize) is typical of Venice, and dark polenta nera is made with buckwheat in Valtellina, where it is enriched with cheese and butter in the dish polenta taragna.
