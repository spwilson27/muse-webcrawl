Soviet space program

The Soviet space program was the national space program of the Soviet Union, active from the 1930s rocketry experiments until the dissolution of the USSR in 1991. It achieved many firsts in space exploration: the first intercontinental ballistic missile (R-7), the first satellite (Sputnik 1, 1957), the first animal in orbit (Laika, 1957), the first human in space (Yuri Gagarin, 1961), the first woman in space (Valentina Tereshkova, 1963), the first spacewalk (Alexei Leonov, 1965), and the first space station (Salyut 1, 1971).

Organization

Unlike the American program under a single agency (NASA), the Soviet program was divided among competing design bureaus (OKBs) led by figures such as Sergei Korolev, Mikhail Yangel, Vladimir Chelomei, and Valentin Glushko. Korolev, the "Chief Designer," led OKB-1 and masterminded the early successes based on the R-7 Semyorka rocket family. The program was highly secretive; failures were concealed, and Korolev's identity was a state secret until his death in 1966.

Human spaceflight

After Gagarin's Vostok 1 flight, the USSR developed the Voskhod and then the Soyuz spacecraft, which — in modernized form — remains in service with Russia today. Ambitious crewed lunar efforts, built around the giant N1 rocket, failed after four launch explosions and were cancelled in the 1970s. The Soviet Union then concentrated on space stations: the Salyut series, Almaz military stations, and finally Mir (1986–2001), the first modular station. The Buran space shuttle flew once, uncrewed, in 1988.

Legacy

After 1991, the program's assets were divided mainly between Russia (Roscosmos) and Ukraine. The Soviet program's legacy includes the Soyuz and Progress spacecraft, the Proton rocket, and the groundwork for the International Space Station.
