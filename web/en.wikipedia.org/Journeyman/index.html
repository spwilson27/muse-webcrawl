Journeyman

A journeyman is a worker, skilled in a given trade or craft, who has successfully completed an official apprenticeship qualification. Journeymen are considered competent and authorised to work in their trade, but they have not yet reached the rank of master craftsman. The term derives not from travel but from the French journée, meaning a day — reflecting that journeymen were originally paid by the day — although in some traditions, notably the German Wanderjahre ("journeyman years"), journeymen did travel from town to town to gain experience with different masters.

Historical guild system

In the medieval guild system, a young person became an apprentice to a master craftsman for a period of years — often seven — in exchange for instruction, food, and lodging. On completing the apprenticeship, the apprentice became a journeyman, free to sell his labour to masters for wages. To advance to master, a journeyman typically had to produce a "masterpiece" judged by the guild and pay substantial fees. In German-speaking Europe, journeymen traditionally undertook years of travel (the Walz), working for masters in different towns; the custom survives in a small form today among carpenters and other tradespeople, who wear distinctive traditional clothing.

Modern usage

In modern trades such as electricians, plumbers, and carpenters, "journeyman" remains a formal licensing grade between apprentice and master, particularly in the United States, Canada, and Australia. A journeyman licence generally requires several years of documented experience and passing an examination. In sports and other professions, the word is also used informally for a competent but unexceptional performer who moves between teams or employers.
