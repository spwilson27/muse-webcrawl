Caesarean section

Other namesC-section, caesarean delivery
SpecialtyObstetrics

A caesarean section, also called a C-section, is a surgical procedure by which one or more babies are delivered through an incision in the mother's abdomen. It is often performed because vaginal delivery would put the baby or mother at risk. Reasons include obstructed labour, twin pregnancy, high blood pressure in the mother, breech birth, and problems with the placenta or umbilical cord. A caesarean delivery may be performed based upon the shape of the mother's pelvis or history of a previous C-section.

Procedure

The operation is most often performed under regional anaesthesia (spinal or epidural). A horizontal incision is made through the abdominal wall and the lower segment of the uterus; the baby is delivered, the placenta removed, and the incisions closed. The procedure typically takes 30–60 minutes. Recovery is longer than after vaginal birth, and risks include infection, bleeding, adhesions, and complications in future pregnancies, such as uterine rupture and abnormal placentation.

Prevalence and debate

Globally, caesarean rates have risen steadily; by the late 2010s roughly one in five births worldwide was by caesarean, with rates above 40% in some countries and below 10% in much of sub-Saharan Africa, where lack of access endangers mothers. The World Health Organization has stated that caesarean sections should ideally be undertaken only when medically necessary, and that population rates above about 10–15% are not associated with further reductions in mortality — a balance between overuse in some settings and dangerous underuse in others.

Etymology

The name is popularly linked to Julius Caesar, supposedly delivered this way, but this is almost certainly a myth — his mother Aurelia lived long after his birth, and in antiquity the operation was performed only on dead or dying mothers, as required by Roman law (the Lex Caesarea), from which the name more plausibly derives.
