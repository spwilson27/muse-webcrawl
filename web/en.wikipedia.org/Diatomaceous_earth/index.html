Diatomaceous earth

Diatomaceous earth consists of the siliceous skeletons of diatoms.

Diatomaceous earth (also called diatomite, or kieselguhr when used in industry) is a naturally occurring, soft, siliceous sedimentary rock and powder composed of the fossilised cell walls (frustules) of diatoms, a major group of single-celled algae. Deposits formed in ancient lakes and oceans are mined in many countries; the largest producer is the United States.

Formation and properties

As diatoms die, their silica frustules sink and accumulate in thick beds, preserving an intricate microscopic architecture. The resulting powder is lightweight, highly porous, chemically inert, and able to absorb several times its weight in liquid — properties that drive nearly all its applications.

Uses

The largest use is as a filter aid in clarifying beer, wine, sugar syrups, pharmaceuticals, and swimming-pool and drinking water. As a mechanical insecticide, the sharp frustule particles damage insects' waxy cuticles, causing fatal dehydration; it is widely used against bed bugs, fleas, and stored-grain pests. Diatomaceous earth also serves as a mild abrasive (historically in toothpaste and metal polishes), an absorbent for spills and cat litter, a filler and anti-caking agent, thermal insulation, and a porous support for catalysts. Its most famous historical use is as the absorbent stabiliser in Alfred Nobel's dynamite (patented 1867), which made nitroglycerin safe to handle.

Safety

Food-grade diatomaceous earth is amorphous silica and is generally considered safe, but calcined grades contain crystalline silica (cristobalite), whose dust is a respiratory hazard and a known cause of silicosis with chronic inhalation; dust precautions are required when handling it.
