Edmond Halley

Edmond Halley (8 November 1656 – 25 January 1742) was an English astronomer, mathematician, and physicist. He catalogued the stars of the southern hemisphere, charted Earth's magnetic field, produced the first mortality tables used by actuaries, and is best remembered for computing the orbit of the comet that now bears his name.

Edmond Halley, Astronomer Royal

Early work

Educated at St Paul's School and Queen's College, Oxford, Halley sailed in 1676 to the island of Saint Helena, where he observed a transit of Mercury and catalogued some 341 southern stars, publishing his southern catalogue in 1678 and earning election to the Royal Society at the age of 22.

Newton and the Principia

In 1684 Halley visited Isaac Newton at Cambridge and learned that Newton had already derived the inverse-square law of gravitation. Halley encouraged, edited, and personally financed the publication of Newton's Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica in 1687 — one of the most consequential acts of scientific patronage in history.

The comet

In his Synopsis of the Astronomy of Comets (1705), Halley showed that the bright comets of 1531, 1607, and 1682 followed nearly identical orbits and were in fact a single object returning roughly every 76 years. He predicted its return in 1758; when the comet reappeared on schedule, sixteen years after his death, it was named Halley's Comet in his honour.

Other contributions

Halley proposed using transits of Venus to measure the astronomical unit, commanded the Paramour on pioneering voyages to map magnetic variation in the Atlantic, constructed a diving bell, compiled the first life tables from Breslau records, and succeeded John Flamsteed as Astronomer Royal in 1720, serving at Greenwich until his death.
