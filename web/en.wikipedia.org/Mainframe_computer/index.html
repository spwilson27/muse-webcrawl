Mainframe computer

A mainframe computer, informally called a mainframe or big iron, is a computer used primarily by large organizations for critical applications such as bulk data processing for tasks such as censuses, industry and consumer statistics, enterprise resource planning, and large-scale transaction processing. They are larger and have more processing power than other classes of computers: minicomputers, servers, workstations, and personal computers.

Design

Modern mainframe design is characterized less by raw single-task computational speed and more by redundant internal engineering resulting in high reliability and security, extensive input-output facilities with the ability to offload to separate engines, strict backward compatibility with older software, and high hardware and computational utilization rates to support massive throughput. These machines can often run for years without interruption, with repairs and hardware upgrades taking place during normal operation.

Characteristics

Mainframes are designed to handle very high volumes of input and output (I/O) and emphasize throughput computing. A single mainframe can replace dozens or even hundreds of smaller servers. Mainframes can run multiple operating systems simultaneously and support virtualization extensively, a capability pioneered by IBM's CP-40 and CP-67 systems. Processor capacity is sometimes expressed in MIPS (millions of instructions per second) or, more recently, MSUs (millions of service units).

History

The first mainframes appeared in the early 1950s, with systems such as the UNIVAC I and IBM 701. IBM's System/360 family, announced in 1964, established the modern mainframe architecture of a compatible family of computers spanning a range of performance levels, all running the same operating system. Successors included System/370, System/390, and today's IBM Z series running z/OS and Linux. Other manufacturers included the "BUNCH" companies (Burroughs, UNIVAC, NCR, Control Data Corporation, and Honeywell) and later vendors such as Fujitsu and Hitachi.

Uses

Banks, airlines, insurance companies and governments use mainframes for transaction processing, reservation systems and batch jobs such as payroll. Despite predictions of their demise, mainframes remain central to industries requiring extreme reliability, availability and serviceability (RAS).
