Valdemar II (1170 – 28 March 1241), called Valdemar the Victorious (Danish: Valdemar Sejr), was King of Denmark from 1202 until his death in 1241. The younger son of Valdemar I and brother of Canute VI, he presided over the greatest territorial extent of the medieval Danish kingdom and over important legal reforms.

Reign and conquests

Valdemar succeeded his brother in 1202, having already served as Duke of Schleswig. He continued Danish expansion around the Baltic: Danish rule was extended over Holstein and the north German coast, and in 1219 he led a crusade to Estonia. Danish tradition holds that during the Battle of Lyndanisse (present-day Tallinn) in 1219 the Danish flag, the Dannebrog, fell from the sky at a critical moment, rallying his forces to victory — a legend that made Valdemar the king associated with the origin of the national flag. Danish Estonia remained a duchy of the crown until it was sold to the Teutonic Order in 1346.

Capture and decline

In 1223 Valdemar and his eldest son were kidnapped while hunting on the island of Lyø by Count Henry of Schwerin and held for ransom. Denmark was forced to surrender most of its north German conquests and pay a heavy ransom. An attempt to recover the lost territories ended in defeat at the Battle of Bornhöved in 1227, confirming the contraction of Danish power in Germany, though Denmark retained Estonia and Rügen.

Lawgiver and legacy

Valdemar is remembered as a lawgiver: the Law of Jutland (Jyske Lov), promulgated in 1241 shortly before his death, opens with the famous words "With law shall the land be built" and remained in force in Jutland until 1683. He also commissioned a cadastral survey of the kingdom, King Valdemar's Census Book (c. 1231), a major source for medieval Danish society. He died at Vordingborg in 1241 and was buried in St. Bendt's Church, Ringsted.
