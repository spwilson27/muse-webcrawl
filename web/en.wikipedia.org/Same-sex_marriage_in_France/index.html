Same-sex marriage has been legal in France since 18 May 2013, making France the thirteenth country in the world to allow same-sex couples to marry. The legislation, officially Law No. 2013-404 and often called the Loi Taubira after Justice Minister Christiane Taubira, also granted married same-sex couples adoption rights. It followed a fierce national debate and some of the largest protest marches of the Fifth Republic.

Background

France introduced the civil solidarity pact (PACS) in 1999, a registered partnership open to both same-sex and opposite-sex couples. In 2004, the mayor of Bègles, Noël Mamère, performed a same-sex marriage that was later annulled by the courts. François Hollande included marriage equality in his 2012 presidential platform.

The 2013 law and the protests

The bill was introduced in November 2012 and provoked months of heated parliamentary sessions and mass demonstrations. The opposition movement La Manif pour tous ("The Demo for All") drew hundreds of thousands of marchers to Paris on several occasions, while supporters held large counter-rallies. The National Assembly approved the final text on 23 April 2013; the Constitutional Council upheld it, and President Hollande signed it into law on 17 May 2013, with publication the next day.

Implementation

The first legal same-sex wedding took place in Montpellier on 29 May 2013. In the law's first decade, more than 70,000 same-sex marriages were celebrated in France, accounting for roughly 3% of all marriages. The law also applies to most French overseas territories, and France recognises same-sex marriages performed abroad.
