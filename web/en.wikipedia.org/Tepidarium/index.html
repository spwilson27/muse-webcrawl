Tepidarium

The tepidarium was the warm (tepidus) bathroom of the Roman thermae, heated by an underfloor hypocaust system rather than by direct contact with hot water. Typically, bathers would pass through the tepidarium between the cold frigidarium and the hot caldarium, using it to acclimatise gradually to changes in temperature. The room was often richly decorated and also served for anointing the body with oils.

Description

The tepidarium was normally a large, vaulted hall warmed indirectly: hot air from furnaces circulated beneath a raised floor (suspensura) supported on brick pillars and through hollow tiles in the walls, keeping the room at a moderate, even heat. In the standard bathing sequence, a visitor undressed in the apodyterium, exercised, then progressed through the warm tepidarium to the hot caldarium — where a hot plunge bath and a labrum of cold water stood — and finally cooled down in the frigidarium. Some scholars have suggested the tepidarium also functioned as a transitional space in which bathers rested and were massaged after the hotter rooms.

Notable examples

Well-preserved tepidaria survive at Pompeii, in both the Stabian Baths and the Forum Baths, the latter notable for its bronze brazier and benches donated by Marcus Nigidius Vaccula. The great imperial thermae of Rome — those of Caracalla and Diocletian — placed their tepidaria between the frigidarium and the caldarium on the central axis. A room in the Baths of Caracalla long identified as a tepidarium illustrates the monumental scale of the imperial examples.
