The Battle of Civitate was fought on 18 June 1053 in southern Italy between the Norman forces of the County of Apulia and Calabria and a coalition army raised by Pope Leo IX. The Normans won a crushing victory and captured the Pope himself, an outcome that paradoxically led within six years to a papal–Norman alliance that legitimised the Norman conquest of southern Italy.

Background

Norman adventurers had seized much of Apulia from the Byzantines and were raiding the borders of the papal Benevento. Pope Leo IX, alarmed at their expansion and abuses, formed an alliance with the Byzantine catapan Argyrus and Emperor Henry III (who sent Swabian troops), and marched south in person at the head of a mixed army of Italians, Lombards, Swabians and other Germans. The Normans, led by Count Humphrey of Hauteville with Robert Guiscard and Richard Drengot of Aversa, were actually short of food and offered to negotiate and hold their lands from the Pope as vassals; Leo, confident of Byzantine support that never arrived, refused.

The battle

Near Civitate on the Fortore river the Norman cavalry — small in number but heavily armoured and fighting in disciplined squadrons — attacked. Guiscard, commanding the left wing (by some accounts the reserve), routed the Italian infantry, while Richard of Aversa smashed the Swabian infantry after a hard fight; the papal army disintegrated. Leo watched the defeat from Civitate's walls and was surrendered by the townspeople to the victors.

Aftermath

The Normans treated their captive with elaborate reverence, escorting him to Benevento where he remained their honoured prisoner for nine months, until he recognised their conquests and lifted his excommunication before his death in 1054. The battle convinced both sides of their need of each other: in 1059, at the Treaty of Melfi, Pope Nicholas II formally invested Guiscard as Duke of Apulia, Calabria and Sicily.
