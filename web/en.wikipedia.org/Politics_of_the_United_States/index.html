Politics of the United States

The politics of the United States functions within a framework of a federal democratic republic and a presidential system of government, established by the Constitution of 1787. The federal government is composed of three branches — legislative, executive, and judicial — whose powers are vested in the Congress, the president, and the federal courts respectively, under a system of separation of powers and checks and balances.

Since the mid-19th century, American politics has been dominated by two major political parties: the Democratic Party and the Republican Party. These parties, along with smaller third parties, contest elections at the federal, state, and local levels. Political scientists describe the United States as having a two-party system arising largely from winner-take-all elections and single-member districts.

Constitutional framework

The Constitution of the United States is the supreme law of the land. It establishes a federal system in which sovereignty is shared between the national government and the governments of the fifty states. The first ten amendments, known as the Bill of Rights, guarantee fundamental civil liberties. Federalism, enumerated powers, and judicial review — established by the Supreme Court in Marbury v. Madison (1803) — remain central features of the political order.

Political culture

American political culture is commonly described as resting on the ideals of liberty, equality, individualism, democracy, and the rule of law — a constellation of values the Danish sociologist Gunnar Myrdal called the "American Creed." Ideological debate in the United States generally takes place within a broad liberal consensus, with the main divide between a center-left "liberal" tradition (associated with the Democratic Party) and a center-right "conservative" tradition (associated with the Republican Party).

Elections and parties

Federal elections are held every two years. The president and vice president are elected every four years through the Electoral College; all 435 seats in the House of Representatives and one-third of the 100 seats in the Senate are contested in each cycle. Campaign finance, primary elections, and redistricting (including gerrymandering) are persistent subjects of political debate.
