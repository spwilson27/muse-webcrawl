Guided bus

A guided bus is a bus capable of being steered by external means, usually on a dedicated track or roll way known as a guided busway, which allows the vehicle to run at speed in a narrow right-of-way while still being able to leave the guideway and operate on ordinary streets. Guided busways are a form of bus rapid transit, intended to offer some of the capacity and reliability of light rail at lower cost.

Guidance systems

The most common system is kerb guidance, developed in Germany in the 1970s (the Spurbus in Essen) and first used in Britain in the 1980s: small horizontal guide wheels mounted ahead of the front axle press against vertical concrete kerbs flanking the track, steering the bus mechanically so the driver need not steer on the guided section. Other technologies include optical guidance, in which a camera follows a painted dashed line (as on systems in Rouen and Las Vegas), magnetic guidance using markers embedded in the road, and central rail guidance, in which a single steel rail guides the vehicle — the principle behind the French "tram on tyres" systems in Nancy and Caen, the latter of which was converted to a conventional tramway.

Notable systems

The O-Bahn Busway in Adelaide, Australia, opened in stages from 1986, is the world's longest guided busway at about 12 kilometres and carries buses at speeds up to 100 km/h. In England, the Cambridgeshire Guided Busway, opened in 2011 along a former railway line between Cambridge and St Ives, became the longest kerb-guided busway in Britain; other British systems operate in Leeds, Bradford, Crawley (Fastway), Luton–Dunstable, and the Leigh–Manchester corridor. Proponents emphasize lower construction cost than rail and the ability of buses to fan out onto streets at the ends of the guideway; critics argue that capacities remain below rail and that concrete guideways have significant embodied costs.
