Genetic cross

A genetic cross is the deliberate mating of two individuals (or, in plants, the controlled pollination of one plant by another) in order to study the inheritance of traits in their offspring. Crosses were the central experimental tool of classical genetics, from Gregor Mendel's experiments with pea plants in the 1860s onward.

Types of cross

A monohybrid cross follows the inheritance of a single trait, whereas a dihybrid cross follows two traits simultaneously and tests whether they assort independently. A test cross mates an individual showing the dominant phenotype with a homozygous recessive individual, revealing the unknown genotype from the phenotypes of the progeny. A backcross mates a hybrid offspring with one of its parents or a genetically equivalent individual, and reciprocal crosses swap which parent contributes which sex to test for sex-linked or maternal inheritance.

Generations and ratios

The parents are designated the P generation; their offspring are the F1 (first filial) generation, and offspring of an F1 cross form the F2 generation. Classical results include the 3:1 phenotypic ratio of a monohybrid F2 and the 9:3:3:1 ratio of a dihybrid F2 under independent assortment. Outcomes are commonly predicted with a Punnett square.

Applications

Beyond basic research, controlled crosses underpin plant and animal breeding, the mapping of genes to chromosomes, and the study of genetic disorders.
