The English Reformation was a series of events in 16th-century England by which the Church of England broke away from the authority of the pope and the Catholic Church. These events were, in part, associated with the wider European Protestant Reformation, but unlike many other cases the English Reformation began from political motives: Henry VIII's desire for an annulment of his marriage to Catherine of Aragon, which Pope Clement VII refused to grant.

Course of events

Through acts of Parliament in 1533–1534 — culminating in the Act of Supremacy (1534), which declared the king Supreme Head of the Church of England — Henry severed English jurisdiction from Rome. Under Thomas Cromwell, the monasteries were dissolved (1536–1541) and their wealth transferred to the Crown. Doctrine under Henry remained largely Catholic, but under Edward VI (1547–1553) and Archbishop Thomas Cranmer the church moved in a distinctly Protestant direction, with the Book of Common Prayer (1549, revised 1552) and the Forty-two Articles. Mary I (1553–1558) restored papal authority and persecuted Protestants, earning her the name "Bloody Mary". The Elizabethan Religious Settlement of 1559 re-established royal supremacy and a moderately Protestant church, whose character was later codified in the Thirty-nine Articles (1571).

Legacy

The Reformation reshaped English religion, politics and culture: it entrenched parliamentary involvement in religion, fuelled later conflicts including the English Civil War and Puritan movements, and produced enduring works such as the Authorized (King James) Version of the Bible (1611). Historians debate whether it was a rapid act of state, as in A. G. Dickens's account, or a slow, contested process imposed on a reluctant population, as argued by Eamon Duffy and Christopher Haigh.
