Flight control system

A flight control system is the collection of mechanical, hydraulic, and electronic components by which a pilot controls the attitude and flight path of an aircraft. Conventional fixed-wing aircraft flight control systems consist of flight control surfaces, the cockpit controls, connecting linkages, and the necessary operating mechanisms to control an aircraft's direction in flight. Aircraft engine controls are also considered flight controls as they change speed.

Primary controls

The fundamental control surfaces are the ailerons, which control roll about the longitudinal axis; the elevator, which controls pitch about the lateral axis; and the rudder, which controls yaw about the vertical axis. In the cockpit these are operated by the control column or stick and the rudder pedals.

Secondary controls

Secondary flight controls include flaps and slats, which increase lift at low speeds; spoilers, which reduce lift and increase drag; air brakes; and adjustable trim tabs and stabilizers, which relieve the pilot of the need to maintain constant control forces.

Mechanical and powered systems

Early aircraft used cables, pulleys, and pushrods to connect the cockpit controls directly to the surfaces. As aircraft grew larger and faster, aerodynamic forces exceeded human strength, leading first to aerodynamic servo tabs and then to fully powered hydraulic systems. Modern airliners and military aircraft use fly-by-wire systems, in which pilot inputs are converted into electrical signals interpreted by flight control computers that command hydraulic or electric actuators.
