Medicaid is a joint federal and state program in the United States that provides health coverage to people with low income and limited resources, including children, pregnant women, parents, seniors and individuals with disabilities. Established in 1965 alongside Medicare by amendments to the Social Security Act under President Lyndon B. Johnson, it is the largest source of health coverage in the United States and the single largest payer for long-term care and for births.

Structure

Each state administers its own Medicaid program within broad federal requirements, so eligibility and benefits vary by state. The federal government pays a matching share of costs (the FMAP), ranging from 50% in wealthy states to over 75% in poorer ones, with a 90% match for adults covered under expansion. Most enrollees receive care through private managed-care organizations contracted by states.

Expansion under the Affordable Care Act

The Affordable Care Act of 2010 originally required states to expand Medicaid to nearly all adults with income up to 138% of the federal poverty level; the Supreme Court's 2012 decision in NFIB v. Sebelius made expansion optional. Most states have since adopted expansion, and studies associate it with large gains in coverage and improvements in access to care, while debates continue over cost, work requirements and state waivers.

Related programs

The Children's Health Insurance Program (CHIP, 1997) covers children in families earning too much for Medicaid but too little for private insurance. Medicaid is distinct from Medicare, the federal program for people 65 and older, though "dual eligibles" are enrolled in both.
