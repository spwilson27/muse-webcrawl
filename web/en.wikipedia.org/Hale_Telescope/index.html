Hale Telescope

Location: Palomar Observatory, California
Diameter: 200 inches (5.1 m)
First light: 1948
Named after: George Ellery Hale

The Hale Telescope is a 200-inch (5.1 m), f/3.3 reflecting telescope at the Palomar Observatory in San Diego County, California, US, named after astronomer George Ellery Hale. With funding from the Rockefeller Foundation in 1928, he orchestrated the planning, design, and construction of the observatory. The Hale was the world's largest effective telescope for more than three decades, from its completion in 1948 until 1976.

History

Hale had previously built the 100-inch Hooker telescope at Mount Wilson, which Edwin Hubble used to discover the expansion of the universe. For Palomar, Corning Glass Works cast a 200-inch Pyrex mirror blank in 1934; the first blank cracked during cooling and the successful second casting became an engineering landmark, transported across the country by rail to national attention. World War II delayed completion; the mirror was figured at Caltech's optical shop and the telescope was dedicated in 1948, the year after Hale's death.

Design

The telescope uses an equatorial mount of the horseshoe type, allowing it to track stars across the sky. Its ribbed Pyrex mirror weighs 14.5 tons, supported so that it keeps its figure as the tube tilts. The Serrurier truss tube keeps the primary and secondary optics aligned under changing gravity loads.

Scientific achievements

With the Hale, astronomers identified the first known quasar, 3C 273, measured distances that refined the cosmic distance scale, and studied the nuclei of galaxies. Modern upgrades, including adaptive optics, have kept the 75-year-old telescope scientifically productive; it remains one of the most productive ground-based telescopes.
