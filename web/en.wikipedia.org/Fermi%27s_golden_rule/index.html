Fermi's golden rule

In quantum physics, Fermi's golden rule is a formula that calculates the transition rate (the probability of a transition per unit time) from one energy eigenstate of a quantum system to a group of other eigenstates, as a result of a weak perturbation. The rule is named after Enrico Fermi, though it was first derived by Paul Dirac using time-dependent perturbation theory.

Γi→f = (2π/ħ) |⟨f|H′|i⟩|2 ρ(Ef)

Here Γ is the transition rate, H′ is the perturbing Hamiltonian, ⟨f|H′|i⟩ is the matrix element between the initial state i and final state f, ħ is the reduced Planck constant, and ρ(Ef) is the density of final states at the energy of the final state.

Derivation and conditions

The rule follows from first-order time-dependent perturbation theory in the long-time limit, where the probability of a transition into a continuum of states grows linearly with time. It applies when the perturbation is weak, the interaction time is long compared with the internal timescales of the system, and the final states form a continuum or dense set — conditions common in scattering, spectroscopy, and decay processes.

Applications

Fermi's golden rule underlies the calculation of radioactive decay rates, including beta decay (Fermi's original celebrated application in his theory of the weak interaction), optical absorption and emission of photons by atoms, scattering cross sections, tunnelling and reaction rates, and transition rates in condensed matter physics such as electron-phonon scattering. In nuclear and particle physics, the phase-space factor ρ is often the dominant consideration, shaping the energy spectra of decay products.
