Human milk oligosaccharide

Human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs) are a family of structurally diverse, unconjugated glycans found in human breast milk. They are the third most abundant solid component of human milk, after lactose and fat, occurring at roughly 5–15 grams per litre, yet they cannot be digested by the infant's own enzymes.

Structure and composition

More than 200 different HMOs have been identified. All are built on a lactose core and elongated with lacto-N-biose or N-acetyllactosamine units, and many are decorated with fucose and sialic acid residues. The most abundant HMO in most mothers' milk is 2′-fucosyllactose (2′-FL). A mother's secretor status and Lewis blood group, determined largely by the FUT2 and FUT3 genes, govern which HMOs she produces.

Functions

Because infants cannot digest them, HMOs pass to the colon where they act as prebiotics, selectively feeding beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis. They also serve as soluble decoy receptors that block pathogens — including Campylobacter jejuni, enteropathogenic E. coli, and norovirus — from attaching to the gut lining, and they modulate immune development and inflammation. Sialylated HMOs supply sialic acid that may support brain development.

Applications

Structurally identical HMOs, produced by engineered microbial fermentation, are now added to some infant formulas — notably 2′-FL and lacto-N-neotetraose (LNnT) — following regulatory approvals in the European Union and the United States. Research continues into therapeutic uses against infections and necrotizing enterocolitis.
