Cancer cell

Cancer cells are cells that divide continually, forming solid tumors or flooding the blood or lymph with abnormal cells. Cell division is a normal process used by the body for growth and repair, but cancer cells escape the controls that normally regulate it, accumulating mutations in genes that govern proliferation, cell death, and DNA repair.

Characteristics

Cancer cells differ from normal cells in many ways: they grow in the absence of growth signals, ignore anti-growth and death signals, replicate their DNA despite damage, achieve replicative immortality (often through telomerase reactivation), induce angiogenesis to supply the tumor with blood, and may invade surrounding tissue and metastasize to distant sites. They frequently show an altered metabolism known as the Warburg effect, favouring glycolysis even in the presence of oxygen, and they can evade destruction by the immune system. These properties were systematized in the influential "hallmarks of cancer" framework proposed by Douglas Hanahan and Robert Weinberg.

Origins

Cancers arise when mutations activate oncogenes or disable tumor suppressor genes such as TP53. The cancer stem cell model holds that within many tumors a subpopulation of cells retains stem-like self-renewal capacity and drives growth and recurrence. Well-studied cultured lines, such as HeLa cells derived from a cervical cancer in 1951, remain essential tools of research.
