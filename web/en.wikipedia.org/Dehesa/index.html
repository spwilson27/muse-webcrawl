A dehesa (Spanish: [de'esa]) is a multifunctional, agrosylvopastoral system and cultural landscape of southern and central Spain and southern Portugal, where it is known as a montado. It consists of open woodland of evergreen oaks — chiefly holm oak (Quercus ilex) and cork oak (Quercus suber) — combined with grazing, hunting and forestry.

Ecology

Dehesas cover roughly 20,000–25,000 km² of the Iberian Peninsula. The widely spaced oaks shelter pasture from the summer sun, drop acorns that fatten livestock in autumn, and support high biodiversity, including endangered species such as the Iberian lynx and Spanish imperial eagle. Dehesas are a recognised example of a high-nature-value farming system.

Uses

The system is famous as the home of the Iberian pig, which grazes on acorns (bellotas) to produce jamón ibérico de bellota. Cork is stripped from cork oaks on a cycle of about nine years, and the land also supports sheep, cattle, cereal crops, charcoal production and hunting.

History and threats

The dehesa landscape developed over centuries of managed grazing and clearing. It is threatened by oak decline (including the disease la seca), lack of tree regeneration because saplings are browsed, rural depopulation and changes in agricultural economics.
