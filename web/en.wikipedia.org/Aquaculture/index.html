Aquaculture

Aquaculture—breeding and harvesting aquatic organisms—now supplies over half the world's seafood.

Aquaculture (less commonly spelled aquiculture), also known as aquafarming, is the controlled cultivation of aquatic organisms such as fish, crustaceans, mollusks, algae, and other organisms of value. It involves interventions in the rearing process—stocking, feeding, protection from predators—to enhance production. In 2022, aquaculture production of aquatic animals surpassed capture fisheries for the first time, supplying the majority of seafood consumed by humans.

Methods

Systems range from extensive ponds and rice-fish culture (practised in China for millennia) to intensive recirculating aquaculture systems (RAS) on land and offshore net pens used for salmon. Mariculture refers to cultivation in seawater, including seaweed farming, oyster and mussel culture on ropes and racks, and shrimp ponds. Major farmed species include carps, tilapia, Atlantic salmon, pangasius, milkfish, whiteleg shrimp, and various bivalves.

Issues

Critics cite environmental concerns: nutrient pollution, habitat destruction (especially mangrove clearance for shrimp farms), disease and sea-lice transfer to wild stocks, escapes of farmed fish, and the use of wild-caught fishmeal and fish oil as feed—though feed formulations increasingly use plant proteins, insect meal, and algal oils. Animal-welfare questions about stocking density and slaughter methods also arise (see animal welfare).

History

Fish farming dates to ancient China (carp culture documented by Fan Li, 5th century BC), ancient Egypt, and Hawaiian fishponds; modern industrial aquaculture expanded rapidly from the 1970s, led by China, which produces more farmed seafood than any other country.
