Epidermolysis bullosa (EB) is a group of rare genetic connective tissue diseases that cause the skin to be very fragile and to blister easily. Blisters form in response to minor injury, heat, rubbing, or scratching, and in severe cases may also form inside the body, such as on the lining of the mouth or the stomach. Because affected children are sometimes described as having skin as delicate as a butterfly's wing, they are often known as "butterfly children".

Most types of EB are inherited, either in an autosomal dominant or autosomal recessive pattern. The condition results from mutations in genes encoding proteins that anchor the layers of the skin together, including keratins, collagen VII, laminin-332, and integrins.

Classification

Epidermolysis bullosa is divided into four major types based on the level of the skin at which blistering occurs:

  • Epidermolysis bullosa simplex — blistering within the epidermis, usually caused by mutations in the keratin genes KRT5 and KRT14. It is the most common and generally mildest form.
  • Junctional epidermolysis bullosa — blistering within the lamina lucida of the basement membrane zone, often involving laminin-332 or type XVII collagen. Severe generalized forms can be lethal in infancy.
  • Dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa — blistering below the basement membrane in the upper dermis, caused by mutations in COL7A1, the gene for type VII collagen. Recessive forms can cause scarring, fusion of the fingers ("mitten" deformities), and a greatly increased risk of squamous cell carcinoma.
  • Kindler syndrome — blistering at multiple levels of the skin, caused by mutations in FERMT1 (kindlin-1).

Signs and symptoms

The hallmark of EB is fragile skin that blisters and tears from the slightest friction. Severity varies widely: some people develop only small blisters on the hands and feet, while others have widespread blistering affecting the eyes, mouth, oesophagus, and internal organs. Complications include infection, anaemia, malnutrition, scarring, and, in recessive dystrophic EB, aggressive skin cancers.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is made by skin biopsy with immunofluorescence mapping and confirmed by genetic testing. There is no cure; treatment is supportive and includes careful wound care, protective bandaging, pain management, nutritional support, and surveillance for skin cancer. Gene therapy approaches have advanced significantly: in 2023, a topical gene therapy gel (beremagene geperpavec) became the first approved gene therapy for dystrophic EB wounds, delivering working copies of COL7A1 directly to the skin.
