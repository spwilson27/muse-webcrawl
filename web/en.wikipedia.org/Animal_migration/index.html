Animal migration

Animal migration is the relatively long-distance movement of individual animals, usually on a seasonal basis. It is found in all major animal groups — birds, mammals, fish, reptiles, amphibians, insects, and crustaceans — and is typically triggered by changes in weather, food availability, or the breeding cycle. Migration is distinguished from other movements by its persistence, its redirection of normal activities, and the special physiological state of the animals involved.

Patterns and examples

Bird migration is the best-known form: the Arctic tern travels between Arctic breeding grounds and Antarctic waters, the longest migration known, while billions of songbirds move between temperate breeding areas and tropical wintering grounds. Mammals migrate too — wildebeest and zebra in the Serengeti, caribou in the Arctic, and humpback whales between feeding and breeding seas. Salmon migrate from the ocean to spawn in their natal rivers (anadromy), while European eels do the reverse (catadromy). Among insects, monarch butterflies travel thousands of kilometres between North America and Mexico over several generations, and vast locust swarms and Christmas Island red crab processions are mass movements of other kinds.

Navigation and physiology

Migrants navigate by the sun, stars, landmarks, odours, and Earth's magnetic field; many birds appear to sense magnetic inclination and possibly use quantum effects in cryptochrome proteins in the retina. Before migrating, animals enter a state of hyperphagia, building fat reserves, and show migratory restlessness (Zugunruhe) in captivity. Migration routes and timing are partly inherited and partly learned.

Ecology and threats

Migration connects ecosystems, moving nutrients and energy and shaping predator–prey dynamics across continents. Habitat loss, barriers such as dams and fences, hunting, light pollution, and climate change threaten many migratory species, whose conservation requires international cooperation along entire flyways and corridors.
