Riemann–Roch theorem

The Riemann–Roch theorem is an important theorem in complex analysis and algebraic geometry, initially proven by Bernhard Riemann (1857), with a missing inequality supplied by Riemann's student Gustav Roch (1865). It relates the complex analysis of a connected compact Riemann surface with the surface's purely topological genus.

The genus of a compact Riemann surface controls the dimensions in the Riemann–Roch formula.

Statement

Let X be a compact Riemann surface of genus g, and let D be a divisor on X. Write ℓ(D) for the dimension of the space of meromorphic functions whose divisor is at least −D, and let K be a canonical divisor. Then

ℓ(D) − ℓ(K − D) = deg(D) + 1 − g.

Consequences

The theorem computes the dimension of spaces of meromorphic functions with prescribed zeros and poles up to a correction term, and it implies that a compact Riemann surface of genus g admits meromorphic functions of richly varied behaviour. Special cases give Riemann's inequality ℓ(D) ≥ deg(D) + 1 − g. Applied to algebraic curves over arbitrary fields, the same formula holds and underlies much of the theory of algebraic curves.

Generalizations

The theorem was successively generalised: to higher-dimensional varieties as the Hirzebruch–Riemann–Roch theorem, and then to the Grothendieck–Riemann–Roch theorem, which inspired the development of K-theory and, in another direction, the Atiyah–Singer index theorem.
