Hermes Trismegistus (Ancient Greek: Ἑρμῆς ὁ Τρισμέγιστος, "Hermes the Thrice-Greatest"; Latin: Mercurius ter Maximus) is the legendary author of the Hermetica, a body of philosophical, religious, alchemical and astrological writings produced in Hellenistic and Roman Egypt. He arose from the syncretic identification of the Greek god Hermes with the Egyptian god Thoth, both deities of wisdom, writing and magic.

Origins

From the early Hellenistic period, Greeks in Egypt equated Thoth — inventor of writing and scribe of the gods — with Hermes. The epithet "thrice-greatest" renders an Egyptian superlative formula attested for Thoth from the New Kingdom and Hellenistic periods. Hermes Trismegistus was regarded as a supremely ancient sage, sometimes treated as a human author later deified, who had revealed divine knowledge before the Flood and whose writings were preserved by Egyptian priests.

The Hermetic writings

The works attributed to him fall into two broad groups: the "philosophical" Hermetica, treatises of theology, cosmology and soteriology, of which the seventeen Greek treatises of the Corpus Hermeticum (dating roughly to the 2nd–3rd centuries AD) and the Latin Asclepius survive; and the "technical" Hermetica — texts on alchemy, astrology, medicine and magic, including the famous Emerald Tablet, source of the axiom "as above, so below".

Influence and reception

Early Christian writers such as Lactantius and Augustine cited Hermes as a pagan prophet. In 1460 a Greek manuscript of the Corpus reached Florence, where Marsilio Ficino translated it for Cosimo de' Medici before finishing his Plato, inaugurating a Renaissance Hermetic revival that influenced Pico della Mirandola, Giordano Bruno and alchemists and natural philosophers for two centuries. In 1614 Isaac Casaubon redated the texts to the post-Christian era, undermining belief in their primeval antiquity. Hermes Trismegistus remains a central figure in Western esotericism, and Hermetic themes permeated Arabic alchemical literature as well.
