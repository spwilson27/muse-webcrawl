Auxin

Auxin redistributes in a shoot tip, causing phototropic bending.

Auxins are a class of plant hormones (or plant growth regulators) with some morphogen-like characteristics. Auxins play a cardinal role in coordination of many growth and behavioural processes in plant life cycles and are essential for plant body development. The Dutch biologist Frits Warmolt Went first described auxins and their role in plant growth in 1928. The principal natural auxin is indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), derived from the amino acid tryptophan.

Functions

Auxin promotes cell elongation in shoots and coleoptiles while inhibiting elongation in roots at higher concentrations. It mediates phototropism and gravitropism through lateral redistribution via PIN efflux carrier proteins, maintains apical dominance, stimulates adventitious root formation (the basis of rooting powders), and influences fruit development and vascular differentiation. Synthetic auxins such as 2,4-D and dicamba are widely used as herbicides, and 2,4,5-T was a component of Agent Orange.

Signalling

Auxin is perceived by the TIR1/AFB family of F-box receptors; binding promotes degradation of Aux/IAA repressor proteins, freeing ARF transcription factors to regulate gene expression. Polar auxin transport from cell to cell generates the local maxima and gradients that pattern the plant body.
