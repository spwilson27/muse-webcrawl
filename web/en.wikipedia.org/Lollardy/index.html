Lollardy

Lollardy, also known as Lollardism, was a proto-Protestant Christian religious movement in England between the late 14th and early 16th centuries. It arose from the teachings of John Wycliffe, an Oxford theologian who criticised the wealth and power of the Church and argued for a vernacular Bible.

Beliefs

Lollards emphasised the authority of Scripture, favoured translation of the Bible into English, and questioned doctrines such as transubstantiation, clerical celibacy, pilgrimages, and the veneration of images. Their views were summarised in documents such as the Twelve Conclusions of 1395, presented to Parliament.

Persecution and legacy

After the Oldcastle revolt of 1414 associated Lollardy with sedition, the movement was driven underground by statutes such as De heretico comburendo (1401), under which heretics could be burned. Small Lollard communities persisted for over a century and their ideas fed into the English Reformation.
