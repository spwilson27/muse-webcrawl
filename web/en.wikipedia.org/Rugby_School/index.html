Rugby School is a public school (English fee-charging boarding school for pupils aged 11–18) in Rugby, Warwickshire, England. Founded in 1567 as a free grammar school for local boys, it is one of the oldest public schools in England and was one of the nine "great" public schools defined by the Public Schools Act 1868. Rugby is best known as the birthplace of rugby football, which according to tradition was invented there in 1823 by William Webb Ellis.

History

The school was founded under the will of Lawrence Sheriff, a local grocer who had become wealthy as purveyor of spices to Queen Elizabeth I. Originally intended for local boys, Rugby grew into a nationally famous boarding school, especially under headmaster Thomas Arnold (1828–1842), whose reforms — the prefect system, chapel-centred moral education and the ideal of the Christian gentleman — influenced public schools throughout the British Empire and were immortalised in Thomas Hughes' novel Tom Brown's School Days (1857).

Rugby football

Legend holds that in 1823, pupil William Webb Ellis, "with a fine disregard for the rules of football", picked up the ball during a school game and ran with it, originating the handling game of rugby. The trophy of the Rugby World Cup, the Webb Ellis Cup, bears his name. The first written laws of the game were drawn up by Rugby pupils in 1845.

The school today

Rugby has been fully co-educational since the 1990s and educates around 800 pupils, the majority boarding. The school is a registered charity and offers means-tested bursaries, including through the Arnold Foundation. Its alumni, known as Old Rugbeians, include the poet Rupert Brooke, the author Lewis Carroll (who attended briefly), and Matthew Arnold, son of Thomas Arnold.
