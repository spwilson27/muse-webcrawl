Pyrocumulonimbus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The pyrocumulonimbus (pyroCb) is a type of cumulonimbus cloud that forms above a source of heat, such as a wildfire, volcanic eruption or industrial fire. Pyrocumulonimbus clouds are the most extreme manifestation of pyrocumulus: the rising thermal above the fire entrains smoke and moisture, and if conditions are unstable the cloud can develop into a full thunderstorm that generates its own lightning.

Effects

PyroCb storms can be dangerous and unpredictable: their lightning can ignite new fires (including "dry lightning" with little rain reaching the ground), their downdrafts and the collapse of the cloud column can produce violent, erratic surface winds that spread the parent fire, and they can generate fire tornadoes. Conversely, rain from a mature pyroCb occasionally helps extinguish a fire.

The smoke injected by large pyroCb events can reach the lower stratosphere, where it may circle the globe and persist for months, with measurable effects on atmospheric chemistry and climate. The Australian "Black Summer" bushfires of 2019–20 produced dozens of pyroCb events whose stratospheric smoke plume was compared in scale to a moderate volcanic eruption. Earlier notable events include the 2003 Canberra firestorm and the 2017 British Columbia wildfires.

Formation

Formation requires an intense heat source, sufficient low-level moisture, and atmospheric instability up to the tropopause. Wildfire smoke particles serve as cloud condensation nuclei, producing very large numbers of small droplets that suppress warm-rain processes and allow the cloud to build to great heights, sometimes overshooting into the stratosphere.
