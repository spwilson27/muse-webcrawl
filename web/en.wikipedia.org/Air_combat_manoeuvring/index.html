Air combat manoeuvring

Air combat manoeuvring (ACM) is the tactical art of moving, turning, and positioning fighter aircraft in order to attain a firing position on an opponent while denying the opponent the same. ACM builds on basic fighter maneuvers (BFM) and involves engagements between two or more aircraft, unlike the one-versus-one emphasis of BFM. It is closely associated with the concept of the dogfight and remains a core skill taught at fighter weapons schools despite the growth of beyond-visual-range missile combat.

Principles

ACM rests on energy management: an aircraft's altitude and airspeed together constitute its energy state, which can be traded — altitude for speed in a dive, speed for altitude in a zoom climb. Pilots seek angles on the opponent's tail while managing turn rate and radius. Situational awareness is paramount; the maxim "lose sight, lose the fight" reflects that most pilots shot down never see their attacker. Classic maneuvers include the lag pursuit, lead pursuit, the high and low yo-yo, the scissors, and the Immelmann turn, named for First World War ace Max Immelmann.

History

Air combat tactics emerged in the First World War, when Oswald Boelcke codified his Dicta Boelcke rules for aerial fighting. In the Second World War, formations such as the finger-four improved mutual support. The Korean and Vietnam wars revealed the costs of neglecting gun-era maneuvering skills when missiles proved unreliable, prompting the creation of the U.S. Navy's TOPGUN school in 1969 and the U.S. Air Force's Red Flag exercises. Modern training uses dissimilar aircraft and adversary squadrons to replicate enemy tactics.
