Resolution (logic)

In mathematical logic and automated theorem proving, resolution is a rule of inference used for constructing proofs and for refutation-based reasoning in first-order logic and propositional logic. It was introduced by John Alan Robinson in a landmark 1965 paper, "A Machine-Oriented Logic Based on the Resolution Principle".

The resolution rule

Given two clauses containing complementary literals, resolution produces a new clause, the resolvent. For example, from the clauses (a ∨ b) and (¬a ∨ c) one may infer (b ∨ c). In first-order logic, the rule is combined with unification: two clauses with literals that can be made complementary by a substitution are resolved after applying that substitution.

Refutation proofs

Resolution is typically used as a refutation procedure: to prove a formula, its negation is converted to conjunctive normal form (a set of clauses), and resolution is applied until the empty clause is derived, showing the clause set is unsatisfiable. The method is sound and refutation-complete for first-order logic.

Applications

Resolution underlies the logic programming language Prolog (which uses the restricted SLD resolution strategy) and is the foundation of many automated theorem provers and of techniques related to SAT solving. Its efficiency motivated research into strategies such as set of support, unit preference, and ordering restrictions on the literals resolved.
