The Palace of Westminster is the meeting place of the two houses of the Parliament of the United Kingdom — the House of Commons and the House of Lords — on the north bank of the River Thames in the City of Westminster, London. It is one of the most famous landmarks in the world, its riverside silhouette dominated by the Elizabeth Tower, home of the bell Big Ben.

History

A royal palace stood on the site from the eleventh century, and Westminster became the principal residence of the kings of England until a fire in 1512; Parliament, however, remained. Nearly all of the old palace was destroyed by a fire on 16 October 1834. In the ensuing competition, the design of Charles Barry, executed in rich Gothic detail by Augustus Pugin, was selected. The new palace was built between 1840 and 1876 in the Perpendicular Gothic Revival style, incorporating the surviving Westminster Hall (1097), the Jewel Tower and the cloisters of St Stephen's Chapel.

The palace contains some 1,100 rooms arranged around two courtyards. The Commons chamber was destroyed by bombing in 1941 during the Blitz and rebuilt in simpler form by Giles Gilbert Scott, reopening in 1950.

Layout

The building's river front is nearly 300 metres long. The House of Lords chamber occupies the southern part, the House of Commons the northern, with the Central Lobby between them. At the south end rises the Victoria Tower, 98.5 metres tall, and at the north end the Elizabeth Tower (renamed from the Clock Tower in 2012 for the Diamond Jubilee of Elizabeth II), 96 metres tall.

Conservation

Much of the palace's fabric and its ageing mechanical and electrical systems are in poor condition, and a major Restoration and Renewal programme has been planned to safeguard the building, which was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987 together with Westminster Abbey and St Margaret's Church.
