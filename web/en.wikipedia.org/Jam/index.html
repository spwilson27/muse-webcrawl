Jam

Jam is a fruit preserve made from fruit and sugar boiled to a thick, spreadable consistency, in which the fruit is crushed or chopped so that the finished product has a fairly uniform texture without distinct whole pieces. It is distinguished from jelly, which is made from strained juice and is clear, and from marmalade, which is made from citrus fruit including the peel.

How jam sets

Jam sets because of pectin, a gelling polysaccharide naturally present in fruit (especially apples, citrus and berries), which forms a gel network when heated with sugar and acid. Fruits low in pectin are often combined with added commercial pectin. Sugar also acts as a preservative by binding water, and jams are typically processed in sealed jars to prevent mould.

History and regulation

Fruit preserved in sugar or honey dates to ancient times, but jam-making on a large scale followed the spread of cheap refined sugar from the 19th century. In the European Union, the name "jam" is legally restricted to products with a minimum fruit content, with "extra jam" requiring more; in the United States, FDA standards of identity distinguish jams and preserves from jellies and fruit butters.

Varieties

Common flavours include strawberry, raspberry, blackcurrant, apricot and plum. Reduced-sugar and freezer jams, set with special pectins without full cooking, are also popular. Jam is used as a bread spread, a filling for cakes, biscuits, doughnuts and pastries such as jam tarts and the Linzer torte.
