Gleason score

The Gleason grading system is used to help evaluate the prognosis of men with prostate cancer using samples from a prostate biopsy. Together with other parameters, it is incorporated into a strategy of prostate cancer staging which predicts prognosis and helps guide therapy. A Gleason score is given to prostate cancer based upon its microscopic appearance: cancers with a higher Gleason score are more aggressive and have a worse prognosis. Pathological scores range from 2 to 10, with higher numbers indicating greater risks and higher mortality. The system was created by Donald Gleason, a pathologist at the Minneapolis Veterans Affairs Hospital, in the 1960s and was developed with statisticians at the hospital.

Method

The pathologist assigns a grade from 1 to 5 to the most common pattern of glandular architecture in the tumour, and a second grade to the second most common pattern; the two are added to give the Gleason score. For example, a tumour whose predominant pattern is 3 with a secondary pattern of 4 is reported as 3 + 4 = 7. Patterns 1 and 2 are rarely assigned on needle biopsy, so scores of 2–5 are almost never seen in modern practice, and 6 is effectively the lowest score given.

Grade Groups

Because a score of 7 can represent either 3 + 4 (favourable) or 4 + 3 (less favourable) disease, the 2014 International Society of Urological Pathology conference introduced five Grade Groups: Grade Group 1 (score ≤ 6), 2 (3 + 4 = 7), 3 (4 + 3 = 7), 4 (8) and 5 (9–10). This grouping better reflects prognosis and simplifies communication with patients.

Clinical significance

The Gleason score, together with PSA level and clinical stage, stratifies patients into risk categories that guide decisions between active surveillance, surgery, radiotherapy and systemic therapy. Higher scores predict faster growth, greater likelihood of extraprostatic spread and biochemical recurrence after treatment. Inter-observer variability among pathologists and tumour heterogeneity within the prostate are recognised limitations.
