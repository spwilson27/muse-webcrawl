Barnett Newman (January 29, 1905 – July 4, 1970) was an American artist. He is seen as one of the major figures in abstract expressionism and one of the foremost of the color field painters. His paintings are exemplary of the sublime, contemplative mode of abstraction: vast expanses of a single colour, divided vertically by thin lines he called "zips".

Career

Newman, born in New York to Polish-Jewish immigrant parents, studied philosophy at the City College of New York and worked in his father's clothing business before turning to art full-time. In the 1940s he destroyed most of his early work and spent years developing his mature style in relative obscurity, writing influential criticism and organising exhibitions. In 1948 he painted Onement I, the breakthrough canvas in which a single vertical band of dark red-orange was applied over a field of deep red — the first of his signature "zip" paintings.

Major works

Vir Heroicus Sublimis (1950–51), over 5 metres wide, is his largest early canvas and a landmark of color field painting. In the 1950s and 1960s he produced the fourteen canvases of The Stations of the Cross: Lema Sabachthani (1958–66), austere black-on-raw-canvas works meditating on Christ's cry of dereliction. Late works include the monumental series Who's Afraid of Red, Yellow and Blue and the sculpture Broken Obelisk (1963–67), an inverted obelisk balanced on a pyramid, now widely cast and installed in public collections.

Legacy

Influential first through the support of critic Clement Greenberg and collectors, Newman came to be regarded as a precursor of minimalism and as one of the most philosophically ambitious of the New York School painters. His 1948 essay "The Sublime Is Now" is a key statement of abstract expressionist aims.
