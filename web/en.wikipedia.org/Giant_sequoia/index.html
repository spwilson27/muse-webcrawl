The giant sequoia (Sequoiadendron giganteum) is the sole living species in the genus Sequoiadendron, and one of three species of coniferous trees known as redwoods. Giant sequoias are among the largest trees on Earth: the average height of a mature tree is 50–85 metres (164–279 ft), with diameters of 6–8 metres (20–26 ft) at the base. Record specimens have been measured at 94.8 m (311 ft) tall. The largest known living single-stem tree, the General Sherman Tree, has a volume of about 1,487 cubic metres (52,500 cu ft), making it the largest known living tree by volume.

Giant sequoias occur naturally only in groves on the western slopes of the Sierra Nevada mountain range of California, at elevations of roughly 1,400–2,150 m (4,600–7,050 ft). There are fewer than eighty distinct groves, most within protected areas such as Sequoia, Kings Canyon and Yosemite national parks.

Description

Giant sequoias can live for more than 3,000 years; the oldest known specimen was about 3,266 years old when cut. Their bark is fibrous, furrowed and may be 90 cm (3 ft) thick at the base of the columnar trunk, providing considerable protection against fire. The foliage consists of scale-like awl-shaped leaves 3–6 mm long arranged spirally on the shoots. Seeds are produced in serotinous cones 4–7 cm long that mature in 18–20 months but may remain green and closed for as long as twenty years; each cone carries an average of about 230 seeds.

Ecology

The species is adapted to a regime of periodic low-intensity fire, which clears competing vegetation, opens cones and creates the mineral-soil seedbeds the seedlings require. A century of fire suppression, combined with drought and rising temperatures, has increased the trees' vulnerability: severe wildfires in 2020 and 2021 killed an estimated 13–19% of the entire population of large giant sequoias.

History and conservation

Although Indigenous peoples of the Sierra Nevada knew the groves for millennia, the trees were first reported by Europeans in 1833 and came to wide attention after 1852. Logging in the late nineteenth century destroyed many large trees before most groves were placed under protection. The species is listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List because of its restricted range, low recruitment and the increasing threat of high-severity fire driven by climate change.
