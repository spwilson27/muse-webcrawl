Corner reflector

A corner reflector is a retroreflector consisting of three mutually perpendicular, intersecting flat surfaces, which reflect waves — electromagnetic waves such as radar and light, or sound waves — directly back toward their source regardless of the angle of incidence (within limits). The principle is the same as that of a ball bouncing in the corner of a room returning along its incoming path.

Radar reflectors

Corner reflectors are widely used to increase the radar cross-section of objects that would otherwise return a weak echo. Small boats, buoys, and bridges carry radar reflectors; the common octahedral type arranges eight corner reflectors so that one always faces the incoming signal. Corner reflectors are also used as passive radar calibration targets and as decoys.

Optical retroreflectors

In optics, a corner cube (or cube-corner retroreflector) is a prism whose three rear faces are mutually perpendicular; light entering the front face is reflected three times and exits parallel to the incoming beam but displaced. Arrays of corner cubes were placed on the Moon by the Apollo 11, 14 and 15 missions and by Soviet Lunokhod rovers; laser pulses fired from Earth at these arrays enable lunar laser ranging, measuring the Earth–Moon distance to centimetre precision. Similar reflectors are carried by satellites such as LAGEOS for satellite laser ranging.

Antennas

A corner reflector is also used as a directional antenna element: a driven dipole placed in front of two joined reflecting planes forms a corner reflector antenna with substantial forward gain, used historically for television reception and point-to-point links.
