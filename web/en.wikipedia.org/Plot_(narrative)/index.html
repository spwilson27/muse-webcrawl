Plot (narrative)

FieldNarratology, literary theory
DefinitionThe sequence of events in a narrative, arranged by causation
Classical sourceAristotle, Poetics
Distinct fromStory (fabula), theme, setting

In a literary work, film, or other narrative, the plot is the sequence of events that make up the story, particularly as they relate to one another through cause and effect. E. M. Forster famously distinguished a mere story ("the king died and then the queen died") from a plot ("the king died, and then the queen died of grief"), in which causality and motivation are emphasized.

Aristotle

The foundational theory is Aristotle's Poetics, which ranks plot (mythos) as the most important element of tragedy, requiring a unified action with beginning, middle, and end, ideally involving reversal (peripeteia) and recognition (anagnorisis). The concept of the plot as an ordered whole with a complication and an unraveling (denouement) descends from this tradition.

Structure

Common models of plot structure include Gustav Freytag's pyramid — exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, and denouement — adapted from his 1863 analysis of drama, and the three-act structure widely taught in screenwriting. Narratology distinguishes the order events occurred (story or fabula) from the order in which they are presented (plot or sjuzhet), encompassing devices such as flashback, foreshadowing, subplots, and in medias res openings.

Criticism and alternatives

Some writers and traditions resist plot-driven narrative: modernist and postmodern fiction often subordinates events to consciousness or language, episodic and picaresque traditions string incidents loosely together, and plotless or anti-plot works deliberately frustrate expectations. Nonetheless, plot remains central to popular genres and to ordinary understandings of storytelling.
