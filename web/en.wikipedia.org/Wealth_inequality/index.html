Wealth inequality

Also calledWealth distribution, wealth gap
Common measureGini coefficient; top wealth shares
Global patternTop 1% own roughly 38–45% of wealth

Wealth inequality, also known as the wealth gap, is the unequal distribution of assets among residents of an economy or of the world. Unlike income inequality, it concerns the stock of accumulated assets — property, savings, investments and business equity, less debts. Wealth inequality is consistently greater than income inequality in almost every country, because assets compound over generations and the poor may hold little or no net wealth, or even negative net worth.

Patterns

According to the World Inequality Report, the richest 10% of the world's population own about 76% of global wealth, the middle 40% own about 22%, and the bottom half own roughly 2%. Within countries, wealth concentration fell in much of the West during the mid-20th century — through war, inflation, welfare states and progressive taxation — but has risen since about 1980, notably in the United States, where the top 1% hold around a third of all wealth. Asset price growth, especially in equities and housing, executive pay, inheritance and differing saving rates are among the causes discussed.

Debates and policy

Economists and political movements disagree on whether wealth inequality is harmful or a natural outcome of differing choices and productivity. Thomas Piketty's Capital in the Twenty-First Century (2013) argued that when the return on capital exceeds economic growth, concentration tends to increase, and proposed progressive wealth taxes; other economists dispute the data or the mechanisms. Policy responses debated include wealth and inheritance taxes, land reform, broader share ownership, and social welfare provision. Campaign groups such as Oxfam publish annual reports highlighting extreme concentration at the very top — the world's billionaires number in the thousands and hold several percent of global wealth.
