Biopower

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Biopower is a term coined by the French philosopher Michel Foucault to describe the practices of modern nation states and their regulation of subjects through techniques directed at the body and the population. In The History of Sexuality (1976) and his lecture courses Society Must Be Defended and Security, Territory, Population, Foucault contrasted this modern form of power with the older sovereign power to "take life or let live".

Concept

Foucault identified two poles of biopower. The first, an "anatomo-politics of the human body", disciplines individual bodies — making them useful and docile — in institutions such as schools, barracks, hospitals and prisons (the subject of his earlier Discipline and Punish). The second, a "biopolitics of the population", regulates collective biological processes — birth rates, mortality, public health, longevity — through statistics, demography and administrative interventions. Together they characterise a power that "makes live and lets die", managing life itself as an object of government.

Development by later thinkers

The concept has been extensively developed and contested. Giorgio Agamben, in Homo Sacer (1995), linked biopower to the production of "bare life" and the state of exception, taking the concentration camp as its paradigm. Michael Hardt and Antonio Negri, in Empire (2000), used "biopolitical production" to describe contemporary capitalism's production of social life itself. Others have applied the term to public health, reproduction, security and pandemic governance.
