Continental margin

The continental margin is the zone of the ocean floor that separates the thin oceanic crust from the thick continental crust. Together with the continental shelf, it comprises the continental shelf, the continental slope, and the continental rise, extending from the shoreline to the abyssal plain.

Components

The continental shelf is the gently sloping submerged extension of the continent, typically less than 200 metres deep and rich in fisheries and hydrocarbon deposits. The continental slope descends steeply — cut by submarine canyons and turbidity currents — to the continental rise, a broad apron of accumulated sediment that grades into the deep ocean floor.

Active and passive margins

Passive margins (e.g. the Atlantic coasts) lie within a tectonic plate, are sediment-rich and seismically quiet. Active margins (e.g. the Pacific Ring of Fire) coincide with plate boundaries; they have narrow shelves, deep trenches, earthquakes and volcanism. The legal continental shelf under UNCLOS extends at least 200 nautical miles from the coast regardless of geology.

Significance

Continental margins hold most offshore oil and gas reserves, major fisheries, and the sedimentary record used to reconstruct past climates and sea levels.
