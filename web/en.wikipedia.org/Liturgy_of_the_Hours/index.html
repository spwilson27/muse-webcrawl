Liturgy of the Hours

[Image: An illuminated medieval breviary]
A breviary contains the texts of the Hours.

The Liturgy of the Hours, also called the Divine Office, is the daily prayer of the Catholic Church, marking the hours of each day and sanctifying the day with prayer. It consists of psalms, hymns, scripture readings and intercessions recited at fixed times, and is obligatory for priests and religious, while being recommended to the laity.

The hours

The principal hours of the current Roman Rite form are:

  • Office of Readings — a longer office with psalms and extended readings, said at any hour
  • Morning Prayer (Lauds) — at daybreak
  • Daytime Prayer — one or more of Terce, Sext and None (mid-morning, midday, afternoon)
  • Evening Prayer (Vespers) — at sunset
  • Night Prayer (Compline) — before retiring

History

The practice derives from the Jewish custom of prayer at fixed hours and the early Christian pattern described in the Acts of the Apostles. Monastic communities, following the Rule of Saint Benedict, developed the full cycle of offices chanted in choir. The reform after the Second Vatican Council, promulgated by Paul VI in 1970, simplified the office, arranged the psalms over a four-week cycle and encouraged wider participation by lay people.

Books and practice

The texts are contained in a book called a breviary, published in Latin as Liturgia Horarum and in vernacular editions. Similar offices exist in the Eastern Orthodox Church (the Horologion) and in Anglican tradition as Morning and Evening Prayer from the Book of Common Prayer.
