Epiclesis

During the epiclesis the celebrant invokes the Holy Spirit upon the bread and wine and upon the congregation.

The epiclesis (also spelled epiklesis; from Ancient Greek ἐπίκλησις, "invocation" or "calling upon") is the part of the Anaphora (Eucharistic Prayer) in which the priest invokes the Holy Spirit (or, in some older texts, the Word) upon the Eucharistic bread and wine, asking that they be changed into the Body and Blood of Christ, and in some traditions also invoking blessings upon the communicants.

In Eastern Christianity

In the Eastern Orthodox and Byzantine Catholic traditions, the epiclesis — for example in the Divine Liturgy of St John Chrysostom, "And we offer unto Thee this reasonable and bloodless service... and make this bread the precious Body of thy Christ, and that which is in this cup the precious Blood of thy Christ, changing them by thy Holy Spirit" — is regarded as the climax and consecratory moment of the Liturgy. The people respond with "Amen, Amen, Amen" and a hymn to the Theotokos follows.

In Western Christianity

In the Roman Rite of the Catholic Church, the epiclesis occurs before the institution narrative in the post-Vatican II Eucharistic Prayers (the "Supplices te rogamus" of the Roman Canon has also been interpreted epicletically). Latin theology historically emphasised the words of institution as the consecratory formula, and the differing emphases were a point of controversy between East and West, debated at the Council of Florence and in later ecumenical dialogues, which have largely converged on seeing the whole anaphora as consecratory. Anglican and Lutheran Eucharistic prayers, especially since the liturgical movement of the 20th century, likewise include explicit invocations of the Holy Spirit.

Other uses

Epicleses also occur at other points of sacramental worship: invocations of the Spirit over baptismal water, over oil for anointing, and in ordination prayers. More broadly, the word is used of any prayer invoking a deity's presence or aid.
