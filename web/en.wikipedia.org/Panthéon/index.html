The Panthéon is a monument in the Latin Quarter of Paris, France. Built between 1758 and 1790 to designs by Jacques-Germain Soufflot, it was originally intended as a church dedicated to Saint Genevieve, the patron saint of Paris, commissioned by King Louis XV. During the French Revolution it was converted into a mausoleum for the remains of distinguished French citizens, a role it has held, with interruptions, ever since. Its façade, modelled on the Pantheon in Rome, bears the inscription AUX GRANDS HOMMES LA PATRIE RECONNAISSANTE — "To great men, the grateful fatherland".

Architecture

The building is a Greek-cross plan crowned by a triple-shelled dome — a landmark of early Neoclassical architecture that combines the lightness of Gothic construction with classical forms. Soufflot died in 1780 and the work was completed by his pupil Jean-Baptiste Rondelet. In 1851 the physicist Léon Foucault used the dome to demonstrate the rotation of the Earth with his famous pendulum; a replica still swings in the nave.

Burials

Among the more than eighty figures interred in the crypt are Voltaire, Jean-Jacques Rousseau, Victor Hugo, Émile Zola, Jean Jaurès, Louis Braille, Pierre and Marie Curie, Alexandre Dumas and André Malraux. Marie Curie was the first woman interred on her own merits (1995). More recent inductees include the writer and résistante Geneviève de Gaulle-Anthonioz, the ethnologist Germaine Tillion, and the American-born entertainer and Resistance figure Josephine Baker (2021).

History of use

The building alternated between church and secular temple through the 19th century — under Napoleon, the Restoration, the July Monarchy and the Second Empire — before being definitively established as a republican mausoleum at the funeral of Victor Hugo in 1885.
