Shapeshifting

In mythology, folklore and speculative fiction, shapeshifting is the ability to physically transform oneself, typically through divine intervention, sorcery, or an innate supernatural power. The transformation may be voluntary or involuntary, temporary or permanent, and may involve changing into an animal, another person, a plant, or an inanimate object. Shapeshifting is one of the oldest motifs in world storytelling, appearing in the Epic of Gilgamesh, Egyptian and Greek myth, Norse saga, Celtic legend, and the folklore of Asia, Africa and the Americas.

Mythological examples

Greek mythology is rich in transformations: Zeus took the forms of a swan, a bull and a shower of gold; Proteus could assume any shape and had to be held fast to compel prophecy; and Ovid's Metamorphoses is an entire epic of transformations, from Daphne into a laurel to Actaeon into a stag. In Norse myth, Loki turned into a mare, a fly and an old woman, and Odin changed shape on his wanderings. Irish and Welsh legend feature figures such as the Cailleach and the magician Gwydion, while the Welsh tale of Blodeuwedd tells of a woman made from flowers.

Animal transformation and folklore

The most famous shapeshifter of European folklore is the werewolf, whose condition (lycanthropy) could be a curse, a hereditary trait, or induced by a magical salve or belt. Related beings include the werecats and werehyenas of Africa, the kitsune (fox spirits) and tanuki of Japan, the seal-folk selkies of Orkney and Shetland, the swan maidens of Eurasian tale, and the skin-walkers of Navajo tradition. Trickster figures — Loki, Coyote, Anansi, Sun Wukong — use shapeshifting as a tool of cunning, disguise and boundary-crossing.

Themes and interpretation

Folklorists and psychologists have read shapeshifting variously as an expression of shamanic practice (the shaman's spirit journey in animal form), of anxieties about the boundary between human and animal, of puberty and identity change, and of the fluidity of the self. In modern fantasy and popular culture, shapeshifters — from Tolkien's Beorn to the Animorphs and the changelings of folklore-inspired fiction — remain a staple device, often governed by strict rules about mass, clothing, or the retention of human minds in animal bodies.
