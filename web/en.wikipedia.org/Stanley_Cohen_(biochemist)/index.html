Stanley Cohen (biochemist)

Stanley Cohen (November 17, 1922 – February 5, 2020) was an American biochemist who, along with Rita Levi-Montalcini, was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1986 for the isolation of nerve growth factor and the discovery of epidermal growth factor. His work transformed understanding of cell growth and development and opened lines of research relevant to cancer and wound healing.

Biography

Cohen was born in Brooklyn, New York, and educated at Brooklyn College and Oberlin College, receiving his PhD in biochemistry from the University of Michigan in 1948. After periods at the University of Colorado and Washington University in St. Louis—where he collaborated with Levi-Montalcini on nerve growth factor—he joined the faculty of Vanderbilt University School of Medicine in 1959, remaining there for the rest of his career.

Research

Working with Levi-Montalcini in the 1950s, Cohen isolated nerve growth factor (NGF) from mouse tumor tissue and snake venom and showed that it stimulated the growth of nerve fibers. While purifying NGF from mouse salivary glands, he noticed that extracts caused the eyelids of newborn mice to open early and teeth to erupt, which led him to isolate epidermal growth factor (EGF) and characterize its receptor—among the first growth factor receptors understood, and a foundation stone of modern signal-transduction and cancer research.

Awards

Besides the Nobel Prize, Cohen received the National Medal of Science (1986), the Albert Lasker Award for Basic Medical Research, and the Louisa Gross Horwitz Prize. He died in Nashville, Tennessee, in 2020 at the age of 97.
