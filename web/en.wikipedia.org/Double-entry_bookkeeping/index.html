Double-entry bookkeeping

A traditional ledger with debit and credit columns.

Double-entry bookkeeping, also known as double-entry accounting, is a method of recording financial transactions in which every entry to an account requires a corresponding and opposite entry to a different account. Each transaction is recorded as a debit in one account and a credit in another, so total debits always equal total credits. The system underlies modern accounting and provides a built-in arithmetic check on the books.

History

The method developed among Italian merchants in the late Middle Ages; the earliest surviving full example dates to 1340 in Genoa. The Franciscan friar Luca Pacioli gave the first printed description in his Summa de arithmetica (1494), earning him the title "father of accounting", though he presented himself as codifying existing Venetian practice rather than inventing it.

Principles

Accounts are classified as assets, liabilities, equity, income, and expenses. The accounting equation — assets = liabilities + equity — remains in balance because every transaction affects at least two accounts. For example, buying equipment for cash debits the equipment (asset) account and credits cash; borrowing money debits cash and credits a loan liability.

Advantages

Because debits must equal credits, the trial balance detects many recording errors. The system also produces complete financial statements (balance sheet and income statement) directly from the ledger and provides an audit trail that single-entry systems lack.
