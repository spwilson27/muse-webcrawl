History of the Czech lands

The history of the Czech lands — Bohemia, Moravia, and Czech Silesia — runs from Slavic settlement and the medieval Kingdom of Bohemia through nearly three centuries of Habsburg rule, the Czechoslovak Republic of 1918, and the independent Czech Republic (Czechia) established in 1993.

Early and medieval history

Slavic tribes settled the region by the 6th century. The 9th-century Great Moravian Empire introduced Christianity through Saints Cyril and Methodius (863). The Přemyslid dynasty built the Duchy of Bohemia within the Holy Roman Empire; in 1212 Bohemia became a hereditary kingdom. Under Charles IV (r. 1346–1378), king and Holy Roman Emperor, Prague flourished and Charles University was founded (1348). The Hussite Wars (1419–1434) followed the execution of reformer Jan Hus.

Habsburg era

After the Battle of Mohács (1526), the Habsburgs acquired Bohemia. The Defenestration of Prague (1618) triggered the Thirty Years' War; the Protestant defeat at White Mountain (1620) led to forced re-Catholicization and Germanization. A Czech National Revival in the 18th–19th centuries revived the language and culture, with figures like František Palacký shaping national consciousness.

Czechoslovakia and Czechia

Czechoslovakia was proclaimed on 28 October 1918 under Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk, becoming one of Europe's few functioning interwar democracies. The Munich Agreement (1938) ceded the Sudetenland to Nazi Germany, followed by occupation in 1939. Restored in 1945, the country fell under communist rule in 1948; the Prague Spring reform movement of 1968 was crushed by Warsaw Pact invasion. The Velvet Revolution of 1989, led by Václav Havel, restored democracy, and on 1 January 1993 Czechoslovakia peacefully split into the Czech Republic and Slovakia. Czechia joined NATO in 1999 and the European Union in 2004.
