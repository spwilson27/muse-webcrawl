Musique concrète

Musique concrète (French for "concrete music") is a form of electroacoustic music that uses recorded natural and industrial sounds as its raw material, assembled and transformed by editing tape. It was pioneered in the late 1940s in Paris by Pierre Schaeffer, whose Cinq études de bruits (Five Noise Studies) of 1948 — including the Étude aux chemins de fer, made from recordings of trains — are generally regarded as its founding works.

Working at the studio of Radiodiffusion-Télévision Française (RTF), Schaeffer and collaborators such as Pierre Henry developed techniques of tape splicing, speed change, reversal and looping, and a theory centred on the objet sonore ("sound object") and "reduced listening" — hearing sound for its own qualities rather than its source. The Groupe de Recherche de Musique Concrète, later the Groupe de Recherches Musicales (GRM), became its institutional home.

Influence

Musique concrète, alongside the electronic music of the Cologne studio, founded modern electroacoustic composition. Its editing and sampling techniques directly anticipate tape collage, sampling in hip hop and electronic dance music, and much contemporary sound design.
