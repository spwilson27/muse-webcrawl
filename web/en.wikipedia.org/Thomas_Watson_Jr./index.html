Thomas John Watson Jr. (January 14, 1914 – December 31, 1993) was an American businessman who led IBM from 1952 to 1971, succeeding his father Thomas J. Watson. He transformed IBM from a maker of electromechanical tabulators into the dominant force of the computer age, betting the company on the System/360 mainframe family.

At IBM

Watson became president in 1952 and chairman in 1961. Under his leadership IBM's revenues grew from about 00 million to more than billion, and employment more than quadrupled. The System/360 project — a compatible line of computers announced in 1964 at a cost of some billion — was described by Fortune as "IBM's ,000,000,000 gamble" and it remade the industry.

Later life

After retiring in 1971 following a heart attack, Watson served as U.S. ambassador to the Soviet Union (1979–1981) and remained a prominent figure in business and philanthropy. His memoir Father, Son & Co. (1990) recounts his difficult relationship with his father and the building of modern IBM.
