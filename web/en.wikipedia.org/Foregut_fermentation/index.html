Foregut fermentation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Foregut fermentation is a form of microbial digestion in herbivorous animals in which plant material is fermented by symbiotic bacteria, protozoa, and fungi in an enlarged chamber of the stomach before it reaches the acid-secreting part of the digestive tract. It contrasts with hindgut fermentation, in which microbial digestion takes place in the caecum and colon.

Ruminants

The best-known foregut fermenters are the ruminants — cattle, sheep, goats, deer, giraffes, and antelopes. Their stomach has four compartments: the rumen, reticulum, omasum, and abomasum. Microbes in the rumen and reticulum ferment cellulose and other plant carbohydrates, producing volatile fatty acids (acetate, propionate, and butyrate), which the animal absorbs as its main energy source. The microbes themselves are later digested in the abomasum and small intestine, providing protein. Ruminants regurgitate and re-chew partially fermented food (cud) to reduce particle size, a process called rumination. Fermentation gases, including methane, are expelled by eructation (belching).

Non-ruminant foregut fermenters

Foregut fermentation has evolved independently in several other groups: macropod marsupials (kangaroos and wallabies), colobine monkeys (langurs and colobus), sloths, the hoatzin (a bird, unique in fermenting in an enlarged crop), hippopotamuses, and peccaries. Camelids (camels, llamas, alpacas) are pseudoruminants with a three-chambered stomach.

Advantages and disadvantages

Foregut fermentation lets animals extract energy and protein from low-quality, fibrous forage, detoxify some plant secondary compounds before absorption, and synthesize B vitamins and microbial protein from poor nitrogen sources (recycled urea). Its disadvantages include the loss of easily digested nutrients to microbes, the energy cost of methane production and heat of fermentation, and a slower digestive strategy requiring large gut capacity. Because fermentation precedes the host's own digestion, soluble sugars and proteins are first consumed by microbes, unlike in hindgut fermenters.
