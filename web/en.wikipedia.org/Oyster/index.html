Oyster

Oyster

Kingdom: Animalia

Phylum: Mollusca

Class: Bivalvia

Principal family: Ostreidae (true oysters)

Oyster is the common name for a number of different families of salt-water bivalve molluscs that live in marine or brackish habitats. The "true oysters" of the family Ostreidae include most species eaten by humans, such as the Pacific oyster (Magallana gigas), the eastern oyster (Crassostrea virginica) and the European flat oyster (Ostrea edulis). Oysters are filter feeders that cement themselves to hard surfaces, often forming reefs that support rich ecosystems. Some oysters — principally the pearl oysters of a different family — produce pearls, and oyster shells and flesh have been valued by humans since prehistory.

Biology

An oyster's shell consists of two irregular valves of calcium carbonate; the lower valve is cupped and cemented to the substrate, while the flatter upper valve acts as a lid. A single powerful adductor muscle closes the shell. Oysters feed by pumping water across ciliated gills, straining out phytoplankton — an adult can filter tens of litres of water a day, measurably improving water clarity. Many species are protandric hermaphrodites, beginning life as males and becoming female as they grow; reproduction is by broadcast spawning of eggs and sperm, triggered by water temperature, and the free-swimming larvae eventually settle ("spatfall") onto hard surfaces, preferring the shell of other oysters.

Oysters as food

Oysters are eaten raw on the half shell, grilled, fried, smoked, and in dishes such as oysters Rockefeller, oyster stew and the oyster po' boy. They are rich in zinc, vitamin B12, iron and omega-3 fatty acids. Raw consumption carries risks of infection with Vibrio bacteria and norovirus, and oysters can accumulate toxins and pollutants from the water, so harvesting is regulated by water-quality classification. The folk rule of eating oysters only in months with an "R" reflects summer spawning and warm-water bacteria rather than any absolute prohibition.

Cultivation and reefs

Oyster farming dates to Roman times — Sergius Orata is credited with early cultivation in the Bay of Naples — and modern aquaculture, based on hatchery-produced spat set on shell or in bags and cages, produces most oysters consumed today; the Pacific oyster dominates world production. Wild oyster reefs have declined drastically from overharvesting, disease (such as MSX and Dermo in Chesapeake Bay) and habitat destruction, prompting large restoration programmes that rebuild reefs for their filtration and habitat value as much as for harvest.

Pearls

Pearls form when an irritant inside a mollusc becomes coated with nacre. The finest natural pearls come from pearl oysters (family Pteriidae) of the genus Pinctada, not from edible oysters; since the early 20th century, cultured pearls, produced by implanting a bead nucleus, have dominated the market.
