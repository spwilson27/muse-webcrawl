Non-classical logic

Non-classical logic
FieldLogic, philosophy, computer science
Deviations fromClassical propositional and predicate logic
ExamplesIntuitionistic, modal, fuzzy, relevance, paraconsistent, many-valued logic

Non-classical logics (and sometimes alternative logics) are formal systems that differ in some significant way from classical logical systems such as propositional and predicate logic. They arise by rejecting or modifying classical assumptions — for instance the law of excluded middle, the principle of bivalence, the explosion principle, or unrestricted monotonicity — usually to model reasoning that classical logic handles poorly, such as vagueness, possibility and necessity, time, knowledge, obligation, constructive proof, or inconsistency.

Major families of non-classical logic

Major families

Intuitionistic logic

Developed by L. E. J. Brouwer and formalized by Arend Heyting, intuitionistic logic rejects the law of excluded middle and double-negation elimination, requiring constructive proof: a statement is true only when a construction establishing it exists. It underlies constructive mathematics and type theory.

Modal and temporal logics

Modal logics extend classical logic with operators for necessity and possibility, and variants treat knowledge (epistemic), obligation (deontic), and time (temporal logic), the last widely used in computer science for verifying hardware and software.

Many-valued, fuzzy, and paraconsistent logics

Many-valued logics, beginning with Jan Łukasiewicz's three-valued system, admit truth values beyond true and false; fuzzy logic allows degrees of truth in the interval [0, 1] to handle vagueness. Paraconsistent logics block the principle of explosion, permitting reasoning from inconsistent premises without triviality. Relevance logics require premises to be genuinely relevant to conclusions, and linear logic treats propositions as resources that are consumed in inference.

Applications and debate

Non-classical logics are applied in artificial intelligence (non-monotonic reasoning about defaults and belief revision), database theory (inconsistent data), linguistics (vagueness and conditionals), and law (normative reasoning). Philosophers debate whether these systems are rivals to classical logic or extensions serving specialized purposes; Graham Priest and others defend genuinely deviant logics as correct accounts of reasoning in their domains.
