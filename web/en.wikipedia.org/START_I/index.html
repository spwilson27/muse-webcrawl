START I

START I (Strategic Arms Reduction Treaty) was a bilateral treaty between the United States and the Soviet Union on the reduction and limitation of strategic offensive arms. Signed on 31 July 1991 by Presidents George H. W. Bush and Mikhail Gorbachev, it was the largest and most complex arms control treaty in history, requiring both sides to reduce their deployed strategic nuclear warheads to 6,000 each. It entered into force on 5 December 1994 and expired on 5 December 2009.

Negotiations

President Ronald Reagan proposed the Strategic Arms Reduction Talks in 1982, shifting the focus from limiting launchers (as in SALT) to reducing warheads themselves. Negotiations proceeded through the 1980s, interrupted by disputes over the Strategic Defense Initiative and the 1983 Soviet walkout, and were completed after the Malta Summit under President Bush as the Cold War ended.

Terms and implementation

START I limited each side to 1,600 deployed strategic delivery vehicles (ICBMs, SLBMs and heavy bombers) and 6,000 accountable warheads, with sublimits on ballistic missile warheads (4,900), ICBM and SLBM throw-weight, and mobile ICBMs. Its elaborate counting rules and verification regime — including data exchanges, notifications, and 12 types of on-site inspection — set the template for later treaties. After the Soviet Union dissolved, the 1992 Lisbon Protocol made Russia, Belarus, Kazakhstan and Ukraine parties; the latter three transferred all nuclear weapons on their territory to Russia by 1996.

Legacy and successors

By the treaty's 2001 implementation deadline, about 80% of the world's strategic nuclear weapons had been removed from deployment. START I was followed by the 2002 Moscow Treaty and by New START (2010), which adopted lower limits and continued inspections.
