Cabinet of curiosities

Cabinets of curiosities, also known as wonder-rooms (German: Wunderkammer), were collections of notable objects emerging in Renaissance Europe. Their broad and varied contents — natural specimens, antiquities, artworks, and exotic artefacts — were intended as catalogues of the world, and the collections are regarded as forerunners of modern museums.

History

The classic cabinet of curiosities emerged in the sixteenth century, although earlier royal and scholarly collections existed. Monarchs, aristocrats, merchants, and scholars assembled objects ranging from preserved animals and fossils to ethnographic items from newly explored lands. Famous examples include the Kunstkamera of Peter the Great in Saint Petersburg and the collection of Ole Worm in Copenhagen.

Contents

Collections were commonly organised into categories such as naturalia (creatures and natural objects), artificialia (works of art and craft), scientifica (instruments), and exotica (objects from distant places). Displays juxtaposed the natural and the artificial, reflecting an encyclopaedic ambition rather than modern disciplinary boundaries.

Legacy

As public museums developed in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries — the Ashmolean Museum in Oxford and the British Museum among them — many private cabinets were absorbed into institutional collections and reorganised along scientific lines. The term survives for the furniture that housed smaller collections and in modern artistic and literary references.
