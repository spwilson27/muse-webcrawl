Açaí

Scientific nameEuterpe oleracea
FamilyArecaceae (palm family)
Native rangeAmazon basin, northern South America

Açaí (Portuguese: açaí, pronounced roughly "ah-sah-EE") refers to the fruit of the açaí palm (Euterpe oleracea), a species of palm tree cultivated for its fruit (açaí berries), hearts of palm, leaves, and trunk wood. The palm is native to floodplains and swamps of the Amazon basin, especially in the Brazilian state of Pará, and has been a staple food in the region for centuries.

The fruit

The fruit is a small, round, blackish-purple drupe about 25 mm in circumference, growing in branched clusters of several hundred on each palm. It has a thin edible skin around a large seed that makes up most of its volume. Fresh açaí is highly perishable, so for export it is processed into frozen pulp, juice or freeze-dried powder.

Culinary use

In the Amazon, açaí pulp is traditionally eaten savoury, with manioc flour (farinha) and fish or shrimp. In southern Brazil and abroad it became popular from the 1990s onward as a sweet frozen preparation — the açaí na tigela ("açaí in the bowl"), blended thick and topped with granola, banana and syrups, and marketed as an energy food among surfers and gym-goers.

Nutrition and claims

Açaí pulp is rich in fats (including oleic acid), fibre and anthocyanin pigments that give it its deep purple colour. Although it is widely marketed internationally as a "superfood" with antioxidant and weight-loss benefits, scientific evidence for specific health effects beyond ordinary nutritional value is limited, and regulators have acted against exaggerated marketing claims.

Harvest and economy

Demand has made açaí one of the most important non-timber forest products of the Amazon estuary, supporting riverine (ribeirinho) communities. Intensive management for fruit production has, however, raised ecological questions, since dense single-species stands can reduce the diversity of floodplain forests.
