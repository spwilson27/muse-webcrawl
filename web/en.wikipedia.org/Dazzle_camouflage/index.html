Dazzle camouflage

Dazzle camouflage, also known as razzle dazzle, was a family of ship camouflage schemes used extensively in the First World War and, to a lesser extent, in the Second World War. Credited to the British artist Norman Wilkinson, it consisted of complex patterns of geometric shapes in contrasting colours intersecting and distorting one another.

Purpose

Unlike other forms of camouflage, dazzle was not intended to conceal a ship but to make it difficult for enemy submarine commanders to estimate its range, speed and heading — the information needed to aim a torpedo through a periscope. Painted false bow waves, distorted bows and sterns, and clashing patterns were meant to confuse visual rangefinders and the observer's judgement of the ship's course.

Use and effectiveness

The Royal Navy and the United States Navy painted thousands of merchant and naval vessels in dazzle schemes from 1917 onward, with designs supervised in Britain by Wilkinson and in America with input from artists and stage designers. Statistical evidence for its effectiveness was inconclusive: dazzled ships were not clearly sunk less often, but the scheme raised crew morale, and no clear disadvantage was shown. In the Second World War, improved rangefinding and radar reduced its value, and it gave way to more conventional disruptive schemes, though its bold patterns have had a lasting influence on art and design.
