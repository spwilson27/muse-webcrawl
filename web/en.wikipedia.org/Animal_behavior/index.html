Animal behavior

Animal behavior is the scientific study of everything animals do, whether singly or with other animals — how they move, feed, communicate, learn, reproduce, and interact socially. The field, commonly called ethology when concerned with behaviour in natural conditions, bridges biology and psychology, drawing on evolution, physiology, and ecology.

History

Naturalists from Aristotle onward described animal habits, but the modern discipline crystallised in the 1930s with Konrad Lorenz, Nikolaas Tinbergen, and Karl von Frisch, who shared the 1973 Nobel Prize for work on imprinting, fixed action patterns, and honeybee communication. Tinbergen's "four questions" — causation, development, function, and evolution — remain the field's organising framework.

Key concepts

Behaviour arises from interaction between inherited predispositions and experience. Innate releasing mechanisms trigger fixed action patterns, such as a greylag goose retrieving a displaced egg. Learning ranges from habituation and classical conditioning to insight learning in primates and corvids. Migration, hibernation, courtship, territoriality, and parental care are shaped by natural and sexual selection to maximise reproductive success.

Social behaviour

Many animals live in groups, gaining protection from predators and help in finding food, at the cost of competition and disease transmission. Eusocial insects, cooperative breeders, and primate societies illustrate the evolution of altruism, explained through kin selection and reciprocity. Communication — visual, acoustic, chemical, and tactile — coordinates group life and underlies elaborate displays in courtship and aggression.

Methods

Ethologists combine field observation, controlled experiments, neuroethology, and comparative genomics. Behavioural ecology models behaviour as optimising fitness, using game theory and optimal foraging models.
