Caste system in India

The caste system in India is a system of social stratification that has divided Hindu society — and, in adapted forms, communities of other Indian religions — into hereditary, endogamous groups ranked in a hierarchy. It is generally traced to ancient India and has evolved over centuries under religious, economic and political influences, including codification during British colonial rule. Caste remains a significant factor in Indian social life, marriage and politics, even as urbanisation and law have weakened many of its traditional restrictions.

Varna and jati

Classical texts such as the Manusmriti describe four varnas: Brahmins (priests and scholars), Kshatriyas (kings and warriors), Vaishyas (merchants, landowners and farmers) and Shudras (labourers and service providers). In practice, society is organised into thousands of jatis — local, birth-based communities traditionally linked to occupations — whose relative rank varies by region. Groups historically considered outside and below the varna system, subjected to "untouchability", are today officially classed as Scheduled Castes; many of their members use the name Dalit ("oppressed").

History

Origins are debated: varna ideology appears in late Vedic texts such as the Purusha Sukta of the Rigveda, and hereditary occupational grouping hardened over the following centuries. Under British rule, from the late 19th century, decennial censuses enumerated and ranked castes, which many historians argue rigidified identities that had been more fluid. Reformers such as Jyotirao Phule, Swami Vivekananda and Mahatma Gandhi (who campaigned against untouchability) challenged the system, while B. R. Ambedkar, himself born into a Dalit community and the principal architect of India's constitution, called for its annihilation and eventually led a mass conversion to Buddhism in 1956.

Modern India

The Constitution of 1950 abolished untouchability and banned discrimination on grounds of caste, and legislation such as the Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act, 1989 criminalises caste-based violence. Reservation policies reserve seats in legislatures, government jobs and educational institutions for Scheduled Castes (about 15%), Scheduled Tribes (about 7.5%) and Other Backward Classes (about 27% in central institutions). Caste continues to shape electoral politics through caste-based parties and voting blocs, and endogamous marriage remains common; at the same time, discrimination and caste violence persist, and debates continue over caste census data and the extension of reservations.
