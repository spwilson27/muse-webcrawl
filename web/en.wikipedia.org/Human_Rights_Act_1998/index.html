The Human Rights Act 1998 (c. 42) is an Act of Parliament of the United Kingdom which received royal assent on 9 November 1998 and came into force on 2 October 2000. Its aim was to incorporate into UK law the rights contained in the European Convention on Human Rights (ECHR), so that people could enforce those rights in British courts rather than having to take cases to Strasbourg.

[Image: The Royal Courts of Justice, London]

Structure

The Act requires public authorities to act compatibly with Convention rights (section 6), requires courts to interpret legislation compatibly "so far as it is possible to do so" (section 3), and empowers higher courts to issue a declaration of incompatibility (section 4) where primary legislation cannot be read compatibly — although such a declaration does not strike down the legislation, preserving parliamentary sovereignty. Schedule 1 sets out the protected rights, including the right to life, prohibition of torture, right to a fair trial, and freedom of expression.

Effect and debate

The Act has influenced British law on privacy, policing, immigration, prisoners' voting, and freedom of the press. It has also been politically controversial: some governments have proposed replacing it with a "British Bill of Rights", and the Bill of Rights Bill introduced in 2022 was dropped in 2023. Devolution statutes for Scotland, Wales, and Northern Ireland also entrench Convention rights.
