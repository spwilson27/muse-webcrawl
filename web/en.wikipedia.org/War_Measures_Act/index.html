War Measures Act

Long titleAn Act to confer certain powers upon the Governor in Council and to amend the Immigration Act
Assented to22 August 1914
Enacted byParliament of Canada
Invoked1914 (First World War); 1939 (Second World War); 1970 (October Crisis)
Repealed1988 — replaced by the Emergencies Act

The War Measures Act was a statute of the Parliament of Canada that allowed the government to assume sweeping emergency powers in the event of "war, invasion or insurrection, real or apprehended". Passed on 22 August 1914, shortly after Canada entered the First World War, it was invoked three times in Canadian history. Because of its association with the internment of civilians and the suspension of civil liberties, it remains one of the most controversial laws in Canadian history. It was repealed in 1988 and replaced by the more circumscribed Emergencies Act.

Powers

Once invoked by the Governor in Council, the Act gave the federal cabinet authority to govern by order-in-council on matters including censorship, control of transportation and trade, arrest, detention, and deportation without trial, and the appropriation of property. Its measures were not subject to the ordinary protections of the Canadian Bill of Rights and could override provincial jurisdiction.

Invocations

First World War

Under the Act, about 8,500 "enemy aliens"—mostly Ukrainians and other Eastern Europeans—were interned in camps across Canada, and some 80,000 others had to register and report to police. Property was confiscated and many internees were used as forced labour.

Second World War

Invoked again in 1939, the Act underpinned the Defence of Canada Regulations and the internment and forced relocation of about 22,000 Japanese Canadians from the Pacific coast beginning in 1942, together with the seizure and sale of their property. Smaller numbers of German and Italian Canadians were also interned.

October Crisis (1970)

The Act's only peacetime use came on 16 October 1970, when Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau's government invoked it at the request of Quebec authorities during the October Crisis, after the Front de libération du Québec (FLQ) kidnapped British trade commissioner James Cross and Quebec labour minister Pierre Laporte. Public protest was effectively banned, and 497 people were arrested; most were released without charge. Laporte was found murdered. Trudeau's televised defence of the measures—"Just watch me"—became a defining moment of his premiership.

Repeal and replacement

Dissatisfaction with the Act's breadth led Parliament to replace it in 1988 with the Emergencies Act, which defines emergencies more narrowly, requires parliamentary review, and expressly preserves Charter rights. The federal government later issued formal apologies and settlements for the First World War internments and for the internment and dispossession of Japanese Canadians.
