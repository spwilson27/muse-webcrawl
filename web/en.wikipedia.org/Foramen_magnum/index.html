The foramen magnum (Latin for "great hole") is a large, oval-shaped opening in the occipital bone of the skull. It is one of the several oval or circular openings (foramina) in the base of the skull. The spinal cord, an extension of the medulla oblongata, passes through the foramen magnum as it exits the cranial cavity.

Structure and contents

Apart from the transmission of the medulla oblongata and its membranes, the foramen magnum transmits the vertebral arteries and the anterior and posterior spinal arteries, as well as the spinal roots of the accessory nerve (CN XI). The alar ligaments, which attach the axis (second cervical vertebra) to the skull, also pass through it.

Anthropological significance

The position of the foramen magnum is a key indicator of bipedalism in fossil hominins: in upright walkers it is located centrally under the skull, balancing the head on the vertebral column, whereas in quadrupedal apes it lies toward the back of the skull. Its forward placement in species such as Sahelanthropus and Ardipithecus has been used as evidence of habitual bipedal posture.
