Mentalism

Mentalism is a performing art in which its practitioners, known as mentalists, use mental acuity, cold reading, hot reading, principles of stage magic and suggestion to present the illusion of extraordinary mental abilities — such as mind reading, psychokinesis, precognition and clairvoyance. Mentalism is commonly classified with stage magic but is distinguished by its presentation: mentalists frame their feats as psychological skill or genuine psychic power rather than as tricks.

Techniques and style

Classic mentalism effects include predictions, book tests, drawing duplications, billet reading and two-person telepathy codes. Performers have used methods ranging from confederates and pre-show work to equivoque (magician's choice), muscle reading and the ideomotor effect. Noted mentalists include Joseph Dunninger, Theodore Annemann — whose journal The Jinx remains influential — the Banachek, Marc Salem, Derren Brown and Oz Pearlman.

History and controversy

Mentalism grew out of the 19th-century séance and stage thought-reading acts. Because its claims mimic those of mediums and psychics, the art has long sat at the boundary between entertainment and deception; organizations of magicians require performers to make clear their feats are achieved by natural means. Some modern performers adopt an explicitly psychological framing, acknowledging trickery while presenting effects as demonstrations of observation, memory and influence.
