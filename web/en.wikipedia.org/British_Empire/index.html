British Empire

The British Empire comprised the dominions, colonies, protectorates, mandates, and other territories ruled or administered by the United Kingdom and its predecessor states. At its territorial peak in 1921 it covered about 35.5 million square kilometres (13.7 million square miles) — roughly a quarter of the Earth's land surface — and ruled some 458 million people, a quarter of the world's population. It remains the largest empire in history, often described as "the empire on which the sun never sets."

Origins and expansion

The empire began with overseas trading posts and colonies in the late 16th and early 17th centuries, in North America, the Caribbean, and India, where the East India Company built a territorial raj. Defeat in the American Revolutionary War cost Britain its Thirteen Colonies, but victory in the Napoleonic Wars and the industrial revolution fuelled a second empire across Africa, Asia, and the Pacific, secured by Royal Navy supremacy and formalised in the scramble for Africa of the late nineteenth century.

Twentieth century and decolonisation

Dominions such as Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and South Africa gained increasing self-government, recognised in the Statute of Westminster 1931. After the Second World War, exhausted Britain presided over rapid decolonisation: Indian independence in 1947, the "Wind of Change" across Africa in the 1960s, and finally the handover of Hong Kong in 1997, often taken as the empire's end. Its legacy lives on in the Commonwealth of Nations, the global spread of the English language, common-law systems, and parliamentary institutions — as well as in continuing debates over colonial violence, exploitation, and restitution. Fourteen British Overseas Territories remain under UK sovereignty today.
