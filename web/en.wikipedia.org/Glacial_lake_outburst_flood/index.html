Glacial lake outburst flood

A glacial lake outburst flood (GLOF) is a type of outburst flood caused by the sudden failure of a dam — composed of glacier ice, moraine, or bedrock — that contains the waters of a glacial lake. In Iceland such floods are called jökulhlaups. GLOFs can release millions of cubic metres of water in a matter of hours, transforming a quiet mountain valley into a destructive torrent carrying boulders, sediment, and debris.

Causes

Outburst floods may be triggered by erosion of the dam, water pressure, avalanches of rock or snow into the lake, earthquakes, or melting of ice within the dam. Displacement waves caused by landslides or calving ice can overtop moraine dams and initiate breaching. In subglacial settings, geothermal heat or volcanic eruptions can accumulate meltwater beneath ice until it escapes through tunnels in the ice.

Notable events

The 1941 outburst of Lake Palcacocha in Peru destroyed part of the city of Huaraz and killed several thousand people, spurring decades of Peruvian lake-control works. In 2013 the failure of Chorabari Lake contributed to catastrophic flooding in Uttarakhand, India. The periodic draining of Grímsvötn beneath Iceland's Vatnajökull produces some of the largest measured jökulhlaups; the 1996 event peaked at roughly 55,000 cubic metres per second, second only to the Amazon among rivers.

Risk and monitoring

Climate warming is enlarging many glacial lakes, increasing the population at risk in regions such as the Himalaya, Andes, and Central Asia. Mitigation measures include lowering lake levels with siphons or channels, reinforcing dams, and satellite-based monitoring of dangerous lakes.
