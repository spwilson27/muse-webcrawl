Vocoder

A vocoder (from voice encoder) is a system for analysing and synthesizing the human voice, originally developed for telecommunications to reduce the bandwidth needed to transmit speech. A channel vocoder divides the speech signal into frequency bands, measures the energy in each band over time, and uses those slowly varying signals to control a synthesizer that reconstructs an approximation of the voice.

History

The vocoder was invented by Homer Dudley at Bell Telephone Laboratories in the 1930s and demonstrated in the Voder speech synthesizer at the 1939 New York World's Fair. During the Second World War it was used in the SIGSALY system, which securely encrypted voice communications between Allied leaders. Linear predictive coding (LPC), developed in the 1960s–70s, became the basis of many modern speech codecs.

Musical use

In music, a vocoder combines a voice (the modulator) with a synthesized sound (the carrier), producing the robotic, singing-synthesizer effect heard in recordings by Kraftwerk, Herbie Hancock, Daft Punk and many others. It is distinct from the talk box, which shapes an instrument's sound with the mouth.
