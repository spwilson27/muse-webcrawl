Algor mortis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Algor mortis (from Latin algor, "coolness", and mortis, "of death"), also called the death chill, is the reduction in body temperature following death. It is one of the recognised stages and signs of death, alongside pallor mortis, livor mortis and rigor mortis, and is used in forensic medicine as a rough means of estimating the post-mortem interval — the time since death.

Cooling curve

After death, the body ceases to generate heat through metabolism, and its temperature gradually approaches that of the surrounding environment. Cooling typically shows a plateau in the first half-hour or so, followed by an approximately linear decline often estimated at roughly 1.5 °F (about 0.8 °C) per hour under ordinary indoor conditions, before levelling off as the body nears ambient temperature. Despite the rule of thumb, the rate varies widely.

Factors affecting cooling

The rate of algor mortis depends on ambient temperature, air movement, immersion in water, body size and fat distribution, clothing and coverings, posture and the surface on which the body lies, and on the body temperature at the moment of death — which may have been raised by fever, exertion or certain drugs, or lowered by hypothermia. Because of these variables, forensic pathologists treat temperature-based estimates (including formal methods such as the Henssge nomogram, which incorporates body weight and correction factors) as only one element in estimating time of death, alongside rigor, livor and decomposition.
