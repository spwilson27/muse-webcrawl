Adam Smith (baptised 16 June 1723 – 17 July 1790) was a Scottish economist and philosopher who was a pioneer in the thinking of political economy and key figure during the Scottish Enlightenment. Also known as "The Father of Economics" or "The Father of Capitalism", he wrote two classic works, The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759) and An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations (1776).

Life

Smith was born in Kirkcaldy, Fife, the posthumous son of a customs comptroller. He studied at the University of Glasgow under Francis Hutcheson and at Balliol College, Oxford. In 1751 he became professor of logic, then of moral philosophy, at Glasgow. After publishing The Theory of Moral Sentiments, he travelled in France as tutor to the young Duke of Buccleuch, meeting Voltaire and the Physiocrats. Returning to Kirkcaldy, he spent about a decade writing The Wealth of Nations. In 1778 he was appointed a commissioner of customs in Edinburgh, where he died in 1790.

The Wealth of Nations

Published on 9 March 1776, The Wealth of Nations attacked mercantilism and argued that national wealth lies in production and trade rather than hoarded bullion. Its first book analyses the division of labour — illustrated by the pin factory — productivity, wages and profit. Smith argued that individuals pursuing their own interest are led by an "invisible hand" to promote the public good, and he defended free trade and competitive markets, while assigning the state duties of defence, justice, public works and education. The book became the foundational text of classical economics.

The Theory of Moral Sentiments

Smith's first book grounded morality in sympathy and the figure of the "impartial spectator", by whose imagined judgment people regulate their conduct. Scholars have debated the apparent tension between sympathy in this work and self-interest in The Wealth of Nations — the so-called "Adam Smith problem" — though modern scholarship generally treats the two as parts of one system.

Legacy

Smith's portrait appears on the Bank of England £20 note (2007 series). His ideas shaped liberal economic thought, and his name is invoked by advocates and critics of market capitalism alike.
