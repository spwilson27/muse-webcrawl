Reform or Revolution

Reform or Revolution (German: Sozialreform oder Revolution?) is a polemic written in 1899 by the Marxist theorist Rosa Luxemburg. The work is one of the foundational documents of revolutionary socialism and a direct reply to Eduard Bernstein's Evolutionary Socialism (1899), in which Bernstein argued that capitalism was adapting to its contradictions and that the working class could achieve socialism gradually through parliamentary reforms, trade-union struggle, and the expansion of democracy.

Author: Rosa Luxemburg
Original title: Sozialreform oder Revolution?
Published: 1899 (expanded 1908)
Subject: Marxist theory, revisionism debate

Argument

Luxemburg argued that the reforms Bernstein pointed to — credit, improved communications, cartels, and the growth of trade unions — did not abolish capitalism's contradictions but displaced and sharpened them. Credit, she contended, hastened overproduction and speculation; cartels raised prices and intensified competition internationally rather than ending anarchy of production.

Her central claim was that the daily struggle for reforms and the final goal of social revolution are inseparably linked: "Between social reforms and revolution there exists an indissoluble tie. The struggle for reforms is its means; the social revolution, its aim." Trade unions and parliamentary activity were necessary schools and instruments of struggle, but they could not by themselves transform capitalist property relations, because the state, however democratised, remained an organ of class rule.

Luxemburg further argued that democracy was indispensable to the socialist movement not as a substitute for revolution but as the terrain on which the proletariat matures politically. She rejected both Bernstein's gradualism and any putschist shortcut, insisting that the conquest of political power grows out of mass struggle.

Legacy

The pamphlet became a canonical text of the revolutionary left and set the terms of the "revisionism debate" within the Second International. It is frequently cited alongside Luxemburg's later works The Accumulation of Capital (1913) and The Russian Revolution (written 1918).
