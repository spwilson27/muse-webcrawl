Baroque painting


Dramatic light and movement typify Baroque painting.

Baroque painting is the painting associated with the Baroque cultural movement, which began in Italy in the 17th century and spread across Europe. The style is characterized by dramatic movement, intense emotion, strong contrasts of light and shadow (chiaroscuro and tenebrism), rich color, and a sense of grandeur intended to evoke awe, often in service of the Counter-Reformation Church and absolutist monarchies.

Characteristics

Baroque painters favored dynamic compositions with diagonal movement, foreshortening, and figures that seem to burst beyond the picture frame. Ceiling frescoes used illusionistic perspective (quadratura) to open architecture into painted skies. Subjects ranged from religious scenes and mythology to portraiture, still life, landscape, and scenes of everyday life.

Regional developments

In Italy, Caravaggio revolutionized painting with stark naturalism and tenebrism, while Annibale Carracci and the Bolognese school pursued a classical reform of painting; Pietro da Cortona and Luca Giordano created vast illusionistic ceilings. In Flanders, Peter Paul Rubens combined Italian influences with Northern detail in exuberant history paintings, and Anthony van Dyck excelled in portraiture. In Spain, Diego Velázquez, Francisco de Zurbarán, and Bartolomé Esteban Murillo produced works for court and church. In the Dutch Republic, a Protestant merchant society supported intimate genre scenes, landscapes, and still lifes by artists such as Rembrandt van Rijn and Johannes Vermeer. In France, Nicolas Poussin and Claude Lorrain developed a classical variant, while Georges de La Tour painted candlelit scenes.
