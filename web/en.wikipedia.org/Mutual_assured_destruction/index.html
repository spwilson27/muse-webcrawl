Mutual assured destruction

Mutual assured destruction (MAD) is a doctrine of military strategy and national security in which a full-scale use of nuclear weapons by an attacker against a nuclear-armed defender would cause the complete annihilation of both. It rests on deterrence by retaliation: each side maintains a survivable arsenal capable of inflicting unacceptable damage in a second strike even after absorbing a surprise first strike.

Development

The logic emerged in the 1950s and 1960s as the United States and the Soviet Union built large thermonuclear arsenals and delivery systems — bombers, land-based intercontinental ballistic missiles, and ballistic-missile submarines, together forming the nuclear triad. U.S. Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara articulated "assured destruction" in the 1960s, defining it by the ability to destroy a set fraction of the adversary's population and industry after riding out an attack. The acronym MAD was coined by critic Donald Brennan, who intended it as a derisive label for a strategy he considered insane.

Mechanism and theory

MAD requires credible second-strike capability, secure command and control, and communication of resolve; its credibility is reinforced by arrangements such as submarine patrols, hardened silos, airborne alert, and automatic or semi-automatic retaliation systems. Game theorists model it as a stable equilibrium provided both sides are rational and arsenals are survivable; critics object to the dependence on rationality, the catastrophic consequences of accident or miscalculation — illustrated by close calls such as the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962 and the 1983 Able Archer scare — and the moral status of threatening mass death.

Arms control and legacy

The doctrine underlies the Anti-Ballistic Missile Treaty of 1972, which deliberately limited missile defences to preserve mutual vulnerability, and subsequent arms-reduction treaties from SALT to New START. With the Cold War's end, the doctrine's applicability to multipolar nuclear rivalries and smaller arsenals remains debated, but the basic logic of mutual vulnerability continues to shape nuclear strategy among the major powers.
