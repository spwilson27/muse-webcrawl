Vitruvius

Vitruvius (c. 80–70 BC – after c. 15 BC), in full Marcus Vitruvius Pollio, was a Roman author, architect, and civil and military engineer of the 1st century BC, best known for his multi-volume treatise De architectura ("On architecture"), the only substantial architectural text to survive from classical antiquity.

[Image: Leonardo da Vinci's Vitruvian Man, the famous drawing inspired by Vitruvius's proportions.]

Life and work

Vitruvius served as a military engineer under Julius Caesar and later under Augustus, to whom De architectura is dedicated. The work, in ten books, covers building materials, temple design, proportion and symmetry, public and private buildings, water supply, sundials and astronomy, and military engines. He set out the famous triad that architecture must satisfy: firmitas (firmness), utilitas (commodity), and venustas (delight).

Influence

Rediscovered in the Renaissance, Vitruvius deeply influenced architects from Alberti and Palladio onward; Leonardo's Vitruvian Man illustrates his canon of human proportions.
