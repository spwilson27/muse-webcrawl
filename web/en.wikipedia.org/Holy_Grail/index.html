Holy Grail

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Holy Grail (French: Saint Graal, Breton: Graal Santel, Welsh: Greol Sant) is a treasure that serves as an important motif in Arthurian literature. Various traditions describe the Holy Grail as a cup, dish, or stone with miraculous healing powers, sometimes providing eternal youth or sustenance in infinite abundance, often guarded in the custody of the Fisher King and located in the hidden Grail castle. By analogy, any elusive object or goal of great significance may be perceived as a "holy grail" by those seeking it.

A "grail" (Old French: graal or greal), wondrous but not unequivocally holy, first appears in Perceval, the Story of the Grail, an unfinished romance written by Chrétien de Troyes around 1190. Chrétien's story inspired many continuations, translators and interpreters in the later 12th and early 13th centuries, including Wolfram von Eschenbach, who perceived the Grail as a stone. In the late 12th century, Robert de Boron associated the Grail with the cup used by Jesus at the Last Supper and in which Joseph of Arimathea later collected the blood of Jesus at the Crucifixion.

Origins and development

Scholars have long debated the origins of the Grail legend. Some trace it to Celtic mythology, pointing to magical vessels in Welsh and Irish tales — cauldrons of plenty and renewal such as the Cauldron of the Dagda — which may have fed into the Welsh sources of Arthurian romance. Others emphasise Christian and liturgical influences, noting the Grail's strong association with the Eucharist in later texts.

In Chrétien's Perceval, the hero witnesses a mysterious procession at the castle of the Fisher King in which a bleeding lance and a golden grail, encrusted with jewels, are carried past; the grail provides a feast, but its exact nature is left unexplained. Robert de Boron's Joseph d'Arimathie gave the vessel its Christian back-story as the cup of the Last Supper. The anonymous authors of the vast Lancelot-Grail (Vulgate) cycle and Sir Thomas Malory in Le Morte d'Arthur made the Quest for the Holy Grail the supreme adventure of the Knights of the Round Table, achieved in full only by the pure knight Sir Galahad, accompanied by Perceval and Bors.

The Grail quest

In the mature legend, the Grail is kept in a castle whose lord, the Fisher King (or Maimed King), suffers from a wound that will not heal, while his land lies waste. The questing knight must ask the right question — in early versions, what the Grail serves or whom it serves — to heal the king and restore the land. Failure condemns Perceval to years of further wandering. The story intertwines themes of purity, kingship, sacrifice, and spiritual attainment, and has been read both as Christian allegory and as a survival of older fertility ritual.

Later tradition and modern culture

The Grail has been linked in popular tradition with numerous relics and locations: the Santo Cáliz of Valencia Cathedral, the Sacro Catino of Genoa, and the Nanteos Cup in Wales have all been proposed as the "real" Grail, while Glastonbury in Somerset became associated with Joseph of Arimathea and the Grail through medieval monastic legend. Richard Wagner's operas Parsifal (1882) and Lohengrin gave the legend a lasting musical form. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the Grail has featured in works from T. S. Eliot's The Waste Land to the films Monty Python and the Holy Grail (1975) and Indiana Jones and the Last Crusade (1989), and in Dan Brown's The Da Vinci Code (2003), which popularised the theory that the Grail is a metaphor for a bloodline.
