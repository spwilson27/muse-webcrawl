Thomas Mann

Paul Thomas Mann
Born: 6 June 1875, Lübeck, German Empire
Died: 12 August 1955 (aged 80), Zurich, Switzerland
Occupation: Novelist, essayist, short story writer
Notable works: Buddenbrooks, The Magic Mountain, Death in Venice, Doctor Faustus
Awards: Nobel Prize in Literature (1929)

Paul Thomas Mann (6 June 1875 – 12 August 1955) was a German novelist, short story writer, social critic, and essayist, widely regarded as one of the most influential German writers of the 20th century. He received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1929, principally for his first novel, Buddenbrooks. His highly symbolic and ironic epic novels and novellas are noted for their insight into the psychology of the artist and the intellectual, and for their exploration of the conflict between art and life, and between bourgeois order and creative vitality.

Life

Mann was born in Lübeck into a wealthy grain-merchant family. After his father's death in 1891 and the liquidation of the family firm, he moved with his mother to Munich. His first novel, Buddenbrooks (1901), a chronicle of the decline of a Lübeck merchant family over four generations, made him famous at the age of 26. The novella Death in Venice (1912) and the novel The Magic Mountain (1924), set in a Swiss sanatorium before the First World War, consolidated his reputation.

Initially a conservative and supporter of the German war effort in 1914 — positions set out in Reflections of a Nonpolitical Man (1918) — Mann gradually moved toward liberal democracy, publicly defending the Weimar Republic from 1922. When the Nazis came to power in 1933, he went into exile, eventually settling in the United States, where he became a citizen in 1944 and broadcast anti-Nazi speeches to Germany for the BBC. His late novel Doctor Faustus (1947) retells the Faust legend as an allegory of Germany's pact with Nazism. After the war he divided his time between the United States and Switzerland, where he died in Zurich in 1955.

Major works

  • Buddenbrooks (1901)
  • Tonio Kröger (1903)
  • Death in Venice (1912)
  • The Magic Mountain (1924)
  • Joseph and His Brothers (1933–1943), a tetralogy
  • Lotte in Weimar (1939)
  • Doctor Faustus (1947)
  • Confessions of Felix Krull (1954, unfinished)

Legacy

Mann's work is characterised by elaborate prose, leitmotif technique borrowed from Wagnerian music, and ironic distance. He was the younger brother of the novelist Heinrich Mann and the father of six children, including the writers Klaus and Erika Mann. He is commonly ranked alongside Franz Kafka and Robert Musil as a defining figure of German-language modernism.
