Linear no-threshold model

The linear no-threshold model (LNT) is a dose-response model used in radiation protection to estimate the long-term, biological damage caused by ionizing radiation. It holds that the response is linear, that there is no threshold dose below which the response ceases, and that the damage at low doses can be extrapolated from data at high doses, notably the studies of atomic bomb survivors.

Use in regulation

The LNT model underpins most radiation protection standards worldwide. It is used to set dose limits for workers and the public and to convert collective dose into predicted numbers of excess cancers, forming the basis of the ALARA principle ("as low as reasonably achievable") that guides exposure minimization.

Controversy

The model is debated. Critics argue that at very low doses, cellular repair mechanisms make linear extrapolation unrealistic, and point to radiation hormesis — the hypothesis that small doses may be harmless or even beneficial. Supporters, including the BEIR VII committee and the International Commission on Radiological Protection, defend LNT as a prudent and practical basis for regulation in the absence of better low-dose data.
