The Golden Bough

The Golden Bough: A Study in Magic and Religion is a wide-ranging comparative study of mythology and religion by the Scottish anthropologist Sir James George Frazer. First published in two volumes in 1890, it grew to twelve volumes in the third edition (1906–15), with a popular one-volume abridgement in 1922.

Argument

Frazer set out to explain the ritual at Nemi in Italy, where the priest of Diana — the King of the Wood — held office until slain by a successor who had plucked a golden bough from a sacred tree. Following that thread, Frazer compared myths and rites worldwide: sacred kingship, dying and rising gods, scapegoats, fertility festivals, tree worship and taboo. His evolutionary scheme held that human thought progresses from magic (sympathetic magic, governed by the laws of similarity and contact) through religion to science.

Influence and criticism

The Golden Bough profoundly influenced modernist literature — T. S. Eliot's The Waste Land, D. H. Lawrence, W. B. Yeats and James Joyce all drew on it — and helped found the study of comparative mythology. Later anthropologists, including Wittgenstein in his Remarks on Frazer's Golden Bough, criticised its armchair method, its collation of practices stripped of context, and its evolutionary framework, yet its literary power and the myth-ritual patterns it traced remain influential.
