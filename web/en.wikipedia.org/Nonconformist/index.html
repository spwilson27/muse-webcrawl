Nonconformist

A Nonconformist was an English Protestant who did not "conform" to the governance and usages of the established church in England, the Church of England. Use of the term intensified after the Restoration of the monarchy in 1660, when the Act of Uniformity 1662 required clergy to assent to the Book of Common Prayer; some two thousand ministers who refused were ejected, an event known as the Great Ejection.

Denominations

Nonconformist traditions include Presbyterians, Congregationalists, Baptists, Quakers and later Methodists — groups collectively also called Dissenters or, after toleration, Free Churches. The Toleration Act 1689 granted limited freedom of worship, but civil disabilities such as exclusion from the universities of Oxford and Cambridge remained until the 19th century.

Influence

Nonconformists were prominent in British industry, commerce, philanthropy and reform politics; the "Nonconformist conscience" shaped Liberal Party politics in the Victorian era. In modern usage, "nonconformist" in lower case denotes anyone who refuses to conform to prevailing norms.
