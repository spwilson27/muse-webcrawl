Thermal conduction

Thermal conduction
Governing lawFourier's law
PropertyThermal conductivity (k), measured in W/(m·K)
MechanismsMolecular collisions, free electrons, phonons

Thermal conduction is the transfer of internal energy by microscopic diffusion and collisions of particles (including molecules, atoms, electrons and phonons) within a body due to a temperature gradient. It is one of the three principal modes of heat transfer, alongside convection and thermal radiation.

Fourier's law

The rate of heat transfer through a material is described by Fourier's law: the heat flux is proportional to the negative gradient of temperature and to the thermal conductivity of the material, q = −k∇T. Materials with high thermal conductivity, such as copper and aluminium, are good conductors; materials with low conductivity, such as wood, foam and still air, act as thermal insulators.

Mechanisms

In solids, conduction occurs through lattice vibrations (phonons) and, in metals, through the movement of free electrons, which explains why good electrical conductors are usually good thermal conductors as well — a relationship described by the Wiedemann–Franz law. In liquids and gases, conduction arises from collisions and diffusion of molecules during their random motion.

Applications

Understanding conduction is central to building insulation, heat sinks for electronics, cookware design, heat exchangers and the thermal management of engines and spacecraft.
