NP-completeness

In computational complexity theory, a decision problem is NP-complete if it belongs to the class NP — its solutions can be verified in polynomial time — and every problem in NP can be reduced to it in polynomial time. NP-complete problems are therefore the "hardest" problems in NP: if any one of them admits a polynomial-time algorithm, then all of them do, resolving the famous P versus NP problem.

History

Stephen Cook introduced the concept in his 1971 paper "The Complexity of Theorem-Proving Procedures", proving that the Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT) is NP-complete; Leonid Levin independently obtained a similar result in the Soviet Union in 1973. In 1972 Richard Karp published a landmark list of 21 NP-complete problems, including the clique problem, the travelling salesman decision problem, and graph colouring, demonstrating the concept's reach across combinatorics and optimisation.

Examples and practice

Thousands of problems are now known to be NP-complete, among them Hamiltonian path, subset sum, vertex cover, and many scheduling and routing problems. Because no efficient exact algorithm is known (and none is expected), practitioners rely on heuristics, approximation algorithms, fixed-parameter algorithms, and exponential methods that work well on typical instances. Modern SAT solvers, for instance, routinely solve large industrial instances despite worst-case hardness.

Proof technique

To prove a problem NP-complete, one shows it is in NP and gives a polynomial-time reduction from a known NP-complete problem. Reductions compose, so a single seed result — Cook's theorem — anchors an enormous web of hardness proofs.
