Fourier analysis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, Fourier analysis is the study of the way general functions may be represented or approximated by sums of simpler trigonometric functions. Fourier analysis grew from the study of Fourier series, and is named after Joseph Fourier, who showed that representing a function as a sum of trigonometric functions greatly simplifies the study of heat transfer.

Overview

The subject encompasses the Fourier series for periodic functions, the Fourier transform for functions on the real line, and their discrete counterparts. A function is decomposed into its constituent frequencies — analogous to expressing a musical chord as the set of its component notes — and the resulting frequency-domain representation often makes problems easier: linear differential equations become algebraic equations, and convolution becomes pointwise multiplication.

Applications

Fourier methods are foundational across science and engineering: signal processing and audio compression (MP3, JPEG's discrete cosine transform), image analysis, telecommunications, optics, quantum mechanics, probability theory (characteristic functions), number theory, and the solution of partial differential equations. The fast Fourier transform (FFT), popularised by Cooley and Tukey in 1965, computes the discrete Fourier transform in O(n log n) time and is one of the most important algorithms of the twentieth century.

Variants

Variants include the discrete-time Fourier transform, the short-time Fourier transform used for time-varying signals, Fourier analysis on groups and manifolds (spherical harmonics), and non-commutative harmonic analysis, of which classical Fourier analysis is the abelian special case.
