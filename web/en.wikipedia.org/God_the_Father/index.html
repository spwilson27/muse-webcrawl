God the Father

God the Father is a title given to God in various religions, most prominently in Christianity. In mainstream trinitarian Christianity, God the Father is regarded as the first person of the Trinity, followed by the second person, God the Son (Jesus Christ), and the third person, God the Holy Spirit. Since the second century, Christian creeds have included affirmation of belief in "God the Father (Almighty)", primarily in his capacity as "Father and creator of the universe."

Christianity

In Christianity, God is addressed as "Father" in part because of the mystery of the Trinity and, more generally, because of his role as creator and caring provider for his children. Jesus taught his disciples to pray addressing God as "Our Father" (the Lord's Prayer, Matthew 6:9). The Nicene Creed of 325 begins: "We believe in one God, the Father Almighty, Maker of heaven and earth, and of all things visible and invisible."

Christians hold that through Jesus Christ, believers become adopted children of God and co-heirs with Christ. The fatherhood of God is thus both creative — he is the source of all that exists — and relational, grounded in the eternal relationship between the Father and the Son within the Godhead.

Judaism

In Judaism, God is sometimes described with fatherly metaphors — for example as the father of Israel in the Hebrew Bible (Deuteronomy 32:6; Isaiah 63:16) — but these are understood metaphorically rather than as a literal, unique title, and God is not addressed as a distinct "person" of a Trinity. Jewish theology emphasises the absolute unity of God.

Islam

Islam rejects the literal fatherhood of God. The Quran teaches that God neither begets nor is begotten (Surah Al-Ikhlas, 112:3), and the concept of God as a literal father is considered incompatible with tawhid, the absolute oneness of God. Muslims may, however, understand "father" only in the loosest metaphorical sense of creator and sustainer, and generally avoid the term.

Depiction in art

Early Christian art rarely depicted God the Father directly, often using a hand emerging from clouds (the Manus Dei). From the Renaissance onward, Western artists such as Michelangelo in the Sistine Chapel ceiling portrayed him as an elderly, bearded patriarchal figure, an image that became canonical in Western culture. Eastern Orthodox tradition is more restrictive, generally avoiding depictions of the Father except in the "Otechestvo" (Fatherhood) icon type.
