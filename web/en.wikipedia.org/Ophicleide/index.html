Ophicleide

The ophicleide is a keyed brass instrument of the bugle family, conical in bore, developed in the early 19th century as a bass member of the keyed bugles. Its name derives from the Greek ophis (serpent) and kleis (key), referring to its keyed, serpent-like form.

ClassificationBrass instrument, aerophone
Inventedc. 1817–1821, Halary (Paris)
RangeBass; built in B♭, C, E♭

History

The ophicleide was patented in 1821 by the Parisian maker Halary (Jean Hilaire Asté). It quickly became the standard bass brass voice in French orchestras and bands, replacing the serpent and bass horn, and figured in works by Berlioz, Mendelssohn, Wagner and Verdi before the tuba displaced it later in the century.

Construction and playing

The instrument is a long conical tube bent into a U or bassoon-like shape, with large padded keys covering the tone holes — typically nine to eleven keys. It is played with a cupped mouthpiece similar to that of a trombone or tuba. Alto and soprano sizes (the quinticlave and smaller variants) also existed.

Repertory

Notable parts include the Symphonie fantastique of Berlioz, in which the ophicleide famously intones the Dies irae, and Mendelssohn's music for A Midsummer Night's Dream. In modern performance the parts are usually taken by the tuba or cimbasso, though period-instrument ensembles have revived the original.
