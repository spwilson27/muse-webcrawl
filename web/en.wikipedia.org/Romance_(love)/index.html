Romance (love)

Romance
FieldPsychology; sociology; cultural studies
Related conceptsCourtly love, limerence, attachment, intimacy
Key theoristsRobert Sternberg; Dorothy Tennov; Helen Fisher

Romantic love combines emotional intimacy with passion and idealisation of the beloved.

Romance or romantic love is a feeling of strong attraction and emotional attachment toward another person, typically associated with courtship, intimacy, passion and the desire for a continuing relationship. Unlike platonic love or familial affection, romance usually includes sexual attraction, but it is distinguished by its emotional intensity, idealisation of the partner, and the wish for reciprocation and exclusivity.

Historical development

The modern concept descends partly from courtly love, the idealised, often adulterous devotion celebrated by troubadours in medieval Provence, and from the chivalric romances of the period. For most of history marriage was arranged for economic and dynastic reasons, and romantic love was widely regarded as a poor or even dangerous basis for it. From the 18th and 19th centuries, especially in Europe and North America, love matches gradually became the expected norm, a shift associated with individualism, urbanisation and the novel — the literary genre also called romance.

Psychology

Psychologists study romance through several frameworks. Dorothy Tennov described limerence, the involuntary state of obsessive longing in early love. Robert Sternberg's triangular theory analyses love as a combination of intimacy, passion and commitment, with "consummate love" containing all three. Helen Fisher and others have used brain imaging to distinguish lust, attraction and attachment systems. Research also distinguishes passionate love, intense and often short-lived, from companionate love, the deep affection characteristic of long-term partnerships.

Cultural variation

The expression and social role of romance vary widely across cultures and eras, from arranged-marriage systems in which affection is expected to develop after marriage, to dating cultures where it precedes it. Romantic gestures, anniversaries and celebrations such as Valentine's Day are commercialised in many societies. Romance is a dominant theme of popular culture, from poetry and opera to the romance film and the romance novel, one of the largest-selling categories of fiction.
