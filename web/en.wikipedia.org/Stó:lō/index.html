Stó:lō

The Stó:lō are a group of Indigenous peoples in British Columbia, Canada, whose traditional territories lie along the Fraser River from the Fraser Canyon to the river's mouth and the surrounding valleys. Their name, meaning "river people" in the Halq'eméylem language, reflects their deep connection to the Fraser and its salmon fisheries.

Stó:lō
Region: Fraser Valley, British Columbia
Language: Halq'eméylem
Meaning: "River people"

Culture

Stó:lō society was historically organized around extended family longhouses, winter ceremonial dances, and a rich oral tradition recorded in part in the collaborative history work known as the Stó:lō Atlas. Salmon fishing, cedar working, and basketry remain central cultural practices.

History

Archaeological evidence shows occupation of the Fraser Valley for thousands of years. Contact with European fur traders in the early 19th century, the Fraser Canyon Gold Rush of 1858, and the imposition of reserves profoundly reshaped Stó:lō life. Smallpox epidemics caused severe population losses.

Modern governance

Today Stó:lō communities are represented by organizations including the Stó:lō Nation and the Stó:lō Tribal Council, and several First Nations participate in the British Columbia treaty process.
