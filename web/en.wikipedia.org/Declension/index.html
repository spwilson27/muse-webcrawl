Declension

In linguistics, declension is the changing of the form of nouns, pronouns, numerals and adjectives in a language to express their syntactic function in a sentence — by way of grammatical case, number and gender categories. The corresponding inflection of verbs is called conjugation. Languages that mark these distinctions extensively, such as Latin, Greek, Sanskrit, Russian, Finnish and Turkish, are called inflected (fusional or agglutinative) languages; English retains declension only in remnants, chiefly in pronouns (I/me/my, he/him) and the possessive suffix -'s.

Cases

The core cases found across Indo-European languages include the nominative (subject), accusative (direct object), genitive (possession), dative (indirect object), ablative (separation/source) and vocative (address). Ancient Greek and Sanskrit had eight or so cases; Latin six; German four; Finnish fifteen or more, many expressing location. The complete set of forms of a word is its paradigm, and nouns are grouped into declension classes by shared endings — five in Latin, three in Greek.

History and loss

Proto-Indo-European is reconstructed with a rich case system, which its daughter languages have variously preserved or simplified. Romance languages lost nominal declension almost entirely (Romanian and Old French retained parts), relying instead on word order and prepositions; English underwent a similar simplification between the Old and Middle English periods. Case marking persists robustly in Slavic, Baltic, Finnic and many other languages.
