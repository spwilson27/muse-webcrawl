Arthur Eddington

Sir Arthur Stanley Eddington OM FRS (28 December 1882 – 22 November 1944) was an English astronomer, physicist and mathematician, and one of the foremost astrophysicists of the early 20th century. He established the theory of stellar structure, derived the Eddington luminosity limit, and led the 1919 eclipse expedition whose measurements of starlight deflection confirmed Einstein's general theory of relativity.

Died22 November 1944 (aged 61), Cambridge, England

Work

Eddington was Plumian Professor at Cambridge from 1913 and director of the Cambridge Observatory from 1914. His book The Internal Constitution of the Stars (1926) founded the physics of stellar interiors, treating stars as spheres of gas in hydrostatic and radiative equilibrium and establishing the mass–luminosity relation. He proposed that stars are powered by nuclear fusion of hydrogen into helium — an insight confirmed decades later.

In May 1919 Eddington led the expedition to Príncipe island (while Frank Dyson's team observed from Sobral, Brazil) that photographed stars near the eclipsed Sun; the measured deflection of their light matched general relativity's prediction and made Einstein world famous. Eddington became relativity's leading English-language expositor (Space, Time and Gravitation, 1920; The Mathematical Theory of Relativity, 1923). A Quaker and conscientious objector, he also wrote popular and philosophical works; his later "fundamental theory" attempting to unify physics and derive the constants of nature was left unfinished and is regarded as numerology by most physicists. The famous anecdote of his reply — asked whether only three people understood relativity, he paused: "I am trying to think who the third person is" — captures his wit.
