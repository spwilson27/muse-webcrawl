Cathodic protection

Cathodic protection (CP) is a technique used to control the corrosion of a metal surface by making it the cathode of an electrochemical cell. It is widely applied to protect steel pipelines, storage tanks, ship hulls, offshore platforms, reinforced concrete and water heaters.

Methods

In galvanic (sacrificial anode) protection, the structure is electrically connected to a more reactive metal — typically zinc, aluminium or magnesium alloy — which corrodes preferentially and is periodically replaced. In impressed current cathodic protection, an external DC power supply drives current through relatively inert anodes, allowing larger structures to be protected with adjustable output. Effectiveness is checked by measuring the structure-to-electrolyte potential, commonly against criteria such as −0.85 V relative to a copper/copper-sulfate reference electrode for buried steel.

History

The principle was described by Sir Humphry Davy in 1824 for protecting the copper sheathing of Royal Navy ships with iron blocks, an early successful application later refined into modern practice.
