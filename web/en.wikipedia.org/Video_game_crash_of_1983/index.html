Video game crash of 1983

Atari buried unsold stock, including E.T. cartridges, in a New Mexico landfill in 1983.

The video game crash of 1983 (known in Japan as the "Atari shock") was a large-scale recession in the video game industry that occurred from 1983 to 1985, primarily in North America. Revenues for home video games peaked at around $3.2 billion in 1983 and fell to about $100 million by 1985, a drop of almost 97 percent, bankrupting several companies and nearly ending the console industry.

Causes

The market was flooded with consoles after Atari lost a court case that allowed third-party developers such as Activision to publish games; dozens of firms rushed in, and retailers were overwhelmed with poor-quality titles. Atari itself overproduced high-profile games: the rushed E.T. the Extra-Terrestrial (1982) and the home port of Pac-Man sold far below expectations, leaving mountains of unsold cartridges — millions of which Atari famously buried in Alamogordo, New Mexico (confirmed by excavation in 2014). Home computers such as the Commodore 64 undercut consoles on price while offering more utility, and retailers lost confidence in the category.

Effects

Atari was sold off in pieces in 1984; Magnavox and other makers exited the business; many developers collapsed. Consumer and retailer faith in video games in North America was destroyed for several years, and industry leadership passed from the United States to Japan.

Recovery

The market recovered in 1985–1987 when Nintendo test-launched the Nintendo Entertainment System in North America, rebranding the console as an "entertainment system" with a robot accessory, and imposing strict quality control through its licensing programme and "Seal of Quality" — reforms designed to prevent a repeat of the glut.
