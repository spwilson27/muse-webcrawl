State collapse

State collapse (or state failure) is a process in which a sovereign state loses the capacity to perform its basic functions: maintaining a monopoly on the legitimate use of force, providing public services, collecting revenue, and enforcing law across its territory. The result is a breakdown of central authority, often accompanied by civil war, humanitarian emergency, and the rise of competing armed groups and de facto authorities.

Causes

Analysts attribute collapse to combinations of weak institutions, economic crisis, corruption, ethnic or regional fragmentation, external intervention, and loss of legitimacy. Robert Rotberg argued that failure proceeds along a spectrum from weakness to failure to collapse, while other scholars distinguish sudden collapse from gradual decay of state capacity.

Examples

Frequently cited modern cases include Somalia after the fall of Siad Barre in 1991, when central government ceased to function for over two decades; Yugoslavia's disintegration in the early 1990s; and the collapse of state authority in parts of Afghanistan, Libya, and Yemen during civil wars. Historical examples range from the fall of the Western Roman Empire to the collapse of Bronze Age palatial states.

Consequences and responses

State collapse generates refugee flows, regional instability, and safe havens for armed groups, prompting international responses from humanitarian relief to peacekeeping and externally supported state-building. The annual Fragile States Index ranks countries by their vulnerability to collapse.
