Slash-and-burn


Slash-and-burn clearing in the tropics

Slash-and-burn agriculture is a farming method that involves the cutting and burning of plants in a forest or woodland to create a field called a swidden. The method begins by cutting down the trees and woody plants in an area. The downed vegetation is left to dry, then burned, producing a nutrient-rich layer of ash that fertilizes the soil for cropping. After cultivation (usually for a few years), the field is left fallow to regenerate forest, and the farmers move to a new plot.

Practice and regional names

The practice is known by many local names: milpa in Mesoamerica, jhuming in northeast India, kaingin in the Philippines, ladang in Indonesia and Malaysia, conuco in Venezuela, and shifting cultivation generally. With long fallow periods (10–20 years or more) and low population density, the system can be sustainable and maintains a mosaic of forest habitats; historically it was used across temperate Europe as well.

Environmental concerns

Under modern pressures — shortened fallows, land scarcity, and commercial clearing — slash-and-burn contributes to deforestation, biodiversity loss, soil erosion, and carbon emissions, particularly in the Amazon, Congo Basin, and Southeast Asia. Distinguishing smallholder swidden cycles from large-scale permanent conversion by fire is important in policy debates. Alternatives promoted include agroforestry, alley cropping, and improved fallows.
