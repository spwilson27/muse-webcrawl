Packet switching

In telecommunications, packet switching is a method of grouping data into short packets that are transmitted over a digital network. Packets are made of a header and a payload. Data in the header is used by networking hardware to direct the packet to its destination, where the payload is extracted and used by an operating system, application software, or higher-layer protocols. Packet switching is the primary basis for data communications in computer networks worldwide, including the Internet.

History

The concept was developed independently in the early 1960s by Paul Baran at the RAND Corporation, who sought a survivable military communications network, and by Donald Davies at the UK National Physical Laboratory, who coined the word "packet". Leonard Kleinrock published foundational queueing-theory work on the subject. The first packet-switched networks were the NPL network and the ARPANET, both operational by 1969, funded in the latter case by DARPA.

Operation

In a packet-switched network, each packet is routed independently and may follow a different path, arriving out of order or not at all. Protocols such as the Internet Protocol provide addressing and forwarding, while the Transmission Control Protocol retransmits lost packets and restores order. By statistically multiplexing many flows over shared links, packet switching uses bandwidth far more efficiently than circuit switching, which reserves a dedicated path for each connection.

Comparison with circuit switching

Packet switching trades the guaranteed bandwidth and fixed delay of circuit-switched telephone networks for efficiency and robustness: if a link fails, packets can be routed around it. Its disadvantages include variable latency (jitter) and the overhead of headers, which motivated techniques such as label switching in carrier networks.
