Paul Bunyan


Statue of Paul Bunyan and Babe the Blue Ox

Paul Bunyan is a giant lumberjack and folk hero in American and Canadian folklore. He is usually accompanied by Babe, an enormous blue ox, and is credited in tall tales with feats such as digging the Grand Canyon by dragging his axe behind him and creating the Great Lakes as watering holes for Babe.

Origins

Stories of Paul Bunyan circulated orally in the logging camps of the Great Lakes region — Michigan, Wisconsin, Minnesota, and Ontario — from the late 19th century. The character may derive from French-Canadian tales of the folk hero Bon Jean and from the real-life logger Fabian "Joe" Fournier. The first printed reference appeared in 1906, and the tales were popularized nationwide by advertising pamphlets for the Red River Lumber Company beginning in 1916, written by W. B. Laughead, which fixed many of the character's now-standard traits and companions.

Characteristics and tales

Bunyan is depicted as a man of immense stature and strength with an outsize appetite, a crew of colorful loggers, and a camp whose scale matches his own: griddles greased by men skating on them with hams tied to their feet, and flapjacks cooked by the acre. Tales credit him with clearing forests across the northern United States, straightening rivers, and shaping the landscape.

Cultural legacy

Statues of Bunyan stand in many towns in the northern United States, notably in Bemidji, Minnesota, where the 1937 statues of Paul and Babe are landmarks. He appears in children's literature, music (including the Disney short of 1958), and the American tall-tale canon alongside figures such as Johnny Appleseed and John Henry.
