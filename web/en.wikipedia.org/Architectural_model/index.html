Architectural model

An architectural model is a type of scale model, a physical representation of a structure built to study aspects of an architectural design or to communicate design ideas. Models may represent an entire building, an interior, a structural system, or the urban context around a site.

Purposes

Concept or sketch models, often made quickly in card, foam, or balsa, help designers test massing and spatial relationships. Presentation models communicate a finished proposal to clients and planning authorities, while working models can be used in wind-tunnel tests, daylighting studies, and structural analysis.

Materials and construction

Traditional model-making employs paper, card, foam board, cork, wood, acrylic, and metal, cut and assembled by hand. Contemporary practice adds laser cutting, CNC milling, and 3D printing, which translate digital models into precise physical components. Common scales range from 1:5000 for urban context models to 1:20 or larger for construction details.

History

Architectural models survive from ancient Egypt and Greece; the architectural model came into its own in Renaissance Italy, where Filippo Brunelleschi used models in developing the Florence Cathedral dome, and Michelangelo's large wooden model for St Peter's Basilica served as a construction guide. Filippo Juvarra and later modernists such as Antoni Gaudí — whose hanging-chain models explored structural form — made the model a central design tool.

Virtual models

Digital 3D models and virtual reality presentations now supplement physical models, but physical models remain valued for exhibitions, competitions, and public consultation because they are immediately legible to non-specialists.
