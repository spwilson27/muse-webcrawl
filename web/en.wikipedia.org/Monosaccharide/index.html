Monosaccharide

Monosaccharides (from Greek monos, "single", and sakchar, "sugar") are the simplest form of sugar and the most basic units of carbohydrates. They cannot be hydrolysed into smaller sugars. Their general formula is (CH2O)n, where n is typically three to seven. The most important monosaccharide in nature is glucose, the primary product of photosynthesis and the main fuel of cellular respiration.

Classification

Monosaccharides are classified by the number of carbon atoms they contain — trioses (3), tetroses (4), pentoses (5, such as ribose and deoxyribose), hexoses (6, such as glucose, fructose and galactose) and heptoses (7) — and by whether they bear an aldehyde group (aldoses) or a ketone group (ketoses).

Structure

In aqueous solution, pentoses and hexoses exist mainly as ring structures formed when a hydroxyl group reacts with the carbonyl carbon, producing a hemiacetal or hemiketal. The ring form generates a new stereocentre, giving α and β anomers. Structures are commonly drawn as Fischer projections (open chain) or Haworth projections (ring).

Reactions

Two monosaccharides can join by a glycosidic bond to form a disaccharide such as sucrose, maltose or lactose; long chains form polysaccharides such as starch, glycogen and cellulose.
