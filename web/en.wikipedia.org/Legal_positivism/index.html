Legal positivism

Legal positivism is a school of thought in jurisprudence and philosophy of law arguing that the existence and content of law depends on social facts and not on its merits. In its core formulation — associated with Jeremy Bentham, John Austin, H. L. A. Hart, and Joseph Raz — a law is valid if it has been created through the procedures recognized by a legal system, regardless of whether it is just or moral.


H. L. A. Hart, the most influential legal positivist of the 20th century

Key claims

Legal positivism is generally associated with the "separability thesis" — that there is no necessary connection between law and morality — and with the idea that legal validity is determined by sources, such as enactment or precedent. Austin's "command theory" defined law as the command of a sovereign backed by sanctions; Hart criticized this and instead explained law as a union of primary rules of obligation and secondary rules (of recognition, change, and adjudication).

Debates

Positivism contrasts with natural law theory, which holds that unjust laws may fail to be laws at all. Ronald Dworkin mounted a famous critique of Hart, arguing that legal practice includes moral principles. Later positivists divided into inclusive (soft) and exclusive (hard) camps over whether a rule of recognition may incorporate moral criteria.
