Passive voice

Passive voice
TypeGrammatical voice
FunctionPromotes the patient (object) to subject; demotes or omits the agent
Contrasts withActive voice; middle voice
English formAuxiliary be/get + past participle
In the passive, the patient becomes the grammatical subject.

The passive voice is a grammatical voice construction in which the subject of a clause is the patient — the entity acted upon — rather than the agent performing the action. Compare the active "The dog bit the man" with the passive "The man was bitten (by the dog)": the object of the active clause becomes the subject, and the agent may be expressed in an optional by-phrase or omitted entirely. Passive constructions exist in many languages, though their forms differ widely.

Formation in English

The canonical English passive uses a form of the auxiliary be (or, informally, get) followed by the past participle: "The window was broken", "He got promoted". English allows further types, including passives of ditransitive verbs ("She was given a prize") and the prepositional passive ("The bed was slept in"). Some languages, by contrast, mark the passive by verb morphology (Latin), by particles (Scandinavian -s passive), or avoid it with impersonal constructions.

Functions

Passives serve discourse purposes: they place the patient in topic position, maintain cohesion, foreground the result of an action, or background an agent that is unknown, obvious, irrelevant or deliberately tactfully omitted — as in the much-criticised bureaucratic formula "Mistakes were made". Scientific and formal writing traditionally made heavy use of agentless passives, though modern style guides more often recommend the active voice for clarity.

Style debates

Usage writers from Strunk and White to George Orwell ("Never use the passive where you can use the active", in Politics and the English Language) have warned against overuse. Linguists such as Geoffrey Pullum have responded that critics frequently misidentify passive clauses, and that the passive is an ordinary, often indispensable construction. The consensus view is that both voices have legitimate uses and that the choice depends on information structure and emphasis.
