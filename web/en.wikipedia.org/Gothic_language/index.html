Gothic is an extinct East Germanic language that was spoken by the Goths. It is known primarily from the Codex Argenteus, a 6th-century copy of a 4th-century Bible translation by the Gothic bishop Wulfila (Ulfilas), who devised the Gothic alphabet for the purpose. Gothic is the earliest Germanic language with a substantial written corpus, and it is of great importance for the comparative study of the Germanic languages.

History

Gothic was spoken by the Visigoths and Ostrogoths across southern and eastern Europe. After the fall of their kingdoms — the Ostrogothic kingdom in Italy in the 6th century and the Visigothic kingdom in Iberia in the 8th — the language declined. A distinct dialect, Crimean Gothic, survived in the Crimea and was documented in a small word list by the Flemish diplomat Ogier Ghiselin de Busbecq in the 16th century.

Features

Gothic preserves archaic features of Proto-Germanic lost in other branches, including a synthetic passive voice, dual number in pronouns and verbs, and reduplicating verbs in the past tense. Its phonology and inflection make it a cornerstone of Germanic comparative linguistics.
