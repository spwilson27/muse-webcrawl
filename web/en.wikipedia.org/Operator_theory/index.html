Operator theory is a branch of functional analysis concerned with linear operators on function spaces and vector spaces, typically infinite-dimensional. It generalises the theory of matrices and linear transformations to operators on Hilbert spaces and Banach spaces, and it provides the mathematical language of quantum mechanics, in which physical observables are represented by self-adjoint operators.

Bounded operators

The basic theory deals with bounded linear operators—those that map bounded sets to bounded sets, equivalently continuous operators. On a Hilbert space, every bounded operator T has an adjoint T*, generalising the conjugate transpose of a matrix. Important classes include self-adjoint operators (T = T*), unitary operators (T*T = TT* = I), normal operators (commuting with their adjoint), and compact operators, which behave in many respects like matrices of finite rank.

Spectral theory

The spectrum of an operator—the set of scalars λ for which T − λI is not invertible—generalises the set of eigenvalues of a matrix. The spectral theorem states that a self-adjoint (or, more generally, normal) operator can be represented as an integral against a projection-valued measure, a result that underpins the measurement formalism of quantum mechanics and the theory of unbounded operators such as position, momentum, and the Hamiltonian.

C*-algebras and von Neumann algebras

Collections of operators closed under natural operations form C*-algebras and von Neumann algebras, studied in their own right as noncommutative analogues of spaces and measure spaces. This operator-algebra perspective, developed by von Neumann, Gelfand, Naimark, and Segal, links operator theory to representation theory, ergodic theory, geometry, and mathematical physics.

Problems and applications

The invariant subspace problem—whether every bounded operator on a complex Hilbert space has a nontrivial closed invariant subspace—remains open in full generality. Operator methods are central to solving differential and integral equations (Fredholm theory), to control theory, and to modern data science, where operators model transformations of signals and high-dimensional data.
