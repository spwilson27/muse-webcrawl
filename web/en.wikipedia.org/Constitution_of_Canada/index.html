Constitution of Canada

The Constitution of Canada is the supreme law of Canada. It is a complex set of documents, conventions, statutes, and court decisions, the most important being the Constitution Act, 1867 (formerly the British North America Act, 1867), which created the Canadian federation, and the Constitution Act, 1982, which "patriated" the constitution from the United Kingdom and added the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms and an amending formula.

History

Canadian Confederation was achieved on 1 July 1867, when the Province of Canada, Nova Scotia, and New Brunswick united into the Dominion of Canada under the 1867 Act. Other provinces and territories joined subsequently, the last being Newfoundland in 1949. Because the federal and provincial governments could not agree on an amending formula, amendments long required an Act of the British Parliament, until patriation in 1982.

The Charter

The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms guarantees fundamental freedoms (conscience, expression, assembly), democratic rights, mobility rights, legal rights, equality rights, and official-language rights, and recognizes Aboriginal and treaty rights (section 35 of the 1982 Act). Section 33 — the "notwithstanding clause" — lets Parliament or a provincial legislature override certain Charter rights for renewable five-year periods.

Amending formula

The general amending procedure (the "7/50 rule") requires resolutions of the Senate, the House of Commons, and the legislative assemblies of at least two-thirds of the provinces representing at least 50 percent of the population. Certain matters, such as the offices of the monarch and the composition of the Supreme Court, require unanimity.
