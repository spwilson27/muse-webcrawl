Psilocin

IUPAC name: 4-Hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine
Formula: C12H16N2O
Class: Psychedelic tryptamine
Related compound: Psilocybin

Psilocin (also known as 4-HO-DMT, psilocine, psilocyn, or psilotsin) is a substituted tryptamine alkaloid and a serotonergic psychedelic substance. It is present in most psychedelic mushrooms together with its phosphorylated counterpart, psilocybin. Psilocybin is converted in the body to psilocin, which is the compound primarily responsible for the mind-altering effects of the mushrooms.

Chemistry

Psilocin was first isolated, along with psilocybin, from Psilocybe mexicana by Albert Hofmann in 1958, and was later synthesized in his laboratory at Sandoz. Chemically it is 4-hydroxy-N,N-dimethyltryptamine, closely related to the neurotransmitter serotonin and to bufotenin (5-HO-DMT). Psilocin is relatively unstable in solution, especially under alkaline conditions, and oxidises to blue and black products — the basis of the characteristic blue bruising of psilocybin mushrooms.

Pharmacology

Psilocin acts as a partial agonist at serotonin receptors, particularly the 5-HT2A receptor, which is considered principally responsible for its psychedelic effects; it also interacts with 5-HT1A and 5-HT2C receptors. Effects typically begin within 20–40 minutes of ingesting psilocybin mushrooms and last around 4–6 hours.

Legal status

Psilocin is listed in Schedule I of the United Nations Convention on Psychotropic Substances of 1971 and is controlled in most countries, although possession of the mushrooms themselves is treated differently in various jurisdictions.
