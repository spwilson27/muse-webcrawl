The Diet of Worms of 1521 (German: Reichstag zu Worms) was an imperial diet — a formal deliberative assembly — of the Holy Roman Empire, held at the Heylshof Garden in Worms, then a Free Imperial City. It is famous above all for the appearance of Martin Luther before the Emperor Charles V, where he refused to recant his writings, and for the resulting Edict of Worms, which declared Luther an outlaw. (A "diet" here is an assembly; the name has nothing to do with food.)

Background

On 15 June 1520, Pope Leo X issued the bull Exsurge Domine, giving Luther sixty days to recant his Ninety-five Theses and other works on pain of excommunication; Luther publicly burned the bull, and was excommunicated by Decet Romanum Pontificem in January 1521. The young Emperor Charles V, newly arrived in Germany for his first diet, was under pressure to enforce the ban, but Elector Frederick the Wise of Saxony secured for Luther a hearing and an imperial safe-conduct to and from Worms.

Luther's appearance

Luther arrived in Worms on 16 April 1521 to popular acclaim. On 17 April, questioned by the imperial spokesman Johann Eck, he acknowledged authorship of the books laid before him but asked for time to consider his answer. The next day, 18 April, he returned and delivered his famous defence. Asked once more whether he would recant, he replied — in words later cast into their classic form:

"Unless I am convinced by the testimony of the Scriptures or by clear reason … I am bound by the Scriptures I have quoted and my conscience is captive to the Word of God. I cannot and will not retract anything, since it is neither safe nor right to go against conscience. … Here I stand, I can do no other. God help me. Amen."

The exact words "Here I stand" may be a later addition, but the substance of the refusal is well attested. After several days of negotiation failed, Luther left Worms under his safe-conduct on 26 April.

Edict of Worms and aftermath

On 25–26 May 1521, Charles V issued the Edict of Worms, declaring Luther an outlaw and a convicted heretic, banning his works and ordering his arrest; anyone might kill him without legal consequence. On his journey home, Luther was "kidnapped" by Frederick the Wise's men and hidden in the Wartburg Castle, where he translated the New Testament into German. The edict could not be enforced in territories whose princes protected the Reformation, and the Diet of Worms thus stands as a defining moment in the fracturing of Western Christianity.

Other business

The diet also dealt with imperial governance, including confirmation of arrangements by which Charles's brother Ferdinand would rule the Austrian hereditary lands — a step toward the later division between Spanish and Austrian Habsburgs.
