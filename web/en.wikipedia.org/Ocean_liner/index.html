Ocean liner

An ocean liner is a type of passenger ship primarily used for transportation across seas or oceans. Ocean liners are usually built to a high standard of seaworthiness with a long hull, a high freeboard and powerful engines to cope with the long passages and adverse conditions encountered on the open ocean. They carried passengers, mail and cargo on scheduled routes ("line voyages"), most famously across the North Atlantic.

History

Regular transatlantic steamship services began in the 1840s with companies such as Cunard Line, whose ships carried mail under government contract. The late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries saw a race for size and speed, symbolised by the competition for the Blue Riband and by famous liners including Mauretania, Olympic, Titanic and Queen Mary. Liners served as troopships in both world wars and carried millions of emigrants to the Americas.

Decline

The rise of long-range jet airliners in the late 1950s destroyed the liner trade; by the 1970s almost all scheduled passenger services had ended. Surviving ships were converted to cruising or scrapped. RMS Queen Mary 2, launched in 2003, is the only ship built as a true ocean liner still providing a regular transatlantic service.

Distinction from cruise ships

Unlike cruise ships, which are built for pleasure voyages in sheltered resort areas with emphasis on onboard amenities, ocean liners were designed for point-to-point transport, with greater speed, range and structural strength for rough seas.
