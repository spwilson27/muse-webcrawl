Norfolk Reed

Norfolk reed
SpeciesPhragmites australis
RegionNorfolk and Suffolk Broads, England
UseThatching material

Norfolk reed is the common name given to stems of the common reed (Phragmites australis) harvested from the wetlands of the Norfolk and Suffolk Broads in eastern England. It is prized as a material for thatching roofs, and is widely regarded as the finest thatching reed available in Britain, producing a durable and tightly packed finish.

Harvesting

Reed is traditionally cut in winter, between December and March, when the stems are leafless, hard and golden-brown. Historically the cutting was done by hand with scythes and hooks by marshmen working from punts or from the frozen marsh; today much of the harvest is mechanised with reciprocating cutting machines mounted on small tracked vehicles. After cutting, the reed is bundled, cleaned of its leafy tops, and graded by length and fineness into bundles sometimes called yealms.

Use in thatching

Water reed, of which Norfolk reed is the premier domestic example, is laid on a roof in tightly packed courses to a thickness of about 12 inches (300 mm). A well-laid Norfolk reed roof can last 50 to 70 years, considerably longer than a long straw roof. The material is used both for new thatch and for the repair and re-coating of historic buildings throughout England.

Conservation value

The reed beds themselves are an important habitat, supporting species such as the bittern, marsh harrier and bearded tit. Regular rotational cutting maintains the beds in a state favourable to wildlife, and the continuation of the commercial reed harvest is therefore considered beneficial to the ecology of the Broads.
