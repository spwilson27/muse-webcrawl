Ibn Khaldun

Ibn Khaldun (27 May 1332 – 17 March 1406) was an Arab Muslim historian, philosopher and sociologist, widely regarded as a founder of the modern disciplines of historiography, sociology, economics and demography. Born in Tunis into a family of Andalusian origin, he served various rulers across North Africa and al-Andalus before settling in Cairo.

The Muqaddimah

His best-known work is the Muqaddimah ("Introduction", 1377), the first volume of his universal history, the Kitāb al-ʿIbar. In it he set out a theory of history based on asabiyyah — social solidarity or group cohesion — arguing that dynasties rise through the solidarity of tribal or desert peoples and decay within three or four generations as luxury and urbanisation erode that cohesion. He also analysed taxation, prices, the division of labour and the effects of geography on society.

Legacy

The Muqaddimah has been compared to the works of Machiavelli, Adam Smith and Durkheim, and Arnold Toynbee called it "the greatest work of its kind that has ever yet been created by any mind in any time or place".
