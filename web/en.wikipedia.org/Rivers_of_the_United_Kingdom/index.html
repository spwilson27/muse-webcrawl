Rivers of the United Kingdom

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The United Kingdom has thousands of rivers; although none rivals the great rivers of continental Europe in length or discharge, they are numerous, short, and closely tied to the country's industrial and urban history. The longest river entirely in the UK is the River Severn at 354 kilometres, followed by the River Thames at 346 kilometres.

Major rivers by nation

In England, the Severn, Thames, Trent, Great Ouse, and Yorkshire Ouse system are the principal rivers; the Tyne, Tees, Mersey, and Aire are historically industrial rivers of the north. In Scotland, the Tay carries the greatest discharge of any British river, and the Tweed, Clyde, Forth, Spey, and Dee are noted for salmon. In Wales, the Severn and the Wye rise on the same mountain massif, and the Usk, Tywi, and Teifi are the main southern rivers. In Northern Ireland, the Bann — flowing through Lough Neagh, the largest lake in the British Isles — and the Foyle system are dominant.

Use and management

British rivers powered the mills of the Industrial Revolution and were linked by the canal network; today they supply drinking water, receive treated effluent, and are managed for flood risk and ecology by the Environment Agency, Natural Resources Wales, the Scottish Environment Protection Agency, and the Northern Ireland Environment Agency. Many chalk streams of southern England are globally rare habitats.
