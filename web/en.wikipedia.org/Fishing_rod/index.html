Fishing rod

A fishing rod is a long, thin rod used by anglers to catch fish. It is usually equipped with a line running along guides, and a reel mounted near the butt used to store, retrieve and adjust the line. The rod acts both as a casting tool — flicking a lure, fly or baited hook out over the water — and as a lever for playing and landing a hooked fish.

Construction

Historically, fishing rods were made from natural materials such as cane (bamboo), tonkin cane and split cane, as well as woods like greenheart and lancewood. Modern rods are almost always built from synthetic composites — fibreglass, carbon fibre (graphite), or blends of the two — formed into hollow tapered blanks. Carbon fibre rods are lighter and more responsive but more brittle than fibreglass; many rods combine both materials. A typical rod consists of the blank, a handle (cork or EVA foam), a reel seat, and a series of line guides along its length, ending in a tip-top guide.

Types

Spinning rods are designed for use with a spinning reel mounted underneath and are the most common general-purpose type. Baitcasting rods carry the reel on top and offer greater accuracy and power with heavier lines. Fly rods are long, flexible rods matched by line weight, designed to cast the weight of the fly line itself rather than a lure. Other specialised types include surf rods, ice fishing rods (short, used through holes in ice), trolling rods, and telescopic rods that collapse for transport.

Action and power

Rods are described by their action — where along the blank the rod bends (fast action rods bend mostly near the tip; slow rods bend down to the butt) — and their power, a rating from ultralight to extra-heavy describing the weight the rod is designed to handle. Matching rod, reel, line and lure weight is essential for effective casting and fish fighting.

History

Depictions of rod fishing date to ancient Egypt, and rods are described in early Chinese and European writings. The telescopic and jointed rod developed in Europe in the 17th and 18th centuries, and the treatise The Compleat Angler (1653) by Izaak Walton reflects the refined tackle of the period. Multiplied reels, split-cane construction and, in the 20th century, synthetic materials successively transformed rod design.
