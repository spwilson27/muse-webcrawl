Plant nutrition is the study of the chemical elements and compounds necessary for plant growth and reproduction, plant metabolism, and their external supply. In its absence the plant is unable to complete a normal life cycle, or the element is part of some essential plant constituent or metabolite, in accordance with the criteria of essentiality formulated by Arnon and Stout (1939) building on the work of Justus von Liebig.

Essential elements

Seventeen elements are considered essential for most plants. Carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen are obtained from air and water. The remaining fourteen come from the soil: the macronutrients nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (K)—the primary fertilizer elements—plus calcium, magnesium, and sulfur; and the micronutrients iron, manganese, zinc, copper, boron, molybdenum, chlorine, and nickel, required in trace amounts. Liebig's "law of the minimum" states that growth is limited by the scarcest nutrient resource.

Uptake and transport

Plants take up nutrients mainly as ions from the soil solution through root hairs, assisted by mycorrhizal fungi in many species. Uptake may be passive or active and is selective; nutrients move to the shoots in the xylem with the transpiration stream, and mobile nutrients are redistributed through the phloem. Soil pH strongly affects availability: for example, iron and phosphorus become less available in alkaline and strongly acid soils respectively.

Functions and deficiency symptoms

Nitrogen is a constituent of proteins, nucleic acids, and chlorophyll; deficiency causes general chlorosis of older leaves. Phosphorus is central to energy transfer (ATP) and nucleic acids; deficiency can produce stunted growth and purple coloration. Potassium regulates stomata and enzyme activation; deficiency shows as marginal leaf scorch. Characteristic symptoms for each nutrient are used diagnostically, alongside soil and tissue analysis.

Fertilizers and practice

Modern agriculture supplements soil nutrients with mineral fertilizers and organic amendments such as manure and compost. Hydroponics—growth in nutrient solution, a technique developed from the nutrient-culture experiments of Sachs and Knop in the 1860s—allows complete control of plant nutrition and is used in research and commercial horticulture. Excess fertilizer use can cause nutrient leaching and eutrophication of waterways, motivating precision application and integrated nutrient management.
