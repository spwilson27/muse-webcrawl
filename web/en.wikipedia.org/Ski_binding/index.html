Ski binding

Ski bindings are devices that connect a ski boot to a ski. They generally hold the boot firmly to allow the skier to manoeuvre the ski, but are designed to release the boot from the ski in case of a fall or collision, to reduce the risk of injury to the skier's legs and knees.

Alpine bindings

Modern alpine (downhill) bindings consist of a toe piece and a heel piece mounted on the ski, clamping the hard plastic sole lugs of the boot. Release values are set according to an industry standard (the DIN setting) adjusted for the skier's weight, height, boot sole length, age and ability. The toe piece releases sideways in a twisting fall and the heel releases upward in a forward fall.

Other types

Cross-country (Nordic) bindings attach only at the toe, leaving the heel free so the skier can stride; standards include NNN and SNS. Telemark bindings similarly free the heel. Alpine touring bindings can switch between a free-heel walking mode with pins engaging inserts in the boot (tech bindings) and a fixed-heel downhill mode. Snowboard bindings, though for a different sport, follow a similar concept of strapping the boot to the board.

History

Early bindings were simple leather straps and cable bindings. The introduction of releasable safety bindings in the mid-20th century, pioneered by inventors such as Hjalmar Hvam, dramatically reduced leg fractures in recreational skiing.
