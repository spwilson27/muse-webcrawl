Jelly

Jelly is a clear or translucent fruit spread made from sweetened and thickened fruit juice, set with the natural or added gelling agent pectin. It is distinguished from jam by the absence of fruit pulp or pieces — jelly is strained so that only the juice is used — and from marmalade, which is made from citrus fruit and includes peel. In British and Commonwealth usage, "jelly" may also denote a gelatin dessert (called "Jell-O" or gelatin in North America), while in American usage jelly refers primarily to the fruit spread; both senses derive from the Latin gelare, "to freeze or congeal," via Old French gelée.

Making jelly

Traditional jelly making begins by simmering fruit in water and straining the pulp through a jelly bag or fine cloth — traditionally overnight, without squeezing, which would cloud the result. The extracted juice is then boiled with sugar; fruits rich in pectin and acid (apples, quinces, redcurrants, sour plums, cranberries) set readily, while pectin-poor fruits such as strawberries require added commercial pectin or combination with high-pectin juice. The setting point (about 104.5 °C / 220 °F at sea level) is tested by whether a drop wrinkles on a chilled plate. The hot jelly is skimmed, potted in sterilised jars, and sealed; the high sugar content and acidity preserve it.

French gelée traditions — such as the redcurrant jelly of Bar-le-Duc, once seeded by hand with a goose quill — achieved particular prestige, and savoury jellies (redcurrant with lamb, rowan with game, apple with pork) remain standard accompaniments in British cooking. Hot pepper jelly is a popular condiment in the southern United States, often served over cream cheese.

Commercial production and terminology

Industrial jelly uses vacuum concentration, standardised pectin, and controlled acidity. In the United States, jelly, jam, and preserves are defined by the Food and Drug Administration's standards of identity, which specify fruit content and soluble solids (typically 65%). "Jelly" in the North American peanut-butter-and-jelly sandwich is most commonly grape jelly, commercialised after the late nineteenth century alongside pasteurised grape juice. In Australia, New Zealand, Britain, and Ireland, the gelatin dessert sense predominates, and fruit spreads are usually labelled jam regardless of straining.

Gelatin desserts

The dessert sense of jelly refers to a sweet, flavoured gelatin gel, made by dissolving powdered gelatin in hot liquid and chilling. Gelatin — a protein derived from collagen — was industrialised in the nineteenth century; in 1897 the flavoured, sweetened powder was trademarked as Jell-O in the United States, becoming a mass-market staple. Agar-based jellies serve a similar role in East and Southeast Asian cuisines (for example Japanese kanten sweets and Chinese grass jelly made from a mint-family plant), and vegetarian gelling agents such as agar and carrageenan substitute for gelatin in both desserts and modern fruit jellies.

Other uses of the term

By extension, "jelly" describes many gel-like substances: petroleum jelly (a purified mixture of hydrocarbons, marketed as Vaseline since the 1870s), royal jelly secreted by honeybees, jellyfish (gelatinous marine animals), and savoury aspic — meat or fish stock set into a gel, historically continuous with medieval meat jellies that predate sweet fruit jelly. The word also appears in technical terms such as jelly fungus and "jelly" batteries.
