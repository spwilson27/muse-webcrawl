Medieval philosophy is the philosophy produced in Europe, the Middle East, and North Africa during the Middle Ages, roughly from the fall of the Western Roman Empire in the 5th century until the Renaissance in the 15th century. It was dominated by the effort to reconcile classical Greek philosophy — especially Aristotle and the Neoplatonists — with the doctrines of the Abrahamic religions, and it developed within monasteries, cathedral schools, and the newly founded universities.

Early medieval philosophy

The Latin West inherited most of its ancient philosophy through late-antique intermediaries. Boethius (c. 477–524) translated Aristotle's logical works and planned, before his execution, to translate all of Plato and Aristotle; his Consolation of Philosophy became one of the most read books of the Middle Ages. John Scotus Eriugena translated Pseudo-Dionysius and produced the ambitious Neoplatonic system of the Periphyseon, while Anselm of Canterbury formulated the ontological argument for God's existence in the Proslogion.

Islamic and Jewish philosophy

In the Islamic world, philosophers such as al-Kindi, al-Farabi, Avicenna (Ibn Sina), and Averroes (Ibn Rushd) preserved, commented on, and extended Greek philosophy. Avicenna's distinction between essence and existence and his "Flying Man" thought experiment profoundly influenced later thought; Averroes' commentaries earned him the title "The Commentator" in Latin scholasticism. Jewish philosophy flourished in the same milieu, culminating in Maimonides' Guide for the Perplexed, which sought to harmonize Aristotelian science with Jewish theology.

Scholasticism

From the 12th century, the recovery of Aristotle's works — translated from Arabic and Greek in Toledo and Sicily — transformed Latin philosophy. Scholasticism, the method of the schools, proceeded by commentary and disputation (quaestiones), weighing authorities against one another. Peter Abelard's Sic et Non exemplified the method; its greatest synthesis came in Thomas Aquinas, whose Summa Theologiae integrated Aristotelian metaphysics with Christian theology. Central debates included the problem of universals, the relation of faith and reason, and proofs for God's existence.

Late medieval philosophy

John Duns Scotus defended the univocity of being and a formal distinction between attributes, while William of Ockham advanced nominalism and the principle of parsimony known as Ockham's razor. Thinkers such as Jean Buridan developed impetus theory in natural philosophy, anticipating later physics, and Nicholas of Cusa explored the limits of knowledge in On Learned Ignorance. By the 15th century, humanism and the Renaissance were reshaping the intellectual landscape.
