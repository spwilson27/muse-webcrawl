Thomas Kuhn

Thomas Samuel Kuhn (July 18, 1922 – June 17, 1996) was an American physicist, historian, and philosopher of science whose 1962 book The Structure of Scientific Revolutions is among the most influential works in the philosophy of science. Kuhn argued that science progresses not by steady accumulation but through periodic "paradigm shifts," and he popularized the term paradigm in its modern sense.

The Structure of Scientific Revolutions

Kuhn described a cycle: normal science proceeds within an accepted paradigm, solving puzzles; anomalies accumulate until the field enters a crisis; a scientific revolution then replaces the old paradigm with a new one. Kuhn argued that rival paradigms are "incommensurable" — their terms and standards do not map neatly onto one another — so paradigm choice cannot be settled by proof alone. The book challenged the positivist image of science and influenced fields far beyond philosophy, from sociology to education.

Life and career

Kuhn earned degrees in physics from Harvard, where teaching a course on Aristotle's physics led him to his revolutionary reading of the history of science. He taught at Harvard, the University of California, Berkeley, Princeton, and finally the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. Later works, including The Copernican Revolution (1957) and essays collected in The Essential Tension (1977), refined and defended his account of scientific change.
