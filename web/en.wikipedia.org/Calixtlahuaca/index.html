Calixtlahuaca

LocationToluca Valley, State of Mexico, Mexico
CultureMatlatzinca; later Mexica (Aztec)
PeriodPostclassic (c. 1100–1521)

Calixtlahuaca (from the Nahuatl Callixtlahuaca, "plain of houses") is a Postclassic-period Mesoamerican archaeological site in the Toluca Valley of the modern State of Mexico, near the city of Toluca. The city was originally a settlement of the Matlatzinca people and was conquered by the Mexica (Aztecs) in the 1470s under Axayacatl, becoming a provincial centre of the Aztec Empire.

Site

The ruins spread over the slopes of Cerro Tenismo and include some 17 major structures. The best known is Structure 3, a circular temple dedicated to Ehecatl, the wind aspect of Quetzalcoatl, recognizable by its round plan intended to offer no resistance to the wind. Structure 17, sometimes called the Calmecac, is a large quadrangular complex, and the site also contains a tzompantli (skull rack) and a cruciform altar with skull carvings.

Excavations

The site was excavated in the 1930s by Mexican archaeologist José García Payón, who restored the circular Ehecatl temple and other buildings. Calixtlahuaca is also associated with a controversial find: a small terracotta head, discovered in a burial in 1933, which some have claimed resembles Roman workmanship — the so-called Tecaxic-Calixtlahuaca Head. Most scholars regard claims of pre-Columbian Old World contact as unproven, and the head's provenance has been questioned.
