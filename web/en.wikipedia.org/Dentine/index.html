Dentine

Dentine (American English) or dentin is a calcified tissue of the body and, along with enamel, cementum, and pulp, is one of the four major components of teeth. It is usually covered by enamel on the crown and cementum on the root and surrounds the entire pulp. By volume, 70% of dentine consists of the mineral hydroxyapatite, 20% is organic material and 10% is water. It is less mineralized and less brittle than enamel, and lies beneath it as a shock-absorbing layer.

Structure

Dentine is secreted by the odontoblasts of the dental pulp. Unlike enamel, dentine continues to form throughout life and can be initiated in response to stimuli such as tooth decay or attrition. It contains microscopic channels called dentinal tubules that radiate outward from the pulp, giving dentine a degree of permeability that can increase the sensation of pain and the rate of tooth decay.

Types

Primary dentine forms before tooth eruption; secondary dentine forms after the root is complete and continues slowly throughout life; tertiary dentine forms in reaction to external stimuli such as caries or restorative procedures. Mantle dentine is the first formed layer adjacent to the enamel.

Development

Dentinogenesis is the formation of dentine by odontoblasts, which differentiate from cells of the dental papilla during the bell stage of tooth development. The process begins at the cusp tips and proceeds inward.
