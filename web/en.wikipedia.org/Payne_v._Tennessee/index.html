Payne v. Tennessee

Payne v. Tennessee, 501 U.S. 808 (1991), was a United States Supreme Court case which held that testimony in the form of a victim impact statement is admissible during the sentencing phase of a trial and, in death penalty cases, does not violate the Cruel and Unusual Punishment Clause of the Eighth Amendment. Thus, states could permit prosecutors to present evidence about the victim and the impact of the murder on the victim's family at sentencing.

Background

Pervis Tyrone Payne was convicted of murdering a woman and her young daughter and assaulting her son. At the sentencing phase, the prosecution presented testimony from the victims' family about the effect of the crimes, and the jury imposed the death penalty. Payne argued that the earlier decisions in Booth v. Maryland (1987) and South Carolina v. Gathers (1989) barred such evidence under the Eighth Amendment.

Decision

Writing for a 6–3 majority, Chief Justice William Rehnquist overruled Booth and Gathers in relevant part, reasoning that a victim's individuality and the harm caused are relevant to a defendant's moral culpability, and that stare decisis carries less weight where earlier decisions were recent, divided, and unworkable. Justice Thurgood Marshall, dissenting, accused the majority of discarding precedent "in the name of expediency".
