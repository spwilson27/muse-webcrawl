Ne bis in idem

Ne bis in idem (Latin for "not twice for the same") is a legal maxim embodying the principle that a person should not be prosecuted or punished more than once for the same offence. It is closely related to the common-law protection against double jeopardy and to the doctrine of res judicata.

Content of the principle

The principle typically has two aspects. Procedurally, it bars a second prosecution after a final acquittal or conviction for the same conduct. Substantively, it prohibits multiple punishments for the same offence. What counts as the "same" — the same act (idem factum) or the same legal offence (idem crimen) — varies between jurisdictions.

International and European law

The principle is guaranteed by Article 14(7) of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights and by Article 4 of Protocol No. 7 to the European Convention on Human Rights. Within the European Union, Article 50 of the Charter of Fundamental Rights and Article 54 of the Schengen Implementing Convention give the principle transnational effect: a person finally tried in one member state generally cannot be prosecuted again for the same acts in another. The European Court of Justice has further applied ne bis in idem to combinations of administrative penalties and criminal proceedings, notably in competition and tax cases.

Origins

The maxim descends from Roman law, where the jurist Paulus recorded the rule that a matter once adjudicated should not be reopened. Similar protections appear in ancient Greek and early English law, and the plea of autrefois acquit or autrefois convict in common law expresses the same idea.
