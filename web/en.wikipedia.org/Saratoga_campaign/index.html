Saratoga campaign

The Saratoga campaign in 1777 was an attempt by the British high command during the American Revolutionary War to gain military control of the strategically important Hudson River valley. It culminated in the Battles of Saratoga and the surrender of General John Burgoyne's army on 17 October 1777 — a turning point that persuaded France to enter the war as an American ally.

The plan

Burgoyne proposed advancing from Canada down Lake Champlain and the Hudson to Albany, where he expected to be met by forces moving up from New York City and eastward from Lake Ontario. The plan unravelled through poor coordination: General Howe took the main British army to Philadelphia instead, and Barry St. Leger's western column was turned back after the siege of Fort Stanwix and the Battle of Oriskany.

Course of the campaign

Burgoyne retook Fort Ticonderoga in July 1777 but was slowed by supply problems and wilderness terrain. A foraging expedition was destroyed at the Battle of Bennington, costing him nearly 1,000 men and Native allies. Crossing the Hudson, he fought the American army under Horatio Gates — with crucial contributions from Benedict Arnold and Daniel Morgan's riflemen — at Freeman's Farm (19 September) and Bemis Heights (7 October). Surrounded and outnumbered, Burgoyne surrendered his roughly 6,000 remaining troops under the Convention of Saratoga.

Consequences

News of the surrender convinced France that the American cause was viable; the Franco-American alliance of February 1778 transformed the war into a global conflict. Saratoga is therefore widely regarded as the turning point of the American Revolution.
