Crouzon syndrome

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Crouzon syndrome (also called Crouzon's disease) is an autosomal dominant genetic disorder characterized by the premature fusion of certain skull bones, a process known as craniosynostosis. It is named after Octave Crouzon, the French physician who first described the condition in 1912. The disorder is one of the craniosynostosis syndromes and affects roughly one in 25,000 to 60,000 newborns.

Cause

Crouzon syndrome is caused by mutations in the fibroblast growth factor receptor genes, most commonly FGFR2 and in some cases FGFR3. These receptors regulate bone development in the skull; activating mutations cause the fibrous sutures between skull bones to ossify too early, restricting growth in some directions while the growing brain pushes expansion in others.

Signs and symptoms

Premature fusion of the coronal and other sutures produces an abnormally shaped head, a prominent forehead, and midface hypoplasia. Characteristic features include widely spaced, protruding eyes (proptosis) due to shallow orbits, a beaked nose, and an underdeveloped upper jaw that can cause dental crowding and breathing difficulties. Unlike Apert syndrome, intelligence is usually normal and the hands and feet are unaffected.

Treatment

Management is multidisciplinary and often involves staged craniofacial surgery in childhood to release fused sutures, protect the eyes and brain, and advance the midface, along with orthodontic and ophthalmological care.
