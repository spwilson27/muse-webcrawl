The abducens nerve, also known as the abducent nerve or cranial nerve VI, is a purely motor (somatic efferent) cranial nerve. It controls the movement of the lateral rectus muscle of the eye, responsible for outward gaze — abducting the eyeball away from the midline, from which the nerve takes its name.

Pathway

The nerve arises from the abducens nucleus in the pons, emerges at the junction of the pons and medulla, and runs a long intracranial course up the clivus, through Dorello's canal, then through the cavernous sinus alongside the internal carotid artery, before entering the orbit through the superior orbital fissure to reach the lateral rectus muscle.

Clinical significance

Because of its long course, the abducens nerve is vulnerable to raised intracranial pressure, fractures, and tumours. Damage causes sixth nerve palsy: the affected eye cannot abduct, rests turned inward (esotropia), and the patient experiences horizontal double vision, often turning the head toward the side of the lesion to compensate.
