Sayes Court

Sayes Court was a house and garden at Deptford in south-east London, famous as the home of the diarist and gardener John Evelyn from 1653 until his death in 1706. Evelyn's garden, laid out from the 1650s, was one of the most celebrated of 17th-century England, and his writings about Sayes Court — including his discourse on trees, Sylva (1664) — made the estate a landmark in English horticultural history.

History

The estate took its name from the Say family, who held the manor in the Middle Ages. Evelyn inherited the property through his wife's family and rebuilt the house and gardens after the Civil War, planting an oval garden, groves, a holly hedge famous in its day, and an orchard and kitchen garden, advised by his wide correspondence with European gardeners.

Sayes Court also had an unlikely Russian connection: in 1698 Evelyn let the house to Tsar Peter the Great of Russia, who was studying shipbuilding at the Deptford dockyard. Peter and his retinue notoriously damaged the house and garden — including, legend has it, by wheeling each other through Evelyn's prized holly hedge in wheelbarrows — and the Crown eventually paid compensation for the damage.

Later history and legacy

The house was demolished in the 18th century and the grounds were progressively built over; part became a workhouse and later Deptford's market and housing. A small public garden, Sayes Court Park, preserves a fragment of the site, including an ancient mulberry tree traditionally associated with Evelyn (and sometimes said to have been planted by Peter the Great). In the 2010s the site, by then largely occupied by the Convoys Wharf development area, was the focus of campaigns to restore Evelyn's garden as part of riverside redevelopment.
