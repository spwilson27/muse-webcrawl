Polyploidy

Polyploidy is a condition in which the cells of an organism have more than two paired sets of chromosomes. Most eukaryotes are diploid, meaning they have two sets of chromosomes — one set inherited from each parent. Polyploid states are described by the number of sets: triploid (3x), tetraploid (4x), pentaploid (5x), hexaploid (6x), and so on. Polyploidy is especially common in plants, and also occurs in some animals such as certain fish and amphibians.

MechanismsMeiotic non-disjunction; genome doubling; hybridisation

Types

In autopolyploidy, extra chromosome sets arise within a single species, typically through failed meiotic division producing unreduced gametes. In allopolyploidy, sets derive from hybridisation between two species followed by chromosome doubling, which restores fertility to the hybrid; bread wheat, cotton, and canola are important allopolyploid crops.

Significance

Polyploidy is a major driver of plant evolution and speciation: a significant fraction of flowering plant species show evidence of ancient whole-genome duplication events, and an estimated 15% of angiosperm speciation events involve ploidy change. In agriculture, polyploid crops often have larger cells and organs — seedless bananas and watermelons are triploid — while in animals polyploidy is rarer and in humans complete triploidy is lethal, though polyploid cells occur normally in tissues such as the liver and placenta.
