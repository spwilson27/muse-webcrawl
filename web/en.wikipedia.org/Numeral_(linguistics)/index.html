Numeral (linguistics)

In linguistics, a numeral (or number word) is a member of a part of speech (or lexical class) characterised by the designation of numbers; some examples are the English word two and the compound seventy-seven.

Numerals are often divided into cardinal numerals, which denote quantity (one, two, three), and ordinal numerals, which denote position in a sequence (first, second, third). Some systems distinguish a wider range of categories, including collective, distributive, multiplicative, and fractional numerals.

Identifying numerals

Numerals may be a separate part of speech, may be a sub-class of some other part of speech, or may overlap several parts of speech. In English, cardinal numerals such as three function as determiners when attributive (three cars) and as nouns when used independently (I saw three). Ordinals such as third usually behave as adjectives or adverbs.

Not all languages have a true class of numerals. Many languages have only a small inventory of number words, and some, famously the Pirahã language of Amazonia, have been claimed to have no exact numerals at all.

Types of numerals

  • Cardinal numerals answer the question "how many?"
  • Ordinal numerals answer the question "which in order?"
  • Multiplicative numerals express how many times (once, twice, thrice).
  • Distributive numerals express distribution (one each, two apiece).
  • Collective numerals refer to sets (a pair, a dozen).

Large numbers

Languages differ greatly in how they form large numbers. English uses a largely decimal system with special words for powers of ten (hundred, thousand, million). Some languages use vigesimal (base-20) counting, such as Basque and traditional Welsh, while others use quinary (base-5) or octal systems.
