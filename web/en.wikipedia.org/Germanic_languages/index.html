The Germanic languages are a branch of the Indo-European language family, spoken natively by about 515 million people, mainly in Europe, North America, Oceania and Southern Africa. All Germanic languages descend from Proto-Germanic, spoken in Iron Age Scandinavia and northern Germany, and are united by innovations such as the Germanic consonant shift (Grimm's law and Verner's law), a distinctive system of strong and weak verbs, and a two-tense verbal system.

Distribution of the Germanic languages in Europe: North Germanic in Scandinavia and the North Atlantic, West Germanic around the southern North Sea.

The earliest attested Germanic language is Gothic, known from Wulfila's 4th-century Bible translation; runic inscriptions in the Elder Futhark preserve older fragments. Most Germanic-speaking peoples Christianised in the early Middle Ages, adopting the Latin alphabet.

Characteristics

Shared features include dental suffixes in the past tense of weak verbs (-ed), a distinction between strong verbs (vowel gradation, or ablaut) and weak verbs, reduction of the Indo-European tense system to present and preterite, fixed stress on the first syllable of words (which drove vowel reductions), and a large stock of vocabulary with no Indo-European cognates (e.g. sea, hand). English, reshaped by Norse and French contact and by global expansion, is by far the most widely spoken, with around 1.5 billion total speakers as a first and second language.
