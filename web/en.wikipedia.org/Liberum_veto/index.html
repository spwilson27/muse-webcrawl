Liberum veto

The liberum veto (Latin for "free veto") was a parliamentary device in the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth that allowed any single deputy to the Sejm to halt proceedings and nullify all legislation already passed in that session by shouting "Sisto activitatem!" or "Nie pozwalam!" ("I do not allow!").

First use (trad.)1652, Władysław Siciński
Abolished1791, Constitution of 3 May
BasisPrinciple of unanimity in the Sejm

Origins

Rooted in the requirement of unanimous consent among the legally equal szlachta, the veto grew from earlier practices of breaking sejmiks and instructions binding deputies. Its first celebrated use is attributed to Władysław Siciński in 1652, though historians note prior dissolutions.

Consequences

In the 18th century foreign powers — especially Russia and Prussia — bribed deputies to veto reforms; a large share of Sejms under the Saxon kings and Stanisław August were dissolved without result. Reformers circumvented it through confederated sejms, which voted by majority. The Constitution of 3 May 1791 abolished it outright — a reform answered by the Targowica Confederation and the partitions.
