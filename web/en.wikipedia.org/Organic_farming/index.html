Organic farming

Rows of crops on an organic farm

Organic farming, also known as ecological farming or biological farming, is an agricultural system that uses fertilizers of organic origin such as compost manure, green manure, and bone meal and places emphasis on techniques such as crop rotation and companion planting. It originated early in the 20th century in reaction to rapidly changing farming practices. Certified organic agriculture accounts for about 1.5% of global farmland.

Organic farming aims to work with natural processes rather than against them: biological pest control, mixed cropping, and the fostering of insect predators are encouraged. Organic standards are designed to allow the use of naturally occurring substances while prohibiting or strictly limiting synthetic substances. Organic produce typically avoids synthetic pesticides, artificial fertilizers, genetically modified organisms (GMOs), antibiotics, and growth hormones in livestock production.

Methods

Key practices include crop rotation to break pest cycles and maintain soil fertility; cover crops and green manures to fix nitrogen and add organic matter; composting to recycle farm wastes into humus; mechanical cultivation and mulching for weed control; and biological control using predators, parasites, or microbial agents against pests. Livestock are raised with access to pasture and fed organic feed, with an emphasis on animal welfare and disease prevention rather than routine medication.

Regulation and certification

Organic standards vary worldwide but are legally enforced in many countries. In the United States, the Organic Foods Production Act of 1990 established the National Organic Program and the "USDA Organic" seal. The European Union has regulated organic production since 1991, and bodies such as the International Federation of Organic Agriculture Movements (IFOAM), founded in 1972, promote harmonized standards. Products are typically certified by accredited third-party inspection agencies.

Debates

Supporters argue that organic farming builds soil health, reduces pollution from pesticide and fertilizer runoff, and supports biodiversity. Critics note that organic yields are on average lower than conventional yields—often estimated at roughly 20–25% lower—raising questions about land use and food prices, and debate whether organic food is meaningfully more nutritious. The organic market has nevertheless grown rapidly, reaching well over one hundred billion US dollars in global sales annually in the 2020s.
