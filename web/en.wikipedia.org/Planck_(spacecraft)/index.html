Planck was a space observatory operated by the European Space Agency (ESA) from 2009 to 2013. It mapped the cosmic microwave background (CMB) at microwave and infrared frequencies with high sensitivity and angular resolution, providing the most precise measurements of the age, composition, and geometry of the Universe produced by any CMB mission to date. The project was named in honour of the German physicist Max Planck.

Mission

Planck was launched on 14 May 2009 aboard an Ariane 5 rocket from Kourou, French Guiana, together with the Herschel Space Observatory. It travelled to the Sun–Earth L2 Lagrange point, about 1.5 million km from Earth, from where it scanned the whole sky in successive surveys. The spacecraft's detectors were cooled to as low as 0.1 kelvin—among the coldest objects in space—to detect temperature fluctuations in the CMB of a few millionths of a degree.

Instruments

The telescope carried two instruments: the Low Frequency Instrument (LFI), an array of HEMT radiometers covering 30–70 GHz, and the High Frequency Instrument (HFI), an array of bolometers covering 100–857 GHz. The HFI exhausted its liquid helium coolant in January 2012, after which the LFI continued observing until 2013.

Results

Planck's data releases in 2013, 2015, and the final legacy release in 2018 produced the most detailed all-sky maps of the CMB. Key results included a refined age of the Universe of 13.787 billion years, a composition of approximately 4.9% ordinary matter, 26.5% dark matter, and 68.9% dark energy, and tight constraints on inflationary models and the sum of neutrino masses. Planck also catalogued tens of thousands of galaxy clusters detected via the Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effect and mapped polarized Galactic dust emission.

End of mission

After completing its science programme, Planck was manoeuvred into a heliocentric disposal orbit and decommissioned; the final command was sent on 23 October 2013. The mission succeeded COBE and WMAP and set the benchmark for precision cosmology.
