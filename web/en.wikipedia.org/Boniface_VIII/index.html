Pope Boniface VIII

Pope Boniface VIII (born Benedetto Caetani; c. 1230 – 11 October 1303) was head of the Catholic Church and ruler of the Papal States from 24 December 1294 until his death in 1303. He is remembered for issuing the papal bull Unam sanctam (1302), the most extreme statement of papal supremacy in the Middle Ages, and for his ruinous conflict with King Philip IV of France, which ended with his arrest and humiliation shortly before his death.

Early career and election

Caetani, from a noble family of Anagni in Lazio, studied law at Bologna and became a skilled canon lawyer and papal diplomat, serving on missions to France and England. He was made cardinal in 1281. When the saintly hermit-pope Celestine V resigned in December 1294 — an unprecedented step — Caetani succeeded him. Critics, including Dante, darkly suggested that Caetani had engineered the abdication; Celestine was kept prisoner by Boniface and died in custody in 1296.

Pontificate

Boniface proclaimed the first Roman Jubilee (Holy Year) in 1300, drawing enormous crowds of pilgrims and revenue to Rome. He was an energetic defender of papal authority, intervening in Sicilian, German and Scottish affairs, and a generous patron who enriched his Caetani family.

Conflict with Philip IV

The conflict with France began over taxation of the clergy: Boniface's bull Clericis laicos (1296) forbade it without papal consent. Escalating quarrels led to Unam sanctam, which declared that "it is altogether necessary to salvation for every human creature to be subject to the Roman Pontiff." Philip's ministers, led by Guillaume de Nogaret, responded with propaganda and, in September 1303, with force: Nogaret and Sciarra Colonna seized the pope at Anagni — the "outrage of Anagni" — intending to drag him before a council in France. Boniface was freed by the townspeople after three days but died in Rome a month later, broken by the experience.

His death opened the way to the Avignon Papacy. Dante placed Boniface in the eighth circle of Hell in the Inferno, among the simoniacs, accusing him of corruption and of purchasing the papacy.
