Grief

DefinitionResponse to loss, particularly bereavement
SignsSadness, yearning, anger, guilt, numbness, sleep and appetite disturbance
Related conceptsBereavement, mourning
ComplicationProlonged grief disorder

Grief is the response to loss, particularly to the loss of someone or something that has died, to which a bond or affection was formed. Although conventionally focused on the emotional response to loss, grief also has physical, cognitive, behavioural, social and philosophical dimensions. Human grief after bereavement is commonly accompanied by mourning, the cultural practices surrounding death.

Reactions

Typical reactions include shock and numbness, intense yearning for the deceased, sadness, crying, anger, guilt, anxiety, difficulty concentrating, and disturbances of sleep and appetite. Physical symptoms such as fatigue and a weakened immune response are also reported, and bereavement is associated with increased mortality in the bereaved, including the so-called "broken heart" effect. Grief is not limited to death: it can follow divorce, job loss, serious illness, or the loss of a home or community, and anticipatory grief may occur before an expected death.

Models

The best-known model is the "five stages" — denial, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance — proposed by Elisabeth Kübler-Ross in On Death and Dying (1969), originally describing patients facing their own death. Later research has treated such stages cautiously: studies find that most bereaved people are resilient, that reactions do not follow a fixed sequence, and that other frameworks, such as the dual-process model of coping (oscillating between loss-oriented and restoration-oriented activity) and theories of continuing bonds with the deceased, better fit the evidence. The psychiatrist J. William Worden described "tasks" of mourning rather than stages.

Complicated and prolonged grief

For a minority — roughly one in ten bereaved people — acute grief remains intense and disabling for a year or more. This condition, now recognised as prolonged grief disorder in the DSM-5-TR (2022) and ICD-11, involves persistent yearning and preoccupation with the death together with functional impairment, and responds to targeted psychotherapy better than to general depression treatment.

Culture

Societies structure grief through funerals, mourning dress, prescribed periods of mourning and rituals of remembrance, varying widely across religions and cultures, from the Jewish shiva and Islamic observances to Mexico's Day of the Dead.
