Jesuit missions among the Guaraní

Period1609 – 1768
RegionPresent-day Paraguay, Argentina, Brazil, Uruguay
Peak population~141,000 (c. 1732) in some 30 towns

The Jesuit missions among the Guaraní were a series of settlements (reductions) founded by the Society of Jesus in the 17th and 18th centuries among the Guaraní peoples of the Río de la Plata region of South America, in what is now Paraguay, northeastern Argentina, southern Brazil, and Uruguay. At their height around 1732, some thirty mission towns housed about 141,000 Guaraní inhabitants.

Organisation

The missions were organised as self-sufficient communities combining communal agriculture, cattle ranching, and crafts with Christian worship and education. The Jesuits learned and codified the Guaraní language, printed books in it, trained Guaraní musicians and artisans to a high standard, and defended the missions with Guaraní militias against Portuguese slave raiders (bandeirantes). Each town was administered by two priests alongside a Guaraní council, and the missions became renowned for their baroque churches, music, and carved stone and woodwork.

Decline and expulsion

The Treaty of Madrid (1750), transferring seven missions east of the Uruguay River to Portugal, provoked the Guaraní War of 1754–1756, in which Guaraní forces resisted removal. Growing European suspicion of Jesuit power led to the order's expulsion from Spanish territories in 1767–1768; the missions then passed to civil administrators and other orders and steadily declined, their populations dispersing.

Legacy

The ruins of missions such as São Miguel das Missões in Brazil and La Santísima Trinidad de Paraná and Jesús de Tavarangue in Paraguay are UNESCO World Heritage Sites. The missions have been alternately idealised as a "Christian republic" or utopia and criticised as theocratic and coercive; the 1986 film The Mission dramatised their fall.
