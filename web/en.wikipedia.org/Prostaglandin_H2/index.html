Prostaglandin H2

Chemical formulaC20H32O5
Molar mass352.47 g/mol
TypeProstaglandin (eicosanoid intermediate)
PrecursorProstaglandin G2, from arachidonic acid

Prostaglandin H2 (PGH2) is a type of prostaglandin and a key intermediate in the biosynthesis of the prostanoids. It is derived from arachidonic acid through the action of the cyclooxygenase enzymes (COX-1 and COX-2), which first convert arachidonic acid to the unstable hydroperoxide prostaglandin G2 (PGG2) and then reduce it to PGH2. PGH2 itself is unstable in aqueous solution, with a half-life of only a few minutes, and serves as the common precursor from which tissue-specific synthases produce the other prostaglandins and thromboxanes.

Downstream products

Different cell types express different terminal synthases, so PGH2 gives rise to a family of signalling lipids with varied — sometimes opposing — effects. Prostacyclin synthase in endothelial cells produces prostacyclin (PGI2), a vasodilator and inhibitor of platelet aggregation; thromboxane synthase in platelets produces thromboxane A2, a vasoconstrictor and platelet activator. Other pathways yield prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), PGD2, and PGF2α, which mediate inflammation, fever, pain, and reproductive functions.

Pharmacology

Because PGH2 production is the committed step of prostanoid synthesis, its forming enzymes are major drug targets. Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as aspirin and ibuprofen inhibit COX enzymes, reducing PGH2 and thereby the prostaglandins responsible for pain and inflammation. Aspirin inhibits the enzyme irreversibly, which underlies its long-lasting antiplatelet effect at low doses. Selective COX-2 inhibitors were developed to spare the protective prostaglandins of the stomach, though some were later withdrawn over cardiovascular risks.
