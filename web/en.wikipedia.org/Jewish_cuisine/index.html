Jewish cuisine


Jewish cuisine comprises the cooking traditions of Jewish communities worldwide, shaped by kashrut and centuries of diaspora.

Jewish cuisine refers to the worldwide cooking traditions of the Jewish people. It has evolved over many centuries, shaped by Jewish dietary laws (kashrut), Jewish festivals, and Shabbat traditions. Jewish cuisine in turn influenced the gastronomy of the many countries in which Jewish communities have settled, and it absorbed the ingredients and techniques of those regions.

Traditions

The cuisine is usually divided into broad traditions: Ashkenazi (from Central and Eastern Europe), Sephardi (from Iberia and the Mediterranean), and Mizrahi (from the Middle East and North Africa), along with the distinctive foods of Yemenite, Persian, Ethiopian, and other communities.

Ashkenazi cuisine

Ashkenazi food reflects the cold climate and poverty of Eastern European Jewish life: bagels, challah, gefilte fish, chopped liver, chicken soup with matzah balls, kugel, knishes, blintzes, borscht, and slow-cooked Sabbath stews such as cholent. Pickled and smoked fish — herring, lox, and whitefish — are staples of the delicatessen tradition that immigrants carried to New York and other cities.

Sephardi and Mizrahi cuisine

Sephardi and Mizrahi cooking uses olive oil, rice, legumes, fresh vegetables, herbs, and spices such as cumin, turmeric, and saffron. Dishes include bourekas, biscochos, hummus, falafel, shakshuka, and elaborate Sabbath stews like hamin and dafina.

Kashrut and holidays

Kosher laws prohibit pork and shellfish, forbid mixing meat and dairy, and require ritual slaughter of animals. Holiday foods carry symbolic weight: matzah at Passover, apples and honey at Rosh Hashanah, fried foods such as latkes and sufganiyot at Hanukkah, and triangular hamantaschen at Purim.
