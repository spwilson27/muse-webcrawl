Transient astronomical event

A transient is an astronomical object or phenomenon whose duration is far shorter than the evolutionary timescales of galaxies.

A transient astronomical event, often shortened to transient, is an astronomical object or phenomenon whose duration may be from milliseconds to days, weeks, or even several years. This is in contrast to the timescale of the other astronomical objects, in which changes typically evolve over millions or billions of years. The term is used for violent deep-sky events, such as supernovae, gamma-ray bursts, transits, microlensing events and tidal disruption events, as well as for fast radio bursts.

Types

Transients span an enormous range of durations and energies: fast radio bursts last milliseconds; gamma-ray bursts seconds to minutes; kilonovae days; supernovae weeks to months; luminous red novae and tidal disruption flares months to years; and changing-look active galactic nuclei vary over years. Historically, the "guest stars" recorded by Chinese astronomers, such as the supernova of 1054 that produced the Crab Nebula, were transient events observed without telescopes.

Detection and study

Because transients are brief, their study depends on wide-field surveys that repeatedly image the sky and on rapid follow-up with other telescopes. Notable surveys include Pan-STARRS, the Zwicky Transient Facility and the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's Legacy Survey of Space and Time, which is expected to detect millions of transient alerts per night. Automated brokers classify the flood of alerts and coordinate spectroscopic follow-up, making time-domain astronomy a central frontier of modern astrophysics.
