The Oregon Trail was a historic east–west wagon route across North America that connected the Missouri River to the valleys of Oregon. From the early 1840s until the completion of the first transcontinental railroad in 1869, hundreds of thousands of settlers, farmers, miners, and ranchers travelled the trail to emigrate to the American West.

Route

The trail began at Independence, Missouri, followed the Platte River across the Great Plains, crossed the Rocky Mountains at South Pass in present-day Wyoming, and continued along the Snake River plain before crossing the Blue Mountains and the Cascade Range to the Willamette Valley. Landmarks such as Chimney Rock, Fort Laramie, Independence Rock, and the Whitman Mission punctuated the roughly five-month journey.

The journey

Emigrants travelled in wagon trains of covered wagons — popularly called "prairie schooners" — typically walking beside ox-drawn wagons for 15 to 20 miles a day. The chief dangers were disease, especially cholera, accidents, river crossings, and harsh weather rather than attack; estimates suggest as many as one in ten emigrants died along the way. The trail's traffic peaked in the early 1850s, following the California Gold Rush, which drew many travellers onto branching routes such as the California Trail.

Legacy

Sections of the trail's ruts remain visible today, and the route is commemorated as the Oregon National Historic Trail. It holds a large place in American folklore and popular culture, including the educational computer game The Oregon Trail.
