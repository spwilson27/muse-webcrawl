Cordite

Cordite is a family of smokeless propellants developed and produced in Britain from 1889 to replace gunpowder as a military propellant. Like gunpowder, cordite is a deflagrating low explosive; it burns to produce a large volume of gas at a controlled rate to propel projectiles.

Composition and history

Patented by Sir James Dewar and Sir Frederick Abel in 1889, the original cordite consisted of nitroglycerin, guncotton (nitrocellulose), and mineral jelly, extruded into spaghettilike cords—hence the name. A modified formulation, Cordite MD, reduced barrel erosion by lowering the nitroglycerin content. During both world wars Britain and the Commonwealth produced cordite in large quantities, including at the Royal Ordnance Factory at Waltham Abbey and in dedicated factories in Scotland, Canada, and India.

Use

Cordite propelled the .303 British rifle cartridge and countless artillery and naval shells. It was gradually replaced after the Second World War by other propellant formulations, though the term is still popularly used for smokeless powders generally.
