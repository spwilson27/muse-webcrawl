Sons of Liberty


The nine-striped flag associated with the Sons of Liberty

The Sons of Liberty was a loosely organized, clandestine political organization active in the Thirteen Colonies, founded to advance the rights of the colonists and to fight the taxation imposed by the British government. The group is best known for undertaking the Boston Tea Party in 1773, which led to the Intolerable Acts and helped spark the American Revolution.

Origins

The organization emerged in 1765 in response to the Stamp Act. Its members — merchants, artisans, tradesmen, and political leaders such as Samuel Adams, John Hancock, and Paul Revere — used protests, boycotts, pamphlets, and at times intimidation and violence, including tarring and feathering tax officials and burning them in effigy, to resist British measures.

Activities

Sons of Liberty groups formed in ports and towns throughout the colonies and corresponded with one another, helping to create a unified colonial resistance. They enforced non-importation agreements against the Stamp Act and the Townshend Acts, erected "liberty poles" and liberty trees as rallying points, and played a central role in the resistance that culminated in the Boston Tea Party on 16 December 1773, when colonists dumped shipments of taxed tea into Boston Harbor.

Legacy

After independence, the name was revived by various organizations. The Sons of Liberty's tactics of organized popular protest and their slogan "no taxation without representation" became enduring symbols of the American founding.
