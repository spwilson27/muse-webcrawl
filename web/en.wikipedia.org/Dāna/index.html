Dāna

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dāna (Sanskrit: दान, IAST: Dāna) is a concept in Indian religions that means giving, generosity, charity, and liberality. It is the practice of donating without any expectation of return, and is considered one of the most important virtues in Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism. Dāna is closely related to the concept of puṇya (merit), the wholesome karma accumulated through generous acts.

Etymology

The word dāna derives from the Sanskrit root dā, meaning "to give." In the Vedas it appears as the act of bestowing gifts, particularly upon priests (brāhmaṇas), as part of the sacrificial ritual. Over time the meaning broadened to encompass charitable giving to the poor, to monks and nuns, and to religious institutions.

In Hinduism

In Hinduism, dāna is one of the ten niyamas (observances) and is praised throughout the epics and law books. The Bhagavad Gītā (chapter 17) classifies giving into three types based on the guṇas: sāttvika giving, done without expectation of reward at the proper time and place to a worthy recipient; rājasika giving, done for the sake of reward or recognition; and tāmasika giving, done contemptuously or to an unworthy recipient. The Manusmṛti and the Mahābhārata both extol generosity as a duty of householders.

In Buddhism

In Buddhism, dāna is the first of the six (or ten) pāramitās (perfections) and the first of the three bases of meritorious action (puññakiriyā-vatthu). The Buddha taught that generosity weakens craving and attachment, preparing the mind for meditation and insight. Giving to the monastic saṅgha is regarded as an especially fruitful field of merit. The practice begins the noble eightfold path's cultivation of virtue (sīla).

In Jainism

Jain texts list dāna among the duties of the laity, and classify charity into four kinds: āhāra-dāna (giving of food), auṣadha-dāna (giving of medicine), jñāna-dāna or śāstra-dāna (giving of knowledge or scriptures), and abhaya-dāna (giving of protection from fear, i.e., saving lives). Jain monks and nuns accept alms daily from lay supporters.

In Sikhism

Guru Nanak taught vaṇḍ chhakṇā — sharing one's earnings with others — as one of the three pillars of Sikh life, alongside honest work (kirat karo) and remembrance of God (nām japo). The institution of the langar, a free community kitchen, embodies dāna in Sikh practice.
