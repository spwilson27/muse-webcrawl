Topographic isolation

Isolation is the minimum distance to a point of higher elevation

The topographic isolation of a summit is the minimum distance to a point of equal or higher elevation. It represents the radius of dominance in which the summit is the highest point. It can be calculated for small hills and islands as well as for major mountain peaks, and can even be calculated for submarine summits.

Determination

Topographic isolation is closely related to topographic prominence, which measures how high a summit rises above its surroundings. Whereas prominence concerns the vertical drop to the key col, isolation concerns the horizontal distance to the nearest higher terrain. A summit with very high isolation is typically the dominant peak of a large region: Mount Everest, for example, has an isolation equal to Earth's maximum possible value (its nearest higher point does not exist), while Aconcagua has an isolation of about 16,534 kilometres, since the nearest higher ground is in the Hindu Kush. Weather conditions and the summit's exposure often correlate with isolation, as isolated summits stand alone against prevailing winds.

Examples

Among the most isolated major summits are Mount Kilimanjaro (isolation about 5,510 km), Mount Logan in Canada, and Pico de Orizaba in Mexico. Island high points frequently have large isolations because the surrounding ocean contains no higher ground: the isolation of Mauna Kea, for instance, extends to the nearest higher summits on the mainland continents. Isolation is one of several measures used by peakbaggers to classify and rank summits, alongside elevation and prominence.
