Canut revolts

The Canut revolts (French: Révolte des canuts) were major uprisings of silk workers (canuts) in Lyon, France, in 1831 and 1834, with a further insurrection in 1848. They are considered among the earliest well-defined worker uprisings of the Industrial Revolution and are famous for the slogan "Live working or die fighting" (Vivre en travaillant ou mourir en combattant).

Background

Lyon was the centre of the French silk industry. The canuts were master weavers who owned their Jacquard looms but depended on merchant-manufacturers (soyeux) for orders and piece rates. Falling prices and competition squeezed their incomes, and the weavers demanded a fixed minimum tariff for piecework.

First revolt (1831)

In November 1831, after manufacturers refused to honour a negotiated minimum rate, the canuts rose, seized the city, and repelled the local garrison. The government sent Marshal Soult with a large army; the city was reoccupied in December and the tariff abolished. The revolt ended without major bloodshed but demonstrated organised working-class power.

Second revolt (1834)

In April 1834 a renewed uprising, now allied with republican societies, was crushed with heavy fighting — the "bloody week" (semaine sanglante) — leaving hundreds dead. Mass arrests and a giant trial in Paris followed.

Legacy

The revolts deeply impressed contemporaries and are often cited as a precursor of the modern labour movement and of the revolutions of 1848.
