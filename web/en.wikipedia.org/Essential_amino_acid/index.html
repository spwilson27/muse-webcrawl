Essential amino acid

An essential amino acid, or indispensable amino acid, is an amino acid that cannot be synthesized by the organism fast enough to supply its demand, and must therefore come from the diet. Of the 21 amino acids common to all life forms, the nine amino acids humans cannot synthesize are phenylalanine, valine, tryptophan, threonine, isoleucine, methionine, histidine, leucine, and lysine.

Essentiality

The classic experiments of William Cumming Rose in the 1930s–1950s, feeding rats and then graduate students on purified amino-acid diets, established which amino acids are indispensable. Histidine was shown essential for adults only later, since short-term deficiency produces no obvious symptoms. Arginine is sometimes classed as conditionally essential, since the body synthesises it but not always fast enough during growth or illness.

Food sources and complete proteins

Animal products such as meat, eggs and dairy contain all nine essential amino acids in adequate proportions and are traditionally called "complete" proteins. Most individual plant proteins are low in one or another essential amino acid — cereals tend to be low in lysine, legumes low in methionine — but a varied plant-based diet supplies them all over the course of a day.

Deficiency

Total protein deficiency produces conditions such as kwashiorkor and marasmus. Specific essential amino-acid shortages limit protein synthesis according to the principle of the limiting amino acid, a concept first formalised in agricultural nutrition.
