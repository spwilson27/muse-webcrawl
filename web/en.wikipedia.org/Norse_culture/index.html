Norse culture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Norse culture refers to the culture of the Norse people — the North Germanic inhabitants of Scandinavia and their overseas settlements — during the late Iron Age and early medieval period, most prominently the Viking Age (c. 793–1066 CE). It is known from archaeology, runic inscriptions, place names, and the rich literature written down in Iceland in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries: the sagas, the Poetic Edda, and the Prose Edda of Snorri Sturluson.

Society

Norse society was organised around kinship and the farm, with social ranks of jarls (earls), free farmers (karlar), and thralls (slaves). Law and dispute settlement operated through assemblies called things — most famously Iceland's Althing, founded in 930. Honour and reputation, maintained through generosity, loyalty, and vengeance for slights, were central social values; feuds structured much of saga narrative. Women held significant legal rights by medieval standards — owning property, initiating divorce, and managing farms during men's voyages — though political power was overwhelmingly male.

Religion and mythology

Pre-Christian Norse religion centred on gods such as Odin, Thor, Freyr, and Freyja, on beings including elves, dwarves, and land spirits, and on a cosmology embracing the world-tree Yggdrasil, the doom of the gods at Ragnarök, and an afterlife in Valhalla or Hel. Practice involved sacrifice (blót), sacred groves and temples, and seeresses (völur). Scandinavia converted to Christianity between the tenth and twelfth centuries, a transition the sagas themselves narrate, and the old mythology survived chiefly through Icelandic textual preservation.

Language, writing, and art

The Norsemen spoke Old Norse, written in the Younger Futhark runic alphabet used for memorial stones, ownership marks, and everyday messages (as in the Bergen runic inscriptions). Visual art developed distinctive styles — Borre, Jellinge, Mammen, Ringerike, and Urnes — of interlaced gripping beasts carved in wood and stone and cast in metal. Burial customs ranged from cremation to elaborate ship burials such as Oseberg and Gokstad.

Seafaring and expansion

Superb shipbuilding — clinker-built longships and knarrs — carried Norse seafarers to raid, trade, and settle from North America (Vinland) in the west to Constantinople and Baghdad trade routes in the east. Norse settlements transformed Iceland, Greenland, the Faroes, Normandy (named for the "Northmen"), the Danelaw in England, and the river routes of the Rus'. The Viking Age faded as Scandinavian kingdoms consolidated and integrated into Christian Europe.
