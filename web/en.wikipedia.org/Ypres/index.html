Ypres (Dutch: Ieper) is a Belgian city in the province of West Flanders, in the Flemish region. Once one of the great cloth towns of medieval Flanders, it is best known internationally for the battles fought around it during the First World War, when it stood at the centre of the Ypres Salient.

History

Ypres prospered in the 12th and 13th centuries as a centre of the Flemish cloth industry, its population reaching perhaps 40,000; its enormous Cloth Hall, begun around 1200, was one of the largest commercial buildings of medieval Europe. Decline followed from the 14th century as the cloth trade waned and wars and plague took their toll. As a fortified frontier town it was repeatedly besieged in the following centuries.

First World War

Between 1914 and 1918, three major battles were fought for control of Ypres and the strategic salient around it. The Second Battle of Ypres (1915) saw the first large-scale use of poison gas on the Western Front; the Third Battle of Ypres (1917), commonly called Passendale (Passchendaele), became synonymous with the mud and slaughter of trench warfare. The city itself was almost entirely destroyed by artillery but was rebuilt after the war largely in its pre-war style, including the Cloth Hall and St Martin's Cathedral.

Remembrance

The Menin Gate memorial bears the names of more than 54,000 Commonwealth soldiers who died in the salient and have no known grave; the Last Post has been sounded beneath it every evening since 1928 (except during the Second World War occupation). The In Flanders Fields Museum, named for John McCrae's famous poem written near Ypres in 1915, occupies the rebuilt Cloth Hall.
