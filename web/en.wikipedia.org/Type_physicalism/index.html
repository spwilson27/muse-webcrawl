Type physicalism

Type physicalism identifies each mental state type with a physical state type
FieldPhilosophy of mind
Also calledType identity theory, mind–brain identity theory
ProponentsU. T. Place, J. J. C. Smart, Herbert Feigl

Type physicalism (also known as type identity theory or mind–brain identity theory) is a physicalist theory in the philosophy of mind. It asserts that mental events can be grouped into types, and that these types can be correlated with types of physical events in the brain: for example, the mental state type pain is held to be identical with a particular type of brain state, such as the firing of C-fibers.

The theory was developed in the 1950s by U. T. Place and J. J. C. Smart, partly by analogy with theoretical identifications in science, such as the identification of lightning with electrical discharge or of heat with molecular motion.

Criticism

The best-known objection is Hilary Putnam's multiple realizability argument: creatures with very different physical make-ups (octopuses, hypothetical silicon-based beings, aliens) could presumably share mental state types without sharing brain state types, so mental types cannot be identical to specific physical types. This objection helped spur the rise of functionalism and of token physicalism, which identifies only individual mental events, not types, with physical events.
