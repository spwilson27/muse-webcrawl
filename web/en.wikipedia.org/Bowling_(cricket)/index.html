Bowling (cricket)

In cricket, bowling is the action of propelling the ball toward the wicket defended by a batsman. A player skilled at bowling is called a bowler. Bowling the ball is distinguished from merely throwing it by a strict definition of the arm action in the Laws of Cricket: the bowler's elbow must not straighten in the delivery swing beyond permitted limits.

Bowling
Governing rules: Laws of Cricket, Law 21 (No ball) and Law 24
Key skills: pace, swing, seam, spin, accuracy
Opposite role: Batting

Delivery action

A bowler runs in, delivers the ball overarm from one end of the pitch toward the batsman at the other end, aiming to dismiss the batsman or restrict scoring. Deliveries are bowled in sets of six legal balls called an over, after which a different bowler bowls from the opposite end.

Types of bowler

Fast bowlers rely on speed, typically delivering the ball at 130–155 km/h, and on swing (curving the ball through the air) or seam movement off the pitch. Spin bowlers bowl more slowly and impart rotation so the ball deviates off the surface; the main styles are off spin, leg spin, and left-arm orthodox or wrist spin. Medium pacers combine moderate pace with swing and accuracy.

Techniques and tactics

Common deliveries include the yorker (pitched at the batsman's feet), the bouncer (short-pitched and rising toward shoulder or head height), the slower ball, and for spinners the googly, doosra, and arm ball. Captains set fields to support particular bowling plans, and bowlers work in partnerships to build pressure.

Legality and controversies

A delivery in which the elbow straightens excessively is deemed a throw and called a no-ball; biomechanical research led the ICC to permit up to 15 degrees of elbow extension for all bowlers. Historical controversies over throwing, bodyline tactics, and ball tampering have repeatedly reshaped the laws and playing conditions.
