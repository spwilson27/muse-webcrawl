Escharotomy

SpecialtyBurn surgery, emergency medicine
IndicationCircumferential full-thickness burns with compartment compromise
ApproachIncision through eschar to subcutaneous fat

An escharotomy is a surgical procedure used to treat full-thickness (third-degree) circumferential burns. In such burns, the tough, leathery burned tissue — the eschar — can act like a tourniquet: as the underlying tissues swell with resuscitation fluid and edema, the inelastic eschar cannot expand, and the rising pressure can cut off circulation, compress nerves, or restrict breathing when the chest is involved. Cutting through the eschar relieves this pressure and restores perfusion or ventilation.

Indications

Escharotomy is indicated when a circumferential full-thickness burn causes signs of compromised circulation in a limb — such as diminished or absent Doppler pulses, delayed capillary refill, paresthesia, or deep pain unrelieved by elevation — or when chest eschar restricts respiratory excursion in a ventilated patient. It is an emergency, bedside or operating-room procedure typically performed within the first hours after injury.

Technique

After sedation and analgesia (the burned tissue itself is usually insensate), longitudinal incisions are made through the eschar down to, but not into, the underlying healthy fascia and subcutaneous fat, using a sterile scalpel or electrocautery. On limbs the incisions are placed along the mid-lateral and/or mid-medial lines, avoiding superficial nerves and vessels; the wound edges should spring apart, confirming adequate release. On the fingers, incisions are made along the mid-lateral lines of the digits. Trunk escharotomies follow the anterior axillary lines, sometimes joined by transverse subcostal incisions. Distal perfusion is reassessed after the procedure; if circulation is not restored, a deeper fasciotomy may be required.

Complications

Possible complications include bleeding, infection, injury to underlying nerves or vessels, and conversion of the wound if incisions are too deep. Because the wounds are left open, they require dressing care and subsequent excision and skin grafting as part of definitive burn management.
