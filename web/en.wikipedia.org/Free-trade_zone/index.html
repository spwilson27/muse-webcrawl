Free-trade zone

A free-trade zone (FTZ) is a designated geographic area in which goods may be imported, stored, handled, manufactured, or re-exported under special customs rules, generally without payment of customs duties. Duties typically become payable only when goods leave the zone and enter the domestic market of the host country. The term is also used loosely for broader arrangements, including free-trade areas formed by agreement among countries, but in customs practice it denotes a specific zone within a single country.

Purposes and operation

Governments establish free-trade zones to promote trade, attract foreign investment, create employment, and encourage export-oriented manufacturing. In the United States, foreign-trade zones operate under the Foreign-Trade Zones Act of 1934 and are overseen by the FTZ Board; major users include the automotive, electronics, and pharmaceutical industries, which benefit from duty deferral, duty elimination on re-exports and scrap, and in some cases "inverted tariff" relief, where the duty on a finished product is lower than on its components. China's special economic zones, beginning with Shenzhen in 1980, are a larger-scale variant combining FTZ customs treatment with broad economic reforms.

Criticism

Critics argue that zones can facilitate trade-based money laundering, counterfeiting, and sanctions evasion because of reduced customs scrutiny, and that incentives may distort competition or erode the domestic tax base. Supporters respond that proper oversight and the demonstrated export gains of many zones justify their use.
