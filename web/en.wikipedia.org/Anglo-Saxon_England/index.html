Anglo-Saxon England

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anglo-Saxon England was the period of English history from the end of Roman Britain in the early 5th century until the Norman Conquest in 1066, during which England was settled and ruled by Germanic peoples — principally Angles, Saxons and Jutes — whose language, Old English, and institutions laid the foundations of the English nation.

Settlement and kingdoms

Following Rome's withdrawal around 410, Germanic settlers crossed the North Sea and established kingdoms that by the 7th century had consolidated into the so-called Heptarchy: Northumbria, Mercia, East Anglia, Essex, Kent, Sussex and Wessex. The settlers displaced or absorbed much of the Romano-British population, though the extent of population replacement remains debated by historians and geneticists.

Christianisation and culture

The mission of Augustine from Rome in 597 and Irish missionaries from Iona gradually converted the kingdoms to Christianity. Monasteries such as Lindisfarne and Jarrow became centres of learning; the Venerable Bede's Ecclesiastical History (731) is the era's masterwork. Anglo-Saxon culture produced heroic poetry such as Beowulf, exquisite metalwork like the Sutton Hoo ship burial treasures, illuminated manuscripts and stone churches.

Vikings and unification

Viking raids beginning at Lindisfarne in 793 turned into conquest: by the 870s Danish armies had destroyed every kingdom except Wessex. Alfred the Great of Wessex halted the advance at Edington (878), and his descendants — Edward the Elder, Æthelstan and their successors — reconquered the Danelaw, creating a unified Kingdom of England by 927. England enjoyed a sophisticated state with shire courts, a national coinage and law codes, but renewed Viking attacks under Æthelred the Unready led to the Danish kingship of Cnut (1016–1035). Edward the Confessor restored the Wessex line in 1042; his death without an heir in 1066 triggered the succession crisis that ended the Anglo-Saxon era.
