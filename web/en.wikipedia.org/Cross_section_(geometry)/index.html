Cross section (geometry)

In geometry and science, a cross section is the non-empty intersection of a solid body in three-dimensional space with a plane, or the analogous in higher-dimensional spaces. Cutting an object into slices creates many parallel cross-sections. The boundary of a cross-section in three-dimensional space that is parallel to two of the axes, that is, parallel to the plane determined by these axes, is sometimes referred to as a contour line; for example, if a plane cuts through mountains of a raised-relief map parallel to the ground, the result is a contour line in two-dimensional space showing points on the surface of the mountains of equal elevation.

Planar cross-sections of solids.

Examples

A cross section of a polyhedron is a polygon. The conic sections — circles, ellipses, parabolas, and hyperbolas — are cross sections of a cone at various angles to its axis. A cross-section of a cylinder taken parallel to its base is a circle; taken perpendicular to the base, it is a rectangle. Cross-sections are extensively used in technical drawing, where sectional views reveal internal features of machinery and buildings, and in medicine, where computed tomography reconstructs cross-sectional images of the body.

Cavalieri's principle

If two solids have cross-sections of equal area at every height, they have equal volume — the classic Cavalieri's principle, an early step toward integral calculus. More generally, volumes can be computed by integrating cross-sectional areas along an axis, a method taught in elementary calculus.

Related terms

In geology, a cross section is a diagram showing subsurface structures along a vertical plane. In anatomy, the transverse plane produces cross-sections of the body, also called axial sections.
