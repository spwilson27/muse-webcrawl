Nonsense verse

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nonsense verse is a form of nonsense literature, usually employing correct verse structure and grammar but semantically meaningless or absurd elements, often including invented words. It delights in sound, rhythm, and wordplay while defying straightforward interpretation.

History

The genre flourished in nineteenth-century England. Edward Lear's A Book of Nonsense (1846) popularised the limerick, and Lewis Carroll's poems "Jabberwocky" (1871) and "The Hunting of the Snark" (1876) became the most famous examples, full of portmanteau words such as "chortle" and "galumphing" that later entered ordinary English. Later practitioners include Hilaire Belloc, Spike Milligan, Dr. Seuss, and Shel Silverstein.

Characteristics

Nonsense verse preserves metre, rhyme, and narrative scaffolding while presenting impossible creatures, absurd journeys, and invented vocabularies. Scholars debate how far such works are truly meaningless: Carroll's poems, for example, sustain coherent moods and story structures despite their invented lexicon.
