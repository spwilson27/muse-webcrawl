Caratacus

Caratacus before the Emperor Claudius at Rome (placeholder)

Caratacus (Brythonic *Caratācos, also rendered Caractacus, and in Welsh Caradog) was a first-century AD British king of the Catuvellauni tribe and son of Cunobelinus. Together with his brother Togodumnus, he led the British resistance to the Roman invasion of Britain launched by the emperor Claudius in AD 43, fighting a guerrilla campaign for eight years before his capture and transportation to Rome, where his dignified plea for mercy persuaded Claudius to spare his life.

Life and campaigns

Before the invasion, Caratacus had expanded Catuvellaunian power, notably by conquering territory of the Atrebates, whose king Verica fled to Rome and provided Claudius with a pretext to invade. After Togodumnus was killed early in the campaign of AD 43 — which included battles at the Medway and the Thames and the fall of Camulodunum (Colchester) — Caratacus escaped westward and continued the war from Wales, allying with the Silures and Ordovices. In AD 51 he was defeated by the governor Ostorius Scapula at the Battle of Caer Caradoc (its exact site in the Welsh borders is disputed). His wife, sons, and daughter were captured; Caratacus himself fled north to Cartimandua, queen of the Brigantes, who handed him over to the Romans in chains.

Surrender speech and later life

Caratacus was paraded in Claudius's triumph in Rome with his family. According to Tacitus, his speech before the emperor — arguing that his stubborn resistance had magnified Roman glory and that sparing him would make him an eternal monument to Claudius's clemency — so impressed Claudius that the family was pardoned. They remained in Italy; a later tradition, popularised in the nineteenth century, identified his daughter with the Gladys (Gwladys) of Welsh legend and him with the "Caradog" of Welsh genealogies, though these links are not historically secure.

Legacy

Caratacus became a romantic hero of British resistance, commemorated in Welsh tradition as Caradog and in literature and art, and his speech remains one of the famous set pieces of Tacitus's Annals.
