Splendid isolation

Splendid isolation is a term used to describe the 19th-century British diplomatic practice of avoiding permanent alliances, particularly under the governments of Lord Salisbury and other late-Victorian administrations. Protected by the sea and by the Royal Navy, Britain sought to preserve a free hand: intervening in European affairs to uphold the balance of power and protect its empire and trade, without committing itself in advance to Continental partners.

Origin of the term

The phrase was popularised in 1896, when the Canadian politician George Eulas Foster used "splendid isolation" in a speech praising Britain's position, and it was taken up by British statesmen including Salisbury himself. Historians debate how deliberate or coherent the "policy" really was: Britain did conclude limited agreements — for example the Mediterranean Agreements of 1887 with Italy and Austria-Hungary — and Salisbury's approach is better described as a preference for detachment and flexibility rather than strict isolation.

End

The strains of the Second Boer War (1899–1902) exposed the risks of friendlessness. Britain ended its isolation by signing the Anglo-Japanese Alliance (1902), followed by the Entente Cordiale with France (1904) and the Anglo-Russian Convention (1907), aligning it informally with France and Russia in the years before the First World War.
