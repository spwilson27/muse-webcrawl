Treaty of Melfi
SignedAugust 1059
LocationMelfi, southern Italy
PartiesPope Nicholas II; Robert Guiscard; Richard of Aversa
EffectNorman leaders recognised as papal vassals; Robert invested as Duke of Apulia, Calabria and (future) Sicily

The Treaty of Melfi (1059) was an agreement between Pope Nicholas II and the Norman leaders Robert Guiscard and Richard of Aversa, concluded at the Council of Melfi in August 1059. It transformed the Normans of southern Italy—hitherto regarded by the papacy as brigands—into legitimate, papally sanctioned rulers.

Terms

Following the papal defeat at Civitate (1053) and a reforming papacy's search for military protectors against the Holy Roman Emperor, Nicholas II invested Robert Guiscard as Duke of Apulia and Calabria and, in prospect, of Sicily—which the Normans had yet to conquer from its Muslim rulers—and Richard as Prince of Capua. In exchange the Normans swore fealty, promised an annual tribute per yoke of oxen, and undertook to defend the papacy and support free papal elections. The council at Melfi simultaneously promulgated the decree reserving papal elections to the cardinal-bishops.

Consequences

The treaty legitimised the Norman conquest of southern Italy and Sicily (completed by 1091) and bound the new Norman states to the papacy as vassals—a relationship revisited in the Treaty of Benevento (1156). The papal–Norman alliance also freed the reform papacy to confront the empire in the Investiture Controversy.
