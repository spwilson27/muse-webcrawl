Cinnabar

Cinnabar (or cinnabarite) is the bright scarlet to brick-red form of mercury(II) sulfide (HgS). It is the most common source ore for refining elemental mercury and is the historic source for the brilliant red or scarlet pigment termed vermilion and associated red mercury pigments.

Occurrence

Cinnabar generally occurs in a massive, granular, or earthy form near recent volcanic activity and alkaline hot springs. Famous localities include Almadén in Spain — the largest mercury deposit in the world, mined since Roman times — Idrija in Slovenia, Monte Amiata in Italy, and deposits in China, Kyrgyzstan, and the western United States.

Properties and structure

Cinnabar has a Mohs hardness of 2 to 2.5 and a remarkably high specific gravity of about 8.1. It crystallizes in the trigonal system, often as rhombohedral crystals with a scarlet streak. Its synthetic counterpart, manufactured by combining mercury and sulfur, is the pigment vermilion.

Uses and history

Cinnabar pigment was used in China from Neolithic times — painted on oracle bones and lacquerware — and in the ancient Near East, Greece, and Rome, where it adorned statues, murals, and cosmetics despite its toxicity. Chinese alchemists prized cinnabar in elixirs of immortality, a tragic irony given mercury poisoning; the First Emperor of China, Qin Shi Huang, is said to have died from such preparations. Mercury extracted from cinnabar was essential to gold and silver refining in the Americas under Spanish rule.

Toxicity

Like all mercury compounds, cinnabar is toxic; dust from mining and pigment grinding caused chronic mercury poisoning among miners and artists. Its use in art has largely been replaced by cadmium and modern synthetic pigments.
