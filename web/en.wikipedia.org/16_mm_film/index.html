16 mm film

Introduced1923 (by Eastman Kodak)
Film width16 mm (0.63 in)
Frame size10.26 × 7.49 mm (standard)
Frame rate18 or 24 frames per second
VariantsSuper 16, Ultra 16

16 mm film is a historically popular and economical gauge of film stock, 16 millimetres (0.63 in) wide. It is generally used for non-theatrical (e.g. industrial, educational, television) film-making, or for low-budget motion pictures. It exists in two main forms: standard 16 mm, with a 1.33:1 frame, and Super 16, which expands the frame into the area formerly used for the soundtrack to give a wider 1.66:1 image.

History

Eastman Kodak introduced 16 mm in 1923 as an inexpensive amateur alternative to 35 mm film, launching the Cine-Kodak camera and the Kodascope projector system. Crucially, 16 mm was sold only on acetate safety base, never on flammable nitrate stock, which made it acceptable for homes, schools and churches. During the Second World War the format was used extensively for training films and combat camera work.

After the war, 16 mm became the workhorse of television news gathering, documentary production, education and avant-garde cinema. Filmmakers such as the Direct Cinema and cinéma vérité movements adopted lightweight sync-sound 16 mm rigs in the 1960s. Many television series and independent features — from documentaries to low-budget dramas — were shot on 16 mm and later on Super 16, which emerged in 1969 and saw wide use for productions intended for blow-up to 35 mm or transfer to HD video.

Technical details

Standard 16 mm film comes in rolls of 100 ft (30.5 m) on daylight-loading spools, or 200, 400 and 1,200 ft core loads for magazine cameras. Film may be single-perforated (sprocket holes along one edge, leaving room for a soundtrack) or double-perforated. At 24 frames per second the film runs at 36 feet per minute. Sound formats include optical tracks and, historically, magnetic stripe. Super 16 requires modified cameras with re-centred lens mounts and widened gates.

Modern use

Digital production displaced 16 mm from news and most television work by the 2000s, but the format persists in film schools, experimental cinema, music videos and period drama, where its grain structure and texture are prized. Kodak continues to manufacture 16 mm stocks, and the gauge enjoyed a modest revival in the 2010s and 2020s as part of the broader analog film resurgence.
