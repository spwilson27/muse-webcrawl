Orphism (religion)

Orphism is the name given to a set of religious beliefs and practices originating in the ancient Greek and Thracian worlds, associated with literature ascribed to the mythical poet Orpheus, who descended into the Underworld and returned. Orphism revolved around myths of the suffering and death of the god Dionysus, and around distinctive doctrines about the soul and the afterlife.

Beliefs

Central to Orphic belief was the myth of Dionysus Zagreus: the infant Dionysus, heir of Zeus, was lured, dismembered, and eaten by the Titans; Athena saved his heart, from which he was reborn, while Zeus blasted the Titans with thunderbolts. From the Titans' ashes humanity arose — explaining, in Orphic terms, why human beings combine a divine, Dionysian nature (the immortal soul) with a Titanic, material nature (the body). The body was thus seen as a prison or tomb (sōma sēma) of the soul, which is subject to a cycle of reincarnation until liberated through ritual purity, initiation, and an ascetic way of life that included abstention from eating meat.

Evidence

Sources include the Orphic Hymns, fragments of poems such as the Derveni papyrus commentary (recovered in 1962 and among the oldest surviving Greek manuscripts), and the small inscribed gold tablets — the "Orphic lamellae" — buried with initiates from the 5th century BCE onward, which give the soul directions for navigating the afterlife and address the goddesses of Memory and Persephone.

Influence

Orphic ideas about the soul, judgement after death, and reincarnation influenced Pythagoreanism and Plato, and through them later Greek philosophy.
