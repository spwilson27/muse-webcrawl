The Zeeman effect is the splitting of a spectral line into several components in the presence of a static magnetic field. It is named after the Dutch physicist Pieter Zeeman, who discovered it in 1896 while studying light emitted by a sodium flame placed between the poles of a strong electromagnet; Hendrik Lorentz immediately supplied a classical explanation in terms of oscillating electrons, and the two shared the 1902 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Explanation

A magnetic field interacts with the magnetic moment associated with an electron's orbital and spin angular momentum, shifting the energies of atomic states according to their magnetic quantum number. Transitions between the split levels then produce multiple, closely spaced spectral lines, polarised in characteristic ways. The simple three-line pattern, the "normal" Zeeman effect, is explained by orbital motion alone; the more complex "anomalous" patterns seen in many atoms required the concept of electron spin, introduced in the 1920s, and are described by the Landé g-factor.

In strong fields the pattern simplifies again into the Paschen–Back effect, where the field decouples spin and orbital angular momenta.

Applications

Because the amount of splitting measures the field strength, the Zeeman effect is a principal tool for measuring magnetic fields in astronomy: George Ellery Hale used it in 1908 to discover magnetic fields in sunspots, and it is now applied to stars, interstellar clouds and laboratory plasmas. It is also exploited in atomic absorption spectroscopy to correct for background absorption, and underlies aspects of magnetic resonance techniques.
