Moïse Kapenda Tshombe (1919–1969) was a Congolese businessman and politician. He led the secession of the mineral-rich Katanga province from the newly independent Republic of the Congo in 1960, sparking the Congo Crisis, and later served as prime minister of the reunified country in 1964–65.

Katanga secession

Tshombe founded the CONAKAT party to represent the peoples of Katanga and the interests of its copper-mining economy. Days after Congolese independence on 30 June 1960 — and amid army mutinies — he declared Katanga's secession on 11 July, with support from Belgian officials and the Union Minière mining company. His regime, defended by a gendarmerie officered by Europeans and later supplemented by mercenaries, fought UN peacekeeping forces (ONUC) sent to restore Congolese unity. The ousted prime minister Patrice Lumumba was transferred to Katanga in January 1961 and murdered there, a crime in which Tshombe's government was deeply implicated. UN offensives in late 1962 finally ended the secession in January 1963, and Tshombe went into exile in Spain.

Prime minister and exile

Recalled in 1964 to head a government of national reconciliation, Tshombe relied on white mercenaries and Western backing to crush the Simba rebellion in the east. Dismissed by President Joseph Kasa-Vubu in October 1965, he returned to exile; after Mobutu Sese Seko's coup he was sentenced to death in absentia for treason. In 1967 his aircraft was hijacked to Algeria, where he was held under house arrest until his death from a heart attack in 1969.

Legacy

Tshombe remains a polarising figure: remembered by critics as a separatist collaborator of colonial interests complicit in Lumumba's fate, and by supporters as a capable administrator and federalist who opposed communist influence in Central Africa.
