Priming (media)

News coverage can prime the criteria by which audiences evaluate public figures.

In media studies and political communication, priming is the process by which the mass media influence the standards that audience members use to evaluate political leaders, parties, and issues. By drawing attention to some matters while ignoring others, media coverage increases the accessibility of certain considerations in memory, so that those considerations weigh more heavily in subsequent judgments. The classic formulation is that the media may not tell people what to think, but they influence what people think about — and the criteria by which they judge.

Origins

The concept was formalised by Shanto Iyengar and Donald Kinder in their 1987 book News That Matters, based on experiments in which participants exposed to news stories emphasising a particular problem, such as national defence or unemployment, subsequently evaluated the president's performance in terms of that problem. Priming is closely related to agenda-setting and framing, and is sometimes described as a second level of agenda-setting theory.

Mechanism

Priming effects are explained by the psychology of memory accessibility, drawing on the concept of priming in cognitive psychology: recently or frequently activated ideas are more likely to be used in later evaluations. In practice, heavy coverage of a scandal can lower a politician's approval ratings even among people whose overall view of the politician's other policies is unchanged, because the primed dimension dominates their assessment. Effects are typically strongest among viewers who are politically aware but not strongly partisan.

Applications

Researchers have applied priming theory to elections, where late-campaign coverage of the economy or of foreign policy can shift the basis of vote choice, and to subjects such as race, immigration, and crime, where news emphasis alters the attributions audiences make for social problems. Critics note that in a fragmented media environment audiences increasingly select their own information diets, which may limit or redirect priming effects.
