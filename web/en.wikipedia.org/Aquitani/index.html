Aquitani

The Aquitani were a people living in ancient times in what is now south-western France, between the Pyrenees and the Garonne river. They gave their name to the region of Aquitaine. Julius Caesar, in his Commentarii de Bello Gallico, divided Gaul into three parts—inhabited by the Belgae, the Aquitani, and the Gauls (Celts)—and noted that the Aquitani differed from the others in language, customs and laws.

Language

The Aquitanian language is attested in inscriptions, mostly names of people and deities carved on funerary and votive stones in Latin script. It is generally considered an ancient relative, or possibly an ancestor, of the modern Basque language, and is classified with the Vasconic languages. Personal names such as Andre (woman/lady), Cison (man) and deity names like Erge show clear parallels with Basque words.

History

The Aquitani were conquered by Rome during the Gallic Wars; in 56 BC, Caesar's legate Publius Licinius Crassus defeated the Sotiates and accepted the submission of most Aquitanian tribes. Under Augustus their territory became the core of the province of Gallia Aquitania, which was enlarged to include many Celtic peoples further north. The name survived through the Roman and medieval duchy of Aquitaine to the modern French region.
