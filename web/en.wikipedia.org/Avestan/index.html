Avestan is an umbrella term for two Old Iranian languages, Old Avestan (or Gathic Avestan) and Young Avestan, which are known solely from their use as the language of Zoroastrian scripture, the Avesta. Along with Old Persian, Avestan is one of the earliest attested Iranian languages. Old Avestan, the language of the Gathas attributed to the prophet Zoroaster, is closely related to the Rigvedic Sanskrit of ancient India and may date to the late second or early first millennium BC.

History

The Avestan texts were transmitted orally for centuries before being committed to writing, probably in the Sasanian period, when a purpose-built Avestan alphabet — derived from the Pahlavi script — was created to record the recitation exactly. The surviving manuscripts are much later, dating from the thirteenth century AD onwards.

Dialects

Old Avestan is preserved in the Gathas (seventeen hymns attributed to Zoroaster), the Yasna Haptanghaiti, and a few sacred formulas. Young Avestan, linguistically later and less archaic, makes up the bulk of the Avesta, including most of the Yasna, the Yashts (hymns to divinities), and the Vendidad.

Features

Avestan preserves many archaic features shared with Vedic Sanskrit: a rich case system of eight cases, three numbers including a dual, and complex verbal morphology. Its phonology is noted for its system of fricatives. Because the language survived only as scripture, its study is central to reconstructing the Iranian branch and, more broadly, Proto-Indo-Iranian.
