Gamut

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gamut
Literal originMedieval music: gamma ut, lowest note Γ plus first solmisation syllable
SensesColour reproduction; music; general: "the whole range"
Key modelsCIE chromaticity diagram; RGB and CMYK colour spaces

In colour reproduction and colour science, a gamut is the complete subset of colours that a given device or process can accurately represent — for example, the colours a monitor can display or a printer can print within a given colour space. The word derives from medieval music theory, where gamut (from gamma ut) denoted the entire range of recognised musical notes; by extension, it now means the full scope or range of anything, as in the phrase "the whole gamut of emotions".

Colour gamut

No device reproduces all colours visible to the human eye. Display gamuts are bounded by their primaries — typically shown as a triangle on the CIE 1931 chromaticity diagram for RGB displays — while printer gamuts, produced by subtractive CMYK inks, have irregular shapes that cover some hues displays cannot show and miss others (saturated cyans and greens are a common display advantage; some deep yellows favour print). Colours outside a device's gamut are "out of gamut" and must be mapped (clipped or compressed) during colour management, governed by rendering intents specified by the International Color Consortium (ICC). Standard working gamuts include sRGB (the web default), Adobe RGB, and the wide-gamut DCI-P3 and Rec. 2020 spaces used in cinema and HDR television.

Gamut mapping and checking

Because source and destination gamuts rarely match, workflows use gamut mapping algorithms and soft-proofing to preview out-of-gamut colours. Three-dimensional gamut volumes, rather than 2D chromaticity slices, are needed to compare devices fully since gamut varies with lightness.

Musical sense

In Guido d'Arezzo's solmisation system, the lowest note was designated by the Greek letter gamma (Γ), sung to the syllable ut; the combined "gamma-ut" named the whole hexachord system and thus the complete range of medieval music — the word's original meaning, preserved in figurative usage.
