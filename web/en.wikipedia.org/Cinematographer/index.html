Cinematographer

A cinematographer or director of photography (often shortened to DP or DOP) is the person responsible for the recording of a film, television production, music video, or other live-action piece. The cinematographer is the chief of the camera and light crews working on such a project and is responsible for making artistic and technical decisions related to the composition and imaging of each shot.

Role

Working closely with the director, the cinematographer chooses the camera, lenses, film stock or digital sensor settings, filters, and lighting to achieve the desired visual style. The DP supervises the camera operators, gaffers, and grips, and oversees the framing, exposure, and movement of every shot, as well as the color timing or digital intermediate in post-production.

History

In the earliest days of cinema the cinematographer literally operated the camera and was often the director as well. As productions grew larger in the 1910s and 1920s, the role specialized, and figures such as Billy Bitzer, Karl Struss, and Gregg Toland elevated cinematography into a recognized art form. Professional societies including the American Society of Cinematographers (founded 1919) and the British Society of Cinematographers codified the craft.

Notable cinematographers

Acclaimed cinematographers include Roger Deakins, Emmanuel Lubezki, Janusz Kamiński, Vittorio Storaro, Sven Nykvist, Gordon Willis, and Jordan Cronenweth. The Academy Award for Best Cinematography has been presented since the first Oscar ceremony in 1929.
