Circuit switching

Circuit switching is a method of implementing a telecommunications network in which two network nodes establish a dedicated communications channel (circuit) through the network before the nodes may communicate. The circuit guarantees the full bandwidth of the channel and remains connected for the duration of the communication session. The session constitutes a single stream of bits that is delivered with constant delay and in order.

Operation

A circuit-switched connection proceeds through three phases: circuit establishment (setup signaling reserves resources along a path), data transfer (the channel is used exclusively by the two parties), and circuit disconnect (resources are released for reuse). Classic examples are the public switched telephone network (PSTN) and the circuit-switched portion of ISDN and early cellular networks (the B channel of ISDN and GSM's voice service).

Comparison with packet switching

Unlike packet switching, in which data is divided into packets routed independently over shared links, circuit switching reserves capacity even when the parties are silent, which can waste bandwidth for bursty traffic but delivers predictable quality of service for continuous streams such as voice. Virtual circuit technologies, including ATM and MPLS, combine aspects of both approaches.

History

Circuit switching was the founding principle of telephony: early switchboards physically connected subscribers' lines with patch cords, and electromechanical and electronic switches later automated the process. From the 1960s, packet switching — pioneered for data networks such as the ARPANET — gradually displaced circuit switching, and modern telephone services increasingly run as voice over IP on packet networks.
