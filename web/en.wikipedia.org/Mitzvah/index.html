Mitzvah

In its primary meaning, the Hebrew word mitzvah (מִצְוָה, "commandment") refers to a commandment or precept in the Torah — one of the commandments that Jewish tradition counts as numbering 613 in total. The word is also used in a secondary sense to mean a good deed performed out of religious duty, such as an act of charity. The 613 commandments (the Taryag mitzvot) comprise 248 positive commandments ("do") and 365 negative commandments ("do not"), traditionally attributed to the enumeration of Maimonides.

Etymology and usage

Mitzvah derives from the root tzavah, "to command". In the Hebrew Bible it denotes divine commandments given to Israel. In later colloquial usage — reflected in expressions such as "it's a mitzvah" — it came to mean any praiseworthy deed, and in the phrase bar mitzvah ("son of the commandment") and bat mitzvah it marks the age (13 for boys, 12 or 13 for girls) at which Jewish children become obligated to observe the commandments.

The 613 commandments

The tradition that the Torah contains 613 commandments is first recorded in the Talmud (Makkot 23b–24a), which associates the number with a sermon by Rabbi Simlai. Medieval authorities compiled lists, the most influential being Maimonides' Sefer ha-Mitzvot (Book of Commandments). Examples include positive commandments such as sanctifying God's name, loving one's neighbour, giving charity and resting on the Sabbath, and negative ones such as the prohibitions of idolatry, murder and theft. Many commandments concern the Temple in Jerusalem or the agricultural life of ancient Israel and cannot be practised in their original form today; rabbinic estimates generally hold that roughly 270–300 remain applicable.

Categories

Commandments are variously divided into rational laws (mishpatim), whose reasons are evident, and decrees (chukim), such as the dietary laws, whose rationale is not obvious; and into duties between a person and God and duties between a person and other people. The Day of Atonement, Yom Kippur, is said to atone only for transgressions of the former unless the injured person has first been appeased.

Performance and reward

Rabbinic teaching emphasises that the reward of a mitzvah is a mitzvah — the deed itself — and the Pirkei Avot urges, "run to perform a small mitzvah as to a great one, for one mitzvah leads to another". The concepts of kavanah (intention) and performing commandments joyfully are central themes in later Jewish thought, including Hasidism.
