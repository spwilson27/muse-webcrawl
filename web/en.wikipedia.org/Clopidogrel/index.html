Clopidogrel, sold under the brand name Plavix among others, is an antiplatelet medication used to reduce the risk of heart disease and stroke in people at high risk. It is also used together with aspirin in heart attacks and after the placement of a coronary artery stent in a procedure known as dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT). It is taken by mouth.

Medical uses

Clopidogrel is used in the prevention of ischemic stroke, myocardial infarction, and other forms of atherosclerotic vascular disease. It is recommended for people who have had a recent heart attack, a recent stroke, or established peripheral arterial disease. In acute coronary syndrome, it is combined with aspirin to prevent further clot formation, particularly after percutaneous coronary intervention with stenting.

Mechanism of action

Clopidogrel is a prodrug that must be converted by liver enzymes, principally CYP2C19, into an active metabolite. That metabolite irreversibly blocks the P2Y12 component of the ADP receptor on the surface of platelets, preventing adenosine diphosphate from activating the platelet and thereby inhibiting platelet aggregation for the lifetime of the platelet, roughly seven to ten days.

Adverse effects

The principal adverse effect is an increased risk of bleeding, including gastrointestinal bleeding and, rarely, intracranial hemorrhage. Other reported effects include rash, diarrhea, and abdominal pain. A rare but serious complication is thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura. People with reduced-function variants of CYP2C19 may derive less benefit because they activate the drug less efficiently.

History

Clopidogrel was developed by the French pharmaceutical company Sanofi and its partner Bristol-Myers Squibb. It was approved for medical use in the late 1990s and became one of the best-selling drugs in the world before losing patent protection. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.
