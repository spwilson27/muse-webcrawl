International Gothic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

International Gothic is a period of Gothic art which developed in Burgundy, Bohemia, France and northern Italy from the late 14th century and the early 15th century. It then spread very widely across Western Europe, hence the name given by the French art historian Louis Courajod in the late 19th century: by "international" he meant that the style transcended national boundaries, uniting the courts of Europe in a shared visual culture.

The style is characterised by elegant, elongated figures in graceful S-shaped poses, rich and decorative detail, meticulously observed natural elements such as plants, birds and insects, and sumptuous materials—gold leaf, luxurious textiles and jewellery rendered with precision. It was above all a courtly style, fostered by the great princely courts of Paris, Dijon, Prague, Milan and the papal court.

Notable works and artists

Masterpieces of the style include the illuminated manuscript Les Très Riches Heures du duc de Berry by the Limbourg Brothers, the paintings of Melchior Broederlam for the Burgundian court, the sculpture of Claus Sluter, the Wilton Diptych in England, and the works of Gentile da Fabriano, such as the Adoration of the Magi (1423), and Pisanello in Italy. In Bohemia the style is associated with the court of Emperor Charles IV and the "Beautiful Style" of Madonnas.

Decline

From the 1420s and 1430s the International Gothic gave way in the Netherlands to the more naturalistic Early Netherlandish painting of Robert Campin and Jan van Eyck, and in Florence to the Early Renaissance innovations of Masaccio and Brunelleschi, though the style persisted in parts of northern Europe into the later 15th century.
