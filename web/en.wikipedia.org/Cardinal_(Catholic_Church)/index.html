A cardinal is a senior member of the clergy of the Catholic Church, appointed by the pope and typically holding the rank of bishop. Cardinals constitute the College of Cardinals, whose chief responsibilities are to advise the pope and, upon his death or resignation, to elect his successor in a conclave. They are often styled "Princes of the Church" and addressed as "Your Eminence."

A cardinal wearing the scarlet choir dress and zucchetto.

History

The title derives from the Latin cardo ("hinge") and was applied first to clergy incardinated in the principal churches of Rome. By the eleventh century the cardinals of Rome had become the pope's electors, a right formalized in 1059 and restricted to cardinals in 1179. Pope Sixtus V in 1586 fixed the College at 70 members in three orders — six cardinal-bishops, fifty cardinal-priests, and fourteen cardinal-deacons — a structure that endured until the twentieth century, when numbers expanded greatly; Paul VI set an age limit of 80 for electors and a target maximum of 120 electors.

Orders and dress

Cardinal-bishops hold title to the suburbicarian sees around Rome (the Dean holds Ostia); cardinal-priests, mostly diocesan archbishops worldwide, receive a titular church in Rome; cardinal-deacons, typically curial officials, receive a Roman diaconia. Cardinals are created at consistories, receiving a red biretta or zucchetto and a ring. Their scarlet dress symbolizes readiness to shed blood for the faith.

Functions

Many cardinals head dicasteries of the Roman Curia or major archdioceses. During a vacancy of the Holy See the College, under the Camerlengo, administers the Church and conducts the conclave in the Sistine Chapel.
