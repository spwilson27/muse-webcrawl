Vibrio cholerae

Vibrio cholerae is a species of Gram-negative, facultative anaerobe, comma-shaped bacterium. It is the cause of cholera, an acute diarrhoeal disease that can kill within hours if untreated. The bacterium was first isolated in 1854 by the Italian anatomist Filippo Pacini, and independently identified in 1883 by Robert Koch, whose work established it as the cholera pathogen. V. cholerae occurs naturally in brackish and estuarine waters, where it associates with copepods and other zooplankton.

Classification and strains

More than 200 serogroups of V. cholerae are distinguished by their O antigen. Only serogroups O1 and O139 are known to cause epidemic cholera. O1 is divided into the classical and El Tor biotypes; the El Tor biotype, first isolated in 1905 and dominant since 1961, drives the ongoing seventh cholera pandemic. Serogroup O139 emerged in the Bay of Bengal in 1992.

Pathogenesis

Disease results from colonisation of the small intestine and secretion of cholera toxin, an AB5 enterotoxin encoded by the CTXφ bacteriophage carried by toxigenic strains. The toxin's A subunit permanently activates the Gs alpha subunit of adenylate cyclase in intestinal epithelial cells, raising cyclic AMP and opening chloride channels (CFTR); the resulting massive secretion of chloride, sodium and water into the gut lumen produces the profuse "rice-water" diarrhoea of cholera, which can exceed a litre per hour and cause fatal dehydration. Colonisation depends on the toxin-coregulated pilus, also used as the receptor for the CTXφ phage.

Genome and ecology

The V. cholerae genome consists of two circular chromosomes, of about 3 and 1 megabases, an unusual arrangement among bacteria. In the environment the organism persists in aquatic reservoirs in association with plankton and chitinous surfaces, and can enter a viable-but-non-culturable state. Epidemics typically follow contamination of drinking water with human faeces, and are prevented by clean water, sanitation and oral cholera vaccines.
