Hot reading

Hot reading is a technique used by mentalists, psychics and faith healers to give the impression of supernaturally detailed knowledge about a person, using information obtained covertly in advance. It contrasts with cold reading, in which the reader extracts information in real time from the subject's responses, appearance and body language, and with warm reading, which uses broadly true generalities (the Barnum effect).

Methods

Historically, hot reading used "blue books"—files compiled by networks of informants on prominent sitters in spiritualist circles, traded and sold among mediums in the early 20th century. Other methods include eavesdropping on audience members before a show, researching ticket purchasers, planting confederates in the audience who gather information, pre-show questionnaires presented as administrative paperwork, and—more recently—searching social media profiles of audience members who booked tickets under their real names. Ian Rowland's book The Full Facts Book of Cold Reading documents several such techniques.

Exposures

Sceptics and former practitioners have exposed hot reading in several prominent cases. In 1986, faith healer Peter Popoff was shown by investigator James Randi to be receiving information about audience members from his wife via a radio earpiece—information collected by his staff before the show—while claiming to receive it from God. Popoff declared bankruptcy shortly afterwards, though he later returned to ministry. Critics of televised psychics frequently allege similar use of audience research, wireless earpieces and edited broadcasts presenting hits while omitting misses.
