The IBM System/360 (S/360) is a family of mainframe computer systems announced by IBM on April 7, 1964. It was the first family of computers designed to cover both commercial and scientific applications and to span a complete range of sizes with a single architecture, so that customers could start small and upgrade without rewriting their applications. The name "360" refers to the 360 degrees of a circle—that is, all points of the compass, all kinds of work.

The bet-the-company gamble

Under president Thomas J. Watson Jr. and project leaders including Gene Amdahl (chief architect) and Fred Brooks (project manager), IBM invested roughly $5 billion—more than its annual revenue—in developing the System/360 and building new factories. The gamble succeeded: System/360 revenues transformed IBM and the computer industry, and the project became a landmark case study in both engineering management and its difficulties—Brooks's account of the effort, The Mythical Man-Month, coined the observation that "adding manpower to a late software project makes it later".

Architecture

The System/360 introduced concepts that became standard: the 8-bit byte as the fundamental addressable unit, byte-addressable memory, 32-bit words, hexadecimal floating point, and a standardised I/O interface (channels) for peripherals. Six initial models (30, 40, 50, 60, 62, and 70, soon revised to the 65 and 75 among others) implemented the same instruction set at different price/performance levels, with microcode used in the smaller models. Its operating systems included DOS/360 and the ambitious OS/360.

Legacy

The System/360 established the mainframe as a platform and software compatibility as an industry principle. Its architecture evolved through the System/370, System/390, and today's IBM Z systems, which remain backward-compatible in principle with 360 code. Plug-compatible manufacturers, notably Amdahl Corporation, built rival machines implementing the same architecture.
