Qilakitsoq

Qilakitsoq is an archaeological site on the Nuussuaq Peninsula in northwestern Greenland, famous for the discovery in 1972 of eight naturally mummified Inuit bodies dating to about 1475 AD. The mummies — six women and two children, including a six-month-old baby — are the best-preserved human remains ever found in Greenland and provide a remarkable record of Thule Inuit life, clothing, and health in the 15th century.

Qilakitsoq
Location: Nuussuaq Peninsula, Greenland
Discovered: 1972
Date of burials: c. 1475 AD
Remains: 8 mummies (6 women, 2 children)
Now held by: Greenland National Museum, Nuuk

Discovery and preservation

Two brothers hunting ptarmigan found the bodies beneath a rock overhang in two adjacent graves. The freezing, dry air and shelter from sun and rain had freeze-dried the corpses, preserving skin, hair, eyebrows, and even tattoos on some of the women's faces. The dead were laid out fully clothed in sealskin and reindeer-skin garments — nearly eighty items of clothing in total — with extra clothing placed beside them, apparently for the journey to the afterlife.

Findings

Examination showed family relationships among the women and revealed evidence of disease and hardship, including intestinal parasites and, in one woman, signs of a malignant tumour. DNA and isotope studies have been used to investigate their kinship, diet, and origins. Four of the best-preserved mummies are displayed at the Greenland National Museum in Nuuk.
