Adverse selection

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Adverse selection is a concept in economics, insurance and risk management describing a situation in which one party to a transaction has more relevant information than the other, so that the less-informed party tends to end up dealing disproportionately with the least desirable counterparties. The term originated in insurance but was generalised by economists studying asymmetric information.

The market for lemons

The classic exposition is George Akerlof's 1970 paper "The Market for 'Lemons'", which used the used-car market to show how information asymmetry can destroy a market. Buyers, unable to distinguish good cars ("peaches") from defective ones ("lemons"), will pay only a price reflecting average quality; sellers of good cars then withdraw because the price is too low, average quality falls further, and the market can unravel until only lemons remain. Akerlof shared the 2001 Nobel Prize in Economics with Michael Spence and Joseph Stiglitz for work on asymmetric information.

In insurance

In insurance, adverse selection occurs when those most likely to claim are the most likely to buy cover: if health insurance is priced for the average risk but mainly purchased by people who expect to be ill, premiums must rise, driving healthier people out and potentially causing a "death spiral" of rising premiums and shrinking pools. Insurers counter this with risk-based pricing, medical underwriting, waiting periods, and deductibles; governments counter it with mandates, subsidies, and community rating.

Solutions

Standard responses to adverse selection include screening (the uninformed party designing contracts that induce self-selection, such as a menu of premium/deductible combinations), signalling (the informed party credibly conveying quality, e.g. warranties or certifications), third-party verification, reputation mechanisms, and regulation such as mandatory disclosure or compulsory participation.
