Chapter 11

Bankruptcy law
Full titleChapter 11 of Title 11 of the United States Code
PurposeReorganization of a debtor's business affairs, debts, and assets
EstablishedBankruptcy Reform Act of 1978
Other chaptersChapter 7 (liquidation), Chapter 13 (individual repayment)

Chapter 11 of Title 11 of the United States Code is the principal form of bankruptcy reorganization in the United States, most prominently used by businesses. A debtor — usually the existing management, as "debtor in possession" — proposes a plan of reorganization to keep its business alive and pay creditors over time, rather than liquidating its assets as under Chapter 7. Individuals may also file under Chapter 11, particularly when their debts exceed the limits of Chapter 13.

Features

Filing a Chapter 11 petition triggers the automatic stay, halting most collection actions, lawsuits, and foreclosures. The debtor in possession operates the business under court supervision; creditors are organized into classes and a creditors' committee, and vote on the reorganization plan, which the court may confirm even over some classes' objections ("cramdown") if statutory fairness requirements are met. Special mechanisms include debtor-in-possession (DIP) financing, sales of assets free and clear of claims under section 363, rejection of burdensome contracts and leases, and avoidance of preferential or fraudulent transfers.

History and notable cases

Chapter 11 was created by the Bankruptcy Reform Act of 1978, consolidating earlier railroad and corporate reorganization provisions. Famous cases include Texaco (1987), General Motors and Chrysler (2009), American Airlines (2011), Enron (2001, then the largest), Lehman Brothers (2008, still the largest filing in U.S. history by assets), and Purdue Pharma. The Small Business Reorganization Act of 2019 added Subchapter V, a streamlined process for small businesses.
