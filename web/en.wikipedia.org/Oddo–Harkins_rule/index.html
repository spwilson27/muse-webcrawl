Oddo–Harkins rule

The Oddo–Harkins rule states that elements with even atomic numbers are more abundant in the universe than neighboring elements with odd atomic numbers. The pattern was first reported by Italian chemist Giuseppe Oddo in 1914 and confirmed in studies of meteorites and terrestrial abundances by American chemist William Draper Harkins in 1917.

Explanation

The rule is a consequence of the physics of nucleosynthesis in stars. Protons and neutrons pair up within atomic nuclei; nuclei with even numbers of protons (and of neutrons) are more tightly bound and more stable than those with odd numbers. Processes such as the alpha process, which builds elements by adding helium-4 nuclei (two protons and two neutrons), preferentially produce even-numbered elements, most famously carbon-12 and oxygen-16.

Exceptions and scope

Hydrogen, a light odd-numbered element, is by far the most abundant element in the universe, and lithium, beryllium, and boron are scarce for separate reasons related to their fragility in stellar interiors. The rule applies most clearly to heavier elements in cosmic abundance data. Notably, beryllium (atomic number 4) is far less abundant than its even-numbered neighbors because its only stable isotope, beryllium-9, is difficult to form in stars.

Significance

The Oddo–Harkins pattern in solar system abundances, first established from meteorite analysis, provided early evidence about how elements are forged in stars and remains a standard illustration of nuclear stability in geochemistry and astrophysics.
