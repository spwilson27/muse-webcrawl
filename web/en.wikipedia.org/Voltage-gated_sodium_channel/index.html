Voltage-gated sodium channel


Type: Ion channel
Ion: Sodium (Na+)
Genes: SCN1A–SCN11A
Blocked by: Tetrodotoxin, local anesthetics

Voltage-gated sodium channels are integral membrane proteins that form ion channels selective for sodium ions (Na+). They are found in the membranes of excitable cells such as neurons, muscle cells and certain endocrine cells, where they are responsible for the rising phase of action potentials.

Structure

The channel is formed by a large alpha subunit of about 260 kDa, which contains four homologous domains (I–IV), each with six transmembrane segments (S1–S6). The S4 segments act as voltage sensors, moving outward when the membrane depolarizes. The alpha subunit may associate with one or more smaller beta subunits that modulate gating and cell adhesion.

Function

When the membrane potential rises past a threshold, the channel's activation gate opens and Na+ rushes into the cell, driving rapid depolarization. Within a millisecond, an inactivation gate — the "ball and chain" formed by an intracellular loop between domains III and IV — blocks the pore, terminating the sodium current and helping to set the refractory period.

Subtypes and disease

Nine alpha-subunit isoforms (Nav1.1–Nav1.9) are encoded by the SCN gene family. Mutations in these genes cause channelopathies including certain epilepsies (such as Dravet syndrome, from SCN1A mutations), periodic paralyses, myotonias, long QT syndrome type 3 (SCN5A, the cardiac isoform), and inherited pain disorders such as erythromelalgia (SCN9A).

Pharmacology

The channels are blocked by the pufferfish toxin tetrodotoxin (TTX) and saxitoxin. Local anesthetics, many anticonvulsants (such as carbamazepine and lamotrigine), and class I antiarrhythmic drugs act by blocking voltage-gated sodium channels in a use-dependent manner.
