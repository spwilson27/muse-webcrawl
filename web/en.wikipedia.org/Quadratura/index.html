Quadratura

Quadratura is an Italian term for a genre of illusionistic ceiling painting that was highly developed in the Baroque period, in which architectural elements are painted on a flat or barrel-vaulted ceiling in perspective so as to appear to continue the real architecture of the room into an imaginary space above.

History

The technique grew out of Renaissance experiments with perspective, including Andrea Mantegna's Camera dei Sposi in Mantua and Melozzo da Forlì's work in the Vatican. It reached its zenith in the 17th century with artists such as Andrea Pozzo, whose ceiling fresco in the church of Sant'Ignazio in Rome (1691–94) is the genre's most celebrated example.

Technique

Unlike simple trompe-l'œil, quadratura subordinates the figures to the painted architecture, which is drawn according to a single vanishing point so that the illusion holds from a privileged viewing position, often marked on the floor. Painted columns, arches and cornices seem to extend the building's real structure toward the heavens.
