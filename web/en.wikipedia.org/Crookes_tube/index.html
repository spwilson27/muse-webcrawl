Crookes tube

A Crookes tube (also called a Crookes–Hittorf tube) is an early experimental electrical discharge tube, with partial vacuum, invented by English physicist William Crookes and others around 1869–1875. In it, a high voltage applied between two electrodes causes the residual gas to glow and produces a stream of cathode rays. Experiments with Crookes tubes were central to late 19th-century physics: they led to J. J. Thomson's discovery of the electron in 1897 and, in related work by Wilhelm Röntgen, the discovery of X-rays in 1895.

Description and operation

The tube is a glass bulb from which most of the air has been evacuated, containing a cathode and an anode. When a voltage of several kilovolts is applied from an induction coil, the remaining gas ionises and a beam travels from the cathode in straight lines, casting sharp shadows of objects inside the tube — Crookes demonstrated this with a small metal Maltese cross. Famous variants include the paddlewheel tube, in which the beam appears to push a small vane, and tubes with fluorescent screens showing where the beam strikes.

Significance

Crookes tubes were the first particle accelerators and the ancestors of the cathode-ray tube later used in televisions and oscilloscopes. Thomson's measurements of the charge-to-mass ratio of cathode rays showed they consisted of particles far lighter than any atom — the first subatomic particles identified.
