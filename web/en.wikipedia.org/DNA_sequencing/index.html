DNA sequencing

DNA sequencing is the process of determining the nucleic acid sequence – the order of nucleotides in DNA. It includes any method or technology that is used to determine the order of the four bases: adenine, guanine, cytosine, and thymine. The advent of rapid DNA sequencing methods has greatly accelerated biological and medical research and discovery.

Knowledge of DNA sequences has become indispensable for basic biological research, DNA profiling, and in numerous applied fields such as medical diagnosis, biotechnology, forensic biology, virology and biological systematics. Modern DNA sequencing technology, the product of rapid advances since the 1970s, enabled the sequencing of complete genomes, including the Human Genome Project's completion in 2003.

Early methods

Early DNA sequencing methods were developed in the mid-1970s. In 1975, Frederick Sanger and colleagues introduced the dideoxy chain-termination method, while Walter Gilbert and Allan Maxam developed a chemical cleavage method in 1976–1977. Sanger's method became dominant because of its speed and potential for automation, and Sanger shared the 1980 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the work.

Sanger sequencing

The chain-termination method uses dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs) that lack the 3'-OH group needed for chain elongation. When incorporated, they terminate DNA synthesis at specific bases. The resulting fragments are separated by size using capillary electrophoresis, and fluorescent labels on each ddNTP allow automated detection of the sequence. Sanger sequencing produces read lengths of roughly 800–1,000 bases with very high accuracy and remains a gold standard for validating sequences.

High-throughput sequencing

Beginning in the mid-2000s, "next-generation" sequencing technologies massively parallelized sequencing, dramatically lowering cost. Major approaches include:

  • Sequencing by synthesis (e.g., Illumina), which images fluorescent reversible terminators added one base at a time to clusters of amplified fragments.
  • Pyrosequencing, which detects light released when pyrophosphate is liberated during nucleotide incorporation.
  • Ion semiconductor sequencing, which detects hydrogen ions released during polymerization.
  • Single-molecule real-time (SMRT) sequencing (PacBio), which observes a polymerase in a zero-mode waveguide and produces very long reads.
  • Nanopore sequencing (Oxford Nanopore), which passes DNA through a protein pore and reads changes in electrical current, enabling portable devices and ultra-long reads.

Applications

Sequencing is used to assemble whole genomes (de novo or by resequencing against a reference), to sequence transcriptomes (RNA-seq), to identify genetic variants associated with disease, to profile microbial communities (metagenomics), to detect pathogens, and in ancient DNA studies. Whole-exome and whole-genome sequencing are now routine clinical tools for diagnosing rare genetic disorders.

Genome projects

The Human Genome Project, completed in 2003, took 13 years and roughly US$3 billion using largely Sanger-based methods. The cost of sequencing a human genome fell to around US$1,000 by the mid-2010s and has continued to decline, following a curve far steeper than Moore's law for a period.
