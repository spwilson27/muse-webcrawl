Glorious Revolution

William of Orange landed at Torbay on 5 November 1688.

The Glorious Revolution, also known as the Revolution of 1688, was the deposition of King James II of England and his replacement by his daughter Mary II and her husband, the Dutch stadtholder William III of Orange.

Background

James II's Catholicism and his policies of religious toleration by royal prerogative alarmed the Protestant political establishment, who feared a Catholic absolutist monarchy allied to France. The birth of a Catholic heir, James Francis Edward, in June 1688, displacing the Protestant Mary in the succession, prompted a group of leading politicians — the "Immortal Seven" — to invite William to invade.

Events

William landed at Torbay on 5 November 1688 with a large Dutch fleet and army. Support for James collapsed as officers and notables defected; James fled to France in December. In 1689 a Convention Parliament declared the throne vacant and offered the crown jointly to William and Mary, who accepted a Declaration of Rights — enacted as the Bill of Rights 1689 — limiting royal power and affirming parliamentary supremacy.

Aftermath

Jacobite resistance continued in Scotland (defeated at Killiecrankie and Dunkeld) and Ireland, where William's victory at the Battle of the Boyne in 1690 secured the settlement. The revolution established the principle that monarchs ruled with Parliament's consent and helped shape the modern British constitutional monarchy; its relative lack of bloodshed in England gave it its "glorious" name.
