Free and open-source software

Logos of prominent FOSS projects

Free and open-source software (FOSS) is software that is available under a license granting users the right to use, study, change, and distribute the software and its source code to anyone and for any purpose. The term combines the perspectives of the free software movement and the open-source movement, which overlap heavily in practice but differ in philosophy.

Definitions

The Free Software Foundation's Free Software Definition emphasizes four essential freedoms: to run the program, to study and change it, to redistribute copies, and to distribute modified versions. The Open Source Initiative's Open Source Definition sets out criteria focusing on the practical benefits of open development models, such as peer review and transparency.

Licenses

FOSS licenses fall roughly into two families. Copyleft licenses, such as the GNU General Public License (GPL), require derivative works to be distributed under the same terms. Permissive licenses, such as the MIT, BSD, and Apache licenses, allow reuse in proprietary software with few conditions.

History

The movement traces its roots to the hacker culture of 1970s computer labs and to Richard Stallman's launch of the GNU Project in 1983 and the Free Software Foundation in 1985. The term "open source" was coined in 1998, the same era in which the Linux kernel, Apache web server, and later Firefox, LibreOffice, and Android demonstrated FOSS's commercial and technical viability.

Impact

FOSS now underpins most of the internet's infrastructure, the majority of supercomputers and smartphones, and large parts of cloud computing. Companies participate by releasing code, funding foundations, and building services atop open-source platforms, while debates continue over sustainability, maintainer funding, and "openwashing."
