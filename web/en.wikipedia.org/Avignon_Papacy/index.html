Avignon Papacy

The Avignon Papacy was the period from 1309 to 1376 during which seven successive popes resided in Avignon, in present-day France, rather than in Rome. Following a conflict between the papacy and the French crown, Clement V — a Frenchman — refused to move to Rome and in 1309 settled the papal court at Avignon, then a papal enclave within the Kingdom of Arles. Petrarch called the period the "Babylonian Captivity" of the Church.

The Avignon popes

The seven popes — Clement V, John XXII, Benedict XII, Clement VI, Innocent VI, Urban V and Gregory XI — were all French, as were most cardinals they created. The court became a byword for administrative sophistication and luxury: the popes built the vast Palais des Papes, centralised Church finance and law, and ran an efficient bureaucracy that also attracted charges of nepotism and simony. Clement VI bought the city of Avignon outright from Joanna I of Naples in 1348.

Return to Rome and schism

Pressured by figures including Catherine of Siena, Gregory XI returned the court to Rome in January 1377 and died the following year. His successor, the Italian Urban VI, alienated the French cardinals, who declared his election invalid and chose Clement VII, who returned to Avignon — beginning the Western Schism (1378–1417), during which rival popes in Rome and Avignon excommunicated each other. The schism was healed at the Council of Constance; Avignon's later antipopes submitted or were deposed, and the city remained papal territory until annexed by France in 1791.
