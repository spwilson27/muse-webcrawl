Mortality rate

Mortality rate, or death rate, is a measure of the number of deaths (in general, or due to a specific cause) in a particular population, scaled to the size of that population, per unit of time. It is typically expressed in units of deaths per 1,000 individuals per year. The crude death rate for the whole world is roughly 7–8 per 1,000 per year, though it varies widely by country and age structure.

Types

  • Crude death rate — total deaths per 1,000 mid-year population.
  • Age-specific mortality rate — deaths within a defined age group, used to build life tables.
  • Infant mortality rate — deaths of children under one year per 1,000 live births; a widely used indicator of health and development.
  • Maternal mortality ratio — maternal deaths per 100,000 live births.
  • Case fatality rate — proportion of diagnosed cases of a disease that end in death, important in epidemiology.
  • Standardised mortality rate — adjusted to a reference population so places with different age structures can be compared fairly.

Interpretation

Because older populations naturally die at higher rates, countries with many elderly residents can show high crude rates despite excellent health care; age standardisation is therefore essential for comparisons. Mortality rates are central to demography, public health, actuarial science and life expectancy calculations.
