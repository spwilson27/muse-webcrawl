Industrial melanism

Industrial melanism is an evolutionary effect prominent in several arthropods, where dark pigmentation (melanism) has evolved in an environment affected by industrial pollution. The classic example is the peppered moth (Biston betularia) in England: the pale typica form, camouflaged against lichened tree bark, was largely replaced during the nineteenth century by the black carbonaria form in polluted industrial regions, where soot had darkened surfaces and killed lichens.

Mechanism

Dark moths resting on soot-blackened bark were better camouflaged from bird predators, while pale moths became conspicuous; in clean countryside the reverse held. Bernard Kettlewell's mark-release-recapture experiments in the 1950s demonstrated differential bird predation as the selective agent. After clean-air legislation reduced soot from the 1960s onward, the frequency of carbonaria declined dramatically — a reversal observed in both Britain and North America. In 2016 the genetic basis was identified as a mutation in the cortex gene, dated to around 1819.

Significance

Industrial melanism became one of the most widely taught examples of natural selection in action, showing rapid evolution in response to a human-made environmental change. Kettlewell's experimental methods were later debated and refined by subsequent researchers, whose further experiments substantially upheld the predation hypothesis.
