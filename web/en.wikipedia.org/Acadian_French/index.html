Acadian French

Acadian French (French: français acadien) is a variety of French spoken by the Acadians, principally in Canada's Maritime Provinces (New Brunswick, Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island), as well as in parts of Quebec, the French island of Saint Pierre and Miquelon, and the US state of Maine. New Brunswick is officially bilingual, and Acadians constitute roughly a third of its population.

History

Acadian French descends from the dialects of west-central France, especially Poitou, Aunis and Saintonge, brought by colonists who settled Port-Royal from 1605 onward. Isolated after the British conquest and the deportation of 1755–1764 (the Grand Dérangement), the variety preserved many 17th-century features lost in standard French, while also absorbing words from English and from Indigenous languages such as Mi'kmaq.

Features

Characteristic features include the preservation of older vowel pronunciations, distinctive verb forms such as the third-person plural in -ont ("ils parlont"), the pronoun je used with plural verb endings in some areas ("je parlons"), and a rich maritime vocabulary. The mixed French-English code called Chiac, spoken around Moncton, blends Acadian French syntax with heavy English borrowing and is a well-known local identity marker.

Status

Acadian French has a growing literary and media presence, including the newspaper L'Acadie Nouvelle, Radio-Canada Acadie broadcasts, and the Université de Moncton, the largest French-language university in Canada outside Quebec. Louisiana Cajun French shares common roots through the deported Acadians who settled there.
