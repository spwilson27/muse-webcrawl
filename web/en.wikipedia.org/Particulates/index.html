Particulates

Particulates or atmospheric particulate matter (also called particulate pollution or particle pollution) are microscopic particles of solid or liquid matter suspended in the air. The term aerosol commonly refers to the particulate/air mixture, as opposed to the particulate matter alone. Sources of particulate matter can be natural or anthropogenic. They have impacts on climate and precipitation that adversely affect human health, in ways additional to direct inhalation.

Types of atmospheric particles include suspended particulate matter (SPM), thoracic and respirable particles, inhalable coarse particles, designated PM10 (diameter 10 micrometres or less), fine particles designated PM2.5 (diameter 2.5 micrometres or less), ultrafine particles, and soot.

Sources

Natural sources include dust carried by wind from deserts, sea salt spray, volcanic ash, and pollen. Human activities such as the burning of fossil fuels in vehicles and power plants, road dust, agriculture, and industrial processes produce the bulk of particulate pollution in cities. Indoor sources include cooking, smoking, and wood-burning stoves.

Health effects

The IARC and WHO designate airborne particulates as a Group 1 carcinogen. Particulates are the deadliest form of air pollution due to their ability to penetrate deep into the lungs, blood streams and brain unfiltered, causing health problems including heart attacks, respiratory disease, and premature death. Fine particles (PM2.5) are small enough to pass from the lungs into the bloodstream, while ultrafine particles may reach the brain via the olfactory nerve.

Regulation

Governments regulate particulate levels through ambient air quality standards. The World Health Organization's 2021 guidelines recommend an annual mean PM2.5 concentration of no more than 5 μg/m³. Many countries set less stringent limits, and exceedances remain common in rapidly industrialising regions.
