Nucleic acid hybridization

In molecular biology, nucleic acid hybridization is a phenomenon in which single-stranded DNA or RNA molecules anneal to complementary DNA or RNA. Complementary base pairing, following the Watson–Crick rules, drives the formation of a double-stranded hybrid molecule. Hybridization is a fundamental property of nucleic acids and underpins many laboratory techniques.

Principle

Double-stranded nucleic acids can be denatured (melted) by heat or alkali into single strands. On cooling or neutralization, strands with complementary sequences re-anneal. The stringency of hybridization — determined by temperature and salt concentration — controls how closely sequences must match for a stable hybrid to form.

Applications

Hybridization is the basis of Southern blotting (DNA detection), Northern blotting (RNA detection), fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH), DNA microarrays, and the annealing step of the polymerase chain reaction, in which short primers hybridize to target sequences. Hybridization assays are widely used in diagnostics, genomics, and evolutionary studies comparing relatedness between species.
