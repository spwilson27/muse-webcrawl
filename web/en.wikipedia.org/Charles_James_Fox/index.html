Charles James Fox, the dominant Whig parliamentarian of his era.

Charles James Fox (24 January 1749 – 13 September 1806) was a British Whig politician and statesman, one of the most celebrated parliamentary orators of the 18th century. Over a career of nearly four decades he served three times, briefly, as Foreign Secretary, but spent most of his life in opposition — championing civil liberties, opposing the war against the American colonies, and defending the principles of the French Revolution against Edmund Burke.

Early career

Son of Henry Fox, the formidable Paymaster General, Fox entered Parliament at 19 and dazzled the Commons with his wit and debating power. He broke with Lord North's government, denounced the coercion of the American colonies, and in 1782 became the first Foreign Secretary in Lord Rockingham's ministry.

Coalition and conflict with Pitt

In 1783 Fox formed the controversial Fox–North coalition; its India Bill was defeated in the Lords after George III intervened, and the King installed the 24-year-old William Pitt the Younger, whose long dominance began. Fox led the opposition through the Regency Crisis of 1788–89, the impeachment of Warren Hastings (which he prosecuted with Burke and Sheridan), and the polarising 1790s, when his sympathy for revolutionary France split the Whigs and drove Burke to denounce him. Fox's Libel Act of 1792 strengthened press freedom by restoring to juries the verdict on libel.

Final years

After Pitt's death in 1806, Fox finally returned to office as Foreign Secretary in the "Ministry of All the Talents." He opened peace contacts with France and pushed through the abolition of the slave trade in the Commons (the act passed shortly after his death in 1806 — the Slave Trade Act received royal assent in 1807). Fox died in September 1806 and was buried in Westminster Abbey near Pitt, his lifelong rival.

Legacy

Fox's enormous private debts and gambling were notorious, but so were his warmth, loyalty, and devotion to liberty; he remains a symbol of principled opposition and is honoured as a founder of the liberal Whig tradition. The Foxite Whigs carried his causes — Catholic emancipation, parliamentary reform, abolition — into the 19th century.
