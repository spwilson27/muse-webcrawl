André-Marie Ampère

André-Marie Ampère (20 January 1775 – 10 June 1836) was a French physicist and mathematician who founded the science of electrodynamics — now known as electromagnetism. The SI unit of electric current, the ampere, is named after him, and he is one of the 72 names inscribed on the Eiffel Tower.

Electrodynamics

In September 1820, days after hearing of Hans Christian Ørsted's discovery that an electric current deflects a compass needle, Ampère presented his first results on the forces between current-carrying wires: parallel currents attract, opposite currents repel. He developed a complete mathematical theory of the magnetic effects of currents, summarised in Ampère's circuital law, and proposed that magnetism itself arises from tiny circulating currents within matter — an early anticipation of the electron. He also invented the astatic needle and improved the galvanometer.

Life

Largely self-educated in his father's library in Lyon, Ampère taught mathematics and held chairs at the École Polytechnique and the Collège de France. His personal life was marked by tragedy — his father was guillotined during the French Revolution — and by lifelong struggles with health and finances.
