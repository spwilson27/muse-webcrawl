Operation Citadel (German: Unternehmen Zitadelle) was the German offensive operation in July 1943 against the Soviet salient around Kursk on the Eastern Front of the Second World War. It opened the Battle of Kursk, widely regarded as the largest tank battle in history. The plan—championed by Field Marshal Erich von Manstein and ordered by Adolf Hitler—was to pinch off the salient with converging attacks from the north (under Walter Model) and south (under Hermann Hoth), destroying the Soviet forces within and shortening the German front.

Planning and delays

Originally scheduled for May 1943, the offensive was repeatedly postponed, partly so that new Panther and Tiger tanks and Ferdinand tank destroyers could reach the front. The delays gave the Red Army, which had learned of the plan through intelligence, months to construct deep defensive belts of minefields, anti-tank strongpoints, and trenches—up to 300 kilometres deep in places—and to position the powerful Steppe Front in reserve.

The battle

The offensive began on 5 July 1943. In the north, Model's Ninth Army advanced only about 15 kilometres before being stopped. In the south, Hoth's Fourth Panzer Army and Army Detachment Kempf drove deeper, culminating on 12 July in the huge armoured clash around Prokhorovka against the 5th Guards Tank Army. Despite heavy tank losses on both sides, the German advance stalled.

Termination and aftermath

On 13 July, Hitler summoned Manstein and Günther von Kluge and called off Citadel, citing the Allied landings in Sicily and the need to transfer forces to Italy, as well as the Soviet counteroffensives (Operations Kutuzov and Rumyantsev) that had struck the German flanks. German losses—roughly 50,000 casualties and several hundred armoured vehicles—were far lighter than Soviet ones, but Germany permanently lost the strategic initiative on the Eastern Front. Never again could the Wehrmacht mount a major summer offensive in the east.
