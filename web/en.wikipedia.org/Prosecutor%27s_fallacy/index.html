Prosecutor's fallacy

The prosecutor's fallacy is a fallacy of statistical reasoning involving a form of the base rate fallacy: it arises when the probability of the evidence given innocence is confused with the probability of innocence given the evidence. It is named for a typical erroneous argument in court: for example, treating a one-in-a-million chance of a coincidental DNA match as a one-in-a-million chance that the defendant is innocent.

Formulation

Formally, the fallacy transposes the conditional: P(E | innocent) is equated with P(innocent | E). By Bayes' theorem these differ by the prior probability of guilt. In a population of millions, even an extremely rare match profile may be shared by several innocent people, so the probability of innocence given a match can be far higher than the random-match probability suggests.

Examples

The fallacy was famously discussed in cases involving sudden infant deaths, such as the trial of Sally Clark in the United Kingdom, where a squared probability was presented as if it were the probability of innocence, contributing to a conviction later overturned. Its mirror image, the defense attorney's fallacy, understates the evidence by emphasizing how many people might match while ignoring other incriminating facts.
