Jean-Antoine Watteau (1684–1721) was a French painter whose brief career revitalised the Baroque idiom and helped create the Rococo style. He is credited with inventing the fête galante — scenes of elegantly dressed figures in dreamy parkland settings — for which the Royal Academy created a special category when it admitted him in 1717.

Life and career

Born in Valenciennes, Watteau trained in Paris under Claude Gillot and Claude Audran III, absorbing influences from Rubens and the Comédie-Italienne theatre. His reception piece, The Pilgrimage to Cythera (1717), depicts lovers visiting the island of Venus. Chronically ill with tuberculosis, he died at 36, leaving works including Gersaint's Signboard, Mezzetin and numerous drawings prized for their spontaneity.

Style and legacy

Watteau's feathery brushwork, shimmering colour and melancholic ambiguity influenced French painting throughout the 18th century, from Boucher and Fragonard to later admirers such as the Impressionists.
