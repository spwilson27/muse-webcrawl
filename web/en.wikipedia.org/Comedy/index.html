Comedy

Comedy is a genre of fiction that consists of discourses or works intended to be humorous or amusing by inducing laughter, especially in theatre, film, stand-up comedy, television, radio, books, or any other entertainment medium. The term originated in ancient Greece: in Athenian democracy, the public opinion of voters was influenced by political satire performed by comic poets in theaters.

History

In ancient Greece, comedy is generally thought to have grown out of the comus, a revel associated with the god Dionysus. Aristotle, in his Poetics (c. 335 BC), observed that comedy originated in phallic processions and argued that it depicts people worse than average, whereas tragedy depicts people better than average. The plays of Aristophanes represent the "Old Comedy" of political satire, while Menander exemplified the "New Comedy" of domestic situations, a model later adopted by the Roman playwrights Plautus and Terence.

During the Middle Ages, comic tales such as fabliaux flourished, and in the Renaissance the commedia dell'arte of Italy developed improvised comedy based on stock characters. William Shakespeare's comedies, including A Midsummer Night's Dream and Twelfth Night, typically end in marriage and reconciliation.

Types

Comedy takes many forms, including farce, satire, parody, slapstick, romantic comedy, black comedy, tragicomedy, and stand-up comedy. In modern media, situation comedies (sitcoms) became a dominant television format in the twentieth century.

Theories of humour

Philosophers and psychologists have proposed several explanations for laughter, including the superiority theory (associated with Plato and Hobbes), the relief theory (developed by Freud), and the incongruity theory, which holds that humour arises from the perception of something unexpected or mismatched.
