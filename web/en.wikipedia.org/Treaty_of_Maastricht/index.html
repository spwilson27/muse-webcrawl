The Treaty of Maastricht, formally the Treaty on European Union (TEU), was signed on 7 February 1992 in Maastricht, Netherlands, by the twelve member states of the European Communities and entered into force on 1 November 1993. It formally created the European Union, introduced European citizenship, and set the path toward the single currency, the euro.

Structure and content

The treaty organised the Union into three "pillars": the European Communities (the existing community method, extended to new areas), a Common Foreign and Security Policy, and cooperation in Justice and Home Affairs — the latter two operating mainly intergovernmentally. It created citizenship of the Union, giving nationals of member states rights including free movement, consular protection by other member states, and the right to vote in local and European Parliament elections in their state of residence. The treaty also strengthened the European Parliament by introducing the co-decision procedure, enshrined the principle of subsidiarity, and expanded Community competence in education, culture, public health, consumer protection, trans-European networks and cohesion.

Economic and monetary union

The treaty's most consequential part was the timetable for Economic and Monetary Union (EMU): stage one liberalised capital movements, stage two created the European Monetary Institute, and stage three — beginning on 1 January 1999 — would fix exchange rates and introduce the single currency under an independent European Central Bank. Participation required meeting the Maastricht convergence criteria on inflation, interest rates, exchange-rate stability, government deficit (below 3% of GDP) and public debt (below 60% of GDP, or approaching it). The United Kingdom and Denmark negotiated opt-outs from the single currency. The euro was launched electronically in 1999 and as notes and coins in 2002.

Ratification

Ratification proved turbulent and revealed a widening gap between political elites and voters. Danish voters rejected the treaty in a referendum in June 1992 (50.7% against), approving it only in May 1993 after the Edinburgh Agreement granted Denmark further opt-outs. In France the treaty passed the September 1992 referendum by a whisker — the famous petit oui of 51.05%. In Britain, John Major's government survived fraught parliamentary votes and a Eurosceptic rebellion, while in Germany the Federal Constitutional Court's Maastricht judgment of October 1993 upheld the treaty while setting limits on further integration. The treaty has since been amended by the treaties of Amsterdam, Nice and Lisbon, the last of which abolished the pillar structure.
