Pleiotropy

One gene, several effects: the essence of pleiotropy

Pleiotropy (from Greek pleio, "many", and tropos, "turn" or "way") occurs when one gene influences two or more seemingly unrelated phenotypic traits. Such a gene that exhibits multiple phenotypic expression is called a pleiotropic gene. Conversely, when a single trait is influenced by many genes, the phenomenon is called polygenic inheritance.

Pleiotropy arises because gene products — proteins such as enzymes, signalling molecules and transcription factors — are typically used in several different tissues, developmental stages or biochemical pathways. A mutation in such a gene therefore produces multiple effects throughout the body. The concept was first formalised by the German geneticist Ludwig Plate in 1910, and it was central to early debates about the genetic basis of evolution.

Examples

A classic example is phenylketonuria (PKU), a human disorder caused by mutation in the gene encoding the enzyme phenylalanine hydroxylase. The single defect leads to intellectual disability, eczema, reduced pigmentation of hair and skin, and a characteristic musty odour. Similarly, sickle cell disease results from one mutation in the beta-globin gene yet affects the shape of red blood cells, the spleen, and resistance to malaria. Marfan syndrome, caused by mutations in the fibrillin-1 gene, affects the skeleton, eyes and cardiovascular system simultaneously.

In plants and animals bred by humans, pleiotropy is familiar: the gene for white coat colour in some cat breeds is linked to blue eyes and deafness, and in chickens the frizzle gene affects feather structure, metabolic rate and egg production.

Antagonistic pleiotropy

George C. Williams proposed in 1957 that ageing can evolve because of antagonistic pleiotropy: a gene that confers advantages early in life (such as greater fertility or growth) may have harmful effects late in life, after selection has weakened. Because natural selection acts most strongly on early reproduction, such genes accumulate. Antagonistic pleiotropy remains a leading evolutionary explanation of senescence.

Significance

Pleiotropy constrains evolution: selection on one trait drags along correlated changes in others, which may be maladaptive. It also complicates genetics research and medicine, because genome-wide association studies frequently find that variants associated with one disease also influence unrelated conditions, and because a drug targeting one gene product may have wide-ranging side effects. Understanding pleiotropy is therefore important in quantitative genetics, evolutionary biology and pharmacology.
