Constructivism (philosophy of mathematics)

Constructivism (mathematics)
FieldPhilosophy of mathematics; logic
Key figuresL. E. J. Brouwer; Arend Heyting; Errett Bishop
Core ideaMathematical objects and proofs must be constructible
LogicIntuitionistic logic; rejects the law of excluded middle in general

Constructivism in the philosophy of mathematics is the view that it is necessary to construct — to explicitly find or exhibit — a mathematical object in order to prove that it exists. A proof that something exists merely by showing that its non-existence would be contradictory is not accepted as valid, because it does not provide a way to produce the object.

Intuitionism

The most famous constructivist programme is L. E. J. Brouwer's intuitionism, which grounded mathematics in the mental constructions of the mathematician and rejected completed infinities and non-constructive methods. Brouwer's follower Arend Heyting formalised intuitionistic logic, which drops the law of excluded middle (that every proposition is either true or false) as a general principle.

Constructive practice

Constructive mathematicians accept proof by contradiction only for negative statements, and require existential statements to be backed by witnesses. Many classical theorems must be reformulated: for instance, the intermediate value theorem in its classical form is not constructively provable without additional hypotheses. Errett Bishop's Foundations of Constructive Analysis (1967) showed that a large part of modern analysis can be rebuilt constructively, giving results extra computational content.

Relation to computing

Constructive mathematics has deep connections with computer science: through the Curry–Howard correspondence, a constructive proof of a statement corresponds to a program computing the promised object, so algorithms can be extracted from proofs. Variants of constructivism include finitism, Bishop-style constructivism, and the Russian school of recursive mathematics.
