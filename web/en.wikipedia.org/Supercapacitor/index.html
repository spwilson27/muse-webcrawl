Supercapacitor

A supercapacitor (SC), also called an ultracapacitor, is a high-capacity capacitor with a capacitance value much higher than solid-state capacitors but with lower voltage limits. It bridges the gap between electrolytic capacitors and rechargeable batteries, typically storing 10 to 100 times more energy per unit volume or mass than electrolytic capacitors.

Principles

Supercapacitors store energy by two mechanisms: electrostatic double-layer capacitance at the electrode–electrolyte interface, and pseudocapacitance from fast, reversible redox reactions. Electrodes are usually made of porous activated carbon, graphene, or carbon nanotubes, giving enormous surface area.

Characteristics and applications

Compared with batteries, supercapacitors charge and discharge far faster, tolerate many more cycles (often over a million), and work across a wide temperature range, but have lower energy density. Applications include regenerative braking in vehicles, backup and ride-through power, pitch control in wind turbines, and memory backup in electronics.

History

The double-layer mechanism was described by Helmholtz in the 19th century; the first practical device was patented by General Electric's H. I. Becker in 1957, and commercial products were developed by Standard Oil and NEC in the following decades.
