Dutch Water Line

The Dutch Water Line (Dutch: Hollandsche Waterlinie) was a series of water-based defences conceived by Maurice of Nassau in the early 17th century, and realised by his half brother Frederick Henry. Combined with natural bodies of water, the Water Line could be used to transform Holland, the westernmost region of the Netherlands, almost into an island. In the 19th century, the Line was extended to include Utrecht.

Old Dutch Waterline

Early in the Eighty Years' War against Spain, the Dutch realised that low-lying land could be flooded to form an impassable obstacle to an invading army, too deep for infantry to wade and too shallow for boats. The Old Waterline was first used in 1629 and famously held back the French army of Louis XIV in 1672, the Rampjaar (Disaster Year), saving Holland from conquest.

New Dutch Waterline

The New Dutch Waterline, constructed from 1815 onwards, ran from the Zuiderzee at Muiden to the Biesbosch near Gorinchem, protecting Utrecht as well as Amsterdam and the western provinces. It consisted of a chain of forts, inundation basins, sluices and canals. The line was mobilised in 1870, 1914 and 1939, but was rendered obsolete by air power and was never breached in battle.

Heritage

The New Dutch Waterline was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2021 as an extension of the Defence Line of Amsterdam site (inscribed 1996). Together they are known as the Dutch Water Defence Lines.
