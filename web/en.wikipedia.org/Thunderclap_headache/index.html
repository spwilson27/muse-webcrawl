Thunderclap headache

A thunderclap headache is a severe headache that reaches maximum intensity within seconds to a minute of onset — often described by patients as "the worst headache of my life" and likened to a clap of thunder. It is regarded as a medical emergency because its most dangerous cause, aneurysmal subarachnoid haemorrhage, is rapidly fatal if untreated. Any first or worst-ever thunderclap headache requires urgent evaluation, typically with non-contrast CT of the head, followed if necessary by lumbar puncture or vascular imaging.

Causes

  • Subarachnoid haemorrhage from a ruptured cerebral aneurysm — the classic and most concerning cause.
  • Reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome (RCVS) — recurrent thunderclap headaches over days to weeks, sometimes triggered by vasoactive drugs, exertion, or the postpartum state.
  • Cervical artery dissection (carotid or vertebral).
  • Cerebral venous sinus thrombosis.
  • Pituitary apoplexy — haemorrhage into a pituitary tumour.
  • Meningitis, spontaneous intracranial hypotension, hypertensive crisis, and colloid cyst of the third ventricle.
  • Primary thunderclap headache — a diagnosis of exclusion when no cause is found.

Evaluation and management

Modern CT performed within six hours of onset is highly sensitive for subarachnoid blood; a negative scan outside this window, or persisting suspicion, prompts lumbar puncture (looking for blood or xanthochromia) and CT angiography to locate an aneurysm or vasoconstriction. Treatment is directed at the underlying cause: ruptured aneurysms are secured by endovascular coiling or neurosurgical clipping, RCVS is managed by removing triggers and sometimes calcium-channel blockers, and analgesia and monitoring are provided in a high-dependency setting. The term "thunderclap headache" was popularised in the 1980s by neurologists including David Dodick and C. Miller Fisher's earlier description of the sentinel headache.
