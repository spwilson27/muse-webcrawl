Medina quarter

A medina (from Arabic madīna, "city") is a distinct historical city section found in a number of North African cities, and in some parts of Spain and Malta. A medina is typically walled, contains many narrow and maze-like streets, and was built by Arabs as early as the 9th century CE. Medinas form the old town of cities such as Fez, Marrakesh, Tunis, Algiers and Tripoli, in contrast to the newer quarters (villes nouvelles) built under colonial rule.

Characteristics

The medina is organised around a congregational mosque, with souks (markets) arranged by trade radiating from it — precious goods nearest the mosque, noisier and dirtier trades towards the periphery. Residential quarters consist of inward-looking courtyard houses along cul-de-sacs, providing privacy and shade. Public amenities include hammams (bathhouses), madrasas, fondouks (caravanserais) and communal ovens and fountains. The dense fabric, narrow lanes and thick walls moderate the hot climate.

Preservation

Many medinas are UNESCO World Heritage Sites, including Fez el-Bali (listed 1981), widely considered the best-preserved old town of the Arab world, and the medinas of Tunis, Marrakesh, Essaouira and the Casbah of Algiers. Conservation faces pressures from population density, building decay and tourism. The word survives in place names such as Mdina in Malta and Medina Sidonia in Spain.
