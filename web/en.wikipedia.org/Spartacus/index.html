Spartacus (c. 103–71 BC) was a Thracian gladiator who became the most famous leader of the Third Servile War, a major slave uprising against the Roman Republic. Escaping with about 70 fellow gladiators from a training school at Capua in 73 BC, he built an army of escaped slaves that may have reached 100,000, defeated several Roman forces, and ranged the length of Italy for two years before Marcus Licinius Crassus crushed the revolt in 71 BC.

The revolt

The escapees, armed initially with kitchen implements, seized weapons and took refuge on Mount Vesuvius, defeating the militia sent against them by sliding down the crater's slopes. Victories over praetorian and then consular armies drew thousands of rural slaves to Spartacus, who organised them with lieutenants including Crixus and Oenomaus. Roman sources (Appian, Plutarch) disagree about his goal — escape over the Alps, plunder, or march on Rome — and about his character, though even hostile accounts credit his intelligence and fairness in dividing spoils.

Defeat

The Senate gave Crassus an extraordinary command with six new legions, reviving decimation to restore discipline. Spartacus was trapped in Bruttium, failed to cross to Sicily, and was defeated and killed in a final battle near the Silarus River in 71 BC; his body was never identified. Some 6,000 captured rebels were crucified along the Appian Way from Capua to Rome. Pompey, returning from Spain, destroyed a fleeing remnant and claimed credit for ending the war.

Legacy

Spartacus became a symbol of resistance to oppression: praised by Marx, his name was taken by the German Spartacus League of Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht, and his story reached a mass audience through Howard Fast's novel and Stanley Kubrick's 1960 film Spartacus, famous for the "I am Spartacus" scene.
