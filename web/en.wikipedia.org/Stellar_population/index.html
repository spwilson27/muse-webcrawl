Stellar population

Globular clusters contain some of the oldest, most metal-poor (Population II) stars.

A stellar population is a group of stars with similar ages, chemical compositions and distributions within a galaxy. The classification was introduced by Walter Baade in 1944, who divided the stars of the Milky Way into Population I and Population II; a hypothetical, still more ancient Population III was later added for the first stars in the universe.

Population I

Population I stars are young and metal-rich — in astronomical usage, "metals" means all elements heavier than helium. They are found in the spiral arms and galactic disc, where continuing star formation enriches the interstellar medium. The Sun, about 4.6 billion years old with a metallicity of roughly 1.4 per cent by mass, is a Population I star.

Population II

Population II stars are old and metal-poor, formed early in a galaxy's history from gas containing few heavy elements. They dominate globular clusters and galactic halos and bulges, following elongated orbits. Their low metal content records the chemical state of the early universe.

Population III

Population III stars are a hypothesised first generation that formed from primordial hydrogen and helium with essentially no metals. Thought to have been very massive and short-lived, they would have produced the first heavy elements through their deaths as supernovae. No Population III star has yet been directly observed; evidence is sought in the spectra of extremely metal-poor stars and in the most distant galaxies observed by telescopes such as the James Webb Space Telescope.
