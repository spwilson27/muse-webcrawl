The ampere (symbol: A), often shortened to "amp", is the unit of electric current in the International System of Units (SI). It is named after the French physicist André-Marie Ampère (1775–1836), one of the founders of the science of classical electromagnetism.

Definition

Since the 2019 revision of the SI, the ampere is defined by taking the fixed numerical value of the elementary charge e to be 1.602176634×10−19 coulombs when expressed in the unit C, which is equal to A·s. Effectively, one ampere is the current produced by the flow of one coulomb of charge per second, or approximately 6.241509×1018 elementary charges per second.

History

Before 2019, the ampere was defined as the constant current which, if maintained in two straight parallel conductors of infinite length placed one metre apart in vacuum, would produce a force of 2×10−7 newtons per metre of length between them. That definition proved difficult to realise precisely, prompting the change to a definition based on the elementary charge.

Examples

A typical USB phone charger supplies 1–3 A; a household circuit breaker is commonly rated at 15 or 20 A; a lightning discharge can carry tens of thousands of amperes for a fraction of a second.
