Cubital tunnel syndrome

Cubital tunnel syndrome

Cubital tunnel syndrome is a peripheral nerve entrapment syndrome caused by compression, traction or friction of the ulnar nerve as it passes through the cubital tunnel behind the medial epicondyle of the humerus at the elbow — the region popularly known as the "funny bone". It is the second most common entrapment neuropathy of the upper limb after carpal tunnel syndrome.

Signs and symptoms

Early symptoms are intermittent numbness and tingling ("pins and needles") in the little finger and the ulnar half of the ring finger, often provoked by prolonged elbow flexion — for example during sleep or holding a telephone — or by leaning on the elbow. As the condition progresses there may be aching at the inner elbow, clumsiness and weakness of grip and pinch, wasting of the intrinsic hand muscles, loss of finger dexterity, and in severe cases clawing of the ring and little fingers (an "ulnar claw").

Causes and diagnosis

Risk factors include prolonged or repeated elbow flexion, direct pressure on the nerve, previous elbow fracture or dislocation, arthritis with bone spurs, ganglia or other space-occupying lesions, and the nerve subluxing (snapping) over the medial epicondyle. Diagnosis is clinical, supported by provocative tests (elbow flexion test, Tinel's sign at the elbow) and confirmed or graded by nerve conduction studies and electromyography; ultrasound can show nerve enlargement.

Treatment

Mild cases are managed conservatively: avoiding prolonged flexion and pressure, padding the elbow, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and night splints holding the elbow in partial extension. Surgery is indicated for persistent symptoms or muscle weakness, and options include in situ decompression (release of the compressing tissues), medial epicondylectomy, and anterior transposition of the nerve; no single technique has been shown clearly superior for most cases.
