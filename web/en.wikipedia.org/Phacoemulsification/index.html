Phacoemulsification

Phacoemulsification is a modern cataract surgery method in which the eye's internal lens is emulsified with an ultrasonic handpiece and aspirated from the eye. The aspirated fluids are replaced with irrigation of balanced salt solution to maintain the anterior chamber. In most cases a foldable intraocular lens (IOL) is then implanted in the emptied capsular bag. It is the dominant technique for cataract removal in the developed world.

History

The technique was developed by the American ophthalmologist Charles Kelman, who was inspired in 1967 by an ultrasonic dental device used to clean teeth. After years of experimentation, Kelman and Anton Banko produced the first phacoemulsifier (marketed with Cavitron in the early 1970s). Early procedures were lengthy and complication rates high, but improvements in machines, viscoelastics, wound construction and foldable IOLs made phacoemulsification the standard of care by the 1990s. Femtosecond-laser–assisted cataract surgery is a later adjunct.

Procedure

Surgery is usually performed under topical or local anaesthesia on an outpatient basis. After a small clear-corneal or scleral incision (typically 2–3 mm), the surgeon performs a continuous curvilinear capsulorhexis to open the front of the lens capsule, hydrodissects the lens, and introduces the phaco tip. Ultrasound energy fragments the nucleus — by techniques such as "divide and conquer" or "phaco chop" — while irrigation–aspiration removes the fragments and cortical material. A foldable IOL is inserted into the intact capsular bag, and the self-sealing wound is usually left sutureless. The operation commonly takes under half an hour.

Advantages and complications

Compared with older extracapsular extraction, phacoemulsification offers a smaller incision, faster visual rehabilitation, less surgically induced astigmatism and quicker return to normal activities. Complications include posterior capsule rupture with vitreous loss, dropped nucleus, corneal endothelial damage and oedema, postoperative infection (endophthalmitis, rare but serious), cystoid macular oedema, and later posterior capsule opacification, which is treated with Nd:YAG laser capsulotomy.
