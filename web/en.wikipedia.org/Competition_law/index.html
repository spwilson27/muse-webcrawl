Competition law

Competition law is the field of law that promotes or seeks to maintain market competition by regulating anti-competitive conduct by companies. Competition law is implemented through public and private enforcement. It is also known as antitrust law in the United States (reflecting its origin in the law of trusts), anti-monopoly law in China and Russia, and in previous years was known as trade practices law in the United Kingdom and Australia.

History

The history of competition law reaches back to the Roman Empire, where laws against price fixing and monopolies existed, and to medieval guild restrictions. Modern competition law begins with the Canadian Combines Investigation Act of 1889 and the United States Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890, which outlawed cartels and monopolisation. The Clayton Act (1914) and the Federal Trade Commission Act followed in the US. In Europe, Articles 101 and 102 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union are the primary competition rules, enforced by the European Commission and national authorities.

Core areas

Modern competition law usually addresses:

  • Cartels and collusive agreements — agreements between competitors to fix prices, limit output, share markets, or rig bids (Article 101 TFEU; Section 1 of the Sherman Act);
  • Abuse of a dominant position — exclusionary or exploitative conduct by firms with substantial market power, such as predatory pricing, tying, or refusal to supply (Article 102 TFEU; Section 2 of the Sherman Act on monopolisation);
  • Merger control — review and possible prohibition of mergers and acquisitions that would substantially lessen competition.

Enforcement

Agencies such as the US Federal Trade Commission and Department of Justice Antitrust Division, the European Commission's Directorate-General for Competition, and the UK Competition and Markets Authority investigate conduct, impose fines, and review mergers. Many jurisdictions offer leniency programmes encouraging cartel members to self-report in exchange for reduced penalties. Private actions for damages also play a role, particularly in the United States, where treble damages are available.
