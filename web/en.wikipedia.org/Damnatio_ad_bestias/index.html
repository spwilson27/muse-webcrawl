Damnatio ad bestias

Damnatio ad bestias (Latin for "condemnation to beasts") was a form of capital punishment in ancient Rome in which the condemned person was killed by wild animals, mainly lions or other large cats. This form of execution, which first appeared during the Roman Republic around the 2nd century BC, formed part of the wider class of blood sports called bestiarii. It was applied particularly to the lowest social classes, including slaves, deserters, and, during persecutions, some early Christians.

Practice

The condemned were tied to stakes, or simply exposed in the arena, and set upon by animals that had been starved and goaded to frenzy. Venationes (beast hunts) were staged in amphitheatres such as the Colosseum, and damnatio ad bestias was often scheduled at midday, between the morning hunts and the afternoon gladiatorial bouts. The fate was considered especially shameful because the victim was denied even the dignity of a human executioner, and the body was usually destroyed entirely.

Victims

Under the Republic and Empire the punishment fell on condemned criminals (noxii), slaves, and prisoners of war. During imperial persecutions, Christians who refused to sacrifice were executed in this way; traditional accounts describe martyrs such as Blandina at Lyon (AD 177) and Perpetua and Felicity at Carthage (AD 203) facing beasts in the arena. Later Christian hagiography magnified these episodes, and the image of martyrs "thrown to the lions" became proverbial.

Decline

The practice declined with the spread of Christianity in the 4th century and the abolition of gladiatorial spectacles. Constantine I is traditionally credited with ending the games in AD 325, although venationes continued in some places into the 6th century.
