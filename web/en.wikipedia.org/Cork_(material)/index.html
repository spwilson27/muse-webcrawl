Cork (material)

Cork
SourceBark of the cork oak (Quercus suber)
Main producersPortugal, Spain, Italy, North Africa
CompositionSuberin (~45%), lignin, polysaccharides, air-filled cells
Key propertiesLight, elastic, buoyant, impermeable, insulating

Cork is a phellem layer of bark tissue harvested primarily from the cork oak (Quercus suber), which is endemic to southwest Europe and northwest Africa. It is composed of suberin, a hydrophobic substance, and its cellular structure of millions of tiny air-filled compartments gives it elasticity, buoyancy, and impermeability to liquids and gases.

Harvesting

Cork is harvested by hand, by stripping the outer bark from the tree without felling or harming it. A tree must be about 25 years old before its first harvest; thereafter bark regrows and can be stripped every 9 to 12 years, and a productive tree lives 150–250 years. The first two harvests yield rougher cork; from the third onward the material is suitable for bottle stoppers. Portugal produces roughly half of the world's cork.

Properties and uses

Cork's honeycomb of sealed cells makes it one of the lightest solid materials, an excellent thermal and acoustic insulator, and naturally fire-retardant. Its dominant use is wine stoppers — billions are produced annually — though plastic and screw-cap competition reduced its share. Other uses include flooring and wall tiles, bulletin boards, fishing floats, shoe soles, gaskets, and the heat-shielding cork ablators used on spacecraft. Wine "cork taint", caused by the compound 2,4,6-trichloroanisole (TCA), affects a small percentage of cork-stoppered bottles.

Ecology

Cork oak landscapes (montado in Portugal, dehesa in Spain) are biodiversity hotspots supporting species such as the Iberian lynx and Spanish imperial eagle. Because harvesting is non-destructive and repeatable, cork is regarded as a highly sustainable material, and a harvested oak retains more carbon than an unharvested one.
