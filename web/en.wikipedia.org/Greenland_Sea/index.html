The Greenland Sea is a body of water that borders Greenland to the west, the Svalbard archipelago to the east, the Fram Strait and the Arctic Ocean to the north, and the Norwegian Sea and Iceland to the south. It is usually considered part of the Arctic Ocean, though in some usages it is treated as part of the North Atlantic Ocean.

Extent

The International Hydrographic Organization defines the Greenland Sea as extending from a line between the northernmost point of Greenland and the northern point of Spitsbergen in the north, to Iceland and Jan Mayen in the south. The sea covers about 1.2 million square kilometres and reaches depths of nearly 4,850 metres in the Greenland Basin.

Oceanography

The Greenland Sea is an important site of ocean convection and deep water formation. Cold, dense water sinks here and feeds the deep limb of the Atlantic meridional overturning circulation. The cold East Greenland Current flows southward along Greenland's east coast, carrying Arctic water and sea ice out through the Fram Strait, while warmer Atlantic water circulates in from the south. Seasonal sea ice covers much of the sea in winter, and the region of open water that formed off northeast Greenland, historically known for the recurving "oddens" ice features, is significant for air–sea exchange.

Wildlife

The sea supports populations of harp seals and hooded seals, which breed on its ice, along with whales, walruses and polar bears on the Greenland coast. Its waters are fished for cod, halibut and shrimp, and its plankton-rich currents underpin food webs extending into the North Atlantic.
