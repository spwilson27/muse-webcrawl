Honkyoku

Honkyoku (Japanese: 本曲, "original pieces") are the pieces of shakuhachi music that were played by the komusō, the mendicant Zen Buddhist monks of the Fuke sect during the Edo period of Japan. The komusō, recognisable by the woven basket hats (tengai) that covered their heads, played the shakuhachi as a form of meditation known as suizen, or "blowing Zen", rather than as entertainment.

History

The honkyoku repertoire was transmitted orally within temples such as Myōan-ji in Kyoto and Icchoken in Kyushu. After the Fuke sect was abolished by the Meiji government in 1871, shakuhachi playing survived in part through secularisation, but the honkyoku continued to be taught as a spiritual discipline as well as a musical tradition. Different temples and lineages preserved distinct collections of pieces; the Kinko school repertoire, derived in part from pieces collected by Kinko Kurosawa in the 18th century, is the most widely known.

Characteristics

Honkyoku pieces are solo works characterised by free rhythm, wide dynamic range, and extensive use of timbral effects such as muraiki (breathy, airy tones produced by blowing hard across the edge of the flute). Phrases follow the length of the player's breath, giving the music a meditative, irregular pacing. Many pieces have evocative titles drawn from nature or Zen themes, such as Tsuru no Sugomori ("Nesting of Cranes") and Shika no Tōne ("Distant Call of Deer").

Legacy

In the twentieth century, players and teachers such as Watazumi Doso and Katsuya Yokoyama brought honkyoku to international audiences and influenced contemporary composers. The repertoire remains central to traditional shakuhachi study.
