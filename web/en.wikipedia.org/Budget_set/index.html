Budget constraint
The budget set: all bundles of two goods a consumer can afford
FieldMicroeconomics, consumer theory

In economics, a budget constraint represents all the combinations of goods and services that a consumer may purchase given current prices and a given income. The budget set (or opportunity set) is the set of all such affordable consumption bundles. Consumer theory uses the concepts of a budget constraint and a preference map to analyse consumer choices.

For a consumer with income m facing prices p1 and p2 for two goods, the budget set is:

{(x1, x2) : p1x1 + p2x2 ≤ m, x1 ≥ 0, x2 ≥ 0}

The boundary of this set, where spending exactly equals income, is called the budget line. Its slope, −p1/p2, gives the market rate at which good 2 can be traded for good 1.

Changes in the budget set

An increase in income shifts the budget line outward in parallel, enlarging the set. A change in the price of one good rotates the line around the intercept of the other good. Government policies — taxes, subsidies, rationing and in-kind transfers — can kink or truncate the budget set, changing behaviour even when total income is unchanged.

Many goods

In the general case of n goods, the budget set is the intersection of the half-space p·x ≤ m with the non-negative orthant of n-dimensional commodity space. Under standard assumptions (positive prices and income), the set is compact and convex, guaranteeing that utility maximisation over it has a solution.

Soft budget constraint

The economist János Kornai popularised the distinction between hard and soft budget constraints: state-owned enterprises in socialist economies that expected bailouts faced soft constraints, weakening financial discipline.
