The Afsluitdijk (Dutch for "enclosure dam") is a major dam and causeway in the Netherlands, constructed between 1927 and 1932 as the centrepiece of the Zuiderzee Works. Running 32 kilometres (20 mi) from Den Oever in North Holland to Zurich in Friesland, it dammed off the Zuiderzee, a shallow bay of the North Sea, transforming it into the freshwater lake known as the IJsselmeer.

History

Plans to enclose the Zuiderzee were developed by the engineer Cornelis Lely in the late 19th century. After the catastrophic flood of 1916, parliament passed the Zuiderzee Act in 1918, authorising the project. Construction of the Afsluitdijk began in 1927 from four work islands. The final gap was closed on 28 May 1932, and the dam was officially opened that September. During the Second World War, Dutch forces defeated a German attack at the Battle of the Afsluitdijk in May 1940, one of the few Dutch defensive successes of the campaign.

Structure

The dam has a total length of 32.5 km with a width of 90 m at water level, and stands about 7.25 m above sea level. Two complexes of discharge sluices, at Den Oever (Stevin sluices) and Kornwerderzand (Lorentz sluices), regulate the level of the IJsselmeer by discharging water into the Wadden Sea at low tide; shipping locks at both ends allow vessels to pass. The dam carries the A7 motorway, a cycle path, and is a landmark of Dutch water engineering. Since 2019 a large reinforcement programme has been raising and strengthening the dam to meet modern flood-protection standards.
