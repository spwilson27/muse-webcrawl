Vesicle (biology and chemistry)

StructureLipid bilayer enclosing fluid
ExamplesSynaptic vesicles, lysosomes, liposomes
FunctionsTransport, storage, secretion, digestion

In cell biology, a vesicle is a small membrane-bound sac that stores and transports substances within or between cells. Vesicles consist of fluid enclosed by a lipid bilayer, the same material as the cell membrane, allowing them to bud from and fuse with membranes throughout the cell. In chemistry, the term also describes artificial bilayer sacs such as liposomes.

Types and functions

Transport vesicles shuttle proteins and lipids between the endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus, and plasma membrane, using coat proteins such as clathrin, COPI, and COPII. Secretory vesicles, including synaptic vesicles filled with neurotransmitter, release their contents by exocytosis. Lysosomes and peroxisomes are specialised vesicle-like organelles for digestion and oxidation, and vacuoles store water and other materials, especially in plant cells.

Medical and technological relevance

Cells also release extracellular vesicles such as exosomes, which carry proteins and RNA between cells and are studied as biomarkers and drug carriers. Artificial liposomes are used to deliver drugs and, notably, the lipid nanoparticles that package mRNA vaccines. The 2013 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine honoured James Rothman, Randy Schekman, and Thomas Südhof for discoveries about vesicle traffic machinery.
