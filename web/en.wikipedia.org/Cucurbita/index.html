Cucurbita

Cucurbita (Latin for gourd) is a genus of herbaceous vegetables in the gourd family, Cucurbitaceae, native to the Americas. Five edible species are grown and consumed worldwide: C. argyrosperma, C. ficifolia, C. maxima, C. moschata, and C. pepo. Their fruits — including pumpkins, squash, and zucchini — are an important food source, and the plants were among the earliest domesticated crops in the New World.

Description

Cucurbita species are fast-growing annual vines or bushy plants with large, lobed leaves, tendrils, and unisexual yellow or orange flowers. The fruit is a type of berry called a pepo, with a thick rind and numerous flat seeds. Wild species contain bitter cucurbitacins, which were largely bred out of domesticated varieties.

Domestication

Archaeological evidence suggests Cucurbita was domesticated in Mesoamerica and South America at least 8,000–10,000 years ago, before maize and beans, and formed part of the "Three Sisters" companion planting system of Indigenous agriculture.

Uses

The fruits are eaten cooked or raw, the seeds are consumed as snacks and pressed for oil, and hollowed dried gourds have served as containers, floats, and instruments. Pumpkins are culturally prominent at Halloween and Thanksgiving in North America.
