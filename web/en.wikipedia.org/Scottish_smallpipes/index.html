Scottish smallpipes

The Scottish smallpipes are a bellows-blown bagpipe of Scotland, developed from earlier mouth-blown small pipes and revived in modern form in the late 20th century. Quieter and mellower than the great Highland bagpipe, they are suited to indoor playing and ensemble work with other instruments, and have become central to the modern Scottish folk scene.

Construction

Air is supplied by a bellows held under the arm, keeping the reeds dry and stable. The chanter — most often in the key of A — has a cylindrical bore and double reed, giving a soft, flute-like tone an octave lower in character than its size suggests. A set typically has three drones (bass, baritone/tenor and alto) resting in a common stock across the shoulder, tuned to the chanter's keynote.

History

Small pipes appear in Scottish sources from at least the 17th century, but mouth-blown forms fell from use. The modern bellows-blown instrument was recreated in the early 1980s by makers such as Colin Ross, drawing on surviving museum examples of Northumbrian and Scottish small pipes. The Lowland and Border Pipers' Society, founded in 1982, promoted the revival of both the smallpipes and the Border pipes.

Repertoire

Smallpipers draw on Lowland and Border tunes, Highland repertoire adapted to the nine-note scale, and a growing body of new compositions. The instrument features prominently in the work of bands and soloists in contemporary Scottish folk music.
