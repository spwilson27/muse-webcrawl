Kermes is a genus of scale insects in the family Kermesidae. Species of Kermes feed on oaks, particularly the kermes oak (Quercus coccifera) of the Mediterranean region. The dried bodies of the females of Kermes vermilio were historically harvested to produce kermes, a rich red dye.

The kermes dye

Kermes was one of the most valuable dyes of the ancient and medieval world, yielding crimson and scarlet colours used for silk and wool. The insects were collected from oak scrub around the Mediterranean, killed with vinegar, and dried until they resembled small berries or grains — which is why the dye was long mistaken for a plant product and called "grain." The words crimson and carmine are ultimately derived from the same root as kermes.

Decline

After the Spanish conquest of Mexico in the 16th century, the brighter and cheaper cochineal dye from the Americas largely displaced kermes in Europe. Kermes continued in local and traditional use and is still occasionally produced for restoration and specialty dyeing.
