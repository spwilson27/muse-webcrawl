Sans-serif

In typography and lettering, a sans-serif typeface (often shortened to sans) is one that lacks the small projecting strokes called serifs at the ends of letter strokes. The name comes from the French sans, "without". Sans-serif typefaces are commonly divided into grotesque, neo-grotesque, geometric and humanist families.

History

Although sans-serif letterforms appear in antiquity, the first printed typeface without serifs was produced by the Caslon type foundry in 1816 and was initially regarded as so odd that such faces were called "grotesque". Through the 19th century sans-serifs were used mainly for display and advertising. In the 1920s the Bauhaus and the New Typography movement elevated them to a symbol of modernity: landmark designs of the era include Paul Renner's Futura (1927), a geometric sans, and Eric Gill's Gill Sans (1928), a humanist design.

Major families

Max Miedinger and Eduard Hoffmann's Helvetica (1957) and Adrian Frutiger's Univers (1957) defined the neo-grotesque style and dominated corporate design; Frutiger later created Frutiger (1976) for airport signage. Humanist sans-serifs such as Frutiger, Gill Sans and Verdana take inspiration from classical letterforms and are widely used in wayfinding and on screens. Notable geometric faces besides Futura include Avenir and Gotham.

Usage

Sans-serif type is now the default for signage, user interfaces and much web text, prized for its clean appearance at small sizes on low-resolution displays, while serif faces remain common for long-form printed text. In the Vox-ATypI classification, sans-serifs are grouped as Lineales.
