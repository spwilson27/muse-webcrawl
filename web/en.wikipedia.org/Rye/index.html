Rye

Scientific nameSecale cereale
FamilyPoaceae (grasses)
OriginCentral and eastern Turkey / Armenia
Main producersGermany, Poland, Russia

Rye (Secale cereale) is a grass grown extensively as a grain, a cover crop, and a forage crop. A member of the wheat tribe and closely related to both wheat and barley, rye is used for flour, bread, beer, whisky, vodka, and animal fodder. It is exceptionally hardy: rye grows well in poor soils and cold climates where most other cereals fail, making it a staple grain of northern and eastern Europe.

Ripe rye, distinguished by its long awns (bristles).

Cultivation and history

Rye probably originated as a weed of wheat and barley fields in southwestern Asia and became a crop in its own right as farming spread north into colder regions. It was widely cultivated in Europe by the Middle Ages and remains central to the bread traditions of Germany, Poland, Russia, and Scandinavia, where dark rye breads such as pumpernickel are staples. Global production is concentrated in a belt across northern Europe and Russia.

Uses and health

Rye flour contains less gluten than wheat flour, producing dense, moist breads with a distinctive flavour. The grain is distilled into rye whisky in North America and into vodkas in Poland, and used in the Dutch spirit genever and in kvass. Rye grain is rich in dietary fibre. Its principal hazard is infection by the ergot fungus (Claviceps purpurea), whose alkaloid-laden sclerotia in flour caused historical outbreaks of ergotism — "St Anthony's fire" — with convulsions and gangrene.
