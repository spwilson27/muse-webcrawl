Thatcherism

Thatcherism is a form of British conservative ideology named after Conservative Party leader Margaret Thatcher, who governed as Prime Minister of the United Kingdom from 1979 to 1990. The term has been used to describe the principles of the British government under Thatcher, as well as, more broadly, the political platform of the era.

Overview

Thatcherism represented a systematic approach to reversing what Thatcher and her supporters saw as Britain's post-war decline. Its central tenets included a commitment to free markets, deregulation, privatisation of state-owned industries, reductions in direct taxation, monetary control of inflation, curbs on the power of trade unions, and a strong but limited state. Thatcher famously declared that there was "no alternative" (TINA) to her economic programme.

Economic policy

Under Chancellor of the Exchequer Geoffrey Howe and later Nigel Lawson, the government pursued monetarist policies influenced by Milton Friedman, raised indirect taxes such as VAT while cutting income tax rates, sold council houses to tenants under the Right to Buy scheme, and privatised major utilities and industries including British Telecom, British Gas, British Airways and the electricity and water industries. The City of London was deregulated in the "Big Bang" of 1986.

Social and political ideas

Thatcher drew on Victorian values of self-reliance, hard work and thrift, and on the liberal economics of Friedrich Hayek. Her remark that "there is no such thing as society" became a defining, if contested, summary of her individualism. She was a committed Atlanticist, close to US President Ronald Reagan, and a sceptic of European integration, expressed in her 1988 Bruges speech.

Legacy

Thatcherism reshaped British politics: the Labour Party under Tony Blair retained much of the market framework after 1997. Critics associate it with deindustrialisation, rising inequality and unemployment in the early 1980s; supporters credit it with restoring economic growth and Britain's international standing, and with victory in the Falklands War of 1982.
