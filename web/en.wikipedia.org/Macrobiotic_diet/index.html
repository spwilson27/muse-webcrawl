Macrobiotic diet

A macrobiotic diet (or macrobiotics) is a diet based on ideas about types of food drawn from Zen Buddhism, popularized in the West by George Ohsawa and Michio Kushi. The diet aims for a balance of yin and yang foods, emphasizing whole grains, locally grown vegetables, beans, and soups, while minimizing processed foods, sugar, and most animal products.

Principles

In the standard macrobiotic diet, whole grains such as brown rice typically form a large share of each meal, supplemented by vegetables, miso soup, beans and bean products such as tofu, sea vegetables, and small amounts of fish, fruit, nuts, and seeds. Foods are preferably organic and locally grown, and cooking methods are considered part of the balance of a meal. Nightshade vegetables, dairy, eggs, refined sugar, and tropical fruits are generally avoided or limited in temperate climates.

History

The term derives from the Greek makros ("large" or "long") and bios ("life"). Earlier writers such as Christoph Wilhelm Hufeland used the term in the 18th century. The modern dietary system was developed by the Japanese philosopher George Ohsawa (Nyoichi Sakurazawa) in the early 20th century, drawing on the earlier stone-cure dietary ideas of Sagen Ishizuka. Ohsawa's student Michio Kushi brought macrobiotics to the United States, where it became influential in the natural-foods movement of the 1960s and 1970s.

Criticism

Nutrition authorities have warned that strict versions of the diet can risk deficiencies of protein, vitamin B12, iron, calcium, and energy, especially for children and pregnant women. Mainstream medical bodies regard claims that macrobiotics can cure cancer as unproven, although a diet rich in whole grains and vegetables is generally consistent with healthy-eating guidance.
