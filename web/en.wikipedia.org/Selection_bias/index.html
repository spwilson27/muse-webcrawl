Selection bias


Selection bias distorts a sample

Selection bias is the bias introduced by the selection of individuals, groups, or data for analysis in such a way that proper randomization is not achieved, thereby failing to ensure that the sample obtained is representative of the population intended to be analyzed. It is sometimes referred to as the selection effect. The phrase "selection bias" most often refers to the distortion of a statistical analysis resulting from the method of collecting samples.

Types

Common types include sampling bias, where some members of a population are more likely to be included than others; attrition bias, arising from loss of participants in a study; volunteer bias, because volunteers differ systematically from non-volunteers; and Berkson's paradox, a form of selection bias in hospital-based studies. Survivorship bias — drawing conclusions only from entities that passed a selection process — is closely related.

Examples

A classic example is the 1936 Literary Digest poll, which predicted a landslide for Alf Landon over Franklin D. Roosevelt based on car and telephone owners, a sample unrepresentative of Depression-era voters. In epidemiology, the healthy worker effect describes how employed populations appear healthier than the general population because those too ill to work are excluded.

Mitigation

Researchers mitigate selection bias through random sampling, random assignment in experiments, weighting adjustments, and careful definition of inclusion criteria. In observational studies, methods such as propensity score matching and sensitivity analysis are used to assess its likely impact.
