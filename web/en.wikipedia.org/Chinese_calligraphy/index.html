Chinese calligraphy

Chinese calligraphy is the stylistic and technical discipline of writing Chinese characters with a brush and ink. It is regarded as one of the highest forms of Chinese art, alongside painting, poetry and music.

History

Calligraphy developed with the earliest Chinese writing on oracle bones in the Shang dynasty, through bronze inscriptions, seal script standardised under Qin Shi Huang, clerical script in the Han dynasty, and the mature regular, running and cursive scripts. Wang Xizhi (303–361), the Sage of Calligraphy, set enduring standards with works such as the Preface to the Poems Composed at the Orchid Pavilion.

Scripts and tools

The principal scripts are seal, clerical, regular (kaishu), semi-cursive (xingshu) and cursive (caoshu). The Four Treasures of the Study are the brush, ink, paper and inkstone. Brush technique centres on the centred tip, controlled pressure, and the balance of wet and dry strokes.

Aesthetics

Calligraphy is judged on line quality, structure, composition and the expression of the writer's character and emotion. It profoundly influenced Japanese, Korean and Vietnamese calligraphy, and was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2009.
