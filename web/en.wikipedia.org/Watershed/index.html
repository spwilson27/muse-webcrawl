A watershed, also called a drainage basin or catchment area, is an area of land where all flowing surface water converges to a single point, such as a river mouth, or flows into another body of water, such as a lake or ocean. The boundary of a watershed is the drainage divide — the line of high ground separating neighbouring basins, such that rain falling on opposite sides flows to different outlets.

Terminology

Usage differs by region: in North America "watershed" commonly denotes the whole basin, whereas in British usage a watershed traditionally means the divide itself. A basin comprises the main stem river and all its tributaries, together with the land draining into them. Watersheds are hierarchical: small tributary catchments nest within sub-basins, which combine into the basin of a major river — for example, the Ohio River basin within the Mississippi River basin, which drains about 40% of the contiguous United States into the Gulf of Mexico.

Characteristics and processes

Key characteristics include area, shape, slope, soils, land cover, and drainage density. Most precipitation falling within a watershed leaves as river discharge, evapotranspiration, or groundwater recharge. Some basins are endorheic (closed), draining to inland seas or sinks such as the Caspian Sea or Great Salt Lake rather than to the ocean; they cover roughly a fifth of Earth's land area.

Importance

Watersheds are the natural unit for water resources management: pollution, sediment, and land-use change anywhere in a basin affect water quality downstream. Authorities such as the Tennessee Valley Authority and river basin commissions in many countries organize management around basin boundaries, a principle central to integrated water resources management. Watershed boundaries also often coincide with political and cultural boundaries, as mountain divides have served as frontiers throughout history.
