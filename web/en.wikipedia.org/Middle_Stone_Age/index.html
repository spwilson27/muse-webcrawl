The Middle Stone Age (MSA) is a period of African prehistory between the Earlier Stone Age and the Later Stone Age, generally dated from about 300,000 to 50,000 years ago. It is associated with the emergence and early history of anatomically modern humans (Homo sapiens), whose earliest known fossils, from Jebel Irhoud in Morocco, date to the beginning of this period.

Technology

MSA industries are characterised by prepared-core (Levallois) techniques and retouched flake tools such as points and scrapers, hafted as composite tools and projectile weapons. Regional industries include the Aterian of North Africa, the Still Bay of southern Africa with its finely made bifacial points, and the Howiesons Poort industry, notable for small backed blades and early use of adhesives.

Behavioural modernity

The MSA yields much of the earliest evidence for behaviour once considered uniquely modern: engraved ochre and shell beads at Blombos Cave in South Africa, systematic use of red ochre pigment, heat treatment of stone, long-distance exchange of raw materials, and exploitation of marine foods at sites such as Pinnacle Point. The timing and pattern of these innovations remain central debates in the study of human origins.

End of the MSA

From around 60,000–50,000 years ago, populations bearing MSA technologies dispersed out of Africa, and within Africa the period gave way to the Later Stone Age.
