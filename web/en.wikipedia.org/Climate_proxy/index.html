Climate proxy

In the study of past climates (paleoclimatology), climate proxies are preserved physical characteristics of the environment that stand in for direct meteorological measurements: natural recorders of climate variability whose measurable properties correlate with temperature, precipitation or other climate variables. Scientists use calibrated proxy records to extend the climate record back far beyond the instrumental period, which covers only about the last 170 years.

Types of proxies

Major proxy archives include ice cores, in which annual layers preserve snow chemistry, isotopic composition and even trapped air bubbles recording past atmospheric gases; tree rings, whose width and density reflect growing conditions and support the field of dendroclimatology; marine and lake sediments, containing pollen, diatoms and the shells of organisms such as foraminifera, whose oxygen isotope ratios track ocean temperature and ice volume; corals, whose skeletal growth bands record tropical ocean conditions; and speleothems, cave deposits dated by uranium-series methods. Written documentary records provide further proxies for recent centuries.

Reconstruction

Proxy measurements are calibrated against the instrumental record over their overlap, then combined statistically to reconstruct past climate — for example Northern Hemisphere temperature series over the last two millennia or estimates of glacial–interglacial change. Uncertainties arise from dating, seasonal sensitivity and the uneven geographic distribution of archives.
