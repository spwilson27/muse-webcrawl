Column

A column or pillar in architecture and structural engineering is a structural element that transmits, through compression, the weight of the structure above to other structural elements below. In other words, a column is a compression member. Columns are frequently used to support beams or arches on which the upper parts of walls or ceilings rest.

Beyond their structural role, columns are among the most expressive elements of classical architecture, organised into the classical orders — Doric, Ionic, Corinthian and their derivatives — each with characteristic proportions and ornament.

Structure and parts

A classical column traditionally consists of a base at the bottom, a shaft — usually gently tapered and often fluted — and a capital at the top, which spreads the load and mediates between the shaft and the entablature above. The slight convex swelling of the shaft is known as entasis. When a column is attached to a wall it is called a pilaster or engaged column.

History

Stone columns appear in ancient Egyptian architecture by the third millennium BC, notably in the Step Pyramid complex of Djoser, and were elaborated in Persia and in Minoan and Mycenaean Greece. The Greeks codified the orders; the Romans adopted and extended them, using concrete and monolithic shafts. Medieval builders used clustered piers in Gothic cathedrals, while the Renaissance revived the classical orders systematically. From the nineteenth century, cast iron, steel and reinforced concrete allowed far more slender columns and freed them from classical proportion.

Structural behaviour

Engineers design columns against crushing and against buckling, the lateral instability analysed by Euler in the eighteenth century: the critical load falls with the square of the column's effective length and rises with the stiffness and cross-sectional moment of inertia of the material.
