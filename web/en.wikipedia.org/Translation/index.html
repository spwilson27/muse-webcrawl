Translation is the communication of the meaning of a source-language text by means of an equivalent target-language text. Whereas interpreting concerns spoken language, translation concerns written text. Translators must balance fidelity to the source with naturalness in the target language, a tension debated since antiquity in terms such as word-for-word versus sense-for-sense rendering.

History

Translation flourished in the ancient world: the Septuagint rendered the Hebrew Bible into Greek, and Buddhist scriptures were translated into Chinese over centuries. The translation movements of Abbasid Baghdad and medieval Toledo transmitted Greek, Persian, and Arabic learning into Latin and the European vernaculars. Bible translation — by Jerome, Luther, and the King James translators — profoundly shaped literary languages.

Process and theory

Professional translation involves analysing the source text, transferring meaning, and restructuring it in the target language, followed by revision. Translation theory addresses equivalence, untranslatability, domestication versus foreignization (Schleiermacher, Venuti), and the skopos or purpose of the translated text. Specializations include literary, legal, medical, technical, and audiovisual translation (subtitling and dubbing).

Machine translation

Computer-assisted translation tools aid human translators with translation memories, while machine translation — from rule-based systems to statistical and then neural methods, including transformer-based models — now produces fluent output for many language pairs, though professional post-editing remains common for high-stakes texts.
