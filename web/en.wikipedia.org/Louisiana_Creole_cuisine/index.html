Louisiana Creole cuisine is a style of cooking originating in Louisiana focused on the city of New Orleans and blending French, Spanish, West African, Native American, Italian, German and Haitian influences. It is closely related to — but distinct from — Cajun cuisine, which developed separately among the Acadian settlers of rural south Louisiana. Creole cooking is traditionally the more cosmopolitan, "city" tradition, historically prepared in the homes and restaurants of New Orleans, while Cajun food is associated with country cooking.

History

Creole cuisine developed in colonial New Orleans from the early 18th century, when French settlers combined European techniques with local ingredients — Gulf shrimp, oysters, crawfish, crabs, rice, peppers, okra and sassafras. Spanish rule (1763–1800) added dishes and ingredients such as jambalaya (related to paella) and a love of tomatoes and peppers. Enslaved West Africans brought okra and one-pot rice cooking traditions central to gumbo; the Choctaw contributed filé powder, ground sassafras leaves used as a thickener. Nineteenth-century immigration, especially Sicilian, added tomato-rich "Creole-Italian" dishes, and celebrated restaurants such as Antoine's (founded 1840), Commander's Palace and Galatoire's codified the cuisine.

Foundations

Many Creole dishes begin with a roux — flour cooked in fat — ranging from pale blond to dark brown, and the "holy trinity" of diced onion, celery and green bell pepper (a local substitution for French mirepoix's carrot). Rice is a near-universal accompaniment. Seasoning is assertive but layered: cayenne, black pepper, thyme, bay leaf, parsley and green onions.

Classic dishes

Gumbo

A rich stew of meats and/or shellfish in a roux-thickened stock, served over rice. Varieties include seafood gumbo, chicken-and-sausage gumbo, and gumbo z'herbes, a meatless Lenten version. Gumbo may be thickened with roux, okra, or filé (added at the table).

Jambalaya

A one-pot rice dish cooked with sausage, chicken, pork or seafood and the trinity, in Creole ("red") style with tomatoes, distinguishing it from the browner Cajun version.

Étouffée and other rice dishes

Étouffée ("smothered") features shellfish, usually crawfish, in a roux-based sauce over rice. Shrimp Creole simmers shrimp in a spicy tomato sauce. Red beans and rice, traditionally cooked on Monday wash days with the Sunday ham bone, remains a New Orleans institution.

Desserts and drinks

Bananas Foster — bananas flambéed in rum, butter, brown sugar and cinnamon over vanilla ice cream — was created at Brennan's restaurant in 1951. Café du Monde's beignets and chicory coffee, bread pudding with whiskey sauce, and cocktails such as the Sazerac and Ramos gin fizz complete the classic repertoire.

Creole versus Cajun

The popular distinction holds that Creole food is urban and tomato-friendly, using butter and cream, while Cajun food is rural, often spicier and based on lard or oil and darker roux. In practice the two traditions have intermingled heavily, and chefs such as Paul Prudhomme and Emeril Lagasse popularised both nationwide in the late 20th century.
