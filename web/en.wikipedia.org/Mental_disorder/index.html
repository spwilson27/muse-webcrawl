Mental disorder

A mental disorder, also known as a mental illness, mental health disorder, or psychiatric disorder, is a behavioural or mental pattern that causes significant distress or impairment of personal functioning. A mental disorder is also characterised by a clinically significant disturbance in an individual's cognition, emotional regulation, or behaviour, often in a social context. Such disturbances may occur as single episodes, may be persistent, or may be relapsing and remitting. There are many different types of mental disorders, with signs and symptoms that vary widely between specific disorders.


Mental disorders affect cognition, emotion, and behaviour.

A mental disorder is one aspect of mental health. The causes of mental disorders are often unclear. Theories may incorporate findings from a range of fields, including genetics, neuroscience, psychology, and sociology. A mental disorder may be diagnosed by a mental health professional, usually a clinical psychologist or psychiatrist.

Definitions and classification

The two most widely used classification systems are the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM), published by the American Psychiatric Association — the fifth edition, DSM-5, appeared in 2013, with a text revision (DSM-5-TR) in 2022 — and the mental and behavioural disorders chapter of the World Health Organization's International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11, adopted in 2019). Both systems define disorders largely by clusters of symptoms, their duration, and their impact on functioning, rather than by known biological causes.

Major categories include anxiety disorders, mood disorders such as major depressive disorder and bipolar disorder, psychotic disorders such as schizophrenia, eating disorders, personality disorders, trauma- and stressor-related disorders such as post-traumatic stress disorder, neurodevelopmental disorders such as autism spectrum disorder and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, and substance-related and addictive disorders.

Causes

Mental disorders are generally understood through a biopsychosocial model combining biological, psychological, and social factors. Genetic studies, including twin studies, indicate substantial heritability for conditions such as schizophrenia and bipolar disorder. Neurochemical research has implicated neurotransmitter systems including serotonin, dopamine, and glutamate, although simple "chemical imbalance" accounts are considered inadequate. Early life adversity, trauma, chronic stress, substance use, and social isolation are established environmental risk factors. Infections, brain injury, and some medical conditions can also produce psychiatric symptoms.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is typically based on a clinical interview and mental state examination, sometimes supplemented by psychological testing and medical investigations to exclude physical causes. Diagnostic categories are criticized for heterogeneity — two patients with the same diagnosis may share few symptoms — and for arbitrary boundaries between disorders and normality. Research frameworks such as the National Institute of Mental Health's Research Domain Criteria (RDoC) attempt to reclassify psychopathology by dimensions of observable behaviour and neurobiological measures.

Treatment

Treatments include psychotherapy — such as cognitive behavioural therapy, psychodynamic therapy, and family therapy — and medication, including antidepressants, antipsychotics, anxiolytics, and mood stabilizers. Severe or treatment-resistant conditions may be treated with electroconvulsive therapy or transcranial magnetic stimulation. Community-based care, supported employment, and peer support are important components of recovery-oriented services. The relative effectiveness of treatments varies by condition and individual; many guidelines recommend combined approaches.

Epidemiology

Mental disorders are common. The World Health Organization estimated that in 2019 nearly a billion people — including 14 percent of the world's adolescents — were living with a mental disorder, and that mental disorders are a leading cause of disability worldwide. Suicide accounts for more than one in a hundred deaths globally. Prevalence estimates vary by methodology and culture; surveys in many high-income countries find that roughly one in four to one in five adults meets criteria for a mental disorder in a given year.

History

Attitudes to mental disturbance have shifted across history, from supernatural explanations to the moral treatment movement of the late eighteenth century, associated with reformers such as Philippe Pinel and William Tuke, who advocated humane care in asylums. The late nineteenth century saw Emil Kraepelin's influential classification distinguishing dementia praecox (later schizophrenia) from manic-depressive illness. The discovery of effective medications in the 1950s, beginning with chlorpromazine, and subsequent criticism of institutional care contributed to deinstitutionalization in many countries. Social stigma remains a significant barrier to treatment.
