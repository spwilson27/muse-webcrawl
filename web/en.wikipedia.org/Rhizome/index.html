Rhizome


Rhizome of a grass, showing nodes, roots and new shoots

A rhizome (from Greek rhízōma, "mass of roots") is a modified subterranean plant stem that grows horizontally, sending out roots and shoots from its nodes. Rhizomes store starches, proteins and other nutrients, allowing the plant to survive unfavourable seasons underground and to reproduce vegetatively: a rhizome broken into pieces, each bearing at least one node, can grow into a new plant.

Structure and function

Unlike a root, a rhizome has nodes, internodes, small scale-like leaves and buds — the hallmarks of a stem. It may grow entirely below ground (as in ginger and turmeric) or partly along the surface. New shoots arise from the apex or from axillary buds, while adventitious roots grow downward from the nodes. Because the growing point advances each season, rhizomatous plants such as bamboos, irises, ferns and many grasses can spread to form extensive colonies; some, such as Japanese knotweed, are notorious invasive weeds largely because of their vigorous rhizomes.

Examples

Economically important rhizomes include ginger, turmeric, galangal and lotus "root" (actually a rhizome), all used as foods and spices, and the rhizomes of asparagus and hops in horticulture and brewing. The orris root of perfumery is the dried rhizome of certain iris species.

Related structures

Rhizomes are distinguished from similar underground storage organs: a stolon is an above- or below-ground horizontal stem that roots at its tip; a bulb is a short vertical stem with fleshy leaves; a corm is a swollen vertical stem; and a tuber is a thickened stem or root tip, such as the potato (a stem tuber formed at a stolon tip).
