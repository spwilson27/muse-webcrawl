Middle Platonism

Middle Platonism is the modern name given to a stage in the development of Platonic philosophy, lasting from about 90 BCE — when Antiochus of Ascalon rejected the scepticism of the New Academy — until the 3rd century CE, when Plotinus founded Neoplatonism. Middle Platonists reasserted Plato's dogmas, increasingly interpreting them through Stoic, Aristotelian and Pythagorean ideas, and developed the theological and cosmological framework that Neoplatonism and early Christian thought inherited.

Doctrine

Middle Platonists typically taught a hierarchy headed by a supreme, ineffable God (the First God or the One), beneath whom a second divine intellect (Nous, sometimes identified with the Demiurge of Plato's Timaeus) orders the cosmos through the Forms, reconceived as "thoughts of God". Many systems add a World Soul and subordinate daemons mediating between gods and humans. Ethics centred on "likeness to God" (homoiōsis theōi, from Plato's Theaetetus) as the goal of life, often combined with Stoic moral psychology.

Figures and texts

Antiochus of Ascalon (c. 130–68 BCE) broke with Academic scepticism and taught an eclectic doctrine blending Plato, Aristotle and the Stoa. Eudorus of Alexandria introduced Pythagoreanising metaphysics. Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BCE–50 CE) fused Platonism with Jewish scripture, developing the doctrine of the Logos. Plutarch of Chaeronea (c. 46–119 CE) wrote dialogues on the soul, daemons and the delay of divine justice. Handbooks such as the Didaskalikos attributed to Alcinous systematised Platonic teaching, while Numenius of Apamea (2nd century CE) influenced Plotinus so strongly that he was accused of plagiarising him.

Legacy

Middle Platonism shaped the intellectual world in which Christianity developed — its Logos theology, demonology and negative theology recur in the Church Fathers — and supplied the platform from which Plotinus launched Neoplatonism.
