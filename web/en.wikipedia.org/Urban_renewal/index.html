Urban renewal

A regenerated inner-city district.

Urban renewal (also called urban regeneration in the United Kingdom and Australia) is a programme of land redevelopment in cities, often involving the demolition of structures in blighted areas and their replacement with new housing, businesses, or public facilities. Beginning with large-scale municipal housing clearances in the late nineteenth century, renewal became a major instrument of planning policy in many countries after the Second World War.

History

Early renewal efforts targeted slum housing deemed unsanitary. In the United States, the Housing Act of 1949 funded large clearance programmes; urban planner Robert Moses reshaped New York City through expressways and housing projects, while critics such as Jane Jacobs argued in The Death and Life of Great American Cities (1961) that renewal destroyed functioning communities and produced sterile, unsafe environments. The demolished Pruitt–Igoe housing project in St. Louis became a symbol of renewal's failures.

Modern practice

Contemporary regeneration tends to emphasise mixed-use development, adaptive reuse of historic buildings, brownfield remediation, and community consultation rather than wholesale clearance. It remains controversial because rising values can displace existing residents — the process known as gentrification.
