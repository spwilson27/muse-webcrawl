Saturn Devouring His Son (Spanish: Saturno devorando a su hijo), also called simply Saturn, is one of the Black Paintings created by the Spanish artist Francisco Goya directly on the walls of his house, the Quinta del Sordo, between about 1820 and 1823. It depicts the myth of the Titan Saturn (the Greek Cronus), who, fearing that one of his children would overthrow him, ate each of them at birth. The work now hangs in the Museo del Prado in Madrid.

Description

The painting shows a gaunt, wild-haired Saturn emerging from blackness, his eyes bulging and mouth stretched open as he grips a headless, armless corpse that he has already begun to consume. A large portion of the left arm appears to have been eaten, and blood covers the figure's mouth and the body. The figure's hunched posture and staring gaze convey both horror and frenzy; unlike earlier treatments of the myth—such as Rubens's Saturn (1636), also in the Prado—Goya's monster is aged, terrified, and almost pathetic.

History

Goya painted the Black Paintings in his old age, after the Peninsular War and during a period of political repression under the restored Ferdinand VII. The works were never given titles by the artist and were not intended for public display. Saturn decorated a wall of the dining room on the ground floor of the Quinta del Sordo. In the 1870s the murals were transferred to canvas by Salvador Martínez Cubells; the Saturn suffered some damage in the process, particularly in the lower right. Photographs taken in situ before the transfer have been used to assess what was lost.

Interpretation

Interpretations of the painting are numerous: a meditation on power devouring its own future, on Spain consuming its children through war and absolutism, on old age and time (Chronos) destroying all things, and on Goya's own fears of madness and death. It has become one of the most widely reproduced and discussed images in Western art, frequently cited in discussions of horror, violence, and the limits of reason.
