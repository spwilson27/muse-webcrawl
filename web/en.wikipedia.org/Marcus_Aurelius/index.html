Marcus Aurelius Antoninus (121–180) was Roman emperor from 161 to 180 and a Stoic philosopher. The last of the rulers traditionally known as the Five Good Emperors, he ruled first jointly with Lucius Verus (until 169) and latterly with his son Commodus. His reign was dominated by wars on the Danube frontier against Germanic tribes (the Marcomannic Wars) and by the Antonine Plague.

Meditations

Marcus's Meditations, written in Greek while on campaign, is a private notebook of Stoic exercises on humility, mortality and duty, never intended for publication. It remains one of the most widely read works of classical philosophy.

Reign

Adopted by Antoninus Pius at Hadrian's direction, Marcus acceded in 161. His reign saw the Parthian War (161–166), plague brought back by returning troops, and continuous warfare on the northern frontier. He broke with the adoptive succession tradition by naming his biological son Commodus as heir—a decision often judged the end of Rome's golden age.

Legacy

The bronze equestrian statue of Marcus Aurelius on Rome's Capitoline Hill, spared in antiquity because it was mistaken for Constantine, became a model for Renaissance monuments. His column in Rome depicts the Danubian wars.
