Biology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Biology is the scientific study of life. Biologists study the structure, function, growth, origin, evolution, and distribution of living organisms, from molecules and cells to ecosystems and the biosphere. Its unifying principles include the cell as the basic unit of life, genes as the units of heredity, and evolution as the process that explains the unity and diversity of life.

History

The study of living things has roots in ancient natural history — Aristotle classified hundreds of species — and in medicine and agriculture. The modern discipline emerged in the 19th century with cell theory, Darwin and Wallace's theory of evolution by natural selection (1859), and Mendel's laws of heredity, later reunited in the modern synthesis and transformed by the discovery of the structure of DNA in 1953.

Branches

Major branches include molecular biology, genetics, microbiology, botany, zoology, physiology, ecology, evolutionary biology, and biochemistry. Biology is organised across levels of organisation: molecules, organelles, cells, tissues, organs, organisms, populations, communities, and ecosystems.

Fundamental concepts

Living things share characteristics: cellular organisation, metabolism, homeostasis, growth, reproduction, response to stimuli, and evolution over generations. Energy flows through living systems, typically entering as sunlight captured by photosynthesis, while matter is cycled.

Applications

Biology underlies medicine, agriculture, conservation, biotechnology, and environmental science, and informs responses to challenges from pandemics to climate change.
