Collective noun

A collective noun is a word referring to a collection of things taken as a whole. Most collective nouns in everyday speech are mundane, such as "group", "team", or "army". Specific collective nouns — sometimes called terms of venery in the case of animals — include a flock of birds, a herd of cattle, a pack of wolves, and a school of fish.

Terms of venery

The tradition of elaborate collective terms for animals descends from medieval hunting culture and works such as the Book of Saint Albans (1486), attributed to Juliana Berners, which lists terms including a "murder of crows", an "exaltation of larks", and a "parliament of owls". Many such terms were humorous or fanciful coinages rather than working vocabulary, and new playful terms continue to be invented in the same spirit.

Grammar

Collective nouns raise a question of verb agreement. In American English, collectives usually take singular verbs ("the team is winning"), while British English more readily allows plural agreement when the group is viewed as its members ("the team are arguing among themselves"). This is an instance of notional agreement.

Derivation

Collective nouns can be derived from other words: for instance, "fleet" (of ships) derives from the verb "to float", and many collectives end in formations borrowed from Latin and French. Other languages have their own systems; Chinese, for example, uses measure words (classifiers) that perform a related individuating function.
