Catalytic triad

A catalytic triad is a set of three amino acid residues that work together in the active site of certain enzymes, most famously the serine proteases such as chymotrypsin, trypsin and elastase, where the triad consists of serine, histidine and aspartate (Ser–His–Asp). The residues are far apart in the amino acid sequence but brought together by protein folding.

Mechanism

The aspartate carboxylate positions and polarises the histidine imidazole ring, which acts as a general base to deprotonate the serine hydroxyl. The resulting highly reactive serine alkoxide attacks the carbonyl carbon of the substrate's peptide bond, forming a tetrahedral intermediate stabilised by an "oxyanion hole", then an acyl-enzyme intermediate, which is finally hydrolysed by water to release the products and regenerate the enzyme.

Convergent evolution

Catalytic triads are a classic example of convergent evolution: unrelated enzyme families such as the subtilisin-family proteases use the same Ser–His–Asp chemistry with a completely different protein fold, while other enzymes use variants such as Cys–His–Asp or the Ser–His–Glu triad. Related arrangements include catalytic dyads, found for example in some viral proteases.
