Conjunctive normal form

In Boolean logic, a formula is in conjunctive normal form (CNF) or clausal normal form if it is a conjunction of one or more clauses, where a clause is a disjunction of literals, where a literal is a variable or its negation. As a normal form, it is useful in automated theorem proving and circuit theory.

For example, the following formulas are in CNF:

  • (A ∨ ¬B ∨ ¬C) ∧ (¬D ∨ E ∨ F)
  • (A ∨ B) ∧ (¬B ∨ C ∨ ¬D) ∧ (D ∨ ¬E)
  • A ∧ ¬B
  • A ∨ B

while the following are not:

  • ¬(A ∧ B) — the negation applies to a conjunction;
  • A ∧ (B ∨ (C ∧ D)) — a conjunction nested inside a disjunction.

Conversion into CNF

Every propositional formula can be converted into an equivalent formula in CNF. A standard conversion replaces implications and biconditionals using their definitions, drives negations inward with De Morgan's laws and double-negation elimination, and distributes disjunction over conjunction. This conversion may cause an exponential increase in formula size. The Tseitin transformation produces an equisatisfiable CNF formula with only linear growth by introducing fresh variables for subformulas.

Use in theorem proving

CNF is the input format of SAT solvers: the Boolean satisfiability problem is canonically posed over CNF formulas, with each clause a set of literals. In first-order logic, formulas are commonly converted to clausal normal form for resolution refutation. A Horn clause is a clause with at most one positive literal; Horn formulas can be solved in polynomial time and underlie logic programming languages such as Prolog.
