Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution

Fourteenth Amendment
Proposed13 June 1866
Ratified9 July 1868
Part ofReconstruction Amendments

The Fourteenth Amendment to the United States Constitution, adopted on 9 July 1868, is one of the Reconstruction Amendments. It addresses citizenship rights and equal protection under the law and was proposed in response to issues related to formerly enslaved people following the American Civil War.

Section One

The amendment's first section contains several clauses: the Citizenship Clause grants citizenship to all persons born or naturalized in the United States (overturning Dred Scott v. Sandford); the Privileges or Immunities Clause; the Due Process Clause, which prohibits states from depriving any person of life, liberty, or property without due process of law and has been used to apply most of the Bill of Rights to the states ("incorporation"); and the Equal Protection Clause, requiring states to provide equal protection under the law to all people within their jurisdictions.

Other sections

Section Two modifies congressional apportionment, penalizing states that deny the vote to male citizens. Section Three disqualifies former Confederates who had sworn to uphold the Constitution from office, unless Congress removes the disability. Section Four validates Union war debt and voids Confederate debts and compensation claims for emancipated slaves. Section Five gives Congress enforcement power.

Major cases

Landmark rulings based on the amendment include Brown v. Board of Education (1954), Roe v. Wade (1973), Loving v. Virginia (1967), and Obergefell v. Hodges (2015).
