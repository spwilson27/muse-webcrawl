The Eucharist is the central sacrament of the Catholic Church, "the source and summit of the Christian life" in the words of the Second Vatican Council. Catholics believe that in the celebration of the Mass, bread and wine are changed into the body and blood of Jesus Christ — a change the Church calls transubstantiation — and that Christ is truly, really, and substantially present under the appearances of bread and wine. In receiving Holy Communion, the faithful are united with Christ and with one another.

Institution and theology

The Church traces the Eucharist to the Last Supper, at which Jesus took bread, said "This is my body", and took the cup, saying "This is my blood of the covenant", commanding his disciples, "Do this in memory of me". Catholic teaching, drawing on the Gospel of John chapter 6 ("my flesh is true food") and on early Church Fathers, holds that the Mass re-presents (makes present) the one sacrifice of Christ on the cross, rather than repeating it. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) employed the term transubstantiation, and the Council of Trent (1551) defined the doctrine against Reformation alternatives.

Celebration and practice

The Eucharist is consecrated by a validly ordained priest within the Eucharistic Prayer of the Mass, using unleavened wheat bread in the Latin Church and natural grape wine. Catholics who are in a state of grace and have observed the Eucharistic fast receive Communion, ordinarily under the species of bread alone, though Communion under both kinds is permitted. Children normally make their First Communion around the age of reason (about seven). The consecrated hosts are reserved in the tabernacle for Communion of the sick and for Eucharistic adoration, including exposition, Benediction, Corpus Christi processions, and perpetual adoration chapels. The Church's feast of Corpus Christi, instituted in 1264, celebrates the sacrament publicly.
