Gene regulation

Gene regulation (or regulation of gene expression) encompasses the mechanisms cells use to control when, where, and how much of a gene's product — RNA or protein — is made. Regulation allows cells to respond to environmental changes and is the basis of differentiation: although nearly all cells in an organism carry the same genome, regulated expression produces distinct cell types.

Stages of regulation

Regulation can act at every step of gene expression: chromatin accessibility and epigenetic modification (DNA methylation, histone modification); transcription, controlled by promoters, enhancers, silencers, and transcription factors; RNA processing and alternative splicing; mRNA export, stability, and translation; and post-translational modification and protein degradation.

In prokaryotes

Bacteria coordinate genes in operons. The classic example is the lac operon of E. coli, described by Jacob and Monod: a repressor blocks lactose-metabolism genes until lactose is present, while the trp operon is repressed when tryptophan is abundant.

In eukaryotes

Eukaryotic regulation is more elaborate, involving combinatorial transcription-factor control, enhancers acting over long genomic distances, chromatin remodelling, non-coding RNAs and microRNAs, and epigenetic states that can be inherited through cell divisions. Altered regulation contributes to development and to diseases including cancer.
