Roti is a round, unleavened flatbread originating from the Indian subcontinent, made from stoneground whole wheat flour (atta) and water. It is a staple food in India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, and Sri Lanka, and is widely eaten in countries with large South Asian diasporas, including the Caribbean, Fiji, Malaysia, Indonesia, and parts of Africa. In everyday Hindi and Urdu usage, "roti" can also refer to a meal in general.

Preparation

The dough is kneaded until soft, rested, divided into balls, and rolled into thin discs. It is cooked on a hot flat griddle (tawa) and often finished directly over a flame or on a hot surface so that steam puffs it into a balloon; the puffed version is called phulka. It is commonly brushed with ghee or oil and served hot alongside curries, dals, and vegetables, torn by hand and used to scoop food.

Varieties

Roti encompasses many regional forms, including the soft South African and Caribbean "paratha roti" (buss up shut), rumali roti (folded like a handkerchief), and roti canai of Malaysia and Indonesia. Related but distinct breads include chapati (often synonymous with roti), paratha (layered and pan-fried), naan (leavened and tandoor-baked), and puri (deep-fried).

History

Wheat cultivation in the Indus Valley dates back thousands of years, and references to wheat breads appear in ancient and medieval Indian texts. The bread spread along trade and migration routes, and in the 19th century indentured Indian labourers carried it to the Caribbean, Fiji, and Southeast Asia, where it took on distinctive local forms.
