History of Moldova

The history of Moldova concerns the lands between the Carpathians, the Dniester and the Black Sea. The region was part of the medieval Principality of Moldavia, was divided between the Ottoman, Russian and Romanian states, formed part of Romania between the world wars, was annexed by the Soviet Union, and became an independent republic in 1991.

Antiquity and early Middle Ages

The territory was inhabited by Dacians and formed part of the Roman sphere after Trajan's conquest of Dacia. In the migration period it was crossed by Goths, Huns, Avars, Slavs, Cumans and Mongols. Romanian-speaking communities crystallised in the region during the Middle Ages.

Principality of Moldavia

The Principality of Moldavia was founded in the mid-fourteenth century, traditionally by Dragoș and consolidated under Bogdan I (r. 1359–1365). Its greatest ruler, Stephen the Great (r. 1457–1504), defended Moldavia against the Ottomans, Hungarians and Poles, winning famous victories such as Vaslui (1475), and founded churches and monasteries throughout the principality. From the sixteenth century Moldavia was an Ottoman vassal, retaining internal autonomy while paying tribute.

Partition and Russian rule

In 1774 Austria annexed the north-western part, Bukovina. By the Treaty of Bucharest (1812), the Ottoman Empire ceded the eastern half of Moldavia between the Prut and Dniester to Russia, which organised it as the Bessarabia Governorate. The remaining western Moldavia united with Wallachia in 1859 under Alexandru Ioan Cuza, forming the nucleus of modern Romania. Southern Bessarabia changed hands several times between Russia and Romania.

Twentieth century

In 1918, amid the collapse of the Russian Empire, Bessarabia united with Romania. In 1940, under the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, the USSR annexed Bessarabia and northern Bukovina, creating the Moldavian Soviet Socialist Republic from most of Bessarabia combined with a strip east of the Dniester (the former Moldavian ASSR). Romania retook the territory in 1941–44, during which the Jewish and Roma populations suffered mass murder and deportation. Soviet rule was restored in 1944 and accompanied by collectivisation, deportations and a famine in 1946–47.

Independence

With glasnost, a national movement demanded recognition of the Romanian identity of the language and a return to the Latin script, achieved in 1989. Moldova declared independence on 27 August 1991. In 1992 a short war with Transnistrian separatists, backed by Russian forces, left the Transnistria region outside government control, a frozen conflict that persists. Moldova has since oscillated between closer ties with Romania and the EU and with Russia; in 2022 it received EU candidate status, and a 2024 referendum narrowly approved constitutional commitment to EU accession.
