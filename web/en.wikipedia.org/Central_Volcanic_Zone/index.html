Central Volcanic Zone

The Central Volcanic Zone (CVZ) is a volcanic arc in western South America. It is one of four volcanic zones of the Andes, extending between latitudes 14°S and 28°S through Peru, Bolivia, Chile and Argentina, and formed by the subduction of the Nazca Plate beneath the South American Plate.

The zone contains the Altiplano–Puna volcanic complex, one of the largest silicic volcanic provinces on Earth, whose huge ignimbrite eruptions over the past 10 million years accompanied the thickening of the continental crust, which reaches 70 km — among the thickest on Earth.

Volcanoes

Some 44 volcanoes in the CVZ have been active in the Holocene, including Huaynaputina (whose 1600 eruption was the largest in South America in historical time and affected global climate), Misti, Ubinas, Sabancaya, Ticsani, Parinacota, Licancabur, Lascar and the resurgent caldera systems of La Pacana and Cerro Guacha. The world's highest active volcano, Ojos del Salado (6,893 m), lies at the southern end of the zone.

Geology

Subduction here is characterised by alternating flat-slab and steep segments; the volcanic arc is separated from the Northern and Southern Volcanic Zones by gaps where the slab subducts at a shallow angle and volcanism is absent (the Peruvian and Pampean flat slabs). Associated mineralisation includes the great porphyry copper deposits of Chile and Peru.
