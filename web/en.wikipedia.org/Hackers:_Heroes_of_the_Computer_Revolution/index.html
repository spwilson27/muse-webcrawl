Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution

AuthorSteven Levy
Published1984
PublisherAnchor Press/Doubleday
SubjectComputer history, hacker culture

Hackers: Heroes of the Computer Revolution is a 1984 book by Steven Levy about the hacker culture and its early history. It is credited with popularising the term "hacker" in its original, positive sense — a person who delights in exploring the details of programmable systems — and with articulating the "hacker ethic".

Content

The book is structured in three parts. The first covers the "True Hackers" of MIT in the late 1950s and 1960s, centred on the Tech Model Railroad Club and the PDP-1, and profiles figures such as Peter Samson, Alan Kotok and Richard Greenblatt. The second follows the "Hardware Hackers" of 1970s California — the Homebrew Computer Club, Lee Felsenstein, Steve Wozniak and the founding of Apple. The third, "Game Hackers", chronicles the young programmers of the early video game industry, including Ken and Roberta Williams of Sierra On-Line and John Harris.

Levy summarised the hacker ethic in principles including: access to computers should be unlimited and total; all information should be free; mistrust authority and promote decentralisation; hackers should be judged by their hacking, not by degrees or status; you can create art and beauty on a computer; and computers can change your life for the better.

Legacy

Anniversary editions (1994, 2001 and a 25th-anniversary edition in 2010) added updates on later figures, including the free software movement and Mark Zuckerberg. The book remains a foundational text on the origins of personal computing and hacker culture.
