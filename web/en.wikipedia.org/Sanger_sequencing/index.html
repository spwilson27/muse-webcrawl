Sanger sequencing

Sanger sequencing is a method of DNA sequencing based on the selective incorporation of chain-terminating dideoxynucleotides by DNA polymerase during in vitro DNA replication. Developed by Frederick Sanger and colleagues in 1977, it was the most widely used sequencing method for approximately forty years and earned Sanger a share of the 1980 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Method

In the classical method, a single-stranded DNA template is mixed with a primer, DNA polymerase, normal deoxynucleotides (dNTPs), and a low concentration of chain-terminating dideoxynucleotides (ddNTPs). Because ddNTPs lack the 3'-hydroxyl group needed to form the next phosphodiester bond, their incorporation halts extension. The result is a set of fragments of every possible length, each ending at a known base.

Automation and fluorescent labelling

In automated Sanger sequencing, each of the four ddNTPs carries a different fluorescent dye. Fragments are separated by capillary electrophoresis, and a laser excites the dyes as fragments pass a detector; the sequence of colours is recorded as a chromatogram (electropherogram) from which software calls the bases. Read lengths of 800–1,000 bases are typical.

Applications and legacy

Sanger sequencing was the workhorse of the Human Genome Project and remains the gold standard for verifying short DNA sequences, sequencing individual genes and amplicons, and clinical diagnostics. Although massively parallel "next-generation" methods have displaced it for whole-genome work, its accuracy keeps it in routine use worldwide.
