History of geography

The history of geography covers humanity's efforts to describe and map the Earth, from Babylonian clay-tablet maps and Greek mathematical geography to the satellite-based geographic information systems (GIS) of today.

Ancient and classical geography

The oldest known world map is the Babylonian Imago Mundi (c. 6th century BC). Greek thinkers systematized the discipline: Eratosthenes coined the word "geography" and calculated Earth's circumference with remarkable accuracy; Strabo wrote a descriptive Geography in 17 books; Ptolemy's Geographia (2nd century AD) codified coordinates and map projections and influenced cartography for over a millennium. Chinese and Indian scholars developed parallel traditions, including Pei Xiu's cartographic principles.

Medieval and Islamic geography

Islamic geographers such as al-Idrisi, who produced a world map for Roger II of Sicily in 1154, and travelers like Ibn Battuta advanced descriptive geography. In Europe, T-O maps coexisted with increasingly accurate portolan charts used by Mediterranean sailors. Chinese cartography flourished under the Song and Ming, and Zheng He's voyages charted the Indian Ocean.

Age of Exploration and modern discipline

European voyages of the 15th–17th centuries filled in the world map; Mercator's projection (1569) aided navigation. In the 19th century, Alexander von Humboldt and Carl Ritter founded modern academic geography, emphasizing systematic study of natural and human phenomena. The 20th century saw the quantitative revolution, regional geography's decline and revival, and the development of remote sensing and GIS, transforming the field into a data-rich science of spatial analysis.
