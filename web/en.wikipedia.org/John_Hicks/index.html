John Hicks

Born: John Richard Hicks, 8 April 1904, Warwick, England
Died: 20 May 1989 (aged 85), Blockley, England
Alma mater: Balliol College, Oxford
Known for: IS–LM model; Hicksian demand; Value and Capital (1939)
Awards: Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences (1972)

Sir John Richard Hicks (8 April 1904 – 20 May 1989) was a British economist regarded as one of the most important and influential economists of the twentieth century. His most familiar contribution is the IS–LM model, which formalised an interpretation of the ideas of John Maynard Keynes. His book Value and Capital (1939) significantly extended general-equilibrium and value theory.

Career

Hicks studied at Clifton College and Balliol College, Oxford, initially in mathematics before switching to philosophy, politics and economics. He lectured at the London School of Economics, where he came under the influence of Lionel Robbins and encountered the work of the Austrian and Lausanne schools, then moved to Cambridge as a fellow of Gonville and Caius College, and later to Manchester and back to Oxford, where he became Drummond Professor of Political Economy.

Contributions

In 1937 Hicks published "Mr. Keynes and the 'Classics'", which introduced the IS–LM diagram relating investment–saving and liquidity preference–money supply curves, a synthesis that dominated macroeconomics teaching for decades. Value and Capital built consumer demand theory on ordinal utility, introducing the distinction between substitution and income effects and the "Hicksian" (compensated) demand function. With R. G. D. Allen he developed the theory of the marginal rate of substitution. Later work ranged over capital theory, traverse analysis, the trade cycle, and economic history in A Theory of Economic History (1969).

In 1972 Hicks shared the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with Kenneth Arrow for "pioneering contributions to general economic equilibrium theory and welfare theory". He was knighted in 1964.
