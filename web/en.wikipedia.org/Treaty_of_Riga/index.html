The Treaty of Riga (also called the Peace of Riga) was signed on 18 March 1921 in Riga, Latvia, between Poland on one side and Soviet Russia and Soviet Ukraine on the other. It ended the Polish–Soviet War (1919–1921) and fixed Poland's eastern frontier for the interwar period, dividing the disputed territories of Belarus and Ukraine between the two sides.

Background

The war had swung dramatically: Polish forces under Józef Piłsudski took Kyiv in May 1920 in alliance with Symon Petliura's Ukrainian People's Republic, but a Red Army counteroffensive drove to the gates of Warsaw, where it was defeated in the Battle of Warsaw in August 1920. Poland then advanced east again before exhaustion and diplomatic pressure brought both sides to negotiate; an armistice and preliminary peace were signed in October 1920. Neither side had achieved its maximal aims — Piłsudski's federation project (Międzymorze) failed, while the Soviet hope of carrying revolution into Germany was abandoned — and the final terms reflected the military stalemate.

Terms

The treaty drew the Polish–Soviet border considerably east of the Curzon Line proposed by the Western Allies, incorporating into Poland large parts of western Belarus and western Ukraine (the Kresy), including the cities of Wilno (Vilnius, separately seized from Lithuania), Lwów, Pinsk and Łuck. Roughly four million Ukrainians and Belarusians became citizens of Poland. Russia recognised Polish independence and the border, agreed to pay Poland 30 million gold roubles as compensation for Polish property and contributions to the Russian economy (never fully paid), and to return Polish cultural treasures and archives removed since the partitions, including the Załuski Library collections — much of which was eventually returned. The treaty also provided for prisoner exchanges, repatriation and an option of citizenship for borderland populations.

Consequences

The settlement left both Ukrainian and Belarusian national movements divided between states, and burdened Poland with restive minorities and an exposed frontier. Soviet leaders regarded the treaty as a temporary expedient. The border fixed at Riga lasted until September 1939, when the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact and the Soviet invasion of eastern Poland erased it; the post-1945 settlement moved the frontier west to approximately the Curzon Line. The Treaty of Riga is remembered in Poland as the close of its war of independence, and in Belarusian and Ukrainian historiography as a partition concluded without their participation.
