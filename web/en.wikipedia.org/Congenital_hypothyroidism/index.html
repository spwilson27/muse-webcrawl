Congenital hypothyroidism

Congenital hypothyroidism (CH) is thyroid hormone deficiency present at birth. If untreated for several months after birth, severe congenital hypothyroidism can lead to growth failure and permanent intellectual disability. Infants born with congenital hypothyroidism may show no effects, or may display mild effects that often go unrecognized as a problem.

It is one of the most common preventable causes of intellectual disability worldwide.

Causes

Worldwide, the most common cause of congenital hypothyroidism is iodine deficiency, but in the developed world and areas of adequate environmental iodine, cases are most often due to thyroid dysgenesis — defective development of the thyroid gland, including absent (agenesis), underdeveloped (hypoplasia), or ectopic glands. Other cases result from dyshormonogenesis, inherited defects in thyroid hormone synthesis, or central hypothyroidism involving the pituitary or hypothalamus.

Signs and symptoms

Infants with CH may be asymptomatic at birth. Features that can develop include prolonged jaundice, a hoarse cry, poor feeding, constipation, umbilical hernia, large fontanelles, macroglossia, dry skin, and hypothermia. Without treatment, growth and development are severely impaired.

Screening and treatment

Newborn screening programs in most developed countries measure TSH or thyroxine (T4) in a heel-prick blood sample taken in the first days of life, allowing early treatment with levothyroxine. Treatment started within the first weeks of life generally results in normal growth and intelligence.
