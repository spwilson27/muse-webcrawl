Air traffic control (ATC) is a service provided by ground-based air traffic controllers who direct aircraft on the ground and through a given section of controlled airspace, and who provide advisory services to aircraft in non-controlled airspace. The primary purpose of ATC worldwide is to prevent collisions, organise and expedite the flow of air traffic, and provide information and other support for pilots.

History

The world's first air traffic control tower opened at Croydon Airport, London, in 1920, issuing basic traffic information by radio and flag signals. Radar-based control developed rapidly after the Second World War, and the 1956 Grand Canyon mid-air collision, which killed 128 people, spurred the creation of a unified federal control system in the United States and the expansion of radar coverage. International standards are set by the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO), founded in 1944.

Divisions of control

Control responsibilities are divided among several types of facility. Aerodrome (tower) controllers manage aircraft on runways and in the immediate vicinity of an airport. Approach and departure controllers, usually radar-based, sequence traffic arriving at and leaving terminal areas. Area (en-route) controllers at area control centres handle aircraft at high altitude between terminal areas, handing flights off from sector to sector and country to country as they cross the globe.

Methods and technology

Controllers maintain separation using radar and satellite-based surveillance (such as ADS-B), standard separation minima, and a structured system of airways, altitudes, and clearances delivered by voice radio using standardised phraseology in English. Flight progress strips and electronic flight data systems record each aircraft's clearance and progress. Modernisation programmes such as NextGen in the United States and the Single European Sky initiative aim to replace ground radar with satellite navigation and data-link communications.
