Good Shepherd


Christ the Good Shepherd, a common image in early Christian art.

The Good Shepherd (Greek: ποιμὴν ὁ καλός, poimḗn ho kalós) is an image used in the pericope of John 10:1–21, in which Jesus Christ is depicted as the Good Shepherd who lays down his life for his sheep. Similar imagery is used in Psalm 23 and Ezekiel 34. The image also appears in the Parable of the Lost Sheep, found in Matthew 18:12–14 and Luke 15:3–7.

In the Gospel of John

In the Johannine discourse, Jesus contrasts himself with the "thief" and the "hired hand" who abandons the sheep when danger comes: "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep" (John 10:11). The passage emphasizes the shepherd's intimate knowledge of his flock — "I know my own and my own know me" — and the voluntary self-sacrifice that Christian theology reads as a foreshadowing of the crucifixion.

Early Christian art

The Good Shepherd was one of the most common representations of Christ in early Christian art, appearing in the catacombs of Rome from the second and third centuries. In these depictions a youthful, beardless figure carries a lamb across his shoulders, an image adapted from the Greco-Roman kriophoros (ram-bearer). As imperial Christian art developed after Constantine, the shepherd image gradually gave way to more majestic portrayals of Christ enthroned.

Interpretation

Commentators have long connected the image with the Old Testament portrait of God as shepherd of Israel (Psalm 23, Isaiah 40:11, Ezekiel 34) and with David, the shepherd-king. The fourth Sunday of Easter in the Roman Catholic and many Protestant lectionaries is traditionally called Good Shepherd Sunday, when the Johannine passage is read.
