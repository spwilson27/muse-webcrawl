Hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer

SynonymsLynch syndrome
SpecialtyMedical genetics; oncology; gastroenterology
CauseInherited pathogenic variants in DNA mismatch repair genes (MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2) or EPCAM deletions
InheritanceAutosomal dominant
RisksColorectal cancer; endometrial cancer; ovarian, gastric, urinary tract and other cancers
DiagnosisFamily-history criteria; tumour microsatellite instability / immunohistochemistry; germline genetic testing

Hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC), universally known as Lynch syndrome, is an inherited cancer predisposition syndrome caused by germline defects in the DNA mismatch repair system. It is the most common hereditary cause of colorectal cancer, accounting for roughly 2–4% of all cases, and it substantially raises the lifetime risk of several other malignancies, especially endometrial cancer.

Genetics

Lynch syndrome is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. Affected individuals carry one defective copy of a mismatch repair gene — most often MLH1 or MSH2, less commonly MSH6 or PMS2; deletions in the neighbouring EPCAM gene can also silence MSH2. When the second copy is lost in a cell, errors in DNA replication — particularly in repetitive microsatellite sequences — go uncorrected, producing the phenomenon of microsatellite instability and accelerating the accumulation of mutations that drive cancer. Unlike familial adenomatous polyposis, Lynch syndrome does not produce hundreds of colonic polyps; cancers instead develop, often quickly, from a small number of adenomas.

Clinical features

Colorectal cancers in Lynch syndrome tend to occur at a young age (average mid-40s), favour the right (proximal) colon, and may be multiple. Women face a high lifetime risk of endometrial cancer, rivalling or exceeding their colorectal risk, and there are increased risks of ovarian, stomach, small bowel, pancreatic, biliary, urinary tract and brain tumours, as well as sebaceous skin neoplasms (the Muir–Torre variant). The syndrome was named after Henry T. Lynch, whose studies of "cancer families" from the 1960s onward established its hereditary basis; the Amsterdam and Bethesda criteria were developed to identify families for testing.

Diagnosis and management

Modern practice increasingly tests all new colorectal and endometrial tumours for mismatch repair deficiency by immunohistochemistry or microsatellite instability analysis, with abnormal results prompting germline testing. Management centres on intensive surveillance: colonoscopy every one to two years from the mid-20s, which substantially reduces colorectal cancer incidence and mortality; consideration of prophylactic surgery in some cases; gynaecological surveillance or risk-reducing hysterectomy for women; and aspirin chemoprevention, which the CAPP2 trial showed reduces cancer risk in carriers. Mismatch-repair-deficient cancers are also notable for responding well to immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy.
