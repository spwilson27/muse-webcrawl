Coandă effect

The Coandă effect is the tendency of a fluid jet to stay attached to a convex surface. It is named after Romanian inventor Henri Coandă, who described and patented applications of the phenomenon in the 1930s.

Explanation

When a jet flows along a curved surface, entrainment of the surrounding fluid and the pressure difference between the two sides of the jet keep it attached, so the jet follows the curve rather than continuing in a straight line. The effect is related to the behaviour seen when water from a tap clings to the back of a spoon.

Applications

The effect is exploited in blown flaps and circulation-control wings that increase aircraft lift, in fluidic devices with no moving parts, in medical ventilators, and in air-conditioning diffusers that throw air along ceilings. Coandă's own experiments included an early jet aircraft design, the Coandă-1910.
