Sampling (signal processing)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sampling

Field: Signal processing

Input: Continuous-time signal

Output: Discrete-time signal (sequence of samples)

Key result: Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem

In signal processing, sampling is the reduction of a continuous-time signal to a discrete-time signal. A common example is the conversion of a sound wave to a sequence of “samples” — values measured at equally spaced points in time. A sample is a value of the signal at one point in time and/or space; a sampler is a device or process that extracts samples from a continuous signal. Together with quantization, sampling is the essential first step in analog-to-digital conversion.

Sampling theorem

The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem states that a band-limited signal containing no frequencies above B hertz can be reconstructed exactly from samples taken at a rate greater than 2B samples per second. The threshold rate of 2B is called the Nyquist rate; half the sampling frequency is the Nyquist frequency. Harry Nyquist derived the criterion in 1928 in the context of telegraph transmission, and Claude Shannon’s 1949 proof made it a foundation of information theory. Reconstruction uses band-limited interpolation, for instance the Whittaker–Shannon interpolation formula, which sums shifted sinc functions weighted by the samples.

Aliasing

If a signal is sampled below its Nyquist rate, frequency components above half the sampling rate are “folded” into lower frequencies, a distortion called aliasing. Practical systems therefore filter the input with an anti-aliasing (low-pass) filter before sampling. Aliasing is visible in everyday life as the apparent backward rotation of wagon wheels and helicopter blades in film and video.

Types of sampling

  • Uniform (periodic) sampling at a fixed interval T, the standard in audio and most data acquisition.
  • Oversampling, sampling far above the Nyquist rate to relax filter requirements and reduce quantization noise, as in sigma-delta converters.
  • Undersampling (bandpass sampling), deliberately sampling a bandpass signal below twice its highest frequency to shift it to a lower band.
  • Non-uniform and compressed sampling, used where samples are expensive or the signal is sparse.

In digital audio, the compact disc standard samples at 44,100 Hz with 16-bit quantization, capturing frequencies up to about 20 kHz; video, radar, medical imaging and software radio all rest on the same sampling principles.
