Avialae

Scientific classification
Domain: Eukaryota
Kingdom: Animalia
Phylum: Chordata
Clade: Dinosauria
Clade: Theropoda
Clade: Avialae
Fossil range: Late Jurassic – Present

Avialae ("bird wings") is a clade containing the only living dinosaurs, the birds. It is usually defined as all theropod dinosaurs more closely related to modern birds (Aves) than to deinonychosaurs, though alternative definitions are occasionally used.

Archaeopteryx lithographica, from the late Jurassic Period Solnhofen Formation of Germany, is usually considered the earliest known avialan. Avialans are a highly diverse and successful group, with more than 11,000 living species of birds, making them one of the most species-rich groups of tetrapod vertebrates.

Definition

The clade Avialae was named by Jacques Gauthier in 1986. Gauthier originally defined it as a node-based clade including birds and all maniraptorans closer to birds than to Deinonychus. In 2001 he redefined it as all dinosaurs with feathered wings used in flapping flight, and the birds that descended from them. Some researchers use slightly different definitions, so the exact membership of the earliest avialans can vary.

Evolution

The earliest avialans are known from the Late Jurassic, roughly 160 million years ago. By the Cretaceous Period the group had diversified enormously: the Enantiornithes ("opposite birds") were the dominant group of birds in the Mesozoic, while the Euornithes included the ancestors of all living birds. Most Mesozoic avialan lineages perished in the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event 66 million years ago, and only a handful of crown bird lineages survived into the Cenozoic, where they underwent an explosive adaptive radiation.

Characteristics

Avialans share a number of anatomical features, including feathers, a pygostyle (fused tail vertebrae) in later forms, a keeled sternum in strong fliers, and hollow, pneumatic bones. Early avialans such as Archaeopteryx retained many ancestral theropod traits, including teeth, long bony tails and clawed fingers.
