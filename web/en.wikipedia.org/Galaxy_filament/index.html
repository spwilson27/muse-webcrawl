Galaxy filament

In cosmology, galaxy filaments are the largest known structures in the universe, consisting of walls of galactic superclusters. These massive, thread-like formations can reach lengths of 80 megaparsecs or more and form the boundaries between large voids in the universe. Galaxy filaments form the cosmic web and define the overall structure of the observable universe.

Formation

Filaments form as baryonic matter and dark matter collapse gravitationally along sheets between overdense regions, a process predicted by the Zel'dovich pancake theory and confirmed by large N-body simulations of cold dark matter cosmology. Matter flows along filaments into galaxy superclusters at their intersections, called nodes.

Discovery

The first large filamentary structures were revealed by galaxy redshift surveys such as the CfA Redshift Survey in the 1980s, which mapped the "Great Wall" of galaxies. Later surveys, including the Sloan Digital Sky Survey and the 2dF Galaxy Redshift Survey, mapped the cosmic web in far greater detail.

Examples

  • The Sloan Great Wall, one of the largest known structures, about 1.38 billion light-years long
  • The CfA2 Great Wall
  • The Hercules–Corona Borealis Great Wall, a candidate structure spanning some 10 billion light-years
  • The Laniakea Supercluster filamentary complex containing the Milky Way
