Hilbert's problems

Hilbert's problems are twenty-three problems in mathematics published by German mathematician David Hilbert in 1900. Presented first in his lecture "The Problems of Mathematics" at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Paris on 8 August 1900, the list was enormously influential: it set much of the agenda of 20th-century mathematics, and individual problems spurred whole new fields, from mathematical logic to algebraic geometry.

The problems

Hilbert grouped the problems by area. They include the continuum hypothesis (1st), the consistency of arithmetic (2nd), the decision problem for Diophantine equations (10th), the classification related to the Riemann hypothesis (8th), and a programme for axiomatising physics (6th). Hilbert framed them optimistically: he believed every definite mathematical problem could be solved, a conviction summed up in his famous phrase Wir müssen wissen — wir werden wissen ("We must know — we will know").

Status

Most of the problems have been resolved, though in ways Hilbert did not anticipate:

  • The continuum hypothesis (1st) was shown independent of ZFC set theory by Kurt Gödel (1940) and Paul Cohen (1963).
  • The consistency of arithmetic (2nd) was undermined by Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931), which show a consistent system cannot prove its own consistency.
  • The tenth problem was settled negatively in 1970 by Yuri Matiyasevich, completing work by Martin Davis, Hilary Putnam and Julia Robinson: no algorithm decides whether a Diophantine equation has integer solutions.
  • The Riemann hypothesis (part of the 8th problem) remains open and is among the most famous unsolved problems in mathematics.
  • Problems such as the 7th (transcendence, solved by the Gelfond–Schneider theorem), 12th, 16th and 18th have been fully or partially resolved.

Influence

A solution to a Hilbert problem brought instant fame, and the list shaped funding, careers and the direction of research for a century. Mathematical historians note the problems' deliberate mixture of the concrete and the programmatic: alongside specific questions, Hilbert issued manifestos — for axiomatics, for invariant theory, and for the solvability of mathematics itself.
