Zamorin

The Zamorin of Calicut (Malayalam: Sāmūtiri) was the hereditary royal title of the Hindu rulers of the medieval Kingdom of Calicut (Kozhikode) on the Malabar Coast of southwestern India. The Zamorins ruled for almost six centuries, between roughly the 12th and 18th centuries CE, becoming the dominant power on the Malabar Coast through control of the lucrative Indian Ocean spice trade, above all in pepper.

According to tradition, the Zamorin's ancestors, the Eradis of Nediyiruppu, received land and the right to the port of Calicut from the last Later Chera ruler in the early 12th century. Calicut grew into one of the great entrepôts of the Indian Ocean, visited by Arab, Chinese and later European merchants. Ibn Battuta visited in the 1340s, and Chinese treasure fleets under Zheng He called repeatedly in the early 15th century.

The Portuguese arrival

In May 1498 the Portuguese navigator Vasco da Gama landed near Calicut and was received by the Zamorin, an encounter that opened the European sea route to India. Initial amity gave way to conflict as the Portuguese demanded trade monopolies and blockaded the coast; the Zamorins, allied at times with the Mappila merchant community, the Marakkar admirals and later the Dutch, resisted Portuguese power for nearly a century. Calicut nevertheless remained a major trading port under the Zamorins' tolerant rule, which welcomed merchant communities of many faiths.

Decline

From the mid-18th century the kingdom was squeezed by the rising powers of Travancore and Mysore. Hyder Ali of Mysore conquered Calicut in 1766, and the Zamorin's family fled into exile. Calicut was ceded to the British East India Company by the Treaty of Seringapatam in 1792, ending the Zamorins' political rule, though the family retained ceremonial and religious prominence.
