Life insurance
Life insurance provides for dependants after the insured's death

Life insurance (or life assurance) is a contract between a policyholder and an insurer under which the insurer promises to pay a designated beneficiary a sum of money upon the death of the insured person, in exchange for premiums paid during the insured's lifetime. Depending on the contract, other events such as terminal or critical illness may also trigger payment.

Types

Term life insurance

Term insurance provides cover for a fixed period (for example 10, 20 or 30 years). It pays a death benefit only if the insured dies during the term, and it is generally the cheapest form of cover per unit of benefit.

Permanent life insurance

Permanent policies — including whole life, universal life and endowment policies — last for the insured's entire life and typically accumulate a cash value that grows on a tax-deferred basis. Premiums are substantially higher than for term cover of the same face amount.

How premiums are set

Insurers use actuarial science and mortality tables to price risk, taking account of the applicant's age, sex, health, smoking status, occupation and medical history through a process called underwriting. Premium income is pooled and invested; the law of large numbers allows the insurer to predict aggregate claims with reasonable accuracy.

History

Precursors existed in Roman burial clubs and medieval guilds. The first modern life office, the Amicable Society for a Perpetual Assurance Office, was founded in London in 1706, and the Society for Equitable Assurances on Lives and Survivorships (1762) pioneered age-based premiums founded on the mortality research of James Dodson. The industry expanded rapidly in the 19th-century United States and Europe, becoming a major channel of long-term savings.
