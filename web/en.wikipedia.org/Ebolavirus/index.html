Ebolavirus

Ebolavirus
Family: Filoviridae
Genome: Negative-sense single-stranded RNA
Species: Six recognised
Natural reservoir: Suspected fruit bats

Ebolavirus is a genus of viruses in the family Filoviridae. Its members are the causative agents of Ebola virus disease (EVD), a severe and often fatal haemorrhagic fever in humans and other primates. The genus includes six species, of which four are known to cause disease in humans.

Species

The species include Zaire ebolavirus (the most virulent and the cause of the 2014–2016 West African epidemic), Sudan ebolavirus, Taï Forest ebolavirus, Bundibugyo ebolavirus, Reston ebolavirus (not known to cause human disease) and Bombali ebolavirus, identified in bats in 2018.

Structure and life cycle

Virions are filamentous and pleomorphic, up to about 14,000 nanometres long, containing a negative-sense RNA genome of roughly 19 kilobases encoding seven proteins. Fruit bats are considered the natural reservoir; spillover to humans occurs through contact with infected animals or their fluids, after which human-to-human transmission proceeds via bodily fluids.

Outbreaks

Ebolaviruses were first recognised in 1976 in simultaneous outbreaks in Nzara (Sudan) and Yambuku (Zaire, now the Democratic Republic of the Congo), the latter near the Ebola River, from which the virus takes its name. The West African epidemic of 2014–2016 was the largest, with over 28,000 cases.
