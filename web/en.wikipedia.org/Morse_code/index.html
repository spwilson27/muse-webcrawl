Morse code

Morse code is a method used in telecommunication to encode text characters as standardized sequences of two different signal durations, called dots and dashes, or dits and dahs. It is named after Samuel Morse, one of the early developers of the system adopted for electrical telegraphy.

History

Samuel Morse and Alfred Vail developed an electrical telegraph system in the 1830s and 1840s in the United States. Vail is credited with much of the work on the code itself, assigning shorter sequences to the most common letters in English — E is a single dot, T a single dash — based on the frequency of type in a printer's type case. The first message sent by Morse's telegraph, "What hath God wrought", was transmitted in 1844 between Washington, D.C., and Baltimore.

A revised version, developed by Friedrich Gerke and adopted in Europe, became the basis of International Morse code, standardized by the International Telecommunication Union. International Morse encodes the 26 Latin letters, ten numerals, and a small set of punctuation and procedural signals (prosigns).

Transmission and use

Morse code can be transmitted as pulses of electric current, tones of radio or audio, or flashes of light. It was the standard mode of radiotelegraph communication for maritime and military services for more than a century; the distress signal SOS (· · · – – – · · ·) became internationally famous. Although commercial Morse services ended in most countries by the end of the 20th century, the code remains popular among amateur radio operators, who call it CW (continuous wave).

Timing

The duration of a dash is three times that of a dot; spaces between elements, letters, and words follow standardized ratios, allowing speed to be measured in words per minute.
