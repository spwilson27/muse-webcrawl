Selim II (Ottoman Turkish: سليم ثانى, Selīm-i sānī; 30 May 1524 – 15 December 1574), also known as Selim the Blond (Sarı Selim) or Selim the Drunkard (Sarhoş Selim), was the Sultan of the Ottoman Empire from 1566 until his death in 1574. He was a son of Suleiman the Magnificent and his wife Hürrem Sultan. Selim had been an unlikely candidate for the throne until his brothers Mehmed and Mustafa died and his elder half-brother Bayezid was executed in the succession struggle of 1561.

Reign

Unlike his father, Selim rarely campaigned in person and left government largely to his able grand vizier, Sokollu Mehmed Pasha. His reign saw the completion of the Selimiye Mosque in Edirne, designed by the royal architect Mimar Sinan and regarded as Sinan's masterpiece. Major events of the reign included the Ottoman conquest of Cyprus from Venice (1570–1571), which provoked the Holy League's destruction of the Ottoman fleet at the Battle of Lepanto in October 1571 — although the fleet was rebuilt within a year and Cyprus remained Ottoman. Ottoman forces also conquered Tunis (finally in 1574), intervened in Yemen and the Caucasus, and suffered a costly defeat at Astrakhan in 1569 in a campaign to dig a canal between the Don and Volga rivers.

Assessment

Traditional Ottoman and European historiography depicted Selim as the first of the "decline" sultans, more devoted to the pleasures of the harem and to wine than to government, and blamed him for the growing influence of palace women — the so-called Sultanate of Women that began with his consort and later queen mother Nurbanu Sultan. Modern historians treat the decline thesis more cautiously, noting that the empire remained formidably powerful, its administration functioned well under Sokollu, and its frontiers still expanded during Selim's eight-year reign. He died in 1574, reportedly after slipping on the marble floor of a bathhouse while ill, and was buried in a mausoleum beside Hagia Sophia; his son Murad III succeeded him.
