Superconducting quantum computing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Superconducting quantum computing is a leading implementation of quantum computing in which qubits are realised as superconducting electronic circuits operated at cryogenic temperatures. The circuits behave as artificial atoms whose discrete energy levels encode quantum information.

Qubit designs

Superconducting qubits derive their nonlinearity from the Josephson effect: a thin insulating barrier between two superconductors forms a Josephson junction, making the circuit an anharmonic oscillator whose two lowest levels serve as the qubit. Common designs include the charge qubit (Cooper-pair box), the flux qubit, the phase qubit, and the transmon, a charge qubit with reduced sensitivity to charge noise that dominates modern processors built by companies such as IBM, Google, and Rigetti.

Operation

Devices are cooled in dilution refrigerators to around 10–15 millikelvin. Microwave pulses manipulate qubit states, resonators mediate coupling and readout, and classical electronics generate and capture signals. Gate times of tens of nanoseconds are fast compared with rival platforms, but coherence times — typically tens to hundreds of microseconds — and error rates require quantum error correction for large-scale fault-tolerant computation.

Milestones

In 2019 Google announced that its 53-qubit Sycamore processor had performed a sampling task claimed to be beyond practical classical reach ("quantum supremacy"), a claim debated by researchers. IBM and others have since demonstrated processors with more than a thousand physical qubits, and the field focuses increasingly on error correction, including surface-code logical qubits with improving fidelity.
