Poincaré–Hopf theorem

In mathematics, the Poincaré–Hopf theorem (also known as the Poincaré–Hopf index formula, Poincaré–Hopf index theorem, or Hopf index theorem) is an important theorem that is used in differential topology. It states that the sum of the indices of the zeros of a vector field on a compact differentiable manifold equals the Euler characteristic of the manifold, provided the vector field has only isolated zeros.

Statement

Let M be a differentiable manifold of dimension n and v a vector field on M with isolated zeroes. If M has boundary, v is taken to point outward at the boundary. Then the sum of the indices of v over all its zeros equals the Euler–Poincaré characteristic χ(M).

History

The theorem was proved for two dimensions by Henri Poincaré and later generalized by Heinz Hopf, after whom the index of a vector field is named.

Consequences

A famous special case is the hairy ball theorem: since the Euler characteristic of the 2-sphere is 2, not zero, every continuous tangent vector field on a sphere must vanish somewhere — a sphere cannot be "combed flat". On a torus, whose Euler characteristic is 0, nowhere-vanishing vector fields do exist.
