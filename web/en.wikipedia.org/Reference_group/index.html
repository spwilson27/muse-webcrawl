Reference group

Individuals evaluate their own status, attitudes and behaviour by comparison with reference groups.

A reference group is a group to which an individual or another group is compared in order to form judgements about their own characteristics — a concept in sociology and social psychology. The term was coined by Herbert Hyman in 1942 and was developed by Robert K. Merton in his theory of reference group behaviour. Reference groups set and enforce standards of conduct and belief, provide the benchmarks against which people measure their achievements and status, and supply the norms by which they judge their own situation — a mechanism underlying the feeling of relative deprivation.

Types

Sociologists distinguish several kinds of reference group. Normative reference groups set standards — such as families, teachers and peer groups that teach a person the values and rules of society. Comparative reference groups provide the yardstick for self-evaluation, as when a worker judges their pay against that of colleagues. A reference group may be a membership group the person belongs to, an aspirational group they wish to join, or a dissociative group whose values they reject and define themselves against. Groups may also be positive or negative reference points, and the anticipated judgements of others — the "generalised other" — can function as an internal reference group even in their absence.

Applications

Reference group theory is used to explain consumer behaviour (people buy brands associated with groups they admire), political attitudes, occupational choice, and the findings of classic studies such as The American Soldier, where satisfaction with promotion depended on comparisons with one's unit rather than objective rates. Marketing and health campaigns deliberately invoke reference groups to shift behaviour, and social media has expanded the range of groups — celebrities, influencers, online communities — against which people compare themselves.
