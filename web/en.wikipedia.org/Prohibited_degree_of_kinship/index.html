Prohibited degree of kinship

A prohibited degree of kinship refers to a degree of consanguinity (blood relatedness) or sometimes affinity between persons that results in a couple being prohibited from marrying. Such prohibitions are found in marriage law across many jurisdictions and religious traditions, and are a central component of incest taboos.

Consanguinity and affinity

Consanguinity describes blood relations: parents and children, siblings, uncles/aunts and nieces/nephews, and cousins. Affinity describes relations created by marriage, such as a spouse's relatives. Degrees are computed in the civil-law (Roman) method by counting generations between the persons through their common ancestor, or in the older canon-law method by counting from the common ancestor down the longer line.

Religious rules

The Hebrew Bible's Book of Leviticus lists prohibited unions, including between close blood relatives and certain affines. Roman Catholic canon law historically prohibited marriage within the fourth degree of consanguinity (third cousins) but since the 1983 Code prohibits marriage in the direct line and up to the fourth degree of the collateral line by the civil computation — effectively second cousins and closer, with dispensations possible for the remoter degrees. Islamic law prohibits marriage with specified relatives named in the Qur'an, including milk kinship (relations through wet-nursing). Hindu law adds rules of sapinda relationship and gotra exogamy in some communities.

Civil law

Nearly all jurisdictions prohibit marriage between parents and children and between siblings. Most also prohibit uncle–niece and aunt–nephew marriages, though some permit them. First-cousin marriage is legal in much of the world, including most of Europe, but is prohibited or restricted in many U.S. states and in China. A few jurisdictions have permitted uncle–niece marriage historically for dynastic or religious reasons.

Rationale

Explanations offered for the prohibitions include the genetic risks of inbreeding, the protection of family structure and minors, and long-standing social norms. Anthropologists have debated the origins of the incest taboo, with theories ranging from innate aversion (the Westermarck effect) to alliance-building through exogamy.
