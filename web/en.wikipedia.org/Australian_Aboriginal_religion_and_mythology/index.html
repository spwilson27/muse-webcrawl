Australian Aboriginal religion and mythology

Rock art sites across Australia record the stories of ancestral beings and their journeys.

Australian Aboriginal religion and mythology encompasses the sacred, spiritual belief systems and narrative traditions of the Aboriginal peoples of Australia, developed over tens of thousands of years. These traditions are centred on the concept often rendered in English as the Dreaming (or Dreamtime), a complex framework linking ancestral beings, the creation of the land, law, kinship and ceremony.

There is no single Aboriginal religion: hundreds of distinct language groups maintained their own bodies of story, ritual and sacred geography, although many themes — ancestral creator beings, songlines traversing the continent, and a deep reciprocal relationship between people and country — are shared widely.

The Dreaming

The Dreaming describes both the distant era in which ancestral beings shaped the world and an ongoing spiritual dimension of existence. During the creative period, ancestral beings — often part human, part animal or natural phenomenon — travelled across a formless land, creating rivers, mountains, waterholes, plants, animals and human society, together with the laws governing them. Their journeys are remembered in songlines: sequences of verses that map routes across the country and function simultaneously as story, law, navigation aid and title deed.

Major ancestral figures

The Rainbow Serpent is among the most widespread ancestral beings, associated with water, fertility, healing and the cycle of seasons. In Arnhem Land traditions, the great mother figure Kunapipi and the Wawalag sisters cycle of ceremonies are central. Other regionally important beings include Baiame and his son Daramulan in the south-east, the Wandjina cloud and rain spirits of the Kimberley, and the Lightning Brothers of the Victoria River district.

Ceremony and art

Ritual life includes initiation ceremonies, increase rites intended to renew species and places, mortuary practices and large inter-group gatherings. Aboriginal art — rock paintings and engravings, bark painting, sand drawing and body decoration — frequently encodes Dreaming narratives, and access to particular stories and images is governed by traditional law, with some knowledge restricted by gender, age or initiation status.

Contact and continuity

European colonisation from 1788 caused catastrophic disruption through dispossession, disease and violence, yet Aboriginal spiritual traditions have persisted and in many places been revitalised. The recognition of native title, notably after the Mabo decision of 1992, acknowledged in Australian law the continuing connection between Aboriginal peoples, their land and their law.
