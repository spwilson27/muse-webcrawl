Quantum computer

A quantum computer is a computer that manipulates information using the principles of quantum mechanics, in particular superposition and entanglement. Unlike a classical computer, which stores information in bits that are always in one of two definite states (0 or 1), a quantum computer uses qubits (quantum bits), which can exist in a superposition of states. Quantum computers are therefore fundamentally different from classical computers and are expected to be able to solve certain problems, such as integer factorisation and quantum simulation, much more efficiently.

Principles

Computation proceeds by preparing qubits in an initial state, applying a sequence of quantum gates (unitary transformations) that form a quantum circuit, and measuring the result. Interference between the amplitudes of the computational paths amplifies correct outcomes and cancels wrong ones. Important algorithms include Shor's algorithm for factorisation and discrete logarithms, which threatens commonly used public-key cryptography, and Grover's algorithm, which gives a quadratic speedup for unstructured search.

Challenges

Qubits are extremely sensitive to their environment, and decoherence and gate errors limit the depth of computations. Quantum error correction, which encodes logical qubits into many physical qubits, is expected to be required for large-scale fault-tolerant machines, at the cost of very large overheads. Physical implementations include superconducting circuits, trapped ions, neutral atoms, photonics and semiconductor spins. Current devices, with tens to thousands of noisy physical qubits, are termed noisy intermediate-scale quantum (NISQ) computers; in 2019 Google claimed a quantum supremacy experiment with its 53-qubit Sycamore processor, a claim that spurred debate and further classical simulations.
