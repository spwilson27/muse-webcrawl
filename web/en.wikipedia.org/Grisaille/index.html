Grisaille

Grisaille (from the French gris, "grey") is a painting technique executed entirely in monochrome or near-monochrome, usually in shades of grey. A grisaille may be a complete work in its own right, or it may be used as an underpainting over which coloured glazes are later applied.

History

Grisaille became prominent in the illuminated manuscripts and panel paintings of the late Middle Ages. Flemish painters of the fifteenth century, including Jan van Eyck and Rogier van der Weyden, used grisaille on the exterior wings of altarpieces, where painted figures imitate stone sculpture. The technique was also employed by Renaissance masters such as Andrea Mantegna and later by Baroque artists including Peter Paul Rubens, who used grisaille oil sketches as preparatory studies.

Technique

The artist works with a limited palette of greys, establishing the full tonal modelling of the composition before colour is introduced. When used as underpainting, the grisaille layer allows a painter to resolve questions of light and shadow separately from questions of hue and chroma, a division of labour later described in the method of optical greys associated with Flemish technique.

Related techniques

Related monochrome techniques include brunaille (in shades of brown) and verdaille (in shades of green). Grisaille is also related to the sculptural effect of trompe-l'œil.
