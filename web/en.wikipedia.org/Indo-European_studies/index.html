Indo-European studies

Indo-European studies (German: Indogermanistik) is a field of linguistics and an interdisciplinary field that deals with the Indo-European languages — both current and extinct — their reconstruction, and the history, culture and hypothesised homeland of the speakers of Proto-Indo-European (PIE). The field was founded in the late 18th and early 19th centuries following William Jones's 1786 observation that Sanskrit, Greek and Latin must have "sprung from some common source", and developed through the comparative method of scholars such as Rasmus Rask, Jacob Grimm (Grimm's law) and Franz Bopp.

Branches of the discipline

  • Comparative linguistics — reconstructing PIE phonology (including the laryngeal theory), morphology and vocabulary.
  • Homeland studies — the Pontic-Caspian steppe ("Kurgan") hypothesis of Marija Gimbutas is the mainstream view, associating late PIE with the Yamnaya culture of c. 3300 BCE; the rival Anatolian hypothesis of Colin Renfrew associates it with early farmers.
  • Indo-European culture — reconstruction of mythology and society (Dumézil's trifunctional hypothesis, the figure of *Dyēus the sky father), aided by archaeology and, since 2015, ancient DNA studies demonstrating large-scale steppe migration into Europe.

Language families studied

The discipline covers the Anatolian, Indo-Iranian, Greek, Italic, Celtic, Germanic, Balto-Slavic, Albanian and Armenian branches, plus the extinct Tocharian languages of the Tarim Basin. Modern scholarship increasingly integrates computational phylogenetics and archaeogenetics with classical philological methods.
