String harmonic

A string harmonic is a string instrument technique which uses the nodes of natural harmonics of a vibrating string to isolate overtones. Playing string harmonics produces high pitched tones, often compared to bells or flutes and described as having a silvery, ethereal quality. Harmonics are marked in music either with a small circle above the note, with diamond-shaped note heads, or by sounding pitch notation.

Natural harmonics

Natural harmonics are produced by lightly touching — but not pressing down — an open string at a nodal point and then plucking or bowing. Touching at the midpoint (the second partial) sounds a note an octave above the open string; at one third of the string length, an octave and a fifth; at one quarter, two octaves. The physics reflects the harmonic series: the touch point forces a node, suppressing partials that do not have a node there.

Artificial harmonics

Artificial (or stopped) harmonics are played on a stopped string: the player presses the string with one finger and touches a node — commonly a perfect fourth above — with another finger, sounding a note two octaves above the stopped pitch. They extend the harmonic range to any pitch and are common in virtuoso violin and cello repertoire.

Use

Composers from Paganini to modern orchestral writers use harmonics for color; guitarists use natural and "pinch" harmonics extensively, and harp harmonics sound an octave above written pitch. On bowed instruments harmonics require precise finger placement and bow contact near the bridge.
