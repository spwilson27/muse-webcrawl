Van de Graaff generator

A Van de Graaff generator is an electrostatic generator that uses a moving insulating belt to accumulate electric charge on a hollow metal sphere, building up very high voltages — from hundreds of thousands of volts in classroom models to several million volts in research machines. It was developed beginning in 1929 by the American physicist Robert J. Van de Graaff at Princeton University and MIT.

Principle of operation

A motor-driven belt of rubber or similar insulating material passes over rollers inside an insulating column. Charge is sprayed onto the belt at the base from a comb of sharp points connected to a power supply (corona discharge), carried up inside the hollow terminal, and removed by a second comb connected to the sphere. Because the electric field inside a conductor is zero, charge can be transferred to the sphere regardless of its existing potential, so charge accumulates on the outer surface until leakage or breakdown limits the voltage. Large research generators enclose the apparatus in pressurised insulating gas to suppress sparking.

Applications

Van de Graaff generators were historically important as power supplies for early particle accelerators used in nuclear physics; tandem Van de Graaff accelerators remain in use for applications such as accelerator mass spectrometry and materials analysis. Smaller generators are ubiquitous as science-museum and classroom demonstrations — famously making a volunteer's hair stand on end as like charges on their body repel one another.
