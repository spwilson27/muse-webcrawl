Tribe

A tribe is a human social group that existed before the development of, or remains outside, states. The term derives from the Latin tribus, one of the three original divisions of the Roman people. In anthropology, tribes have traditionally been characterised as societies organised on the basis of kinship — extended families, clans and lineages — occupying a position in classical social-evolution schemes between the band and the chiefdom or state.

Definitions and debate

Definitions vary widely and are contested. Morton Fried argued that most so-called tribes are "secondary tribes" — products of interaction with states rather than pristine pre-state forms — and Elman Service's influential typology (band, tribe, chiefdom, state) has been criticised for implying a unilinear evolution. Tribal societies are often egalitarian, led by informal or achieved leadership (chiefs or elders) rather than centralised coercive authority, and integrated by segmentary lineage systems, age sets, and shared language, culture and territory.

Modern usage

The word remains legally and politically significant: many Indigenous peoples are officially recognised as tribes (for example, federally recognised tribes in the United States with a degree of sovereignty, and Scheduled Tribes in India). Tribal identity also remains central in parts of Africa, the Middle East and Central Asia. Some anthropologists and Indigenous groups regard the term as pejorative or imprecise, preferring "people", "nation" or community-specific names; in popular usage "tribe" has also been extended metaphorically to tightly knit social, professional or consumer groups.
