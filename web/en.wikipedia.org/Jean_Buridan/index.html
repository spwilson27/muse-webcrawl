Jean Buridan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jean Buridan (French: [byʁidɑ̃]; Latin: Joannes Buridanus; c. 1301 – c. 1359/62) was an influential 14th-century French philosopher. He was a leading figure of the nominalist school and a major contributor to logic, metaphysics, ethics, and natural philosophy. His most famous idea is Buridan's ass, a paradox of free will illustrating a rational agent unable to choose between two equally attractive options.

Life and career

Buridan was born probably in Béthune, France. He was educated at the University of Paris under the scholastic philosopher William of Ockham and became a master of arts there, twice serving as rector of the university (in 1328 and 1340). Unlike many contemporaries, he remained a secular cleric in the faculty of arts rather than proceeding to theology, and taught at Paris for his entire career, influencing a generation of students across Europe.

Impetus theory

In natural philosophy, Buridan developed the theory of impetus, an early step toward the modern concepts of inertia and momentum. He held that a mover impresses into a moving body an impetus proportional to its speed and mass, which keeps it moving and is diminished by resistance and contrary inclination. He applied the idea to the heavens: God, at creation, impressed impetuses in the celestial bodies which continued without need of intelligences or angels to move them, since there was no resistance to corrupt them — a striking anticipation of later mechanics.

Logic and ethics

Buridan's logical works, especially his Summulae de dialectica, advanced the nominalist theory of supposition and consequences. In ethics he argued that the will tends to follow the judgment of reason, being free to suspend decision when reasons seem equal — the situation from which the later example of "Buridan's ass", starving between two equal bales of hay, was drawn; the example does not appear in that form in his writings, though he did discuss a dog choosing between equal portions of food.

Legacy

Buridan's works were printed and widely taught in European universities into the 16th century, and historians of science, notably Pierre Duhem, have credited him as a forerunner of classical mechanics.
