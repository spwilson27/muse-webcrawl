Flavius Aetius

Flavius Aetius (also spelled Aëtius; c. 390 – 21 September 454) was a Roman general and statesman of the closing period of the Western Roman Empire. For two decades (433–454) he was the most powerful man in the western Empire and the principal defender of its frontiers against the barbarian peoples pressing into Gaul and Italy. He is best known for halting the advance of Attila the Hun at the Battle of the Catalaunian Plains in 451, and has often been called "the last of the Romans".

Early life

Aetius was born at Durostorum in Moesia (modern Silistra, Bulgaria), the son of Gaudentius, a Roman general of Scythian origin, and an Italian noblewoman. As a boy he served as a hostage first at the court of Alaric I, king of the Visigoths, and later among the Huns — experiences that gave him personal relationships with barbarian leaders and an intimate knowledge of their warfare that he exploited throughout his career.

Rise to power

After the death of the emperor Honorius in 423, Aetius supported the usurper Joannes and marched a Hunnic army into Italy, arriving too late to save him but in time to negotiate a settlement with the eastern-backed regime of Valentinian III. A struggle for supremacy with the generals Felix and Bonifacius followed; Aetius defeated Bonifacius at Rimini in 432 and, after Bonifacius died of his wounds, became the dominant military figure in the West, with the title of patrician and magister militum.

Campaigns in Gaul

Through the 430s and 440s Aetius campaigned almost continuously in Gaul, fighting the Franks, Burgundians, Alans and Visigoths, frequently using Hunnic auxiliaries. In 437 his Hunnic troops destroyed the Burgundian kingdom of Worms — an event remembered in the Germanic heroic tradition behind the Nibelungenlied. In 440 he settled Alans around Orléans, and in 443 settled Burgundian remnants in Sapaudia (Savoy).

The Catalaunian Plains

In 451 Attila invaded Gaul. Aetius assembled a coalition of Romans, Visigoths under Theodoric I, Alans, Franks and Burgundians, and met Attila near Châlons in one of the largest battles of the ancient world. Theodoric was killed, but Attila withdrew from Gaul. The following year Attila invaded Italy; Aetius, lacking the forces to give battle, harassed his rear, and Attila withdrew after negotiations with Pope Leo I and amid famine and disease in his army.

Death and legacy

Fearing Aetius's power after these successes, the emperor Valentinian III murdered him with his own hands during an audience at Ravenna on 21 September 454 — an act a courtier is said to have called cutting off his right hand with his left. Valentinian himself was assassinated the following year by Aetius's loyal soldiers. With Aetius gone, the Western Empire lost its last effective general, and historians have often treated his death as a turning point in its final collapse.
