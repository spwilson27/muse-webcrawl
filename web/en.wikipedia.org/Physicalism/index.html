Physicalism

FieldMetaphysics, philosophy of mind
ThesisEverything is physical, or supervenes on the physical
Opposed toDualism, idealism, neutral monism
Key figuresOtto Neurath (term), J. J. C. Smart, David Lewis, Frank Jackson, Daniel Stoljar

Physicalism is a philosophical position asserting that everything is physical — that there are no entities or properties beyond those recognized by physics, or that everything supervenes on the physical. It is the dominant form of monism in contemporary philosophy of mind, holding that mental states, consciousness, and all other phenomena are physical phenomena or wholly dependent upon them.

Definitions

Defining physicalism precisely is notoriously difficult. A common formulation is in terms of supervenience: any minimal physical duplicate of the actual world is a duplicate in all respects. Hempel's dilemma presses two problems: define "physical" by current physics, and the thesis is probably false (current physics is incomplete); define it by some ideal future physics, and the thesis becomes unclear or trivial. Physicalists respond with various strategies, including the "via negativa" (the physical is the non-mental) or reference to paradigmatic physical entities.

Forms in philosophy of mind

Reductive physicalism identifies mental states with physical states — in its type-identity form (Place, Smart) or through functionalism, which defines mental states by their causal roles and is usually classified as non-reductive physicalism because mental states can be "multiply realized." Eliminative materialism, associated with Paul and Patricia Churchland, goes further, holding that folk-psychological concepts such as belief may be discarded by mature neuroscience. Anomalous monism (Donald Davidson) combines token identity with denial of strict psychophysical laws.

Objections

Principal objections include the knowledge argument (Frank Jackson's "Mary's room"), conceivability arguments involving philosophical zombies (David Chalmers), Thomas Nagel's critique about subjective experience ("What is it like to be a bat?"), and the "hard problem of consciousness." Physicalists have replied with phenomenal-concept strategies, illusionism, and arguments that such intuitions are unreliable.
