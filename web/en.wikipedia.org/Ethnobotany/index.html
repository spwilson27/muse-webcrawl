Ethnobotany

Medicinal plants collected in a traditional pharmacopoeia

Ethnobotany is the study of a region's plants and their practical uses through the traditional knowledge of a local culture and people. An ethnobotanist thus strives to document the local customs involving the practical uses of the local flora, including for food, medicine, clothing, shelter, fuel, and ritual. The term was coined by the American botanist John William Harshberger in 1895.

History

Although systematic ethnobotany is modern — Richard Evans Schultes is widely regarded as the father of the discipline for his work in the Amazon from the 1940s — the documentation of plant uses is ancient, from the Ebers Papyrus of Egypt to Dioscorides' De Materia Medica, which remained authoritative for over a millennium. Botanists on voyages of exploration recorded indigenous knowledge, sometimes without credit; many modern drugs, including quinine, aspirin (from willow), and artemisinin (identified through Chinese medical texts by Tu Youyou), trace to ethnobotanical leads.

Methods and issues

Fieldwork combines plant collection and voucher specimens with interviews, participant observation, and documentation of local names and classification systems. Ethnobotanical knowledge is eroding as languages and traditional livelihoods disappear, prompting conservation efforts. The field also raises legal and ethical questions about intellectual property: the Convention on Biological Diversity and the Nagoya Protocol seek to ensure that indigenous communities share benefits arising from commercial use of their knowledge, a response to cases of alleged "biopiracy," such as early patents on neem and turmeric.
