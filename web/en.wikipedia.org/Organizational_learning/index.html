Organizational learning

Organizational learning is the process of creating, retaining and transferring knowledge within an organization, so that the organization's behaviour and performance improve with experience. An organization is said to learn when its members detect errors or opportunities and correct them in ways that become embedded in shared routines, systems and culture — beyond the knowledge of any single individual.

Single- and double-loop learning

Chris Argyris and Donald Schön distinguished single-loop learning — correcting errors within existing rules and assumptions — from double-loop learning, in which the governing assumptions themselves are questioned and changed. They also emphasised the gap between "espoused theory" and "theory-in-use", and the defensive routines that block inquiry.

Knowledge creation

Ikujiro Nonaka's model of knowledge creation describes the conversion between tacit and explicit knowledge (socialisation, externalisation, combination, internalisation). Peter Senge's The Fifth Discipline (1990) popularised the "learning organization", built on systems thinking, personal mastery, mental models, shared vision and team learning.

Barriers and practice

Barriers include hierarchy, blame culture, turnover that drains institutional memory, and success that discourages experimentation. Practices associated with organizational learning include after-action reviews, communities of practice, mentoring and systematic knowledge management.
