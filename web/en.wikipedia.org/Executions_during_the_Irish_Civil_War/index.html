During the Irish Civil War (1922–1923), the government of the Irish Free State carried out official executions of anti-Treaty IRA prisoners under emergency legislation. In total, 81 men were executed by firing squad, a number exceeding the 24 republicans executed by the British during the preceding War of Independence.

Background

In September 1922 the Dáil passed the Army (Special Powers) Resolution, authorising military courts to impose the death penalty for offences such as possession of arms. The first executions took place on 17 November 1922, when four anti-Treaty volunteers were shot in Dublin.

Reprisals

After the assassination of pro-Treaty TD Seán Hales on 7 December 1922, the government ordered the reprisal execution of four prominent republican prisoners — Rory O'Connor, Liam Mellows, Richard Barrett and Joe McKelvey — who had been held since the start of the war and were unconnected with the killing. The policy remains one of the most controversial episodes of the conflict.

Legacy

The executions, together with unofficial killings, left a lasting bitterness that shaped Irish politics for generations, hardening the divide between the parties descended from the two sides of the civil war.
