Egyptian chronology

[Image: images/Egyptian_chronology.png — The Abydos King List]

Egyptian chronology is the framework of dates used by Egyptologists to organise the history of ancient Egypt, spanning some three thousand years from the unification of the country around 3100 BC to the Roman conquest in 30 BC. It is built from king lists, dated inscriptions and monuments, astronomical observations recorded in Egyptian texts (notably the heliacal rising of Sirius), genealogies, and synchronisms with neighbouring civilisations, increasingly supplemented by radiocarbon dating.

Structure

Following the Hellenistic historian Manetho, Egyptian history is divided into thirty dynasties, grouped into the Old, Middle and New Kingdoms separated by intermediate periods of fragmentation:

Sources and problems

Key sources include the Palermo Stone annals, the Turin Royal Canon, and temple king lists at Abydos, Saqqara and Karnak, along with Manetho's Aegyptiaca, surviving only in excerpts. Reigns may overlap where rival lines ruled simultaneously, regnal years may be miscounted, and the Sothic (Sirius) dates used to anchor the system depend on the observation point, so that alternative "high", "middle" and "low" chronologies can differ by decades. Radiocarbon studies of museum samples published in 2010 broadly supported the conventional chronology, particularly for the New Kingdom.
