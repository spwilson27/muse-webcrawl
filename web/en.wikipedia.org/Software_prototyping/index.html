Software prototyping

Software prototyping
FieldSoftware engineering, HCI
ApproachesThrowaway (rapid), evolutionary, incremental, extreme prototyping
Fidelity levelsLow (sketches, wireframes) to high (interactive mock-ups)
PurposesRequirements elicitation, usability testing, feasibility, stakeholder communication

Software prototyping is the activity of creating prototypes of software applications—that is, incomplete versions of the software being developed. A prototype typically simulates only a few aspects of the final product and may be entirely different from it. Prototyping has several benefits: designers and implementers can get valuable feedback from users early in the project, clients can compare the prototype with their expectations, and misunderstandings about requirements can be exposed before costly full-scale development.

Types

  • Throwaway (rapid) prototyping: a quick model is built to explore requirements and then discarded; the final system is developed from the clarified specification.
  • Evolutionary prototyping: an initial robust prototype is built and continually refined into the final system, with users evaluating each version.
  • Incremental prototyping: the product is divided into parts, each prototyped and built separately, then combined.
  • Extreme prototyping: used in web development—first a static page model, then functional screens with a simulated service layer, then implementation of real services.

Fidelity and tools

Prototypes range from low-fidelity paper sketches and wireframes, cheap and fast for testing concepts, to high-fidelity interactive mock-ups that closely mimic the final interface. Storyboards, Wizard of Oz experiments (in which a human simulates unimplemented system behaviour), and dedicated tools such as Figma and Axure are widely used. Prototyping is integral to user-centered design, the spiral model, and agile practices such as building a minimum viable product.

Advantages and risks

Advantages include reduced risk of building the wrong product, earlier detection of usability problems, and improved stakeholder engagement. Risks include users mistaking the prototype for a nearly finished product, pressure to ship prototype-quality code, excessive time spent refining the model, and neglect of non-functional requirements such as performance and security that prototypes rarely demonstrate.
