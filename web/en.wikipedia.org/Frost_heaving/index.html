Frost heaving

Frost heaving (or a frost heave) is an upward swelling of soil during freezing conditions caused by a growing accumulation of ice as it draws water toward the freezing front. When the freezing front penetrates soil, water migrates through soil pores toward it and freezes into segregated ice lenses. Because water expands by about 9 percent on freezing and the lenses keep growing as long as water is supplied, the heave can exert enormous pressure — enough to lift roads, fence posts, foundations, and even buildings.

Mechanism

Frost heave requires a frost-susceptible soil (typically silt), a supply of water, and a slowly advancing freezing front. Fine-grained silty soils are most susceptible because their pore sizes support capillary rise while still permitting water flow; clean gravels let water drain and clays conduct it too slowly. Repeated freeze–thaw cycles can ratchet stones and posts progressively upward, a process that also sorts stones in patterned ground in periglacial environments.

Damage and prevention

Frost heaving cracks pavements, buckles railway tracks, tilts utility poles, and damages shallow foundations. Prevention measures include extending foundations below the frost line, replacing frost-susceptible soil with coarse gravel, insulating the ground, and ensuring drainage so water cannot collect near foundations.
