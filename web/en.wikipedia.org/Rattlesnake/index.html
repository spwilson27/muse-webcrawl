Rattlesnake


Placeholder image: Rattlesnake

Rattlesnakes are venomous pit vipers of the genera Crotalus and Sistrurus, native to the Americas. They are named for the rattle at the end of the tail, made of interlocking keratin segments, which the snake vibrates as a warning.

Biology

Like other pit vipers, rattlesnakes have heat-sensing pit organs between the eye and nostril that detect warm-blooded prey. Their venom, delivered by hinged hollow fangs, is typically haemotoxic, though some species (notably the Mojave rattlesnake) have strong neurotoxic components. A new rattle segment is added at each moult. Rattlesnakes give birth to live young.

Species and distribution

There are more than 60 recognised species and subspecies, ranging from southern Canada to Argentina, with the greatest diversity in the southwestern United States and Mexico. The eastern and western diamondbacks are the largest species. Bites are dangerous but fatalities are rare where antivenom is available.
