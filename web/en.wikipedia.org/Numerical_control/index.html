Numerical control

Numerical control (NC) is the automated control of machining tools by means of a program of coded instructions, rather than by handwheels, levers, or hard-wired automation. When the control is implemented by a computer it is called computer numerical control (CNC). NC transformed manufacturing by making it practical to machine complex, precise parts repeatedly and to change production by changing the program.

History

Modern NC began after the Second World War. John T. Parsons conceived of machining helicopter rotor blade templates from computed coordinates, and, working with MIT's Servomechanisms Laboratory under U.S. Air Force funding, demonstrated a milling machine driven by punched-tape instructions in 1952. Early programs were written in the APT language. Through the 1960s and 1970s, minicomputers and then microprocessors replaced hard-wired controllers, giving rise to modern CNC.

Operation

Programs are usually written in G-code (RS-274), specifying tool paths, spindle speeds, feed rates, and tool changes; CAM software generates this code from CAD models. Servo or stepper drives move the machine's axes and compare commanded with actual position. CNC now controls lathes, mills, routers, grinders, laser and waterjet cutters, and many 3D printers.

Impact

CNC reduced setup times, improved repeatability to tolerances of micrometres, and enabled geometries — such as turbine blades and sculptured moulds — that are impractical by manual machining.
