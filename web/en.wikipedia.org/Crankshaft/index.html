Crankshaft

Type: Mechanical component
Function: Converts reciprocating motion to rotation
Used in: Piston engines, compressors, pumps

A crankshaft is a mechanical component used in a piston engine to convert the reciprocating motion of the pistons into rotational motion. It consists of a series of crankpins and crank arms, together called cranks, to which the connecting rods attach, supported by main journals running in the engine block's bearings.

Design

The geometry of the crankshaft — stroke length, firing order, and arrangement of counterweights — determines the engine's displacement, balance, and smoothness. Counterweights offset the mass of each crank throw to reduce vibration. Crankshafts are usually forged from steel or cast from ductile iron, then machined and hardened; fillets between journals and webs are rolled or treated to resist fatigue cracking, since the shaft endures severe alternating torsional and bending stresses.

History

Crank-and-connecting-rod mechanisms appeared in Roman-era mills and were described in detail by the medieval engineer al-Jazari, whose 1206 water-raising machine used a crankshaft in recognizable form. The crankshaft became indispensable with the steam engine and then the internal combustion engine.

Associated parts

The crankshaft drives, directly or by belt, chain, or gears, the camshaft, oil pump, and accessories, and carries a flywheel or flexplate at one end to smooth power delivery and a harmonic damper at the other to absorb torsional vibration.
