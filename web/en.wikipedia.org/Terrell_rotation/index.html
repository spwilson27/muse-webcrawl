Terrell rotation

Terrell rotation (also called the Penrose–Terrell effect or Terrell inversion) is the apparent rotation of a rapidly moving object as seen by a distant observer, caused by the differing travel times of light from different parts of the object. It means that the Lorentz contraction of a moving object is not directly visible in a photograph.

History

The effect was derived independently in 1959 by Roger Penrose, who showed that a moving sphere always presents a circular outline, and by James Terrell, who generalized the result: a passing object appears rotated by an angle whose sine equals the object's speed as a fraction of the speed of light, rather than simply flattened along its direction of motion.

Explanation

Lorentz contraction is defined for the positions of an object's parts at a single instant in the observer's frame. A photograph, however, records photons that arrive simultaneously but were emitted at different times: light from the far side of the object left earlier, when the object was further back. Combining these emission positions with the contraction produces an image equivalent to that of an uncontracted object rotated through the Terrell angle, sin θ = v/c.

Consequences

For a moving cube photographed from the side, the trailing face becomes visible, exactly as if the cube had rotated. At relativistic speeds a moving sphere can show parts of its far side. The effect also involves aberration and Doppler shifts in a complete treatment of relativistic imaging.
