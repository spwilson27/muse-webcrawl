Goulash Communism (Hungarian: gulyáskommunizmus), also known as Kádárism, refers to the variety of state socialism practiced in the Hungarian People's Republic under János Kádár from the early 1960s until 1989. Named after the Hungarian stew goulash — implying a mixed, distinctively Hungarian recipe — it combined continued one-party rule and a command economy with relatively liberal cultural policies and limited market mechanisms, giving Hungary the reputation of "the happiest barracks in the camp" among Eastern Bloc states.

Origins

After the Hungarian Revolution of 1956 was crushed by Soviet intervention, Kádár initially ruled through repression. From about 1961–62, he reversed course with the slogan "whoever is not against us is with us" — a pointed inversion of the earlier Rákosi-era line — releasing prisoners, easing travel and cultural restrictions, and seeking passive acquiescence rather than active loyalty.

Economic reforms

The system's centrepiece was the New Economic Mechanism of 1968, which introduced market elements — profit incentives, partially freed prices, and scope for small private businesses — into the planned economy. Agriculture, based on cooperatives that tolerated private household plots, became a relative success, and consumer goods were more available than elsewhere in the bloc. From the 1970s, though, the reforms were partly rolled back and living standards were maintained increasingly through Western loans, building up a large hard-currency debt.

End

Economic stagnation in the 1980s eroded the system's legitimacy. Kádár was removed in 1988, and Hungary negotiated a peaceful transition to democracy in 1989.
