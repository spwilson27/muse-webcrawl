Hamiltonian economic program

The Hamiltonian economic program was the set of financial and economic measures designed and promoted by Alexander Hamilton, the first United States Secretary of the Treasury, during the presidency of George Washington (1789–1795). It aimed to put the young republic's finances on a sound footing, establish public credit, and encourage commerce and manufacturing.

Components

In his Report on Public Credit (1790), Hamilton proposed that the federal government fund the national debt at par and assume the remaining war debts of the states, binding creditors to the new Union. His Report on a National Bank led to the chartering of the First Bank of the United States in 1791. His Report on Manufactures (1791) advocated protective tariffs, bounties, and other support for infant industries, though Congress enacted only parts of it. An excise tax on distilled spirits, intended to service the debt, provoked the Whiskey Rebellion of 1794.

Opposition

The program was fiercely opposed by Thomas Jefferson and James Madison, who objected to assumption, the bank's constitutionality, and the growth of federal power and a "monied interest". The controversy helped crystallise the first American party system, pitting Hamilton's Federalists against the Democratic-Republicans. The assumption fight was resolved by the Compromise of 1790, which paired assumption with locating the permanent capital on the Potomac.

Legacy

Hamilton's system established federal creditworthiness, a stable currency framework, and precedents for broad construction of federal power that were later upheld in cases such as McCulloch v. Maryland.
