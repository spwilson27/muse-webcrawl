Adaptive cruise control

Adaptive cruise control (ACC) is a driver-assistance system for road vehicles that automatically adjusts the vehicle's speed to maintain a safe following distance from vehicles ahead. Unlike conventional cruise control, which holds a fixed speed, ACC uses sensors to detect traffic and slow down or accelerate as conditions require.

Technology

ACC systems typically use radar mounted behind the grille, and in some designs lidar or camera systems, to measure the distance and relative speed of a lead vehicle. A control unit adjusts the throttle and brakes to hold a driver-selected time gap — commonly between one and three seconds. More advanced "stop-and-go" systems can bring the car to a complete halt in traffic and resume automatically, while simpler systems operate only above a minimum speed.

History and development

Precursors appeared in the 1990s, including Mitsubishi's distance-warning systems in Japan and laser- and radar-based systems from Toyota, Mercedes-Benz (Distronic, 1998), and others. ACC is now widely available across vehicle classes and is a building block of higher-level driving automation, often combined with lane-centering assistance.
