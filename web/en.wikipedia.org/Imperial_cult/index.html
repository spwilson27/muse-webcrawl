Imperial cult

An imperial cult is a form of state religion in which an emperor or a dynasty of emperors — or rulers in another form — are worshipped as demigods or deities. Such cults served to unify diverse peoples under a common loyalty and to sacralize political authority. The best-known example is the imperial cult of ancient Rome, but similar institutions existed in pharaonic Egypt, imperial China, Japan, and pre-Columbian empires.

Rome

The Roman imperial cult developed from Hellenistic ruler worship and Roman veneration of ancestors. Julius Caesar was formally deified after his assassination, and his heir Augustus carefully promoted worship of his genius (guardian spirit) and of deceased, deified emperors (divi). In the provinces, temples to "Rome and Augustus" gave local elites a way to demonstrate loyalty to the empire. Emperors were usually deified only after death by a vote of the Senate (apotheosis), though some, such as Caligula and Domitian, demanded divine honors while alive. Refusal to participate in the cult contributed to the persecution of Christians, who rejected emperor worship.

Other examples

In Egypt, pharaohs were regarded as gods incarnate for three millennia. In China, emperors ruled by the Mandate of Heaven and offered sacrifices to Heaven and their ancestors, while deceased emperors received posthumous temple worship. In Japan, the emperor was held to descend from the sun goddess Amaterasu, and State Shinto promoted imperial divinity until Hirohito's 1946 declaration renouncing it. Inca and Aztec rulers likewise combined political and divine status.

Function and decline

Imperial cults legitimized rule, provided a focus for loyalty across linguistic and ethnic divisions, and channeled elite competition into priesthoods and festivals. The Roman cult faded in the fourth century AD as Christianity became the religion of the empire, though echoes persisted in Byzantine court ceremonial.
