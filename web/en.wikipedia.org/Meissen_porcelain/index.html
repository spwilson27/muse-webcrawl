Meissen porcelain

Meissen porcelain was the first hard-paste porcelain produced in Europe, developed in 1708 in Meissen, Saxony, and manufactured at the royal factory established there in 1710. For half a century Meissen dominated the European porcelain market, setting the standard for tableware and figurative sculpture. Its trademark — two crossed swords in underglaze blue — is one of the oldest trademarks still in use.

Discovery

Augustus the Strong, Elector of Saxony and King of Poland, was obsessed with porcelain ("the white gold") and with gold-making. He detained the alchemist Johann Friedrich Böttger, who, working with the scientist Ehrenfried Walther von Tschirnhaus, discovered in 1708 the recipe for true hard-paste porcelain using kaolin, alabaster and feldspathic materials, fired at very high temperatures. Tschirnhaus died that year; Böttger perfected the process, and in 1710 production began in the Albrechtsburg castle at Meissen under strict secrecy. The arcanum leaked within a decade as workers defected to Vienna and beyond, but Meissen retained technical and artistic leadership.

Golden age

Under the painter Johann Gregor Höroldt, Meissen developed brilliant enamel colours and chinoiserie decoration; from 1731 the model-master Johann Joachim Kändler created the factory's famous figurines — commedia dell'arte characters, animals, miners and court figures — and grand table services such as the Swan Service (1737–42) for Count Brühl, comprising some 2,200 pieces. Famous patterns include the Blue Onion pattern (actually depicting pomegranates and peaches) and "Chinese Tree". War disrupted production — Frederick the Great seized the factory during the Seven Years' War — and leadership gradually passed to Sèvres in France, but Meissen remained prestigious and innovative.

Modern era

The state factory (Staatliche Porzellan-Manufaktur Meissen) survived both world wars and the East German period, and continues today producing hand-painted porcelain, reproductions of historic models and contemporary designs. Genuine pieces are identifiable by the crossed-swords mark, used with minor variations since the 1720s.
