Projection bias

In behavioural economics, projection bias is the tendency to falsely assume that one's present preferences, feelings, or states will remain the same in the future, leading people to over-project their current state onto predictions of their future tastes and behaviour. Formally, individuals are assumed to exaggerate the degree to which their future preferences will resemble their current preferences.

FieldBehavioural economics, psychology
Key researchersGeorge Loewenstein, Ted O'Donoghue, Matthew Rabin
Related biasesHot–cold empathy gap, impact bias, present bias

Definition and model

The term was given a formal model by economists George Loewenstein, Ted O'Donoghue and Matthew Rabin in their 2003 paper "Projection Bias in Predicting Future Utility". In their framework, a person in a current state s predicts the utility they will derive from consumption in a future state by weighting the true future utility with the utility they would get in their current state, with a parameter measuring the strength of the bias.

Examples

Common illustrations include shopping for groceries while hungry and over-purchasing food one later does not want; ordering menu items for a group days in advance in ways swayed by current appetite or weather; choosing future activities or purchases under the weather conditions one is currently experiencing (for example, buying convertibles or real estate based on the weather on the day of viewing); and catalog orders varying with current weather, as documented in studies of clothing purchases and returns.

Relation to other concepts

Projection bias is closely related to the hot–cold empathy gap, in which people in a "cold" (calm) state underestimate the influence of "hot" (visceral) states and vice versa. It also resembles the psychological phenomenon of false consensus and the empathy gaps that affect planning for medical care, saving, and addiction.
