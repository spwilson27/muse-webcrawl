Uracil (symbol U or Ura) is one of the four nucleobases in the nucleic acid RNA that are represented by the letters A, G, C and U. The others are adenine (A), cytosine (C) and guanine (G). In RNA, uracil binds to adenine via two hydrogen bonds. In DNA, the uracil is replaced by thymine (T). Uracil is a demethylated form of thymine; the substitution is thought to make DNA more stable and easier to repair, since cytosine can spontaneously deaminate to uracil, allowing repair enzymes to distinguish such damage from genuine uracil.

Properties

Uracil is a planar, unsaturated compound with the ability to absorb light, and is a weak acid compared to its neutral pH of around 7.4 in solution — its first pKa is about 9.45. It is a naturally occurring pyrimidine derivative, first isolated in 1900 by Alberto Ascoli from yeast nuclein by hydrolysis. Uracil can be found in RNA, where it forms the nucleoside uridine when attached to a ribose sugar, and uridine triphosphate (UTP) serves both as an RNA precursor and as an energy-transfer molecule in carbohydrate metabolism (in the form of UDP-glucose).

Synthesis and reactions

In the cell, uracil is synthesised de novo as part of pyrimidine biosynthesis: carbamoyl phosphate and aspartate are combined to form the pyrimidine ring, which is oxidised to orotate and then decarboxylated to give uridine monophosphate (UMP). In the laboratory, uracil can be prepared by the condensation of urea with malic acid in fuming sulfuric acid, or by reacting urea with propiolic acid derivatives. Uracil undergoes electrophilic substitution readily at the 5-position, which is exploited in the manufacture of the anticancer drug 5-fluorouracil, in which a fluorine atom substitutes for hydrogen at that position and interferes with DNA synthesis in rapidly dividing cells.

Occurrence beyond Earth

Uracil has been detected in the Murchison meteorite and reported in samples returned from asteroid Ryugu, findings cited in discussions of the extraterrestrial delivery of prebiotic organic molecules. In 2023, analyses of Ryugu samples confirmed uracil among other organic compounds, supporting the idea that nucleobases may form in interstellar and asteroidal environments.

Uses

Beyond its biological role, uracil and its derivatives are used in pharmaceuticals (5-fluorouracil, tegafur, and the herbicide bromacil), as biochemical reagents, and as a standard in studies of RNA structure and evolution. Uracil is also important in molecular biology because the enzyme uracil-DNA glycosylase removes uracil that arises in DNA from cytosine deamination, a key DNA-repair pathway.
