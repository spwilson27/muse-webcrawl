Arbitrage

Arbitrage is the practice of profiting from price differences of the same, or an equivalent, asset in different markets or forms. An arbitrageur simultaneously buys and sells the asset so as to lock in the difference, ideally without risk or net investment. In efficient markets arbitrage opportunities are small and short-lived, because arbitrage trading itself pushes prices back into line.

Types

Common forms include spatial arbitrage (buying where a price is lower and selling where it is higher), triangular arbitrage among currency exchange rates, merger arbitrage (buying shares of an acquisition target at a discount to the agreed takeover price), statistical arbitrage based on quantitative models of related securities, and convertible arbitrage involving convertible bonds and the underlying stock. In practice, most real-world "arbitrage" carries some risk — execution, liquidity or model risk — and is not the riskless ideal of textbook theory.

Role in markets

Arbitrage links prices across markets and instruments, enforcing relationships such as the law of one price and put–call parity, and thereby contributes to market efficiency. The no-arbitrage principle is also the foundation of modern derivatives pricing, including the Black–Scholes model.
