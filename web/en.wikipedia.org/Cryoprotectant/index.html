Cryoprotectant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A cryoprotectant is a substance used to protect biological tissue from freezing damage. Ice crystals formed during freezing can rupture cell membranes and destroy cells, but cryoprotectants reduce ice formation and moderate the harmful concentration of solutes as water freezes out. Arctic and Antarctic insects, fish, and amphibians naturally produce cryoprotectants — such as glycerol, sugars, and antifreeze proteins — in their bodies to survive winter temperatures.

Types

Cryoprotectants are commonly divided into penetrating agents, which pass through the cell membrane and act intracellularly, and non-penetrating agents, which remain outside the cell. Important penetrating cryoprotectants include dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), glycerol, and ethylene glycol; non-penetrating agents include sugars such as trehalose and sucrose, and polymers such as hydroxyethyl starch.

Applications

Cryoprotectants are essential to cryopreservation, allowing the long-term storage of sperm, eggs, embryos, blood products, stem cells, and other cell suspensions, as well as assisted reproduction and cell banking. Their use is a balance between protection and toxicity: at the high concentrations needed for vitrification — freezing without ice formation — cryoprotectants themselves can damage cells, a problem known as cryoprotectant toxicity that remains an obstacle to preserving whole organs.
