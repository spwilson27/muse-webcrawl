Optical printer

An optical printer is a device used in filmmaking, consisting of one or more film projectors mechanically linked to a film camera, that allows filmmakers to re-photograph film footage frame by frame. It was the principal tool of visual effects and film manipulation for most of the twentieth century, used for compositing elements, creating transitions such as fades and dissolves, repositioning or resizing images, producing slow or fast motion, and making matte shots and titles.

How it works

In a simple set-up the projector presents a frame of the original film; the camera, with its shutter synchronised, photographs it onto new stock, often with filters, masks or additional projector heads contributing foregrounds, backgrounds or travelling mattes. More advanced printers — such as the Acme-Dunn printer developed in the 1940s and later aerial-image and computer-controlled machines — allowed multiple passes with precise registration, enabling complex composites like those in Star Wars (1977), for which industrial Light & Magic's work with optical printers won widespread acclaim.

Decline and legacy

From the 1990s digital compositing progressively replaced optical printing, offering greater flexibility and avoiding generational loss. Optical printers remain in use for film restoration, format conversion, duplication of archival material and by experimental filmmakers who value their photochemical character.
