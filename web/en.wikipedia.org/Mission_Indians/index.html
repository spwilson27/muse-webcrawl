Mission Indians

Mission Indians is a term used for the Indigenous peoples of California who were relocated, largely by coercion, to live and work at the Spanish missions established in Alta California between 1769 and 1823. The name is also applied more specifically to the federally recognised tribes of Southern California descended from mission communities, grouped historically around missions San Luis Rey, San Gabriel, San Juan Capistrano, San Fernando, and others.

History

Under the mission system administered by Franciscan friars, Native people — including Luiseño, Gabrieliño (Tongva), Juaneño (Acjachemen), Fernandeño, Cahuilla, Kumeyaay, and Chumash peoples — were gathered into mission settlements, baptised, and required to adopt European agriculture, crafts, and Catholic religious practice. Disease, harsh discipline, and disruption of traditional subsistence caused catastrophic population decline during the mission era.

After secularisation

Following Mexican secularisation of the missions in the 1830s, mission lands were largely granted to private ranch owners, and many Native communities lost their land base. In 1891 the United States passed the Mission Indian Act, which authorised the establishment of reservations for Southern California mission tribes, creating many of the small reservations that exist today.

Modern tribes

Today, Mission Indian tribes include federally recognised nations such as the Pala Band of Mission Indians, the Pechanga Band of Luiseño Indians, the Morongo Band of Mission Indians, and the San Manuel Band of Mission Indians. Several operate gaming enterprises under the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act, while others, including many Tongva and Acjachemen communities, continue to seek federal recognition.
