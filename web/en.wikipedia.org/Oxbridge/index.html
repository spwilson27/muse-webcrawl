Oxbridge

Comprises: University of Oxford; University of Cambridge

Founded: Oxford by 1096; Cambridge 1209

Structure: Collegiate universities

Oxbridge is a portmanteau of Oxford and Cambridge, the two oldest universities in the English-speaking world. The term is used to refer to them collectively, in contrast to other British universities, and more broadly as an adjective describing their shared collegiate structure, tutorial and supervision teaching, and dominant position in British public life.

Both universities are federations of self-governing colleges — 39-plus at Oxford and 31 at Cambridge — which handle admissions, accommodation and small-group teaching, while the central university provides lectures, laboratories, libraries and examinations. Students apply to a college, and undergraduates are taught in Oxford "tutorials" or Cambridge "supervisions", typically in groups of one to three.

History

Teaching existed at Oxford by 1096, and the university grew rapidly after 1167 when English students were recalled from Paris; Cambridge was founded in 1209 by scholars fleeing hostility in Oxford. For centuries the two were England's only universities, closely tied to the Church and the training of clergy, and religious tests restricted membership until the 19th century. Their ancient rivalry is celebrated in the Boat Race (first held in 1829), the Varsity Match in rugby, and competitions between counterpart colleges.

Admissions and debate

Because applicants to UK universities apply through UCAS to a maximum of five courses, and because Oxford and Cambridge have separate early deadlines (15 October) and admission tests and interviews, candidates may apply to only one of the two in a given year. The universities' prestige and the disproportionate share of their students drawn from private schools and from the south of England have made Oxbridge admissions a perennial subject of debate about social mobility and access. Both run outreach and "contextual" admissions programmes, and state-school entrants have risen to roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of UK intakes in recent years.

Influence

Oxbridge alumni include a large share of British prime ministers (the majority of them Oxford-educated), senior judges, civil servants, and Nobel laureates. The word "Oxbridge" itself appears in print from the mid-19th century — Thackeray used it for a fictional university in Pendennis (1849) — and has since spawned imitations such as "Doxbridge" (Durham added) and "Loxbridge" (London added) in discussions of educational concentration.
