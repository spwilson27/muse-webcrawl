Pugachev's Rebellion
Date: 1773–1775
Location: Russian Empire (Volga and Ural regions)
Leader: Yemelyan Pugachev
Result: Rebellion suppressed; Pugachev executed

Pugachev's Rebellion (1773–1775), also called the Peasants' War of 1773–1775 or Cossack Rebellion, was the largest peasant revolt in the history of the Russian Empire. It was led by Yemelyan Pugachev, a Yaik Cossack who claimed to be the murdered emperor Peter III, husband of Catherine the Great.

Origins

The revolt began among the Yaik Cossacks, whose traditional privileges were being eroded by the centralising state. Serfs suffering under heavy obligations, factory workers in the Urals, and non-Russian peoples such as the Bashkirs joined the rising. Pugachev promised the serfs freedom, land and the abolition of taxes and recruitment.

Course of the war

Pugachev's army captured a string of fortresses and the town of Samara, besieged Orenburg, and in July 1774 stormed Kazan, burning much of the city. At its height the rebellion threatened Moscow itself. Regular troops under generals including Alexander Suvorov were redeployed from the war against the Ottoman Empire, and after defeats near Tsaritsyn Pugachev was betrayed by his own Cossacks and delivered to the authorities in September 1774.

Aftermath

Pugachev was executed in Moscow on 21 January 1775. Catherine responded with punitive expeditions and administrative reforms: the Yaik River was renamed the Ural, the Yaik Cossacks became the Ural Cossack Host, and the provincial reform of 1775 tightened state control over the countryside. The rebellion haunted the Russian imagination and inspired Pushkin's historical study The History of Pugachev and his novel The Captain's Daughter.
