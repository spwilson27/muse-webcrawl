Gini coefficient

The Gini coefficient (also called the Gini index or Gini ratio) is a measure of statistical dispersion intended to represent the income inequality or wealth inequality within a nation or any other group of people. It was developed by the Italian statistician Corrado Gini and published in his 1912 paper Variability and Mutability.

The coefficient is most often defined mathematically on the basis of the Lorenz curve, which plots the proportion of the total income of the population that is cumulatively earned by the bottom x% of the population. The Gini coefficient is equal to the area between the line of perfect equality and the Lorenz curve, divided by the total area under the line of equality.

Interpretation

A Gini coefficient of 0 expresses perfect equality, where all values are the same (for example, where everyone has exactly the same income). A Gini coefficient of 1 (or 100%) expresses maximal inequality among values, for example where a single person holds all the income. In practice, income Gini coefficients for countries typically range from about 0.25 for the most egalitarian European states to above 0.60 for some highly unequal countries.

Applications and limitations

The World Bank and other institutions publish Gini estimates for most countries, and the measure is widely used in economics, sociology, and public policy to track inequality over time. Criticisms include that identical Gini values can arise from very different income distributions, that the measure is relatively insensitive to changes at the extremes of the distribution, and that estimates depend heavily on the quality and definition of the underlying survey data (income versus consumption, before or after taxes and transfers).
