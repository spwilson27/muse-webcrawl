Displaced persons camp

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A displaced persons camp (DP camp) is a temporary facility for displaced persons, typically refugees. The term is most strongly associated with the camps established across Germany, Austria and Italy at the end of the Second World War, which housed the millions of people uprooted by the conflict: liberated concentration camp survivors, former forced labourers, prisoners of war and people fleeing the advance of the Red Army.

Post-war DP camps

At the end of the war in 1945 there were an estimated 11 million displaced persons in Europe. Most were repatriated quickly, but over a million people — including Poles, Ukrainians, Balts, Yugoslavs and Jewish Holocaust survivors — refused or were unable to return to their home countries, many of which had come under communist rule. The camps were initially administered by the Allied military authorities and the United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration (UNRRA), and later by the International Refugee Organization.

Conditions varied widely. Camps such as Bergen-Belsen (for Jewish DPs) and Föhrenwald became small towns with schools, newspapers, political organisations and religious life. Jewish survivors organised to press for emigration to Mandatory Palestine; most Jewish DPs ultimately emigrated to Israel after its founding in 1948 or to the United States under the Displaced Persons Act of 1948.

Closure and legacy

Most DP camps had closed by the early 1950s; Föhrenwald, the last, closed in 1957. The post-war DP experience shaped modern international refugee law, including the 1951 Refugee Convention and the principle of non-refoulement. The term is still used today for facilities housing internally displaced persons in conflicts around the world.
