Policing by consent

OriginUnited Kingdom, 1829
Associated withSir Robert Peel; Peelian principles
Core ideaPolice legitimacy rests on public approval

Policing by consent is a philosophy of law enforcement, originating in the United Kingdom, in which the power of the police derives from the common consent of the public rather than from the power of the state. It holds that police officers are citizens in uniform whose authority depends on maintaining public approval, respect, and cooperation.

Peelian principles

The philosophy is traditionally summarized in nine principles associated with Sir Robert Peel, founder of the Metropolitan Police in 1829, and his first commissioners. They state that the basic mission of police is to prevent crime and disorder; that the ability of the police to perform their duties depends on public approval; that police must secure the willing cooperation of the public; and that the minimum degree of force should always be used.

Practice

British constables traditionally patrol unarmed, and the model emphasizes legitimacy and procedural justice over militarized enforcement. Similar consent-based philosophies influenced policing in other Commonwealth countries. Critics note that consent can be unevenly distributed across communities and that the principles were codified retrospectively rather than written by Peel himself.

Related models

Policing by consent is closely related to community policing and is contrasted with paramilitary or purely reactive models of law enforcement.
