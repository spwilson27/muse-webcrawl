Judicial disqualification

Judicial disqualification, also referred to as recusal, is the act of a judge abstaining from participation in an official action such as a legal proceeding due to a conflict of interest of the presiding court official or administrative officer. The term is used mainly in the legal system of the United States.

Grounds

Applicable statutes or canons of ethics may provide standards for recusal in a given proceeding or matter. Common grounds include a personal or financial interest in the outcome, prior involvement in the case as a lawyer or witness, a close relationship with a party, and bias or the appearance of bias. Federal law in the United States (28 U.S.C. § 455) requires a judge to disqualify himself "in any proceeding in which his impartiality might reasonably be questioned," as well as in specified situations such as holding a financial interest or having expressed an opinion on the merits.

Procedure and consequences

A judge may recuse voluntarily, or a party may move for disqualification, with the decision typically made by the challenged judge or, on appeal, by a reviewing court. If a judge is disqualified, another judge is assigned. Failure to recuse where required can be grounds for reversal of the judgment on appeal. For appellate courts, a recusal leaves the remaining panel to decide; for the US Supreme Court, justices decide their own recusals individually and historically give no reasons.
