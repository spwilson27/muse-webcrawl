The saguaro (Carnegiea gigantea) is a tree-like cactus species in the monotypic genus Carnegiea, native to the Sonoran Desert of Arizona, the Mexican state of Sonora, and a small area of California. With its columnar trunk and upturned arms, the saguaro is an iconic symbol of the American Southwest and appears on Arizona license plates; its blossom is Arizona's state wildflower.

Description

Saguaros grow extremely slowly — a ten-year-old plant may be only a few centimetres tall — but can eventually reach 12 to 18 metres and live for 150 to 200 years. They typically begin growing arms at 50–70 years of age. A pleated, spiny skin expands to store water after rains; a fully hydrated plant can weigh several tonnes. The shallow, wide-spreading root system is anchored by a taproot, and a woody internal rib skeleton supports the trunk even after death.

Ecology

White flowers open at night in late spring and are pollinated by bats, bees and white-winged doves; the red summer fruit is a traditional food of the Tohono O'odham people. Gila woodpeckers and gilded flickers excavate nest holes ("saguaro boots") later used by owls and other animals. Nurse plants such as palo verde shelter seedlings from sun and frost.

Conservation

Saguaros are protected by Arizona law and by Saguaro National Park near Tucson. Threats include habitat loss, theft, invasive buffelgrass-fuelled wildfire, and hotter droughts that have reduced seedling survival in recent decades.
