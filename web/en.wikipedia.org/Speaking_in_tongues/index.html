Speaking in tongues

Speaking in tongues
Also known as: Glossolalia
Biblical sources: Acts 2; 1 Corinthians 12–14
Modern movements: Pentecostalism, Charismatic movement

Speaking in tongues, also known as glossolalia, is a practice in which a person utters speech-like sounds or words believed by practitioners to be a language given by the Holy Spirit and not previously known to the speaker. It is a central practice of Pentecostal and charismatic Christianity, and phenomena described similarly occur in some other religions.

Biblical accounts

In the New Testament, Acts 2 describes the apostles at Pentecost speaking so that visitors from many nations heard them in their own languages (often interpreted as xenoglossy, speaking real unlearned languages). In 1 Corinthians 12–14, Paul discusses tongues as one of the spiritual gifts (charismata), valuing it below prophecy unless an interpretation is given, and regulating its use in worship.

Modern Pentecostalism

The modern movement began in the early 20th century, notably with Charles Parham's Bethel Bible School in Topeka (1901) and the Azusa Street Revival in Los Angeles (1906) led by William J. Seymour. Most classical Pentecostal denominations teach tongues as the initial physical evidence of baptism in the Holy Spirit. From the 1960s the practice spread into mainline Protestant and Catholic churches as the charismatic movement.

Linguistic and theological perspectives

Linguists who have studied recordings generally conclude that glossolalia is not a real language: it has the sound patterns of the speaker's native tongue but lacks the consistent syntax and semantics of language. Theologians differ: Pentecostals affirm its supernatural character, while cessationists hold that the gift ended with the apostolic age.
