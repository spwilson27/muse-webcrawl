Hoax

A hoax is a deliberately fabricated falsehood made to masquerade as truth, typically intended to deceive a wide audience for amusement, publicity, financial gain, or social commentary. Hoaxes range from pranks and urban legends to elaborate scientific frauds and media manipulations; unlike simple lies, a successful hoax depends on its victims believing and spreading it.

Etymology and characteristics

The word "hoax" emerged in the late 18th century, probably as a contraction of "hocus" (as in hocus-pocus). Hoaxes commonly exploit trust in institutions, media hunger for sensational stories, or the audience's prior beliefs. April Fools' Day jokes by media outlets — such as the BBC's 1957 spaghetti-tree broadcast — form a sanctioned subset of the genre.

Famous examples

Notable hoaxes include the Great Moon Hoax of 1835, in which the New York Sun reported life on the Moon; the Piltdown Man fossil fraud, exposed in 1953; Orson Welles's 1938 War of the Worlds radio drama (whose panic was itself exaggerated); the Cardiff Giant (1869); crop circles; and, in the internet era, viral misinformation and email hoaxes. Scientific hoaxes such as the Sokal affair (1996) were designed to expose weak peer review. Governments and courts distinguish hoaxes from satire and fraud, with hoax threats (e.g., bomb hoaxes) often criminal offenses.
