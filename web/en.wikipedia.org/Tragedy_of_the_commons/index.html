Tragedy of the commons

Tragedy of the commons
Concept byGarrett Hardin (1968)
FieldEconomics, ecology, game theory
RelatedCommon-pool resource, Free-rider problem

The tragedy of the commons is the concept that, if a shared resource is available to all, individual users acting in their own self-interest will deplete or spoil it, contrary to the long-term interest of the group. The idea was popularized by ecologist Garrett Hardin in a 1968 article in Science, using the example of herders adding cattle to a shared pasture until it is overgrazed.

The model

Each herder receives all the benefit of adding an animal but bears only a fraction of the cost of overgrazing, so the rational choice is always to add more — a structure resembling a multi-player prisoner's dilemma. The concept had earlier antecedents, including William Forster Lloyd's 1833 lectures and Aristotle's observation that what is common to the greatest number receives the least care.

Examples

Fisheries collapse, groundwater depletion, air pollution, climate change, and congestion are commonly analysed as commons tragedies, as are digital examples such as spam and overuse of shared computing resources.

Responses and critiques

Proposed remedies include regulation, privatization, and community self-governance. Elinor Ostrom's field research showed that real communities frequently devise durable rules, monitoring, and sanctions to manage common-pool resources, winning her the 2009 Nobel Prize in Economics and complicating Hardin's original assumption that ruin is inevitable without state or market control.
