Summer solstice

The summer solstice (also estival solstice or midsummer) occurs when one of Earth's poles has its maximum tilt toward the Sun. It happens twice yearly, once in each hemisphere: for the Northern Hemisphere around 20–21 June, and for the Southern Hemisphere around 21–22 December. On this day that hemisphere experiences its longest period of daylight and shortest night of the year; above the Arctic (or Antarctic) Circle the Sun does not set at all — the midnight sun.

Astronomy

At the June solstice the Sun reaches its most northerly declination, +23.44°, standing directly overhead at noon on the Tropic of Cancer. The solstice marks astronomical midsummer; although it has the longest day, the warmest weather typically lags by several weeks because land and oceans heat slowly. In the Northern Hemisphere the June solstice is the traditional start of astronomical summer, while meteorologists define summer as June–August.

Cultural significance

The solstice has been celebrated since prehistory — Neolithic monuments such as Stonehenge and Newgrange are aligned to solstitial sunrise and sunset. Midsummer festivals with bonfires and maypoles persist across northern Europe, notably in Sweden and Finland, and the day is marked by the International Day of Yoga and by celebrations at sites such as Chichen Itza.
