Fourier transform

The Fourier transform is a mathematical transform that decomposes a function of time (or space) into the frequencies that make it up, in a way analogous to expressing a musical chord as the volume and frequency of its constituent notes. Named after Joseph Fourier, who introduced the ideas in his 1822 work on heat conduction, it is fundamental to signal processing, physics, engineering and harmonic analysis.

Definition

The Fourier transform of an integrable function f(x) is defined by

f̂(ξ) = ∫ f(x) e−2πi x ξ dx

over all real x. The original function can be recovered by the inverse transform, and Plancherel's theorem guarantees that the transform preserves the total energy of the signal.

Properties

The transform is linear; it converts convolution into pointwise multiplication and differentiation into multiplication by the frequency variable. Shifting a function modulates its transform, and vice versa, an instance of time–frequency duality underlying the uncertainty principle.

Discrete transform and FFT

For sampled data the discrete Fourier transform (DFT) is used. The fast Fourier transform (FFT), popularized by Cooley and Tukey in 1965, computes the DFT in O(n log n) operations, making real-time spectral analysis practical in audio, images, medical imaging and telecommunications.

Applications

Fourier methods underpin JPEG and MP3 compression, MRI reconstruction, the solution of partial differential equations, crystallography, and the analysis of vibrations and waves throughout physics.
