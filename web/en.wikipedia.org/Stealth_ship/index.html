Stealth ship

The angular surfaces of a stealth ship deflect radar signals away from their source

A stealth ship is a naval vessel designed with stealth technology to reduce its detection by radar, infrared, acoustic, and other sensors. Stealth is achieved primarily through hull and superstructure shaping that scatters radar waves away from the emitter rather than back to it, combined with radar-absorbent materials, reduced thermal and acoustic emissions, and in some designs enclosed masts and weapons.

Design principles

The most visible feature is faceting: flat surfaces angled so that radar energy reflects in directions other than its origin, and the avoidance of right-angle corners that act as strong reflectors. Superstructures are often enclosed in a single streamlined block, masts are shrouded, and deck fittings are minimised or hidden behind bulwarks. Waterline exhausts and seawater cooling reduce infrared signature, while raft-mounted machinery and quiet propellers reduce underwater noise. No design makes a ship invisible — stealth reduces detection range, buying time and complicating an enemy's targeting.

Examples

Notable stealth ships include Sweden's Visby-class corvettes, built of carbon-fibre composite with extensive signature reduction; France's La Fayette-class frigates, an early widely-exported design; Norway's Skjold-class fast attack craft; Singapore's Formidable class; and the United States' Zumwalt-class destroyers, whose tumblehome hull gives a much smaller radar cross-section than their size suggests. The experimental US vessel Sea Shadow (1985) pioneered many of these ideas.
