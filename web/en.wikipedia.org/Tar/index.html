Tar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Tar is a dark brown or black viscous liquid of hydrocarbons and free carbon, obtained from a wide variety of organic materials through destructive distillation. Tar can be produced from coal, wood, petroleum, or peat. Mineral products resembling tar can be produced from fossil hydrocarbons, such as asphalt. The word "tar" derives from the Proto-Germanic *terwą and is cognate with the word "tree".

Production and uses

Production and trade in tar was a major contributor in the economies of Northern Europe and Colonial America. Its main use was in preserving wooden sailing vessels against rot; ships' rigging and hulls were tarred, and sailors themselves came to be nicknamed "tars". The largest user was the Royal Navy. In Scandinavia, pine tar was produced in quantity from stumps and roots for export for centuries.

Coal tar, a by-product of coke and coal gas production, was the feedstock of the nineteenth-century chemical industry, yielding dyes, pharmaceuticals and explosives; its derivatives gave rise to aniline dyes and the first synthetic medicines. Wood tar creosote and coal tar are both listed as carcinogens, although wood tar has a long history of use in traditional medicine and in food flavouring in Scandinavia, for example in the Finnish liquorice-like sweet terva (tar candy).

Related materials

Tar is often confused with bitumen and asphalt, which are naturally occurring or petroleum-derived mixtures with similar appearance; the La Brea Tar Pits in Los Angeles contain asphalt rather than true tar.
