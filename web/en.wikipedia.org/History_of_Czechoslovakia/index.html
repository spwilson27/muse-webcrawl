The history of Czechoslovakia covers the state that existed in Central Europe from 1918 to 1992, uniting the Czech lands (Bohemia, Moravia, Czech Silesia) with Slovakia and, until 1939/45, Subcarpathian Ruthenia. It dissolved peacefully on 1 January 1993 into the Czech Republic and Slovakia — the "Velvet Divorce".

First Republic (1918–1938)

Emerging from Austria-Hungary's collapse, the First Republic under Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk and Edvard Beneš was Central Europe's most durable democracy and an industrial powerhouse. Its weakness was ethnic: three million Sudeten Germans, plus Slovaks who felt overshadowed by Prague centralism. The Munich Agreement of September 1938, concluded without Czechoslovak participation, ceded the Sudetenland to Nazi Germany; in March 1939 Germany occupied the Czech lands as the Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia while Slovakia became a client state.

War and communist takeover

The Beneš government-in-exile in London directed resistance, including the 1942 assassination of Reinhard Heydrich, followed by the Lidice massacre. Liberated in 1945, the restored republic expelled its German population. In February 1948 the Communist Party seized power in a coup, aligning the country with the Soviet bloc.

Communism and its end

Stalinist terror in the 1950s gave way to the reformist Prague Spring of 1968 under Alexander Dubček — "socialism with a human face" — crushed by a Warsaw Pact invasion in August 1968. Two decades of "normalisation" followed, challenged by the Charter 77 dissident movement. In November 1989 the Velvet Revolution peacefully ended communist rule; Václav Havel became president. Disagreements between Czech and Slovak governments after the 1992 elections led to negotiated dissolution.
