Airline Deregulation Act

The Airline Deregulation Act is a 1978 United States federal law that removed government control over fares, routes, and market entry of new airlines, introducing free market forces into the commercial airline industry and leading to a large increase in the number of flights, a decrease in fares, an increase in passenger traffic and miles flown, and a consolidation of the industry.

Background

From 1938 to 1978 the Civil Aeronautics Board (CAB) tightly regulated the U.S. airline industry, controlling which carriers could fly which routes and at what fares. Critics argued the system kept fares high and stifled competition, pointing to intrastate carriers in California and Texas that offered much lower fares outside CAB control. Economists and reformers in the Carter administration, led by CAB chairman Alfred E. Kahn, pushed deregulation through Congress.

Provisions

The Act phased out CAB control over fares and routes, allowed airlines to enter and exit markets freely, and provided for the eventual sunset of the CAB itself, which occurred on January 1, 1985. Safety regulation remained with the Federal Aviation Administration. The Essential Air Service program was created to guarantee continued service to small communities.

Effects

Average real airfares fell substantially in the decades after deregulation, and passenger numbers grew enormously. At the same time, the industry experienced waves of bankruptcies, the rise and fall of many new entrants, the growth of hub-and-spoke networks, and eventual consolidation into a small number of large carriers. Labor relations were also transformed, with concessions extracted from airline unions during restructuring.
