Pyrococcus furiosus

Pyrococcus furiosus is a species of hyperthermophilic archaeon first isolated from geothermally heated marine sediments near Vulcano Island, Italy, and described in 1986 by Karl Stetter and Gerhard Fiala. Its name, meaning "rushing fireball", refers to its rapid swimming and its growth at temperatures around the boiling point of water. It is an important model organism for the study of extremophiles and a source of heat-stable enzymes for biotechnology.

Characteristics

P. furiosus is a roughly spherical coccus about 0.8–1.5 µm across with a tuft of flagella (archaella) at one pole. It is a strict anaerobe that grows optimally at 100 °C, fermenting peptides and sugars and producing hydrogen gas and carbon dioxide; strikingly, it can use elemental sulfur, reducing it to hydrogen sulfide, though sulfur is not essential. Its doubling time at optimum temperature is only about 37 minutes. The genome, about 1.9 million base pairs encoding roughly 2,000 proteins, was sequenced in 2001 and notable for many genes seemingly unrelated to those of other archaea, hinting at horizontal gene transfer.

Biotechnology

The organism's DNA polymerase, Pfu polymerase, is prized for PCR because it is thermostable and possesses 3'→5' proofreading exonuclease activity, giving far higher fidelity than Taq polymerase. P. furiosus is also unusual in using tungsten-containing enzymes—among the very few organisms known to depend on tungsten—and its rubredoxin and other proteins have been studied for the structural basis of protein thermostability.

Ecology

Like other Pyrococcus species, it inhabits deep-sea hydrothermal systems and shallow marine hot springs, environments that may resemble those of early Earth, making it relevant to research on the origin of life and astrobiology.
