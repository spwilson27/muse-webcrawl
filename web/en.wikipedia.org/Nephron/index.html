Nephron

SystemUrinary system
LocationKidney (cortex and medulla)
ComponentsRenal corpuscle (glomerulus, Bowman's capsule), proximal tubule, loop of Henle, distal tubule, connecting tubule

The nephron is the microscopic structural and functional unit of the kidney. It filters the blood, reclaims water and useful solutes, secretes additional wastes, and produces the fluid that becomes urine. Each human kidney contains on the order of a million nephrons, though numbers vary widely between individuals and decline with age and disease.

Structure

Each nephron begins with a renal corpuscle in the kidney cortex: a tuft of capillaries called the glomerulus, enveloped by Bowman's capsule. Blood plasma is filtered across the glomerular filtration barrier — fenestrated endothelium, basement membrane, and the podocyte slit diaphragms (whose key protein is nephrin) — into the capsular space. The filtrate then traverses, in sequence:

  • Proximal tubule — reabsorbs about two-thirds of the filtrate: all glucose and amino acids, most sodium, water, phosphate, and bicarbonate.
  • Loop of Henle — descending and ascending limbs that establish the medullary osmotic gradient by countercurrent multiplication, concentrating the urine.
  • Distal convoluted tubule — finer adjustment of sodium, potassium, calcium, and pH; includes the macula densa, which senses NaCl and regulates glomerular filtration (tubuloglomerular feedback) and renin release.
  • Connecting tubule and collecting duct (shared by many nephrons) — aldosterone-regulated sodium and potassium handling and ADH (vasopressin)-regulated water reabsorption.

Cortical (superficial) nephrons have short loops; juxtamedullary nephrons, whose corpuscles lie near the medulla, have long loops essential for concentrating urine. Around the tubules, peritubular capillaries and vasa recta carry away reabsorbed fluid.

Function

Together the nephrons filter roughly 180 liters of plasma per day in an adult, reabsorbing over 99% of it. By adjusting filtration and the reabsorption and secretion of ions, water, urea, acids, and drugs, the nephron regulates blood volume and pressure, plasma osmolality, electrolyte balance, and acid–base status, under hormonal control by the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, ADH, natriuretic peptides, and parathyroid hormone.

Clinical significance

Loss of nephron function defines chronic kidney disease; remaining nephrons compensate by hyperfiltration, which may itself cause progressive damage. Glomerular diseases (glomerulonephritis), tubular disorders, and toxins can each damage specific nephron segments, and many major drug classes — diuretics above all — act on defined nephron transporters.
