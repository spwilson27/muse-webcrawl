Speakeasy

Speakeasy
Also called: Blind pig, blind tiger
Era: Prohibition (1920–1933)
Country: United States

A speakeasy, also called a blind pig or blind tiger, is an illicit establishment that sells alcoholic beverages. The term became widespread in the United States during Prohibition (1920–1933), when the manufacture, sale and transportation of alcohol were banned nationwide under the Eighteenth Amendment and the Volstead Act.

Etymology

The word is said to derive from the practice of speaking quietly — "speaking easy" — when ordering or asking for alcohol, so as not to attract the attention of police or neighbours. The term predates Prohibition, appearing in the 19th century for unlicensed saloons, including in western Pennsylvania in the 1880s.

Prohibition era

Speakeasies proliferated after 1920: New York City alone was estimated to have tens of thousands at the height of Prohibition. They ranged from squalid basements to glamorous clubs such as New York's 21 Club and the Cotton Club, serving bootlegged liquor supplied by organised crime figures including Al Capone. Raids by federal Prohibition agents were common, and establishments employed peepholes, passwords and hidden compartments. Many speakeasies were notable for racially integrated audiences and for making jazz venues of the Harlem Renaissance possible.

Legacy

After repeal in 1933 most speakeasies closed or became licensed bars. The term survives as a style: modern "speakeasy-style" cocktail bars, hidden behind unmarked doors or disguised as shops, became fashionable in the 2000s craft cocktail revival.
