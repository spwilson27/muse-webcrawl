The Mono are a Native American people who traditionally live around Mono Lake (see Mono County) and the eastern Sierra Nevada, the Owens Valley, and the western foothills of the Sierra in what is now California. They call themselves Nim ("the people") in their own language. The Mono are traditionally divided into the Eastern Mono, often called the Owens Valley Paiute, and the Western Mono or Monache, who lived on the Sierra's western slope in the drainages of the San Joaquin, Kings, and Kaweah rivers.

Culture and subsistence

Traditional Mono life centred on seasonal rounds of hunting and gathering. Staple foods included pine nuts (especially from the pinyon pine), acorns among western groups, seeds, and the brine-fly pupae harvested from Mono Lake, known as kutsavi. The Owens Valley Paiute practised a notable system of communal irrigation of wild seed plots, unusual among Great Basin peoples. Trade across the Sierra crest — obsidian and salt from the east exchanged for acorns and other goods from the west — linked Mono communities with Yokuts and Miwok neighbours.

Language and present day

The Mono language belongs to the Western Numic branch of the Uto-Aztecan family and is closely related to Northern Paiute. It is critically endangered, with fluent first-language speakers now few, though tribal programmes in communities such as Bishop, Big Pine, Lone Pine, North Fork, and Auberry work to teach and document it. Several Mono communities are federally recognized tribes today.
