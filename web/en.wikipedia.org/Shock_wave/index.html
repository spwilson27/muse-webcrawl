Shock wave

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In physics, a shock wave (also spelled shockwave), or shock, is a type of propagating disturbance that moves faster than the local speed of sound in the medium. Like an ordinary wave, a shock wave carries energy and can propagate through a medium, but is characterized by an abrupt, nearly discontinuous change in pressure, temperature and density of the medium.

Properties

Shock waves form when pressure waves in a fluid "pile up" and steepen, for example when a body moves through the fluid faster than sound, when an explosion rapidly expands gases, or when supersonic flow encounters an obstacle. Across a shock, the flow velocity decreases while pressure and temperature increase, and entropy rises — the process is irreversible. The relationships across a normal shock are given by the Rankine–Hugoniot conditions.

Types

  • Normal shocks — perpendicular to the flow direction, as in supersonic engine intakes.
  • Oblique shocks — inclined to the flow, generated by wedges and cones, deflecting the flow.
  • Bow shocks — curved shocks standing ahead of blunt bodies in supersonic flow, and in astrophysics ahead of planets and stars moving through plasma.
  • Detonation waves — shocks coupled to an exothermic chemical reaction.

Occurrences

Shock waves are produced by supersonic aircraft (causing sonic booms), explosions, lightning (thunder), bullets, meteor entries, and solar-wind interactions. In medicine, focused shock waves are used in lithotripsy to break up kidney stones. In astronomy, shocks shape supernova remnants and the structure of the interstellar medium.
