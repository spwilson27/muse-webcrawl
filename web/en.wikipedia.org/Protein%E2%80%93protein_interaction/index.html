Protein–protein interaction

Protein–protein interactions (PPIs) are physical contacts of high specificity established between two or more protein molecules as a result of biochemical events steered by interactions that include electrostatic forces, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic effects. Such interactions are at the core of virtually every process in a living cell.

Types

Interactions may be stable, forming permanent protein complexes such as hemoglobin or the ribosome, or transient, as in signaling cascades where kinases bind substrates briefly. They are also classified as homomeric (between identical proteins) or heteromeric, and as obligate or non-obligate.

Methods of study

Experimental techniques include yeast two-hybrid screening, affinity purification coupled with mass spectrometry (AP-MS), co-immunoprecipitation, FRET, and protein microarrays. Computational prediction uses structural information and machine learning. Curated databases such as STRING, BioGRID, and IntAct catalog millions of interactions, and the resulting networks are studied as interactomes in systems biology.

Significance

Because PPIs mediate signal transduction, metabolism, and gene regulation, their disruption is implicated in many diseases, and interaction interfaces are increasingly targeted by small-molecule drugs, despite historically being considered difficult ("undruggable") targets.
