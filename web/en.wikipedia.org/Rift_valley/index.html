Rift valley

A rift valley is a linear shaped lowland between several highlands or mountain ranges produced by the action of a geologic rift. Rifts are formed as a result of the pulling apart of the lithosphere due to extensional tectonics. The linear depression may subsequently be further deepened by the forces of erosion. More generally the valley is likely to be filled with sedimentary deposits derived from the rift flanks and the surrounding areas. In many cases rift lakes are formed. One of the best known examples of this process is the East African Rift.

On Earth, rifts can occur at all elevations, from the sea floor to plateaus and mountain ranges in continental crust as well as in oceanic crust. They are often associated with a number of adjoining subsidiary or sub-parallel valleys, which are typically considered part of the principal rift valley geologically.

Formation

Rift valleys form where two plates of the Earth's crust are moving apart, or diverging. As the lithosphere stretches and thins, the central block drops down between roughly parallel normal faults, producing a graben — the technical German term for a down-dropped fault block. Continued extension may lead to the formation of new oceanic crust and eventually a mid-ocean ridge.

Examples

  • The Great Rift Valley of East Africa, running from the Afar Triangle to Mozambique.
  • The Baikal Rift Zone in Siberia, containing Lake Baikal, the deepest lake in the world.
  • The Rhine Graben in western Europe.
  • The Basin and Range Province of western North America.
