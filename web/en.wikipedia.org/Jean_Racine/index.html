DiedApril 21, 1699, Paris

Jean Racine (1639–1699) was a French dramatist, one of the three great playwrights of 17th-century French classical theatre alongside Molière and Corneille. His tragedies, written in rhymed alexandrine verse of exceptional purity, explore destructive passion within strict neoclassical unities.

Life

Orphaned young, Racine was educated by the Jansenists of Port-Royal, whose austere theology marked his work. His breakthrough came with Andromaque (1667); a rapid series of masterpieces followed — Britannicus (1669), Bérénice (1670), Bajazet (1672), Iphigénie (1674) and Phèdre (1677), generally held his greatest play.

Later years

After the intrigues surrounding Phèdre, Racine withdrew from the public theatre, became historiographer royal to Louis XIV with Nicolas Boileau, married, and returned to drama only with the biblical plays Esther (1689) and Athalie (1691), written for the schoolgirls of Saint-Cyr.

Legacy

Racine's psychological intensity and verbal economy made him the benchmark of French tragedy; his plays remain central to the Comédie-Française repertoire.
