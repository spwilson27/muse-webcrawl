Home run

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In baseball, a home run (abbreviated HR) is scored when the ball is hit in such a way that the batter is able to circle the bases and reach home safely in one continuous play without the benefit of a fielding error. In modern baseball the feat is typically achieved by hitting the ball over the outfield fence between the foul poles (or making contact with either foul pole) without it first touching the ground, resulting in an automatic home run. A home run is also scored as an "inside-the-park home run" when the batter reaches home in one play without the ball leaving the field of play and without an error.

A home run with no runners on base scores one run (a "solo home run"). With one, two, or three runners aboard, it scores two, three, or four runs respectively; a home run with the bases loaded is a grand slam, the most runs that can be scored on a single play. Home runs are among the most popular aspects of baseball and, consequently, prolific home run hitters are often highly paid and celebrated.

History

In the early game, when fields were large and the ball was softer, inside-the-park home runs were common and over-the-fence hits rare; some early parks treated balls hit over short fences as ground-rule doubles. The live-ball era beginning in 1920, symbolised by Babe Ruth, transformed the home run into the game's central offensive weapon. Ruth set the single-season record of 60 in 1927, broken by Roger Maris with 61 in 1961, then by Mark McGwire (70 in 1998) and Barry Bonds, whose 73 in 2001 remains the Major League record; Bonds also holds the career record with 762, ahead of Hank Aaron's 755 and Ruth's 714. Aaron Ohtani-era sluggers aside, the career record in Japanese professional baseball belongs to Sadaharu Oh, with 868.

Types and terminology

A home run hit with two outs or in a late, decisive situation takes on special names: a walk-off home run ends the game immediately when hit by the home team in the bottom of the final inning. Consecutive home runs by successive batters are "back-to-back home runs," and home runs in consecutive at-bats or games form celebrated streaks. A home run hit to the opposite field, pulled down the line, or measured for exceptional distance ("tape-measure home run") all feature in baseball vocabulary. Sluggers who hit home runs are sometimes called "tater," "dinger," "bomb," "blast," "big fly," or "gopher ball" producers, the last term also used (unhappily) of the pitchers who surrender them.
