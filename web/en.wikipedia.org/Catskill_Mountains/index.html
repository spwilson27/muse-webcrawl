The Catskill Mountains, often referred to simply as the Catskills, are a large mountainous region in southeastern New York State, lying northwest of New York City and southwest of Albany. Technically a dissected plateau rather than a true mountain range, the Catskills rise from the Hudson Valley and were formed by erosion of uplifted Devonian-age sedimentary rock.

Geography

The region covers roughly 6,000 square miles (15,500 km²) across five counties. The highest peak is Slide Mountain in Ulster County, at 4,180 feet (1,274 m). Much of the area is protected within the Catskill Park, a forest preserve of about 700,000 acres, including large tracts of state-owned "forever wild" land.

History

The Catskills were home to Native American peoples for thousands of years before Dutch colonization in the 17th century. In the 19th century the region became famous through the paintings of the Hudson River School, including Thomas Cole, and Washington Irving's tales of Rip Van Winkle. Later, the area became a major resort destination, particularly the "Borscht Belt" hotels that flourished in the mid-20th century.

Recreation

Today the Catskills are popular for hiking, skiing at resorts such as Hunter Mountain and Windham Mountain, fly fishing in streams like the Beaver Kill and Willowemoc Creek, and camping. The watersheds of several Catskill reservoirs supply much of New York City's drinking water.
