The Mohawk people (Mohawk: Kanien'keháːka, "People of the Flint") are an Indigenous people of North America and the easternmost nation of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy), in which they hold the role of Keepers of the Eastern Door. Their ancestral homeland is the Mohawk Valley in what is now upstate New York, centred on the Mohawk River.

History

The Mohawk were one of the five founding nations of the Iroquois Confederacy, formed — by oral tradition — under the Great Law of Peace promulgated by the Peacemaker and Hiawatha. Women held central authority in the matrilineal clan system: clan mothers selected and could depose chiefs (sachems), and descent and property passed through the female line. The economy rested on maize, beans and squash agriculture, supplemented by hunting and fishing from fortified longhouse villages.

From the seventeenth century the Mohawk were deeply involved in the fur trade and the Beaver Wars against French-allied nations. Many converted to Catholicism and moved to mission communities near Montreal, such as Kahnawake. In the French and Indian War and the American Revolutionary War the Confederacy fractured; Mohawk leader Joseph Brant (Thayendanegea) allied with Britain, and after American independence many Mohawk resettled in Canada, at the Bay of Quinte and the Grand River, while others remained in New York.

Modern communities

Today major Mohawk communities include Kahnawake, Akwesasne (St. Regis) and Kanesatake in the St. Lawrence valley, Tyendinaga and Six Nations of the Grand River in Ontario, and communities in New York State. Mohawk ironworkers became famous from the 1880s onward for high-steel construction work on bridges and skyscrapers across North America. Land and sovereignty disputes, most dramatically the Oka Crisis of 1990 at Kanesatake, have marked relations with Canada. Language revitalisation programs, including immersion schools, work to sustain Kanien'kéha, an Iroquoian language now spoken fluently by a minority of the population.
