Sovereign immunity

Sovereign immunity limits lawsuits against the state

Sovereign immunity, or crown immunity, is a legal doctrine whereby a sovereign state or state cannot commit a legal wrong and is immune from civil suit or criminal prosecution, strictly speaking in modern texts in its own courts. A similar, stronger rule as regards foreign courts is named state immunity.

In its older form, the doctrine was derived from the Latin maxim rex non potest peccare, meaning "the king can do no wrong". In some countries, the ruler was considered above the law and could not be sued. Modern forms of sovereign immunity have been modified by statutes that waive immunity in many kinds of cases.

United States

In the United States, sovereign immunity typically applies to both the federal and state governments. The Eleventh Amendment confirms state immunity from suit in federal court by citizens of other states. However, immunity may be waived by statute — for example, the Federal Tort Claims Act waives federal immunity for certain tort claims — or abrogated by Congress under certain constitutional provisions. States may also be sued where they have consented, and suits against state officials for prospective injunctive relief are permitted under the doctrine of Ex parte Young.

International law

State immunity in foreign courts is codified in instruments such as the United Nations Convention on Jurisdictional Immunities of States and Their Property. Most modern systems apply the restrictive theory of immunity, under which a state enjoys immunity for sovereign acts (acta jure imperii) but not for commercial acts (acta jure gestionis). In the United States, this is implemented by the Foreign Sovereign Immunities Act of 1976.

Criticism

The doctrine has been criticised as anachronistic and capable of producing injustice where injured parties are denied a remedy against the state, though supporters argue it protects public funds and prevents interference with governmental functions.
