The House of Wettin is a dynasty of German counts, dukes, prince-electors, and kings that once ruled territories in the present-day German states of Saxony, Saxony-Anhalt, and Thuringia. The family originated at Wettin on the Saale river and is one of the oldest surviving European dynasties, with documented origins around the year 1000. Branches of the Wettins have occupied the thrones of the United Kingdom, Belgium, Portugal, and Bulgaria, as well as Saxony and Poland.

Origins and the Saxon inheritance

The Wettins rose as margraves of Meissen in the eleventh century. In 1423 Frederick the Warlike received the Electorate of Saxony. In 1485 the Leipzig Partition split the dynasty into the Ernestine and Albertine lines, a division that shaped Saxon politics for centuries; the Ernestines lost the electoral dignity to the Albertines after the Schmalkaldic War in 1547.

Ernestine and Albertine branches

The Ernestine line fragmented into numerous Thuringian duchies — Saxe-Weimar, Saxe-Gotha, Saxe-Coburg, Saxe-Meiningen, Saxe-Altenburg, and others. From the Saxe-Coburg and Gotha branch came Prince Albert, consort of Queen Victoria; Leopold I, first King of the Belgians; Ferdinand II of Portugal; and Ferdinand I of Bulgaria. The Albertine line ruled Saxony as electors and then kings until 1918, and twice supplied kings of Poland in the eighteenth century (Augustus II and Augustus III).

Modern position

The British royal house belonged to the Wettin line of Saxe-Coburg and Gotha until the name was anglicised to Windsor in 1917. The Belgian royal family remains a Wettin line to this day, and the head of the Albertine branch, Margrave of Meissen, continues to represent the Saxon royal house.
