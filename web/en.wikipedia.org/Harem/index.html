Harem

EtymologyArabic ḥaram, "forbidden place"
ContextsOttoman, Persian, Mughal, and other courts
RelatedPolygyny, concubinage, zenana

A harem is the separate part of a Muslim household reserved for women — wives, concubines, female relatives, and children — and the women living there. The word comes from the Arabic ḥaram, meaning a sacred or forbidden place, and comparable institutions existed in many pre-modern societies, including imperial China, Persia, Byzantium, and Mughal India.

Ottoman imperial harem

The most famous example is the Imperial Harem of the Ottoman sultans in Istanbul's Topkapı Palace, home to the sultan's mother (valide sultan), wives, concubines, and female servants, guarded by eunuchs. During the period sometimes called the Sultanate of Women, influential valide sultans such as Kösem Sultan exercised great political power.

Social reality and depictions

For most households a harem was simply the women's quarters of the home, organised around family privacy rather than sensuality. Western Orientalist art and literature of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, however, depicted harems as exotic, erotic spaces, images that modern scholarship treats largely as fantasy.

Other uses

In zoology, a harem is a group of female animals associated with one or a few males, as in some primates, deer, and seals.
