Zaidiyyah (also spelled Zaydism) is a branch of Shia Islam that emerged in the 8th century following the unsuccessful revolt of Zayd ibn Ali against the Umayyad Caliphate in 740 CE. Zaydis are concentrated in northwestern Yemen, where they form a substantial part of the population.

Beliefs

Zaydis hold that leadership (the imamate) belongs to descendants of Muhammad through his daughter Fatimah — specifically the line of Hasan and Husayn — but, unlike Twelver Shias, they do not require a designated line of infallible imams nor believe in a hidden Mahdi. Any qualified descendant who rises and calls to allegiance may be imam. Zaydi theology is close to the Mu'tazila in emphasising reason and free will, and its jurisprudence is relatively close to Sunni schools. Zayd's great-grandson tradition traces doctrine also to his father Zayn al-Abidin and to works such as the Musnad of Imam Zayd.

History in Yemen

Yahya ibn al-Husayn established a Zaydi imamate at Sa'dah in 893–897. Zaydi imams ruled highland Yemen, centred later on Sanaa, for over a thousand years until the 1962 revolution created the Yemen Arab Republic. In the 1990s–2000s the Zaydi revivalist Houthi movement (Ansar Allah) arose in the north, fought wars with the Yemeni government, and seized Sanaa in 2014, precipitating civil war and a Saudi-led intervention.
