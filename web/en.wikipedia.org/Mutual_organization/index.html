Mutual organization

A mutual organization, or mutual society, is an organization based on the principle of mutuality: it is owned by its members or customers rather than by external shareholders, and is run for their benefit. Profits are typically reinvested in the organization or returned to members rather than paid out as dividends to investors.

Forms

  • Mutual insurance companies, owned by their policyholders, historically a dominant form in life and agricultural insurance.
  • Building societies in the United Kingdom and similar savings institutions, owned by savers and borrowers.
  • Credit unions, member-owned financial cooperatives.
  • Mutual exchanges and utilities, including some stock exchanges and water or energy providers.
  • Friendly societies and fraternal benefit societies providing sickness and burial benefits.

Demutualization

From the 1980s onward, waves of demutualization converted many mutual insurers and building societies into joint-stock companies, motivated by access to capital markets and, in some cases, windfall payouts to members. Notable conversions included major British building societies in the 1990s, most of which were absorbed by banks; the surviving Nationwide Building Society remains a prominent mutual. Critics and supporters continue to debate whether mutuality better aligns institutional incentives with customers' long-term interests.
