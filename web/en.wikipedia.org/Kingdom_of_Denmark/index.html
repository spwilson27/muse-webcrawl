The Kingdom of Denmark (Danish: Kongeriget Danmark) is a sovereign state comprising three constituent parts: metropolitan Denmark in northern Europe and the two autonomous territories of Greenland and the Faroe Islands in the North Atlantic. The arrangement, in Danish called Rigsfællesskabet ("Unity of the Realm"), dates in its modern form from the mid-20th century.

Structure

Denmark proper is a unitary state governed under the 1953 constitution, with the monarch as head of state and the Folketing as legislature. Greenland (Home Rule from 1979, expanded Self-Government from 2009) and the Faroe Islands (Home Rule from 1948) each have their own parliaments and governments controlling most domestic matters, including natural resources in Greenland's case. The Danish state retains responsibility for foreign affairs, defence, currency, and the constitution, and provides annual block grants to both territories. Each territory elects two members to the Folketing.

History

The realm's Atlantic possessions descend from Norse settlement and the medieval Norwegian crown, whose dependencies passed to Denmark-Norway. When Norway was ceded to Sweden in 1814, the North Atlantic islands remained with Denmark. Greenland's status was clarified after the 1933 Eastern Greenland case at the Permanent Court of International Justice; a 1953 constitutional change made Greenland an integral part of the kingdom, ending its colonial status, and the Faroe Islands had already received home rule in 1948.

Geography and people

Metropolitan Denmark covers some 43,000 km² of the Jutland peninsula and islands, home to roughly 5.9 million people. Greenland, the world's largest island at about 2.16 million km², mostly covered by ice sheet, has about 56,000 inhabitants, most of them Inuit. The Faroe Islands (about 1,400 km²) have around 54,000 inhabitants. Total population of the kingdom is about 6 million, and the Danish krone is used across the realm, with Faroese banknotes issued as a local variant.
