Endoparasite

An endoparasite is a parasite that lives inside the body of its host, feeding on its tissues, blood, or intestinal contents. Endoparasites contrast with ectoparasites, which live on the host's surface. Most endoparasites belong to two broad assemblages: single-celled protozoa, such as Plasmodium (the cause of malaria) and Giardia, and helminths — parasitic worms including tapeworms (cestodes), flukes (trematodes), and roundworms (nematodes).

Life cycles

Endoparasites often have complex life cycles involving several hosts. A definitive host harbours the adult, sexually reproducing stage, while intermediate hosts harbour larval stages. Taenia tapeworms mature in the human intestine, with larvae encysted in beef or pork muscle; Schistosoma flukes pass through freshwater snails before penetrating human skin; Plasmodium alternates between humans and Anopheles mosquitoes. Some endoparasites are intracellular — Plasmodium in red blood cells, Leishmania in macrophages, Toxoplasma in many cell types — while others live extracellularly in the gut lumen, bile ducts, or blood plasma.

Effects on the host and transmission

Diseases caused by endoparasites range from mild gastrointestinal upset to fatal malaria, which kills hundreds of thousands of people annually. Chronic helminth infections cause anaemia, malnutrition, and impaired childhood development, and constitute a large share of the neglected tropical disease burden. Transmission occurs through contaminated food and water, insect vectors, skin penetration, and faecal–oral routes. Diagnosis typically uses microscopy of stool or blood, antigen tests, or molecular methods, and treatment relies on antiparasitic drugs such as albendazole, praziquantel, and artemisinin combinations; control combines medication, sanitation, and vector control.

Evolutionary notes

Many endoparasites show striking adaptations to internal life: tapeworms have lost their own digestive tracts and absorb nutrients across their body surface; parasites broadly exhibit reduced genomes, elaborate attachment organs, and immune-evasion strategies such as antigenic variation in trypanosomes and Plasmodium. The immune system's response to helminths (the Th2 pattern) is also implicated in the hygiene hypothesis explaining the rise of allergies in low-parasite environments.
