Great Recession

PeriodLate 2000s; US recession dated Dec 2007 – Jun 2009 (NBER)
Trigger2007–2008 financial crisis; US housing bubble collapse
EffectsDeepest global downturn since the Great Depression; US unemployment peaked at 10% (Oct 2009)
ResponsesBank bailouts (TARP), fiscal stimulus, quantitative easing, near-zero interest rates
SequelEuropean sovereign debt crisis (2010–2012)

The Great Recession was a period of marked general decline in national economies worldwide in the late 2000s and early 2010s, following the financial crisis of 2007–2008. The United States National Bureau of Economic Research dates the American recession from December 2007 to June 2009, but the downturn was global: world output contracted in 2009 for the first time in the post-war era, unemployment and foreclosures soared, and many economies took years to regain their pre-crisis output — in parts of the eurozone, more than a decade.

Causes

The immediate cause was the collapse of the United States housing bubble and the subprime mortgage market. Years of low interest rates, lax lending standards, securitisation of risky mortgages into mortgage-backed securities and CDOs, failures of credit rating agencies, and extreme leverage in the financial system left banks exposed when house prices fell. The bankruptcy of Lehman Brothers in September 2008 froze global credit markets and turned a financial crisis into a general economic contraction.

Policy responses

Governments and central banks responded with lender-of-last-resort lending, bank recapitalisations and guarantees (the US$700 billion US TARP programme), large fiscal stimulus (such as the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act of 2009 and China's stimulus package), cuts in policy rates to near zero, and unprecedented quantitative easing. The G20 coordinated much of the response in 2009.

Aftermath

The recession drove up public debt, which in Europe mutated into the sovereign debt crisis of 2010–2012, with bailouts for Greece, Ireland, Portugal, Spain and Cyprus and austerity programmes that deepened southern Europe's slump. In the United States, some 8.7 million jobs were lost and unemployment peaked at 10% in October 2009. Long-run consequences included slow productivity and "secular stagnation" debates, widening inequality, regulatory reform (the Dodd–Frank Act, Basel III), and political aftershocks from populist movements to the Occupy protests.
