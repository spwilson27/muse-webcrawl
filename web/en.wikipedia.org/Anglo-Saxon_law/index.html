Anglo-Saxon law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Anglo-Saxon law is the body of law that developed in England between the Germanic settlements of the fifth century and the Norman Conquest of 1066. Unusually for early medieval Europe, the Anglo-Saxon law codes were written in the vernacular, Old English, rather than Latin.

Law codes

The earliest surviving code is that of King Æthelberht of Kent (c. 602), which lists tariffs of compensation for injuries. Later codes were issued by kings of Wessex, notably Alfred the Great, whose domboc combined Mosaic law with earlier Kentish, Mercian and West Saxon codes, and by Cnut in the eleventh century. The codes were issued in the king's name but largely recorded existing custom.

Features

Central features included wergild, a fixed payment to the family of a slain person, scaled by the victim's rank; bōt, compensation for lesser injuries; and oath-taking supported by oath-helpers (compurgation). Disputes were heard in local assemblies — the hundred court and the shire court — and guilt could be tested by ordeal. Kinship groups bore collective responsibility for their members, and outlawry was the punishment for those who fled justice.

Legacy

After 1066 the Normans retained much of the Anglo-Saxon administrative and legal framework, including shires, hundreds and the writ system, which fed into the later development of the English common law.
