The Douro (Portuguese: Douro; Spanish: Duero) is the largest river of the Iberian Peninsula by discharge, rising in the Picos de Urbión in north-central Spain and flowing generally west for 897 km through Spain and northern Portugal to empty into the Atlantic Ocean at Porto. Its basin, of about 97,000 km², is the largest river basin in Iberia; the final 112 km of its course form part of the Spanish–Portuguese border.

Course

From its source at over 2,000 m in the Sierra de Urbión, the river crosses the northern Meseta past Soria, Aranda de Duero, and Zamora, where it enters the dramatic gorges of the Arribes del Duero along the frontier. In Portugal it flows through the wine country of the Douro Valley to Peso da Régua, Pinhão, and the twin cities of Porto and Vila Nova de Gaia at its mouth.

Wine

The Portuguese Douro Valley is among the world's oldest demarcated wine regions, regulated since 1756, and is famous above all for port wine, which is aged in the lodges of Vila Nova de Gaia, as well as for unfortified Douro DOC red and white wines. Vineyards climb the steep schist slopes in hand-built terraces, an agricultural landscape inscribed by UNESCO as the Alto Douro Wine Region World Heritage Site in 2001. Traditionally the wine was carried downriver to Porto in flat-bottomed rabelo boats.

Other features

The lower river is extensively dammed for hydroelectric power and made navigable by locks as far as Barca d'Alva. The Côa Valley, near the frontier, preserves one of the world's largest concentrations of Palaeolithic open-air rock engravings, also a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
