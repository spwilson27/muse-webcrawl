Edict of Potsdam

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Edict of Potsdam (German: Edikt von Potsdam) was an edict issued on 29 October (Julian calendar) / 8 November (Gregorian calendar) 1685 by Frederick William, Elector of Brandenburg and Duke of Prussia, in response to the Edict of Fontainebleau issued by King Louis XIV of France, which revoked the Edict of Nantes and outlawed Protestantism in France.

The Edict of Potsdam, 1685

The edict invited the persecuted French Huguenots to settle in Brandenburg-Prussia, offering them safe passage, free transport of their goods, tax privileges, financial assistance, the right to their own churches and French-speaking pastors, and freedom to establish manufactures. Agents were stationed in Frankfurt and other cities to help refugees on their journey.

Effects

Some 20,000 Huguenots accepted the invitation and settled in Prussia, roughly two-thirds of them in Berlin, where they came to constitute about a fifth of the population around 1700. The immigrants — many of them skilled textile workers, craftsmen, merchants, officers, and intellectuals — made a major contribution to the economic, military, and cultural development of the rising Prussian state, and French influence persisted in Berlin for generations. The Great Elector's policy of religious tolerance, extended earlier to persecuted Jews from Vienna, is considered a landmark in the development of Prussian toleration.
