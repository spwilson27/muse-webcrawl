Genetic engineering

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Genetic engineering, also called genetic modification or genetic manipulation, is the modification and manipulation of an organism's genes using technology. It is a set of technologies used to change the genetic makeup of cells, including the transfer of genes within and across species boundaries to produce improved or novel organisms. New DNA is obtained by either isolating and copying the genetic material of interest using recombinant DNA methods or by artificially synthesising the DNA. A construct is usually created and used to insert this DNA into the host organism.

History

The first recombinant DNA molecule was made by Paul Berg in 1972, and Herbert Boyer and Stanley Cohen produced the first transgenic organism (a bacterium) in 1973. Rudolf Jaenisch created the first transgenic animal in 1974. Commercial applications followed: bacteria producing human insulin were developed by Genentech in 1978, and genetically modified crops were commercialised from 1994 (the Flavr Savr tomato). Genome editing tools have successively increased precision: restriction enzymes and ligases, then zinc-finger nucleases and TALENs, and from 2012 the CRISPR–Cas9 system, adapted from a bacterial immune mechanism by Jennifer Doudna, Emmanuelle Charpentier and others.

Applications

Genetic engineering is used in research (knockout and reporter organisms), medicine (production of insulin, vaccines and monoclonal antibodies; gene therapy for inherited diseases; engineered immune cells in cancer treatment), agriculture (herbicide-tolerant and insect-resistant crops such as Bt corn and soy, biofortified crops such as Golden Rice), and industry (microbes producing enzymes, biofuels and materials). Genetically modified organisms (GMOs) are regulated differently around the world.

Ethics and controversy

The technology raises debates over food safety assessment, environmental effects such as gene flow, corporate control of seed, animal welfare, and human germline editing — highlighted by the 2018 announcement of genome-edited babies in China, which was widely condemned. Scientific consensus statements hold that approved GM foods are no riskier than conventional counterparts, while public opinion remains divided in many regions.
