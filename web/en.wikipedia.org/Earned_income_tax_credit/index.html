Earned income tax credit

The earned income tax credit (EITC or EIC) is a refundable tax credit in the United States designed to benefit low- to moderate-income working individuals and families, particularly those with children. Enacted in 1975, it reduces the amount of tax owed dollar-for-dollar and, because it is refundable, can result in a payment to the taxpayer even when no taxes are owed.

Overview

The amount of the credit depends on the recipient's income and number of qualifying children. For taxpayers without children, the credit is much smaller. The credit phases in with earned income, reaches a maximum, and then phases out at higher income levels, a design intended to encourage and reward work while targeting assistance to lower-income households.

History

The EITC was enacted in 1975, partly as a response to proposals for a negative income tax and as an alternative to welfare programs. It has been expanded several times, notably in 1986, 1990, 1993, and 2009, becoming one of the largest anti-poverty programs in the United States. Studies have credited it with lifting millions of people, including many children, above the poverty line each year.

Eligibility

To claim the credit, a taxpayer must have earned income from employment or self-employment, meet adjusted gross income limits, and satisfy rules regarding filing status, residency, and qualifying children. Special rules apply to taxpayers who are members of the military, clergy, or who receive disability benefits.
