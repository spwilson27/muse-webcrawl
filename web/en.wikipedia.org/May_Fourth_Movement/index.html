May Fourth Movement

The May Fourth Movement (五四運動) was a Chinese cultural and anti-imperialist political movement which grew out of student protests in Beijing on May 4, 1919. Students gathered in front of Tiananmen to protest the Chinese government's weak response to the Treaty of Versailles, which allowed Japan to keep territories in Shandong captured from Germany at the start of World War I. The demonstrations sparked national strikes and a wave of protests, leading to the dismissal of pro-Japanese officials and China's refusal to sign the treaty.

The movement is conventionally dated from 1915–1921, encompassing the broader New Culture Movement, which promoted science and democracy ("Mr Science" and "Mr Democracy"), attacked traditional Confucian values, and championed the use of vernacular Chinese (baihua) in literature through figures such as Chen Duxiu, Hu Shih, and Lu Xun.

Background

After the death of Yuan Shikai in 1916, China fragmented among warlords. Intellectuals at Peking University, under chancellor Cai Yuanpei, launched the journal New Youth in 1915, attacking Confucian tradition. China's hopes that the Paris Peace Conference would restore Shandong were dashed when the Allied powers transferred German concessions there to Japan, provoking the May 4 student demonstration.

Legacy

The movement radicalised a generation of Chinese intellectuals and politicians. It created the conditions for the founding of the Chinese Communist Party in 1921 — both of its principal founders, Chen Duxiu and Li Dazhao, were May Fourth leaders — and for the reorganisation of the Kuomintang. May 4 is commemorated in China as Youth Day, and the movement remains a touchstone of Chinese patriotism and modernisation debates.
