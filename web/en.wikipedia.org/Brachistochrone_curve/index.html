Brachistochrone curve

In physics and mathematics, a brachistochrone curve (from Ancient Greek βράχιστος χρόνος, "shortest time") is the curve between two points along which a bead sliding frictionlessly under gravity traverses the path in the least possible time, among all curves joining the points. The brachistochrone curve is the same shape as the tautochrone curve and the isochrone; all are cycloids. However, the portion of the cycloid used for each of the three varies.

History

The problem was posed by Johann Bernoulli in the journal Acta Eruditorum in June 1696. He introduced the problem as follows:

"I, Johann Bernoulli, address the most brilliant mathematicians in the world. Nothing is more attractive to intelligent people than an honest, challenging problem, whose possible solution will bestow fame and remain as a lasting monument. Following the example set by Pascal, Fermat, etc., I hope to gain the gratitude of the whole scientific community by placing before the finest mathematicians of our time a problem which will test their methods and the strength of their intellect."

Johann had already solved the problem before issuing his challenge; according to Newton's biographers, solutions were received from Leibniz, l'Hôpital, Jakob Bernoulli, and Newton himself — Newton's arriving anonymously, though Bernoulli reputedly recognised its author "as the lion by its claw mark" (tanquam ex ungue leonem).

The solution

The curve of fastest descent is an arc of a cycloid — the curve traced by a point on the rim of a rolling circle. Johann Bernoulli's own solution used the principle that light travels along the path of least time (Fermat's principle) through a medium whose density varies continuously, invoking Snell's law of refraction at each infinitesimal layer. Because the speed of the bead grows with the square root of the distance fallen (by conservation of energy), the successive refraction yields a differential equation whose solution is the cycloid.

Properties

The brachistochrone curve has the striking property that it is also a tautochrone: a bead released from rest anywhere on the curve reaches the lowest point in the same amount of time, regardless of its starting position. This property was discovered by Christiaan Huygens in 1659 and published in his Horologium Oscillatorium (1673); Huygens used it in attempting to build a perfectly isochronous pendulum clock.

Counterintuitively, the fastest path is not the straight line: although the bead accelerates faster on a steeper initial descent, the longer path length of a very steep curve makes it slower overall. The cycloid dips below the straight line, trading a steeper initial slope for greater speed through the remainder of the descent.

Modern formulation

In the modern calculus of variations, the brachistochrone problem is written as minimising the time functional

T = ∫ √[(1 + y′²) / (2gy)] dx,

where y is measured downward from the starting point. Applying the Euler–Lagrange equation (in its Beltrami identity form, since the integrand does not depend explicitly on x) gives a parametric solution in terms of an angle θ: x = R(θ − sin θ), y = R(1 − cos θ) — the parametric equations of a cycloid generated by a circle of radius R.
