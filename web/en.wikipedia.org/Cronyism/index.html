Cronyism

Cronyism is the practice of partiality in awarding jobs, contracts, and other advantages to friends or trusted colleagues (cronies), especially in politics and in relationships between businesspeople and government officials. It is a form of favouritism closely related to nepotism, which favours relatives, and is generally regarded as a marker of corruption and poor governance because it bypasses merit-based selection and fair competition.

Usage and origins

The word "crony" first appeared in 17th-century London slang, possibly derived from the Greek chronios ("long-lasting"), and originally meant a close friend or chum without pejorative connotation. "Cronyism" acquired its critical meaning in the 20th century, becoming prominent in American political discourse — notably in criticism of appointments in the Truman administration in the early 1950s.

Effects and related concepts

Economists use the related term "crony capitalism" for an economy in which business success depends on close relationships with government officials, through favourable legislation, state-granted monopolies, or preferential access to permits and credit. Anti-cronyism measures include transparent procurement, conflict-of-interest rules, merit-based civil service systems, and disclosure requirements.
