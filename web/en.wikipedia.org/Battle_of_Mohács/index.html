Battle of Mohács

The Battle of Mohács (Hungarian: mohácsi csata, Turkish: Mohaç Meydan Muharebesi) was fought on 29 August 1526 near Mohács, in the Kingdom of Hungary, between the forces of the Kingdom of Hungary and its allies, led by King Louis II, and those of the Ottoman Empire, led by Sultan Suleiman the Magnificent. The Ottoman victory led to the partition of Hungary for several centuries between Ottoman Hungary, the Habsburg Royal Hungary, and the Eastern Hungarian Kingdom. The battle marked the effective end of the medieval Kingdom of Hungary as an independent great power.

Background

By the early 16th century the Ottoman Empire had expanded steadily into the Balkans. Hungary, under the Jagiellonian kings Vladislaus II and Louis II, was weakened by internal factionalism, a depleted treasury, and the refusal of the nobility to fund a standing army. Sultan Suleiman, who had taken Belgrade in 1521 and Rhodes in 1522, demanded tribute from Hungary; when the Hungarian court refused and detained his envoy, he marched north in the spring of 1526 with a large army.

The battle

The Hungarian army, commanded in the field by Archbishop Pál Tomori, took up position on the plain south of Mohács. Rather than await reinforcements from Transylvania under John Zápolya, the Hungarian command chose to attack. On the afternoon of 29 August the Hungarian heavy cavalry charged the Ottoman centre, but the charge was broken by Ottoman regular infantry, the Janissaries, supported by artillery chained together in the Ottoman fashion. The Hungarian army collapsed within roughly two hours. King Louis II died during the retreat, probably drowning in the Csele stream while fleeing the field.

Aftermath

Some 14,000–20,000 Hungarian soldiers were killed, along with much of the kingdom's political elite, including the king, two archbishops, and numerous bishops and barons. The Ottomans advanced to Buda, which they occupied and plundered, though they withdrew before winter. The vacant throne was claimed both by John Zápolya, crowned by one faction of the nobility, and by Ferdinand of Habsburg, brother-in-law of Louis II, beginning a civil war and the tripartite division of Hungary that lasted until the end of the 17th century. In Hungarian historical memory, Mohács became a byword for national catastrophe, summed up in the saying "More was lost at Mohács" (Több is veszett Mohácsnál).
