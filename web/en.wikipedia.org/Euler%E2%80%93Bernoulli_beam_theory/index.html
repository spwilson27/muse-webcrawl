Euler–Bernoulli beam theory

Developed byJacob Bernoulli, Daniel Bernoulli, Leonhard Euler
Periodc. 1750
Key equationEI d⁴w/dx⁴ = q(x)
DomainStructural mechanics, civil and mechanical engineering

Euler–Bernoulli beam theory (also known as engineer's beam theory or classical beam theory) is a simplification of the linear theory of elasticity that provides a means of calculating the load-carrying and deflection characteristics of beams. It was first enunciated circa 1750, arising from the work of Jacob Bernoulli, his nephew Daniel Bernoulli, and Leonhard Euler, and it remained the standard tool of structural analysis through the age of railroads and skyscrapers.

Assumptions

The theory rests on the Bernoulli hypothesis: plane cross-sections of the beam, initially normal to the beam's axis, remain plane and normal to the deformed (bent) axis. This implies that shear deformation is neglected — a good approximation for slender beams whose length greatly exceeds their depth — and that vertical displacement varies only along the beam's length. The resulting strains vary linearly through the depth of the beam.

The Euler–Bernoulli equation

For a beam of flexural rigidity EI under a distributed load q(x), the deflection w(x) satisfies the fourth-order differential equation EI·d⁴w/dx⁴ = q(x). Bending moment and shear force follow from derivatives of the deflection: M = EI·d²w/dx² and V = dM/dx. Combined with boundary conditions (simple support, cantilever, clamped), the equation yields deflections, slopes, and reactions throughout the beam.

Limitations and extensions

For short, deep beams the neglected shear deformation becomes significant; Timoshenko beam theory corrects this by allowing cross-sections to rotate independently of the slope. Euler–Bernoulli theory also assumes small deflections and linear, homogeneous, isotropic material behaviour. Despite its simplifications, it remains the default model in most structural engineering practice and in finite-element codes for beam elements.
