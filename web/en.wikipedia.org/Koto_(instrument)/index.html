Koto (instrument)

Koto
TypeLong zither (chordophone)
Strings13 (traditional); 17- and more-string versions exist
OriginJapan; adapted from the Chinese zheng

The koto (箏) is a Japanese plucked half-tube zither and the national instrument of Japan. It is about 180 centimetres long, carved from paulownia wood, and traditionally strung with thirteen strings stretched over movable bridges, which the player plucks with picks worn on the thumb and first two fingers of the right hand while the left hand presses strings to bend pitches.

History

The koto derives from the Chinese zheng and was introduced to Japan in the early Nara period, becoming part of the gagaku court ensemble. In the Edo period it became a vehicle for chamber music, especially through the composer Yatsuhashi Kengyō (1614–1685), often credited with establishing its solo repertoire, including the famous piece Rokudan no Shirabe.

Music and modern use

Standard tuning (hirajōshi) and other tunings are adjusted by moving the bridges. In the 20th century, composer Michio Miyagi developed a seventeen-string bass koto and wrote the celebrated Haru no Umi ("The Sea in Spring"). Today the koto appears in contemporary works and popular music alongside its traditional role in the sankyoku ensemble with shamisen and shakuhachi.
