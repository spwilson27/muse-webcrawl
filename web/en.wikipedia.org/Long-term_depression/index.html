Long-term depression

In neurophysiology, long-term depression (LTD) is an activity-dependent reduction in the efficacy of neuronal synapses that lasts for hours or longer. It is one of several phenomena underlying synaptic plasticity, the ability of synapses to strengthen or weaken over time, and is considered — together with its counterpart, long-term potentiation (LTP) — a principal cellular mechanism of learning and memory. LTD has been most thoroughly characterised in the hippocampus and the cerebellum.

LTD involves the removal of AMPA receptors from the postsynaptic density

Mechanisms

The best-studied form, NMDA receptor-dependent LTD in the hippocampal CA1 region, is induced by prolonged low-frequency stimulation (about 1 Hz). Modest calcium influx through NMDA receptors preferentially activates protein phosphatases (calcineurin and protein phosphatase 1), rather than the kinases that drive LTP, leading to dephosphorylation and endocytosis of postsynaptic AMPA receptors and a lasting weakening of transmission. A second form depends on metabotropic glutamate receptors (mGluR-LTD) and requires rapid local protein synthesis. In the cerebellum, LTD at parallel fibre–Purkinje cell synapses — induced by conjunctive parallel-fibre and climbing-fibre activity — underlies motor learning.

Functions

LTD is thought to prevent synaptic saturation, sharpen stored information by weakening irrelevant connections, and support memory flexibility and forgetting. In the cerebellum it fine-tunes motor commands; in the hippocampus it contributes to novelty detection and the encoding of spatial information. Early theories also proposed that LTD erases outdated memory traces, complementing LTP's strengthening role.

Clinical relevance

Dysregulated LTD has been implicated in Alzheimer's disease (amyloid-β facilitates LTD and impairs LTP), drug addiction, and neurodevelopmental disorders such as fragile X syndrome, in which mGluR-LTD is exaggerated — the basis of the "mGluR theory" of fragile X.
