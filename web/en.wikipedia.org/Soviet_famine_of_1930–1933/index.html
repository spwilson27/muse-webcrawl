Soviet famine of 1930–1933


Victims of the famine in Ukraine, 1933

The Soviet famine of 1930–1933 was a major famine that swept the grain-growing regions of the Soviet Union in the early 1930s, killing an estimated five to seven million people. It affected Ukraine, southern Russia, the Volga region, and Kazakhstan, and was closely connected with the forced collectivization of agriculture and the grain-procurement policies of Joseph Stalin's government.

Causes

Beginning in 1929, the Soviet state forcibly collectivized peasant agriculture, expropriating land, livestock, and equipment and driving peasants into collective farms. The campaign of "dekulakization" deported millions of the more prosperous peasants. Resistance, slaughter of livestock, and chaotic implementation caused agricultural output to collapse. The state nonetheless imposed grain procurement quotas that took a large share of reduced harvests, exported grain abroad, and punished shortfalls with confiscation of all foodstuffs under decrees such as the "Law of Five Ears of Grain" (August 1932).

The famine by region

In Ukraine, the famine of 1932–33 is known as the Holodomor. Blacklists imposed on villages and districts, internal passport restrictions, and the closure of Ukraine's internal borders prevented peasants from leaving to find food; estimates of deaths in Ukraine range from about 3.3 to 5 million. In Kazakhstan, the famine of 1930–33 — called Asharshylyk — destroyed the traditional nomadic pastoral economy as Kazakhs were forced to settle; roughly 1.3 to 1.5 million people, around a third of all ethnic Kazakhs, died, and many more fled to China and neighbouring republics. Famine also struck the Volga basin, the North Caucasus, and the Urals.

Historiography

Scholars agree that government policies were central to the famine's scale, though there is debate about intentionality. Ukraine and a number of other countries recognize the Holodomor as a genocide of the Ukrainian people; other historians emphasize region-wide policy failures and repression directed at peasants generally rather than a single national group. The famine was officially denied in the Soviet Union for decades.
