En bloc clip

An en bloc clip is a device used to hold cartridges together, ready for loading into a firearm's internal magazine as a single unit. Unlike a stripper clip, which is removed after the cartridges are pushed into the magazine, an en bloc clip is inserted into the magazine together with its cartridges and remains inside the weapon until the last round is fired, at which point it is ejected or falls free. The term en bloc is French for "in one block".

The eight-round en bloc clip of the M1 Garand rifle

History

The en bloc system was pioneered by Ferdinand Mannlicher for the Austrian M1885 and M1886 straight-pull rifles, using a five-round clip that dropped out of the bottom of the magazine when empty. France adopted an en bloc clip for the Lebel Model 1886's successor systems, and Italy and other nations followed. The system allowed rapid reloading of a full clip at once in an era when detachable box magazines were uncommon on service rifles.

The M1 Garand

The most famous en bloc design is that of the American M1 Garand rifle, standard issue in the Second World War. Its eight-round clip of .30-06 Springfield cartridges is pressed down into the receiver; when the last round is fired, the empty clip is ejected upward with a distinctive metallic "ping" — a sound veterans recalled fondly, though popular claims that enemies exploited the noise are largely anecdotal. General George S. Patton praised the Garand as "the greatest battle implement ever devised". A drawback of the Garand system is that the rifle cannot be easily topped up with a partial clip in the ordinary way.

Characteristics

Because the clip forms part of the feeding mechanism, a rifle designed for en bloc clips generally cannot function without one (except by loading single rounds laboriously). Clips are cheap, stamped from spring steel, and treated as semi-disposable. Later designs replaced en bloc clips with stripper-clip loading or detachable magazines, which allowed partial reloading and larger capacities.
