Informal logic

Informal logic is a branch of logic and of critical thinking concerned with the analysis and evaluation of arguments as they occur in natural language and everyday contexts — in newspapers, debates, legal proceedings, advertising and ordinary conversation — rather than in the symbolic formal systems of formal logic.

Scope

Informal logic studies the standards, criteria and procedures for analysing ordinary reasoning. Its characteristic topics include the identification and reconstruction of arguments, the assessment of premises for relevance, acceptability and sufficiency, argumentation schemes and their critical questions, and the theory of fallacies — patterns of reasoning that appear persuasive but are defective, such as ad hominem attacks, straw man arguments, appeals to ignorance and hasty generalisations.

History

Interest in the logic of everyday argument grew in North America in the 1970s, partly in reaction to logic textbooks that treated fallacies briefly and formally. Works such as Howard Kahane's Logic and Contemporary Rhetoric (1971) taught logic through contemporary public issues. J. Anthony Blair and Ralph H. Johnson, at the University of Windsor, became leading figures; they founded the journal Informal Logic in 1978 and organised the first international symposium on informal logic. Their textbook Logical Self-Defense helped define the field's approach to evaluating arguments by the criteria of premise acceptability, relevance and sufficiency of grounds.

Relation to other fields

Informal logic overlaps with argumentation theory, pragma-dialectics (developed by Frans van Eemeren and Rob Grootendorst in Amsterdam), Stephen Toulmin's model of argument layout in The Uses of Argument (1958), and the critical thinking movement in education. It differs from formal logic in that it does not restrict validity to deductive form, recognising instead conductive and defeasible reasoning in which premises support a conclusion presumptively and can be outweighed by counter-considerations.

Methods

Practitioners reconstruct arguments by supplying implicit premises, diagram argument structure, test arguments against critical questions appropriate to their scheme (for example, whether an expert cited is genuinely expert in the relevant field), and evaluate dialogue context to decide which standards of proof apply.
