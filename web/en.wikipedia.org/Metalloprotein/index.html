Metalloprotein

A metalloprotein is a protein that contains a metal ion cofactor. Metalloproteins make up a very large fraction of all proteins — for some estimates, roughly a quarter to a half of all proteins require a metal to function. The metal ions are usually coordinated by nitrogen, oxygen or sulfur atoms belonging to amino acid residues such as histidine, cysteine, aspartate and glutamate.

Roles

Metals in proteins perform a wide range of tasks. In enzymes they serve as catalytic centres and as Lewis acids; iron in haemoglobin and myoglobin binds oxygen for transport and storage; copper and iron proteins such as cytochrome c oxidase carry electrons in respiration; zinc often plays a structural role, as in zinc finger domains, but is also catalytic in enzymes such as carbonic anhydrase. Molybdenum, manganese, cobalt, nickel and vanadium also appear in specialised enzymes — molybdenum in nitrogenase, which fixes atmospheric nitrogen, and manganese in the oxygen-evolving complex of photosynthesis.

Metalloenzymes and storage

Enzymes containing metal ions are called metalloenzymes. Organisms carefully manage metal supply because essential metals are toxic in excess: proteins such as ferritin store iron, metallothioneins bind heavy metals, and dedicated chaperones deliver ions like copper to their target proteins. Disruption of metal homeostasis is implicated in diseases including Wilson's disease (copper) and haemochromatosis (iron).

Study

Bioinorganic chemistry studies metalloproteins using spectroscopy, X-ray crystallography and computational chemistry. The field also inspires artificial metalloenzymes and biomimetic catalysts. Haemoproteins such as haemoglobin, with their iron-containing haem group, were among the earliest metalloproteins characterised.
