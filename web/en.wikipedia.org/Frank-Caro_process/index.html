Frank–Caro process

The Frank–Caro process is an industrial process for the production of calcium cyanamide (CaCN2), developed in Germany by Adolph Frank and Nikodem Caro in the 1890s. It was the first process used commercially to fix atmospheric nitrogen, preceding the Haber–Bosch process, and calcium cyanamide became an important nitrogen fertilizer sold under names such as Kalkstickstoff ("lime nitrogen").

Process

In the process, calcium carbide (CaC2) is heated to around 1,000 °C in an atmosphere of pure nitrogen. The carbide absorbs nitrogen exothermically to form calcium cyanamide and carbon:

CaC2 + N2 → CaCN2 + C

The nitrogen was obtained by fractional distillation of liquid air. The resulting product, containing roughly 20–24% nitrogen along with lime and free carbon, could be applied directly to soil, where it slowly hydrolyses to release ammonia usable by plants.

History and significance

Frank and Caro patented the process in 1895, and large-scale production began in the early 1900s, notably at plants in Italy and Germany. The process tied nitrogen fixation to the carbide industry and therefore to cheap hydroelectric power. Although the Haber–Bosch synthesis of ammonia largely displaced it for fertilizer production, calcium cyanamide manufacture by the Frank–Caro route continued, and the compound remains in niche use both as a fertilizer and as a chemical intermediate, for example in the production of melamine, dicyandiamide, and thiourea.
