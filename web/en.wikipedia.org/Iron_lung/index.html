Iron lung

An iron lung is a type of negative pressure ventilator (NPV), a form of medical ventilator that enables a person to breathe when normal muscle control has been lost or the work of breathing exceeds the person's ability. The patient lies inside a sealed cylindrical chamber with only the head outside, and pumps rhythmically lower and raise the air pressure inside the chamber, causing the chest to expand and contract.

History

The widely used design was developed by Philip Drinker and Louis Agassiz Shaw of Harvard and first used clinically in 1928 on a young girl with polio at Boston Children's Hospital. In 1931 John Haven Emerson introduced an improved, cheaper model that dominated the market. During the polio epidemics of the 1940s and 1950s, hospital wards were filled with rows of iron lungs sustaining patients whose respiratory muscles were paralysed.

Operation and use

The airtight tank encloses the body up to the neck, sealed by a rubber collar. When the pressure inside falls, air flows into the patient's lungs; when it rises, air is expelled. Patients could need the machine for a few weeks during acute illness — or for life. Paul Alexander, paralysed by polio in 1952, lived using an iron lung for over seventy years until his death in 2024.

Decline

The mass deployment of the polio vaccines of Jonas Salk (1955) and Albert Sabin ended the epidemics that had sustained demand, and modern positive-pressure ventilators — smaller and allowing easier nursing care — replaced negative-pressure tanks from the 1960s onward. Small numbers of users, and cuirass-style negative pressure devices, remain in use today.
