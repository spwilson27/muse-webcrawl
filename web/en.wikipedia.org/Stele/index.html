Stele

Material: Stone or wood
Uses: Funerary, commemorative, legal, boundary markers
Cultures: Egypt, Mesopotamia, Greece, Maya, China

A stele (plural stelae or steles) is a stone or wooden slab, generally taller than it is wide, erected in the ancient world as a monument. Stelae were often used for funerary or commemorative purposes, to publish laws and decrees, to mark territory or boundaries, or as votive offerings.

Notable examples

Among the most famous stelae are the Code of Hammurabi stele from Babylon, inscribed with one of the earliest surviving law codes, and the Rosetta Stone, a Ptolemaic decree whose parallel texts in hieroglyphic, Demotic and Greek enabled the decipherment of Egyptian hieroglyphs. In ancient Egypt, rounded-top funerary stelae recorded the name and titles of the deceased with offering formulae. Greek grave stelai of the Classical period bear relief sculpture of the dead.

In Mesoamerica, Maya stelae were tall carved stone shafts, frequently paired with circular altars, depicting rulers and recording dynastic history in hieroglyphic texts; large examples stand at sites such as Tikal and Copán. In China, inscribed stone tablets (bei) recorded texts, commemorated events and were erected at temples and tombs.
