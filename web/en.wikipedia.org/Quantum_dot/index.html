Quantum dot

Quantum dots (QDs) are semiconductor particles a few nanometres in size with optical and electronic properties that differ from those of larger particles via quantum mechanical effects. They are a central topic in nanotechnology and materials science. When a quantum dot is illuminated by UV light, an electron in the quantum dot can be excited to a state of higher energy; when it returns to the ground state it releases its energy as light whose colour depends on the size of the dot.

Properties

The larger the dot, the longer the wavelength (redder) of the emitted light, because confinement raises the band gap energy as size decreases. This size-tunable emission, together with high brightness and photostability, makes quantum dots useful fluorophores. Quantum dots confine electrons in all three spatial dimensions — unlike quantum wells (one dimension) and quantum wires (two).

History and production

Quantum dots were first synthesised in glass matrices by Alexey Ekimov in 1981 and in colloidal solutions by Louis Brus in 1983. The 2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry was awarded to Moungi Bawendi, Louis Brus and Alexey Ekimov "for the discovery and synthesis of quantum dots". They are now commonly produced by colloidal synthesis, molecular beam epitaxy (self-assembled dots), and lithographic techniques.

Applications

Applications include displays (QLED televisions, where quantum dot films enhance colour gamut), light-emitting diodes, photovoltaics and solar cells, biological imaging and labelling, photodetectors, lasers, and experimental quantum computing architectures using loss-DiVincenzo style spin qubits.

Health and environmental concerns

Many high-performance quantum dots contain cadmium or other heavy metals, raising concerns about toxicity and disposal; this has driven research into indium phosphide and other heavy-metal-free alternatives, and into core–shell structures that encapsulate the toxic core.
