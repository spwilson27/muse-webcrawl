Computational complexity theory

Computational complexity theory is a branch of theoretical computer science that focuses on classifying computational problems according to their inherent difficulty, measured by the resources—chiefly time and space—needed to solve them, and relating these classes to each other. A problem is regarded as inherently difficult if solving it requires substantial resources no matter what algorithm is used.

Computational problems and models

Problems are typically formulated as decision problems (yes/no questions) or function problems, and resources are measured as a function of input size on abstract models such as the Turing machine. Key complexity classes include P (problems solvable in polynomial time deterministically), NP (problems whose solutions can be verified in polynomial time), PSPACE (polynomial space), and EXPTIME (exponential time).

The P versus NP problem

The most famous open question of the field, and one of the Millennium Prize Problems, asks whether P = NP: whether every problem whose solution can be quickly verified can also be quickly solved. The Cook–Levin theorem (1971) showed that Boolean satisfiability is NP-complete—the hardest kind of problem in NP—so a polynomial algorithm for any NP-complete problem would imply P = NP. Thousands of problems in optimisation, logic, and combinatorics are known to be NP-complete.

Other areas

The field also encompasses randomised complexity classes such as BPP, interactive proof systems, circuit complexity and lower bounds, approximation and hardness of approximation, average-case complexity, and quantum complexity classes such as BQP.
