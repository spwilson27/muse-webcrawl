The Dodecanese (Greek: Δωδεκάνησα, "the twelve islands") are a group of 15 larger plus more than 150 smaller Greek islands in the southeastern Aegean Sea and eastern Mediterranean, off the coast of Turkey. Despite the name, which dates from Byzantine times, the group today comprises many more than twelve inhabited islands; the name originally referred to the twelve islands that formed a federation in antiquity, a number later fixed by the Ottoman administration.

Rhodes is the best known and historically most important island of the group, and serves as the seat of the regional unit. Other significant islands include Kos, birthplace of Hippocrates; Patmos, where tradition holds the Book of Revelation was written; Kalymnos, famous for sponge diving; and Karpathos and Symi.

History

The islands were settled in antiquity and flourished under the Knights Hospitaller, who ruled Rhodes from 1309 to 1522. The Ottomans then held the islands for nearly four centuries. In 1912, during the Italo-Turkish War, Italy occupied the islands; Italian rule, which included ambitious building programmes and attempted Italianisation, lasted until the Second World War. After a brief British military administration, the Dodecanese were formally united with Greece in 1948 under the 1947 Paris Peace Treaty.

Geography and economy

The islands are generally rocky and mountainous with a Mediterranean climate of hot, dry summers. Tourism is the mainstay of the modern economy, supported by beaches, medieval old towns (the medieval city of Rhodes is a UNESCO World Heritage Site) and ferry links to Piraeus and the Turkish coast.
