Twelve-tone technique

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The twelve-tone technique — also known as dodecaphony, twelve-tone serialism, and, in British usage, twelve-note composition — is a method of musical composition devised by Austrian composer Arnold Schoenberg (1874–1951). It ensures that all 12 notes of the chromatic scale are sounded as often as one another in a piece of music while preventing the emphasis of any one note through the use of tone rows, orderings of the 12 pitch classes.

Method

The composer first arranges the twelve pitch classes into an ordering called the basic row (or series). The row may be used in four canonical forms: the prime (original), inversion (intervals flipped), retrograde (reversed) and retrograde inversion, each transposable to all twelve pitch levels — giving a 12×12 matrix of 48 forms from which the material of a work is drawn. Notes are freely assigned octave, rhythm, register and timbre unless the composer serialises those parameters too.

History

Schoenberg developed the method in the early 1920s, first using it in movements of his Suite for Piano, Op. 25 (1921–23), after a period of "free atonality". His pupils Anton Webern and Alban Berg — the Second Viennese School — adopted it, Berg combining it with tonal references. After the Second World War the technique strongly influenced composers such as Milton Babbitt, Pierre Boulez and Luigi Nono, and was extended into total serialism; Igor Stravinsky adopted twelve-tone methods in his late works.

Criticism

The technique has been praised as a rigorous new organising principle for post-tonal music and criticised as overly theoretical, since listeners cannot ordinarily perceive row structures. It remains a central topic in 20th-century music theory.
