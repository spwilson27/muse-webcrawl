Rabies

Rabies
Cause: Rabies virus (Lyssavirus)
Transmission: Bites from infected animals, esp. dogs
Incubation: Usually 1–3 months
Prognosis: Almost invariably fatal once symptomatic
Deaths: ~59,000 per year worldwide

Rabies is a viral disease that causes inflammation of the brain in humans and other mammals. It is transmitted when an infected animal scratches or bites another animal or human, most commonly dogs. Saliva from an infected animal can also transmit rabies if it contacts mucous membranes. Once clinical symptoms appear, rabies is virtually 100% fatal, but it is entirely preventable by prompt post-exposure vaccination.

Symptoms

Early symptoms include fever and tingling at the wound site. As the virus travels along nerves to the brain, two forms may develop: furious rabies, with hyperactivity, agitation and hydrophobia — terror of water caused by painful spasms of the throat — and paralytic rabies, a slower progression to paralysis and coma. Death usually follows within days of symptom onset, typically from respiratory failure.

Prevention and treatment

Louis Pasteur developed the first rabies vaccine in 1885, famously treating the boy Joseph Meister. Modern control relies on mass vaccination of dogs, which cause up to 99% of human cases, and on post-exposure prophylaxis: thorough wound washing, a course of rabies vaccine and, for severe exposures, rabies immunoglobulin. The induced-coma Milwaukee protocol has saved a handful of symptomatic patients since 2004 but remains unreliable.

Epidemiology

Rabies causes about 59,000 deaths annually, overwhelmingly in Africa and Asia, while countries including Britain, Australia and Japan are free of terrestrial rabies.
