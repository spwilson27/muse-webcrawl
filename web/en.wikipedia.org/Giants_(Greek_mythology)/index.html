Giants (Greek mythology)

The Gigantomachy — the battle of the gods against the Giants — was a favourite subject of Greek art.

In Greek mythology, the Giants (Greek: Γίγαντες, Gígantes) were a race of beings born from Gaia (Earth), fertilised by the blood of the castrated Uranus (Sky). They are best known for the Gigantomachy, their war against the Olympian gods, a conflict that mirrored the earlier Titanomachy.

The Gigantomachy

According to Apollodorus, the Giants attacked Olympus, piling mountains Pelion and Ossa upon one another to reach the heavens. An oracle declared that the gods could prevail only with the help of a mortal, and Zeus summoned Heracles, whose arrows struck down Giant after Giant as the gods wounded them. Named Giants include Alcyoneus, who was immortal while he remained on his native soil; Porphyrion, who attacked Hera and was struck by Zeus's thunderbolt and Heracles' arrow; and Enceladus, buried beneath Mount Etna.

Depictions

Early Greek art generally depicted the Giants as normal-sized hoplite warriors; the familiar image of monstrous, sometimes serpent-legged beings developed in the Hellenistic period, most famously in the frieze of the Pergamon Altar. The Giants should not be confused with the Titans, an earlier generation of divine beings, or with the one-eyed Cyclopes.
