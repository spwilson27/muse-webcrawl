Supervenience

In philosophy, supervenience refers to a relation between sets of properties or facts. A set of properties A supervenes upon another set B just in case no two things can differ with respect to A-properties without also differing with respect to their B-properties. In slogan form: "there cannot be an A-difference without a B-difference."

Forms of supervenience

Philosophers distinguish weak, strong and global supervenience, and whether the dependence holds within a single possible world or across all possible worlds. Global supervenience states that two worlds identical in all B-respects are identical in all A-respects. The varieties differ in modal strength and in whether they support reduction of the supervening properties to the base properties.

Applications

Donald Davidson used supervenience in his anomalous monism to claim that mental properties supervene on physical properties while denying strict psychophysical laws. In ethics, R. M. Hare and others held that moral properties supervene on natural properties: two situations identical in every natural respect cannot differ morally. Supervenience also figures in aesthetics, and in debates about whether biological or social facts supervene on physical facts.

Criticism

Critics, notably Jaegwon Kim, have argued that supervenience by itself explains nothing: it states a pattern of covariation without identifying the underlying dependence, and mere supervenience is compatible with the supervening level being causally inert — a worry pressed in the debate over mental causation and the causal exclusion problem. Defenders reply that supervenience is a useful minimal commitment that captures dependence without reduction.
