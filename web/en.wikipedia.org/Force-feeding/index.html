Force-feeding

Force-feeding is the practice of feeding a human or animal against their will. The term generally applies to humans when food is delivered by mouth, but can also refer to introducing nutrition through a feeding tube (gavage). Force-feeding is widely considered a violation of bodily autonomy and, in many contexts, a form of cruel or inhuman treatment.

A suffragette being force-fed in Holloway Prison, London, c. 1911.

Hunger strikes

Force-feeding is most associated with the response of authorities to hunger strikes by prisoners. In Britain, suffragettes on hunger strike in the early 20th century were force-fed through tubes, a painful and dangerous practice that contributed to the passage of the "Cat and Mouse Act" in 1913. Irish republican prisoners, Soviet dissidents, and detainees at Guantanamo Bay have also been force-fed. The World Medical Association's Declaration of Malta states that force-feeding of competent hunger strikers is never ethically acceptable.

Animals

In animal agriculture, force-feeding (gavage) is used in the production of foie gras, in which ducks and geese are fed large quantities by tube to enlarge their livers. The practice is banned in many countries on animal-welfare grounds and remains a subject of legal and ethical controversy.

Medical contexts

Distinguished from coercive force-feeding is assisted enteral nutrition for patients unable to eat, such as those with severe neurological impairment. Feeding in severe anorexia nervosa under mental-health law is a further contested area, balancing the duty to preserve life against patient autonomy.
