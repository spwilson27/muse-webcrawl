The Second Temple period in Jewish history is the period between the construction of the Second Temple in Jerusalem (traditionally dated c. 516 BCE, after the return from Babylonian exile under Zerubbabel) and its destruction by the Roman Empire in 70 CE during the First Jewish–Roman War. The period spans some six centuries of Persian, Hellenistic, Hasmonean, and Herodian/Roman rule, and is of foundational importance to Judaism and to early Christianity.

Periods

Under Persia, the small temple province of Yehud was governed by high priests and Persian-appointed governors; figures such as Ezra and Nehemiah are credited with restoring the Temple cult, the Law and Jerusalem's walls. Conquest by Alexander the Great (332 BCE) began the Hellenistic era, in which Judea passed between the Ptolemies and the Seleucids. The attempted Hellenisation and desecration of the Temple by Antiochus IV provoked the Maccabean Revolt (167–160 BCE) led by Judas Maccabeus — commemorated by Hanukkah — and ultimately produced the independent Hasmonean kingdom (140–37 BCE).

Herodian and Roman periods

Herod the Great, installed by Rome in 37 BCE, rebuilt the Temple on a vast scale, creating the great platform whose Western Wall survives today. After his death Judea was divided among his sons and, from 6 CE, largely ruled directly by Roman prefects and procurators, among them Pontius Pilate (26–36 CE), under whom Jesus of Nazareth was crucified. The period saw the flourishing and rivalry of sects — Pharisees, Sadducees, Essenes (associated with the Dead Sea Scrolls) and Zealots — and the writing of much of the Hebrew Bible's final form, the Septuagint, and apocryphal and pseudepigraphal literature. Rising tensions erupted in the revolt of 66 CE; after a terrible siege, Jerusalem and the Temple were destroyed in 70 CE, ending the sacrificial cult and reshaping Judaism toward the rabbinic, synagogue-centred form centred at Yavne.
