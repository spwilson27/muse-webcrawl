Information-theoretic security

Information-theoretic security is a security property of a cryptographic system that holds even against an adversary with unlimited computational power. A system with information-theoretic (or unconditional) security cannot be broken even if the adversary has unlimited computing resources, because the available information is mathematically insufficient to determine the secret.

Perfect secrecy

The classic example is the one-time pad. In his 1949 paper "Communication Theory of Secrecy Systems", Claude Shannon proved that the one-time pad achieves perfect secrecy: the ciphertext reveals no information about the plaintext, provided the key is truly random, at least as long as the message, kept secret, and never reused. Shannon also proved that any perfectly secret cipher must have keys at least as long as the messages it encrypts, which limits the practicality of perfect secrecy.

Related concepts

Information-theoretic methods also underpin secret sharing schemes (such as Shamir's scheme), unconditionally secure message authentication codes, and quantum key distribution, whose security rests on the laws of physics rather than computational assumptions. The semantic security of most practical cryptosystems, by contrast, is computational: it relies on unproven assumptions about the difficulty of problems such as integer factorisation or discrete logarithms.

Applications

Because of their key-management costs, information-theoretically secure systems are used mainly in high-assurance niches, such as diplomatic and military links, and as components within larger systems. Research continues into bounded-storage models and other relaxations that make unconditional security more practical.
