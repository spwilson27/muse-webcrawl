Being and Nothingness: An Essay on Phenomenological Ontology (French: L'Être et le néant, 1943) is the major philosophical work of Jean-Paul Sartre and a foundational text of existentialism. Drawing on Husserl and Heidegger, Sartre develops an account of consciousness, freedom and human relationships.

Key concepts

Sartre distinguishes being-in-itself (en-soi), the inert being of things, from being-for-itself (pour-soi), the self-aware, self-negating being of consciousness. Consciousness introduces "nothingness" into the world, making freedom inescapable: humans are "condemned to be free". Bad faith (mauvaise foi) is the self-deception by which people deny their freedom, famously illustrated by the waiter who plays at being a waiter. The analysis of the "look" of the Other grounds his account of interpersonal conflict.

Reception

The book made Sartre the leading figure of French existentialism, though its dense style was famously mocked as well as admired. Sartre later sought to reconcile existentialism with Marxism in the Critique of Dialectical Reason (1960).
