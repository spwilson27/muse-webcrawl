Stealth aircraft

Stealth aircraft are designed to avoid detection using a variety of technologies that reduce reflection and emission of radar, infrared, visible light, radio frequencies, and audio (collectively known as stealth technology). The goal is to make it more difficult for an enemy to detect, track, and engage the aircraft, increasing its survivability in contested airspace.

Principles

The most important measure is reducing the radar cross-section (RCS). This is achieved primarily through shaping: flat, angled surfaces and sharp edges reflect radar energy away from its source rather than back to it, while radar-absorbent materials and coatings dissipate some incoming energy. Other measures include burying engines deep in the airframe and cooling exhausts to reduce infrared signature, carrying weapons internally to avoid radar-reflecting external stores, and using emission control of onboard radars and radios.

History

The theoretical foundation was laid by Soviet physicist Pyotr Ufimtsev's 1962 work on the diffraction of electromagnetic waves, which was translated and exploited in the United States in the 1970s. Lockheed's Have Blue prototype (1977) demonstrated that a faceted design could achieve extremely low RCS, leading to the F-117 Nighthawk, revealed publicly in 1988 and used in combat over Panama and Iraq. The B-2 Spirit flying-wing bomber followed, applying smooth blended curves made possible by improved computing.

Modern aircraft

Fifth-generation fighters such as the F-22 Raptor and F-35 Lightning II combine stealth with supersonic performance and sensor fusion. Russia (Su-57) and China (J-20, J-35) have fielded their own low-observable designs, and stealth technology has extended to unmanned aircraft such as the RQ-170 Sentinel.

Countermeasures

Stealth shaping is optimized mainly against high-frequency fire-control radars; low-frequency (VHF) radars, passive detection systems, and infrared search-and-track sensors can partially mitigate it. In 1999 a Yugoslav air-defence unit using an S-125 system shot down an F-117, demonstrating that stealth confers an advantage, not invisibility.
