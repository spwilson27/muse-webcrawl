Villus

A villus (plural villi) is a small, finger-like projection of tissue. The term is most commonly used for the intestinal villi, the millions of tiny projections that line the inner wall of the small intestine and greatly increase its surface area for the absorption of nutrients. Villi also occur elsewhere in the body, notably as the chorionic villi of the placenta.

Intestinal villi

The inner surface of the small intestine is folded into circular folds (plicae circulares), upon which sit the villi. Each villus is covered by a single layer of epithelial cells, the enterocytes, whose apical surfaces bear still smaller projections called microvilli, forming the brush border. Together these structures amplify the absorptive surface of the intestine by orders of magnitude.

Inside each villus is a core of connective tissue (the lamina propria) containing a network of blood capillaries and a central lymphatic vessel called a lacteal. Sugars and amino acids absorbed by the enterocytes pass into the capillaries and travel via the hepatic portal vein to the liver, while fatty acids and monoglycerides, repackaged into chylomicrons, enter the lacteal and reach the blood through the lymphatic system.

Renewal and damage

Villus epithelial cells are continuously renewed: stem cells in the crypts of Lieberkühn at the base of the villi produce new cells that migrate upward and are shed at the tip within a few days. In coeliac disease, an immune reaction to gluten causes villous atrophy — the villi flatten and absorption fails — while certain infections and chemotherapy can also damage them.

Chorionic villi

In the placenta, chorionic villi are branching projections of the chorion that grow into the maternal blood spaces of the uterus, maximising contact for exchange of nutrients, gases and wastes between mother and fetus. A sample of these villi can be taken in chorionic villus sampling for prenatal genetic diagnosis.
