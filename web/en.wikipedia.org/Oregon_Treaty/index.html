Oregon Treaty

Oregon Treaty
Signed: 15 June 1846, Washington, D.C.
Parties: United States and United Kingdom
Effect: Settled the Oregon boundary dispute at the 49th parallel

The Oregon Treaty is a treaty between the United Kingdom and the United States that was signed on 15 June 1846, in Washington, D.C. It ended the Oregon boundary dispute by settling competing American and British claims to the Oregon Country, which had been jointly occupied since the Treaty of 1818.

Terms

The treaty set the border between the United States and British North America along the 49th parallel of north latitude from the Rocky Mountains to the Strait of Georgia, then through the Strait of Juan de Fuca to the Pacific, leaving all of Vancouver Island to Britain. The Hudson's Bay Company retained navigation rights on the Columbia River. The ambiguous water boundary later gave rise to the "Pig War" of 1859 between the San Juan Islands, finally settled by arbitration in 1872.

Background

The dispute had intensified as American settlers travelled the Oregon Trail in the 1840s. The American expansionist slogan "Fifty-four forty or fight!" demanded the whole territory up to latitude 54°40′. President James K. Polk's administration, at war with Mexico and eager to avoid a second conflict, accepted a compromise at 49° — a line the British had long proposed — and the Senate ratified the treaty in June 1846.
