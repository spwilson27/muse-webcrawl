CPU power dissipation

CPU power dissipation is the process in which central processing units (CPUs) consume electrical energy and dissipate it as heat. Managing this heat — through heatsinks, fans and thermal design — is a central constraint on processor performance, since excessive temperature can damage the chip or force it to throttle.

Sources of power

Power consumption in a CMOS processor has two main components. Dynamic (switching) power is consumed when transistors change state and is approximated by P = C·V²·f, depending on capacitance, the square of the supply voltage, and clock frequency. Static power arises from leakage currents that flow even when transistors are nominally off; leakage grew sharply as transistors shrank to nanometre scales.

Vendors specify thermal design power (TDP), the maximum heat a cooling system is expected to dissipate under realistic workloads. Historically, Dennard scaling allowed each generation of smaller transistors to run faster at constant power density, but its breakdown around 2005–2006 ended the era of rapid clock-speed growth — the "power wall" — and pushed the industry toward multicore designs, dynamic voltage and frequency scaling (DVFS), clock gating and heterogeneous architectures.

Cooling

Dissipated heat is typically removed by a heat spreader and finned heatsink with fan-driven airflow; high-performance and overclocked systems use heat pipes, vapour chambers or liquid cooling. Temperature sensors on the die allow firmware to reduce voltage and frequency (thermal throttling) or shut the processor down before damage occurs.
