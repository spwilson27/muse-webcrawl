Anticlericalism

Anticlericalism is opposition to religious authority, typically in social or political matters. Historically, anticlericalism has been opposed to the influence of Roman Catholicism in public affairs, though it has also been directed against other forms of religious power. Anticlericalism does not necessarily constitute opposition to religion itself, but rather to the political and institutional power of the clergy and religious hierarchies.

History

Anticlerical movements emerged strongly in Europe during the Enlightenment, when thinkers such as Voltaire criticized the privileges and wealth of the clergy. During the French Revolution, the Civil Constitution of the Clergy (1790) subordinated the Catholic Church to the state, and a campaign of dechristianization followed. In the 19th century, anticlericalism was a major force in liberal politics in France, Spain, Italy, and Latin America.

In France, the Third Republic passed laws separating church and state, culminating in the 1905 law on the Separation of the Churches and the State. In Mexico, the Constitution of 1917 and the Calles Law provoked the Cristero War (1926–1929). In Spain, anticlerical violence during the Spanish Civil War (1936–1939) saw the killing of thousands of clergy and religious.

By country

Anticlerical policies have appeared in many states. The Soviet Union pursued state atheism and suppressed religious institutions. In Turkey, Kemalism restricted the public role of Islam. In Quebec, the Quiet Revolution of the 1960s rapidly reduced the Catholic Church's role in education and health care.
