Suffering Servant

The Suffering Servant is a figure described in the Book of Isaiah in a series of passages known as the Servant songs: Isaiah 42:1–4, 49:1–6, 50:4–7, and 52:13–53:12. The fourth song depicts a servant of God who is despised and rejected, bears the suffering and sins of others ("he was wounded for our transgressions"), is put to death, and is ultimately vindicated and exalted. The identity of the servant is one of the most debated questions in biblical scholarship.

Interpretations

In Jewish interpretation, the servant is commonly understood as the nation of Israel personified — Isaiah elsewhere explicitly calls Israel God's servant (e.g., Isaiah 41:8; 44:1–2; 49:3) — or as a righteous remnant or a figure such as the prophet himself. In Christian interpretation, the songs are read as prophecies of Jesus, and the New Testament repeatedly applies them to him: Isaiah 53 is quoted in Matthew 8:17, John 12:38, Acts 8:26–40 (the Ethiopian eunuch passage), and 1 Peter 2:21–25, and the songs strongly shaped early Christian understanding of Jesus's death as vicarious atonement.

Scholarly discussion

The Servant songs were identified as a distinct set by Bernhard Duhm in 1892, who attributed them to "Deutero-Isaiah" (Isaiah 40–55). Scholars debate whether the servant is an individual (a king, prophet, or the future Messiah), a collective, or moves between the two, and how the songs relate to their literary context and to ancient Near Eastern ideas of kingship and substitutionary suffering.
