Canard (aeronautics)

In aeronautics, a canard is a wing configuration in which a small forewing, rather than the main wing, carries the horizontal stabilizer and elevator at the front of the aircraft. The term is French for "duck", alluding to the appearance of the Santos-Dumont 14-bis of 1906, one of the earliest canard aircraft, which itself followed the Wright brothers' canard designs.

Types

Canards are broadly classified as control-canards, which act mainly as control surfaces (common on modern fighter aircraft), lifting-canards, which share the lifting load with the main wing, and close-coupled canards, positioned near the main wing so that their vortices energize airflow over the wing and improve lift at high angles of attack.

Advantages and disadvantages

A properly designed canard can stall before the main wing, making the aircraft resistant to departure and spin, and can reduce trim drag because both surfaces produce positive lift. Drawbacks include the risk that canard downwash interferes with the main wing, difficulty hiding the foreplane's radar signature, and the possibility that a badly balanced design stalls the main wing first, with severe consequences. Modern fighters such as the Saab Gripen, Dassault Rafale, and Eurofighter Typhoon use close-coupled canards combined with delta wings and fly-by-wire controls.
