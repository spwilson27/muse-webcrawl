Car layout

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Car layout describes the placement of the engine and driven wheels in an automobile. Layouts are conventionally abbreviated by engine position followed by drive configuration: FF (front-engine, front-wheel drive), FR (front-engine, rear-wheel drive), MR (mid-engine, rear-wheel drive), RR (rear-engine, rear-wheel drive), and corresponding all-wheel-drive variants. The layout strongly affects packaging, weight distribution, handling, and cost.

Front-engine layouts

Front-engine, front-wheel drive (FF) places the engine over or ahead of the front axle and drives the front wheels, often with the engine mounted transversely. Popularized in mass-market cars from the 1960s (e.g., the Mini and later the Volkswagen Golf), FF offers compact packaging, good cabin space, and predictable handling, and is now the dominant layout for passenger cars.

Front-engine, rear-wheel drive (FR) was the classic arrangement for much of the 20th century: the engine sits at the front and drives the rear wheels through a propeller shaft. It remains common in sports cars, luxury saloons, pickup trucks, and SUVs for its balanced weight distribution and towing capability.

Mid- and rear-engine layouts

Mid-engine layouts (MR or MF) place the engine between the axles, near the car's centre of mass, giving agile handling at the cost of luggage and sometimes cabin space; they are standard in supercars. The rear-engine, rear-wheel drive (RR) layout, exemplified by the Volkswagen Beetle and Porsche 911, puts the engine behind the rear axle, with characteristic traction advantages and pendulous handling traits that engineers have progressively tamed.

Electric vehicles

Battery-electric cars free designers from traditional constraints: flat underfloor battery packs and compact motors permit layouts such as single-motor front or rear drive and dual-motor all-wheel drive, with low centres of gravity.
