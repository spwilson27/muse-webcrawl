Newton's method in optimization

In calculus, Newton's method (also called the Newton–Raphson method) is an iterative method for finding the roots of a differentiable function f, which are the solutions to f(x) = 0. In optimization, the method is applied to the derivative f′ of a twice-differentiable function f in order to find the roots of the derivative — that is, the stationary points of f, which include its local maxima and minima.

The method

Starting from a guess x0, the iteration is xn+1 = xn − f′(xn) / f″(xn). In multiple dimensions, the scalar second derivative is replaced by the inverse of the Hessian matrix of second derivatives, giving the Newton step −[∇²f(x)]−1∇f(x). Near a well-behaved optimum the method converges quadratically, much faster than gradient descent.

Advantages and drawbacks

Newton's method uses curvature information, so it is affine-invariant and typically needs few iterations. However, computing and inverting the Hessian is expensive in high dimensions; the Hessian may not be positive definite far from the solution, so the Newton direction may not be a descent direction; and the method can converge to a saddle point or maximum, or fail to converge from poor starting points. Practical implementations modify the Hessian, add line searches or trust regions, and quasi-Newton methods such as BFGS approximate the Hessian instead of computing it exactly.
