The Tories were one of the two great political factions and later parties in the Parliament of England and then Great Britain, from the later 17th century into the 19th century. Originally the label — from an Irish word for an outlaw or robber — was an insult applied during the Exclusion Crisis (1678–1681) to those who opposed excluding the Catholic James, Duke of York, from the succession. The Tories championed the hereditary monarchy, the established Church of England and the landed interest against their rivals, the Whigs.

History

Under Queen Anne the Tories reached a high point with the ministry of Robert Harley and Henry St John, but the Hanoverian succession of 1714 and the failed Jacobite rising of 1715 — with which some Tories were tainted — consigned them to decades in opposition while Whig ministries dominated. The younger William Pitt, who led the government from 1783, is usually described as a Tory, and his successors held office almost continuously through the Napoleonic Wars. The long ministry of Lord Liverpool (1812–1827) spanned the post-war unrest of Peterloo and the Cato Street conspiracy.

Reform and transformation into Conservatives

The party split repeatedly over Catholic emancipation, enacted under the Duke of Wellington and Robert Peel in 1829 at the cost of deep internal division. After the Great Reform Act of 1832, Peel's Tamworth Manifesto (1834) redefined the party as "Conservative" — committed to preserving institutions while accepting measured reform — and historians accordingly treat the Tories as the forerunners of the modern Conservative Party, whose members are still popularly called Tories. The final rupture came in 1846, when Peel's repeal of the Corn Laws split the party; his followers (Peelites) eventually joined the Liberals, while the remaining protectionist Conservatives under Lord Derby and Disraeli rebuilt the party.
