A container port or container terminal is a facility where cargo containers are transshipped between different transport vehicles, for onward transportation. The transshipment may be between container ships and land vehicles, in which case the terminal is described as a maritime container port, or between land vehicles, typically between train and truck, in which case it is described as an inland container port or dry port.

Operations

At a maritime terminal, ship-to-shore gantry cranes unload containers onto trucks or automated guided vehicles, which move them to stacking yards served by rubber-tyred or rail-mounted gantry cranes. Containers are sorted by destination — import, export, transshipment, or empty — and loaded onto outbound ships, trains, or trucks. Modern terminals use terminal operating systems, automated stacking cranes, and increasingly remote-controlled or fully automated equipment.

Scale and rankings

Throughput is measured in twenty-foot equivalent units (TEUs). The busiest container ports handle tens of millions of TEUs annually: the Port of Shanghai has been the world's busiest since 2010, exceeding 49 million TEUs in 2023, followed by Singapore and major Chinese ports such as Ningbo-Zhoushan and Shenzhen. Containerization, pioneered in the 1950s and 1960s, dramatically cut freight costs and reshaped global trade geography around such mega-ports.

Development

Building a container port requires deep-water access, extensive paved backland, and strong road and rail links. Expansions increasingly involve land reclamation and automated terminals, while ports face pressures from ever-larger vessels, decarbonization, and supply-chain resilience concerns.
