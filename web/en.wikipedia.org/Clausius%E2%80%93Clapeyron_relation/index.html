Clausius–Clapeyron relation

Clausius–Clapeyron relation
Field: Thermodynamics
Describes: Slope of phase-boundary lines
Named after: Rudolf Clausius, Benoît Paul Émile Clapeyron
Key result: dP/dT = L / (T Δv)

The Clausius–Clapeyron relation, named for Rudolf Clausius and Benoît Paul Émile Clapeyron, is a fundamental equation in thermodynamics that describes the relationship between pressure and temperature along the boundary between two phases of matter, such as liquid and vapour, in equilibrium. It gives the slope of the phase boundary on a pressure–temperature diagram:

dP/dT = L / (T Δv)

where dP/dT is the slope of the coexistence curve, L is the specific latent heat of the phase transition, T is the absolute temperature, and Δv is the change in specific volume during the transition.

Derivation and history

Clapeyron stated the relation in 1834 in the course of his work on Sadi Carnot's theory of heat; Clausius later (1850) derived it rigorously from thermodynamics. It follows from the equality of chemical potentials (Gibbs free energies) of the two phases at equilibrium, using the Maxwell relations. For liquid–vapour equilibrium, if the vapour is treated as an ideal gas and the liquid volume is neglected, the relation integrates to the August–Roche–Magnus-type vapour-pressure formula, showing that vapour pressure rises approximately exponentially with temperature.

Applications

The relation is used in chemistry and meteorology to predict vapour pressures, to estimate enthalpies of vaporization from vapour-pressure data, and in climate science: it implies that the water-vapour holding capacity of air increases by roughly 7% per degree Celsius of warming, a key factor in amplifying precipitation extremes. Because the entropy change of melting is typically much smaller than that of vaporization, melting curves are much steeper; water is anomalous because ice is less dense than liquid water, giving its melting curve a negative slope.
