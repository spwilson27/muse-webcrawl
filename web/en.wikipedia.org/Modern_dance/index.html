Modern dance

Modern dance emphasises expressive, grounded movement

Modern dance is a broad genre of western concert dance, created largely in the United States and Germany, that arose in the late 19th and early 20th centuries as a deliberate departure from the strictures of classical ballet. Its pioneers rejected ballet's formal vocabulary, pointe shoes, and fairy-tale narratives in favour of movement drawn from emotion, gravity, the floor, and the natural actions of the body — walking, running, falling, and breathing.

Origins

Precursors included Loie Fuller, who experimented with silk and electric light, and Isadora Duncan, who danced barefoot in tunics inspired by ancient Greek art and sought to restore dance as a serious art. Ruth St. Denis and Ted Shawn founded the Denishawn school in Los Angeles in 1915, training the generation that would define the form. In Germany, Mary Wigman and Kurt Jooss developed Ausdruckstanz ("expressionist dance").

Key figures and techniques

Martha Graham developed a technique built on the contraction and release of the torso, rooted in breath, which became the first codified modern dance technique. Doris Humphrey worked with fall and recovery; José Limón extended Humphrey's approach. Merce Cunningham, influenced by John Cage, introduced chance procedures and separated dance from music. Later figures such as Alvin Ailey, Paul Taylor, Twyla Tharp, and Pina Bausch — whose Tanztheater blended dance and theatre — expanded the genre's range. Contact improvisation, developed by Steve Paxton in 1972, explored movement through shared weight.

Postmodern dance

In the 1960s and 1970s the Judson Dance Theater in New York challenged even modern dance's conventions, embracing pedestrian movement, improvisation, and non-dancers. This "postmodern dance" generation — Yvonne Rainer, Trisha Brown, Lucinda Childs — paved the way for contemporary dance, the umbrella term now used for the field's eclectic current practice.
