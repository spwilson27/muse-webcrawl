Ultra

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

For the World War II intelligence designation, see Ultra (cryptography). For other uses, see Ultra (disambiguation).

Ultra may refer to a number of things. Its most historically prominent use is as the designation adopted by British military intelligence during World War II for wartime signals intelligence obtained by breaking high-level encrypted enemy radio and teleprinter communications, particularly those of the German Enigma and Lorenz ciphers. The term is derived from the classification "ultra secret", reflecting that the material was considered more sensitive than the highest existing security classification.

The word "ultra" comes from Latin, meaning "beyond". It is also used in naming numerous products, organisations, and cultural works, including:

  • Ultras, organised fan groups in association football and other sports
  • Ultra (cryptography), the British WWII codebreaking designation centred at Bletchley Park
  • Ultra Music Festival, an annual outdoor electronic music festival in Miami, Florida
  • Ultra (Depeche Mode album), the 1997 studio album by the English band Depeche Mode
  • MKUltra, a CIA programme of human experimentation on mind control
  • An ultramarathon, any footrace longer than the traditional marathon length of 42.195 kilometres

Etymology and general usage

In English, "ultra" functions both as a prefix meaning "extreme" or "beyond" (as in ultraviolet, ultrasound, and ultramarine) and as a noun denoting a person holding extreme political views, most famously the ultras (ultra-royalists) of Restoration France. The prefix derives from the Latin adverb and preposition ultra, "on the far side, beyond".
