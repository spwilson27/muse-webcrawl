Pericles

Pericles (c. 495 – 429 BC) was a Greek statesman and general during the Golden Age of Athens. He was prominent and influential in ancient Athenian politics, particularly between the Greco-Persian Wars and the Peloponnesian War, and was acclaimed by Thucydides, a historian of the time, as "the first citizen of Athens". Pericles turned the Delian League into an Athenian empire and led his countrymen during the first two years of the Peloponnesian War.

Life and career

Pericles was born into the aristocratic Alcmaeonid family; his father Xanthippus was a politician and his mother Agariste was a descendant of Cleisthenes. He was educated by the philosophers Anaxagoras and Zeno of Elea and became renowned as an orator of unmatched power. From 461 BC he was the leading figure of the democratic faction, reducing the powers of the aristocratic Areopagus and introducing pay for jurors so that poorer citizens could serve.

The building programme

Using funds of the Delian League, Pericles launched an ambitious programme to rebuild the Acropolis, destroyed by the Persians. Under his direction rose the Parthenon (447–432 BC), the Propylaea, the Erechtheion and the temple of Athena Nike — monuments that remain symbols of classical Athens. He also completed the Long Walls linking Athens to its port at Piraeus.

The Peloponnesian War and death

Pericles' strategy in the war against Sparta (from 431 BC) was to avoid land battle, withdraw the population behind the city walls, and rely on Athenian sea power. The overcrowded city was struck by a devastating plague in 430 BC, which killed a large share of the population. Pericles himself died of the plague in 429 BC, having briefly been deposed and fined before being re-elected strategos. His funeral oration, as reported by Thucydides, remains a defining statement of Athenian democracy.
