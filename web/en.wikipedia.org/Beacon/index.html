Beacon

A beacon is an intentionally conspicuous device designed to attract attention to a specific location. Historically, beacons were fires lit at well-known locations on hills or high places, used either as lighthouses for navigation at sea or as early warning signals of approaching enemies. Modern beacons include aeronautical beacons, radio beacons, radar transponders and emergency locator devices.

History

Fire beacons are among the oldest long-distance signalling systems. Chains of beacon fires were used in ancient China along the Great Wall, in the Byzantine Empire to transmit messages across Anatolia, and in Britain, where a network of beacons — famously lit to warn of the Spanish Armada in 1588 — warned of invasion. The word derives from the Old English bēacen, meaning a sign or signal.

Types

Navigational beacons include lighthouses, lightships and aerodrome beacons that guide ships and aircraft. Radio beacons, such as non-directional beacons (NDBs) and VOR stations, transmit signals that receivers use to determine position. Emergency beacons — including EPIRBs for ships, ELTs for aircraft and personal locator beacons — broadcast distress signals to search-and-rescue satellite systems such as Cospas-Sarsat. In computing, a "web beacon" is a tiny image or request used to track whether content has been accessed, and Bluetooth beacons broadcast short-range signals for indoor positioning.

Modern usage

Beacons remain standard safety equipment in aviation and maritime transport, and the term is widely used metaphorically, as in "a beacon of hope". Many historic beacon hills survive as place names in Britain, such as Beacon Hill.
