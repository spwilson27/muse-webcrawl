Phonation

Phonation is the process by which the vocal folds in the larynx produce sound through quasi-periodic vibration, providing the voice source for voiced speech sounds and singing. Air from the lungs passes between the adducted vocal folds, setting them into vibration according to the myoelastic-aerodynamic theory.

Mechanism

Subglottal air pressure forces the folds apart; the Bernoulli effect and the elasticity of the tissue draw them closed again, producing a repeating cycle that chops the airflow into pulses. The rate of vibration is the fundamental frequency, perceived as pitch. Speakers adjust it by changing the length and tension of the folds, chiefly with the cricothyroid muscles.

Phonation types

Linguists distinguish phonation types by the configuration of the glottis: modal voice (regular vibration), breathy voice, creaky voice (vocal fry), harsh voice, and the voiceless state. Some languages use phonation contrasts phonemically, distinguishing, for example, breathy and modal vowels or tones.
