Central Committee

A central committee is a political organisation's steering group, which usually denotes the executive leadership body of a communist party. In most such parties, the committee is elected by the party congress and in turn elects the Politburo and the party's general secretary, who together direct the party between congresses.

History

The institution developed in the Russian Social Democratic Labour Party; after the October Revolution the Central Committee of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union became, in practice, a central organ of state power, with its Politburo and Secretariat directing the government. Similar structures were adopted by ruling communist parties in China, Vietnam, Cuba, Laos and North Korea, and by many non-ruling communist and some socialist parties worldwide.

Examples

The Central Committee of the Chinese Communist Party has about 200 full members and meets in plenary sessions ("plenums") at least once a year; its Political Bureau (Politburo) and Politburo Standing Committee exercise day-to-day leadership. In the Soviet Union, formal powers of the Central Committee were often eclipsed by the Politburo and the General Secretary, though it occasionally decided leadership questions, as in the removal of Nikita Khrushchev in 1964 by the Presidium and Central Committee.
