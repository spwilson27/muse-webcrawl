Electric chair

The electric chair is a device used to execute a person by electrocution. The condemned prisoner is strapped to a specially built wooden chair and electrocuted through electrodes fastened to the head and leg. Originating in the United States in the late 19th century, it was for decades the predominant method of execution in many American states.

"Old Sparky", the electric chair once used in New York.

History

New York State adopted electrocution in 1888 as a supposedly more humane alternative to hanging, amid the "war of the currents" between Edison's direct current and the Westinghouse alternating current systems. The first execution, of William Kemmler at Auburn Prison in 1890, was notoriously botched. Nevertheless, many states and the Philippines adopted the method. Famous executions by electric chair included those of Sacco and Vanzetti, Julius and Ethel Rosenberg, and serial killer Ted Bundy.

Method

The prisoner is shaved and strapped into the chair; a moistened sponge and electrode cap are placed on the head and another electrode on the leg. One or more cycles of high-voltage alternating current—typically around 2,000 volts—are applied, intended to cause fatal damage to the brain and heart.

Decline

From the 1980s, lethal injection largely replaced electrocution in the United States, especially after several visibly botched executions in Florida in the 1990s. A few states retain the electric chair as an option or fallback; it was used as recently as 2020 in Tennessee. Outside the United States, it was used only in the Philippines, which abolished the death penalty in 2006.
