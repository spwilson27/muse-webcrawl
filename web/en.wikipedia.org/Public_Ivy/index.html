Public Ivy

Public Ivy is an informal term for public universities in the United States that are considered to provide an education comparable to that of the private Ivy League at public-school prices. The term was coined by Richard Moll, a former admissions officer at Yale University, in his 1985 book Public Ivies: A Guide to America's Best Public Undergraduate Colleges and Universities.

The original list

Moll's original list comprised eight universities: the College of William & Mary, Miami University of Ohio, the University of California system, the University of Michigan, the University of North Carolina at Chapel Hill, the University of Texas at Austin, the University of Vermont, and the University of Virginia. He also named nine "worthy runners-up," including the University of Colorado Boulder, Georgia Institute of Technology, the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign, and the University of Wisconsin–Madison.

Later usage

Later guidebooks expanded the concept; Howard and Matthew Greene's The Public Ivies (2001) listed thirty public universities. The label is widely used in college marketing and journalism, though critics note that rising out-of-state tuition and declining state appropriations have narrowed the price gap with private institutions, and that the Ivy League comparison conflates undergraduate teaching quality with research prestige and endowment wealth.
