Geoid

The geoid is the shape that the ocean surface would take under the influence of the gravity of Earth and its rotation alone, if other influences such as winds and tides were absent. This surface is perpendicular to the direction of gravity at every point, and serves as the reference surface from which topographic heights and ocean depths are measured in geodesy.

Definition and properties

The geoid is an equipotential surface of Earth's gravity field that coincides with mean sea level over the oceans and extends conceptually beneath the continents. Because Earth's mass is unevenly distributed, the geoid is irregular: it departs from the best-fitting reference ellipsoid by as much as about 100 metres, dipping south of India and rising near New Guinea and in the North Atlantic. Unlike the ellipsoid, the geoid has no simple mathematical description and is determined from gravity measurements.

Heights

Heights used in maps and engineering (orthometric heights) are measured above the geoid, whereas GPS yields heights above the ellipsoid. The difference between the two, the geoid undulation, must be modelled — using products such as the Earth Gravitational Model EGM2008 — to convert satellite positions into meaningful elevations above sea level.

Determination

The geoid is mapped from terrestrial and shipborne gravimetry, satellite altimetry over the oceans, and dedicated gravity missions. The GRACE satellites measured temporal changes in the field by tracking the distance between a pair of spacecraft, while ESA's GOCE (2009–2013) carried a gradiometer that produced the most detailed static geoid of its time.
