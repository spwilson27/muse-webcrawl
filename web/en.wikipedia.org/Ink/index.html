Ink

Ink is a gel, sol, or solution that contains at least one colourant, such as a dye or pigment, and is used to colour a surface to produce an image, text, or design. Ink is used for drawing or writing with a pen, brush, reed pen, or quill, and in printing processes of all kinds. Thicker inks, in paste form, are used extensively in letterpress and lithographic printing.

Composition

Inks consist of a colourant dispersed or dissolved in a vehicle (solvent or resin medium), together with additives controlling viscosity, drying, gloss, and permanence. Carbon black (soot) is the classic black pigment; historically, iron gall ink — made from tannic acids and iron salts — was the standard writing ink in Europe for over a millennium, despite its tendency to corrode paper. Modern inks include dye-based and pigment-based fountain pen inks, ballpoint pastes, and the cyan, magenta, yellow, and black inks of inkjet and offset printing.

History

Ink was used in ancient Egypt and China by at least the third millennium BCE; Chinese ink sticks of soot and animal glue were ground with water on an inkstone. The development of printing, from woodblock to Gutenberg's movable type, drove demand for oil-based printing inks. Fountain pens, ballpoints (popularised from the 1940s), and late-twentieth-century inkjet printers each required new formulations.

Types and uses

Specialised inks include invisible (sympathetic) inks, magnetic inks for cheque processing (MICR), conductive inks for printed electronics, security inks for banknotes, and tattoo inks. Environmental concerns have encouraged soy- and water-based printing inks with reduced volatile organic compounds.
