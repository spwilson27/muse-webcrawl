James Cook (7 November 1728 – 14 February 1779) was a British explorer, cartographer, and naval officer famous for his three voyages between 1768 and 1779 in the Pacific Ocean and to New Zealand and Australia in particular. He mapped lands from New Zealand to Hawaii in greater detail and on a scale not previously achieved by Western explorers.

Voyages

On his first voyage (1768–1771) aboard HMS Endeavour, Cook observed the transit of Venus from Tahiti, then charted New Zealand and the eastern coast of Australia, claiming it for Britain. The second voyage (1772–1775) on Resolution circumnavigated the globe at high southern latitudes, disproving the existence of a habitable Terra Australis and coming close to Antarctica. He also achieved remarkable success in preventing scurvy among his crews through diet and hygiene. The third voyage (1776–1779) sought the Northwest Passage; Cook charted the northwest coast of America and became one of the first Europeans to visit the Hawaiian Islands.

Death and legacy

Cook was killed at Kealakekua Bay, Hawaii, in February 1779 during a violent confrontation with Hawaiians over a stolen boat. His charts remained navigational standards for generations. Modern reassessments also examine the harm his voyages brought to Indigenous peoples through disease, violence, and colonization, and many places named for him have been the subject of debate and renaming campaigns.
