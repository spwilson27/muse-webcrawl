Autochrome Lumière

Autochromes have a distinctive soft, dreamlike colour quality produced by their mosaic filter layer.

The Autochrome Lumière was an early colour photography process, patented in 1903 by the Lumière brothers and marketed from 1907. It was the first industrially produced and commercially successful colour photographic process, and remained the principal medium of colour photography until the arrival of subtractive colour films such as Kodachrome in the 1930s.

How it worked

An Autochrome plate was coated with millions of microscopic grains of potato starch dyed orange-red, green and blue-violet, which acted as tiny colour filters. The spaces between grains were filled with carbon black, and over this mosaic layer a panchromatic silver-halide emulsion was applied. The plate was exposed through the glass, so light passed through the starch filters before reaching the emulsion; after reversal processing, viewing the plate against light produced a positive colour image. The starch mosaic gives Autochromes their characteristic soft, pointillist appearance.

Use

Autochrome was taken up by pictorialist photographers, by National Geographic for its early colour features, and by the French banker Albert Kahn, who commissioned photographers to document cultures around the world for his "Archives of the Planet" (1909–1931). During the First World War, French aerial reconnaissance and official photographers recorded the conflict in colour. Because exposures were long and the plates were fragile glass transparencies, the process required a tripod, daylight and patience.

Decline

Film-based versions (Filmcolor and Lumicolor) appeared in the early 1930s, but the process was overtaken by modern integral tripack films — Kodachrome (1935) and Agfacolor Neu (1936) — which were faster, sharper and could be printed on paper. Production of Autochrome plates ended around 1932.
