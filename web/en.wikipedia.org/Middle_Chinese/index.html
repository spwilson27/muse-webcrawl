Middle Chinese

Erac. 6th–10th centuries AD
RegionChina (Sui and Tang dynasties)
FamilySino-Tibetan → Sinitic
Key sourceQieyun (601 AD)

Middle Chinese is the historical variety of Chinese recorded in the Qieyun, a rime dictionary first published in 601 AD, and used through the Tang and Song dynasties. Earlier scholars treated it as the spoken language of the Tang capital, Chang'an, but it is now more often understood as a literary reading standard reflecting distinctions drawn from several regions.

Phonology

Middle Chinese syllables are conventionally analysed into an initial consonant, a final (comprising a glide, vowel and coda), and one of four tones. Codas could be vowels, glides, nasals (m, n, ŋ) or stops (p, t, k); stop-final syllables were grouped as the "entering tone". The system is reconstructed from the categories of the rime dictionaries and rime tables, the fanqie spellings that indicate a character's pronunciation using two other characters, and evidence from modern Sinitic languages and Sino-xenic borrowings into Korean, Japanese and Vietnamese.

Reconstruction

Swedish sinologist Bernhard Karlgren pioneered the phonetic reconstruction of Middle Chinese in the early 20th century. Later systems by scholars such as Li Rong, William Baxter, and Baxter and Laurent Sagart differ in details — for example in the treatment of vowel distinctions and the so-called divisions of the rime tables — but agree on the broad structure of the language, the ancestor of nearly all modern Chinese varieties except Min.
