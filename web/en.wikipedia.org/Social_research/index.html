FieldSocial sciences
MethodsSurveys, interviews, observation, experiments, statistics
FoundersÉmile Durkheim; Max Weber; Charles Booth

Social research is the systematic investigation of social life, conducted by sociologists, anthropologists, political scientists, economists, psychologists, and other social scientists to describe, explain, and predict patterns of human behaviour and social organisation. It underpins academic knowledge and much government policy and market analysis.

Methods

Social research employs both quantitative methods — surveys with sampling, experiments, and statistical analysis of secondary data — and qualitative methods such as in-depth interviews, focus groups, ethnography, case studies, and document analysis. Mixed-methods designs combine the two. Key methodological concerns include validity, reliability, sampling bias, and research ethics, including informed consent and confidentiality.

History

Early landmarks include Charles Booth's surveys of London poverty (1889–1903) and W. E. B. Du Bois's The Philadelphia Negro (1899). In the twentieth century, government statistical agencies, opinion polling, and university research institutes professionalised the field, and longitudinal studies such as the British birth cohort studies became central resources.

Applications

Findings from social research inform social policy evaluation, public health, education, urban planning, and commercial market research. Debates continue over the replication crisis, the ethics of big-data research, and the place of participatory and action research.
