Insular Cases

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Insular Cases are a series of opinions by the Supreme Court of the United States in 1901, concerning the status of U.S. territories acquired in the Spanish–American War. The Court held that full constitutional rights do not automatically extend to all places under American control, drawing a distinction between incorporated territories destined for statehood and unincorporated territories, in which only certain "fundamental" rights apply.

Background

Following the Treaty of Paris (1898), the United States acquired Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines. Questions arose as to whether the Constitution "followed the flag" — for example, whether Congress could impose duties on goods shipped from Puerto Rico despite the Uniformity Clause. Congress's Foraker Act of 1900 imposed such tariffs, prompting litigation.

Principal decisions

In Downes v. Bidwell (1901), a fractured Court upheld the tariff, with Justice Edward Douglass White's concurrence articulating the doctrine of territorial incorporation. Other 1901 decisions included De Lima v. Bidwell, Dooley v. United States, and Armstrong v. United States. Later cases, such as Balzac v. Porto Rico (1922), extended the framework, holding that the right to jury trial did not extend to Puerto Rico.

Legacy and criticism

The decisions remain controversial. Critics, including some sitting justices, have condemned them as resting on racial assumptions about the peoples of the new territories and as lacking foundation in the constitutional text. They nonetheless continue to govern the status of Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, American Samoa, and the Northern Mariana Islands; residents of unincorporated territories are U.S. citizens (or, in American Samoa, U.S. nationals) but cannot vote in federal elections and lack voting representation in Congress.
