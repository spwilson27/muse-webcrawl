Reformed tradition

John Calvin (1509–1564), whose Institutes of the Christian Religion shaped Reformed theology.

The Reformed tradition, also called Calvinism or Reformed Protestantism, is a major branch of Protestantism that began in the 16th-century Reformation under reformers such as Huldrych Zwingli in Zurich and John Calvin in Geneva. Reformed churches are characterised by the doctrines summarised in historic confessions of faith — including the Belgic Confession, the Heidelberg Catechism and the Westminster Standards — by a theology centred on the sovereignty of God, covenant theology and predestination, and by presbyterian or congregational church government.

History

The Reformed movement began independently of Luther's Wittenberg in the Swiss cantons: Zwingli's reforms in Zurich from 1519, followed by work in Bern, Basel and Strasbourg, and Calvin's Geneva, which became a model and training ground for reformers across Europe. From Switzerland the tradition spread to France (the Huguenots), the Netherlands, Scotland under John Knox, parts of Germany and Hungary, and — through English Puritans and Dutch and Scottish settlers — to North America, South Africa and beyond. The Synod of Dort (1618–19) defined Reformed orthodoxy against Arminianism in the "five points of Calvinism", remembered by the acronym TULIP.

Theology

Reformed theology stresses the authority of Scripture (sola scriptura), salvation by grace through faith, the absolute sovereignty of God in election and providence, and the covenant as the organising structure of God's dealings with humanity. Worship historically followed the regulative principle, favouring psalm singing, preaching and simplicity, and the sacraments of baptism (including infant baptism) and the Lord's Supper, understood as a spiritual — neither merely memorial nor physically corporeal — presence of Christ. Reformed churches are typically governed by elders in graded courts (presbyterian polity) or by local congregations.

Today

The World Communion of Reformed Churches unites more than 230 member churches with an estimated 80–100 million members, including Presbyterian, Reformed, United and Waldensian churches. Reformed Christians have exercised an outsized influence on education, democratic theory, missions and the development of capitalism — the subject of Max Weber's famous, contested thesis about the "Protestant ethic".
