Mustafa Kemal Atatürk (c. 1881 – 10 November 1938) was a Turkish field marshal, revolutionary statesman, author, and the founding father of the Republic of Turkey, serving as its first president from 1923 until his death in 1938. The surname Atatürk, meaning "Father of the Turks," was granted to him by the Turkish parliament in 1934.

Military career

An Ottoman army officer, Mustafa Kemal distinguished himself commanding Ottoman divisions at the Gallipoli campaign in 1915, repelling the Allied landing. After the Ottoman defeat in the First World War, he led the Turkish National Movement in the Turkish War of Independence (1919–1923), defeating Greek forces at the Battle of Sakarya and the Great Offensive, and forcing the renegotiation of the post-war settlement at the Treaty of Lausanne.

Presidency and reforms

The Republic of Turkey was proclaimed on 29 October 1923 with Ankara as its capital. As president, Atatürk launched sweeping political, social, and cultural reforms—known as Atatürk's Reforms—aimed at creating a modern, secular nation-state: the abolition of the sultanate and caliphate, adoption of European legal codes, replacement of the Arabic script with a Latin-based Turkish alphabet (1928), secular education, and full political rights for women, who gained the vote in 1934.

Legacy

Atatürk died of cirrhosis at Dolmabahçe Palace in Istanbul in 1938 and is buried in the Anıtkabir mausoleum in Ankara. His principles, summarized as Kemalism, remain foundational to the Turkish republic, and his image is ubiquitous in Turkish public life.
