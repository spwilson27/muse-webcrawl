Methanol

Methanol (also called methyl alcohol and, historically, wood alcohol) is an organic chemical compound with the formula CH3OH. It is the simplest aliphatic alcohol — a light, volatile, colourless, flammable liquid with a distinctive, slightly sweet odour similar to ethanol but far more toxic.

Production

Methanol was once produced by the destructive distillation of wood, hence its old name. Today it is made industrially, on a scale of over 100 million tonnes per year, by catalytic hydrogenation of carbon monoxide and carbon dioxide derived from natural gas (syngas), using copper/zinc oxide/alumina catalysts at moderate pressure. Methanol can also be made from biomass and from captured CO2 with green hydrogen, routes of growing interest for low-carbon fuel and shipping.

Uses

Methanol is mainly a chemical feedstock: it is converted to formaldehyde, acetic acid, methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE) and, via the methanol-to-olefins process, to ethylene and propylene for plastics. It is used as a solvent, antifreeze, denaturant for ethanol, and fuel — in racing cars, in Chinese taxi and boiler fuels, and increasingly in marine engines as a lower-emission shipping fuel.

Toxicity

Methanol is highly poisonous to humans. The liver converts it to formaldehyde and then formic acid, which causes metabolic acidosis and destroys the optic nerve; as little as 10 mL can cause permanent blindness and 30 mL can be fatal. Outbreaks of poisoning regularly occur from adulterated or counterfeit spirits. Treatment involves administering ethanol or the antidote fomepizole to block methanol metabolism, plus dialysis.

Occurrence

Small amounts occur naturally in fruit juices, fermented drinks and human metabolism, and astronomers have detected methanol in star-forming regions and in interstellar clouds, where it is a building block of more complex organic chemistry.
