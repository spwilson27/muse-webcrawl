In mathematics, an algebraic curve is a curve that is defined by a polynomial equation. In modern terms it is an algebraic variety of dimension one: the set of solutions of one or more polynomial equations in two variables (for a plane curve) or, more generally, a one-dimensional variety in projective space. Algebraic curves are among the oldest and richest objects of algebraic geometry.

Plane curves

An affine plane curve over a field k is the zero set of a polynomial f(x, y) in two variables. The degree of the curve is the degree of the polynomial. Lines are curves of degree 1, conic sections (circles, ellipses, parabolas and hyperbolas) have degree 2, and cubic curves — including elliptic curves — have degree 3. Passing to the projective plane by homogenising the equation adds the points at infinity and makes intersection theory uniform, as formalised by Bézout's theorem, which states that two projective curves of degrees m and n meet in exactly mn points when counted with multiplicity over an algebraically closed field.

Genus and the Riemann surface connection

A smooth projective curve over the complex numbers is a compact Riemann surface, and its most important topological invariant is its genus g: 0 for the projective line (a sphere), 1 for elliptic curves (tori), and higher for more complicated surfaces. The genus of a smooth plane curve of degree d is given by the formula g = (d − 1)(d − 2)/2. Singular points reduce the genus according to the degree–genus formula of Max Noether. The Riemann–Roch theorem relates the geometry of divisors on a curve to the dimensions of spaces of meromorphic functions and is foundational to the whole subject.

Rational points and applications

The study of rational points on curves links algebraic geometry to number theory: elliptic curves underlie modern cryptography and were central to the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem, while Faltings's theorem (the Mordell conjecture) shows that a curve of genus greater than 1 has only finitely many rational points. Error-correcting codes constructed from curves (geometric Goppa codes) can beat classical bounds, and curves over finite fields are counted by the Weil conjectures, proved in full by Pierre Deligne.
