Edward, the Black Prince

Edward of Woodstock (15 June 1330 – 8 June 1376), known to history as the Black Prince, was the eldest son and heir apparent of King Edward III of England. One of the most successful English commanders of the Hundred Years' War, he won famous victories at Crécy (1346) and Poitiers (1356) but predeceased his father and never became king; his son succeeded as Richard II.

Military career

Created Prince of Wales in 1343, Edward commanded a division at the Battle of Crécy aged sixteen, where his father reportedly refused him aid so he could "win his spurs". As commander in Aquitaine he led the great chevauchées of 1355–56, culminating in the Battle of Poitiers, where he captured King John II of France. In 1367 he won the Battle of Nájera in Castile, restoring Peter of Castile — a victory that ruined his finances and health.

Aquitaine and later years

As Prince of Aquitaine from 1362, Edward held a brilliant court at Bordeaux, but heavy taxation to pay for the Spanish campaign provoked appeals to the French king and helped restart the war. The sack of Limoges in 1370 darkened his reputation. Ill with a lingering disease, probably dysentery, he returned to England in 1371 and died in 1376, a year before his father.

The name and legacy

The origin of "Black Prince" is uncertain; it may derive from his black shield or armour, or from his harshness in France, and the name appears in records only after his death. He was buried in Canterbury Cathedral, where his tomb and funeral achievements survive. He was a founding knight of the Order of the Garter, and his motto, Ich dien ("I serve"), is still used by the Prince of Wales.
