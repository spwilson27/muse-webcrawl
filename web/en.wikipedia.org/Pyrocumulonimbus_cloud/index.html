Pyrocumulonimbus cloud

The pyrocumulonimbus (abbreviated pyroCb) is a type of cumulonimbus cloud that forms above a source of intense heat, such as a large wildfire or a volcanic eruption. It is the most extreme manifestation of a pyrocumulus cloud: a fire-generated thunderstorm capable of producing lightning, strong winds, hail, and even tornadoes, and of injecting smoke and aerosols into the upper troposphere and lower stratosphere.

Formation

When a wildfire is intense enough, its heated plume rises rapidly, cools, and condenses into a pyrocumulus cloud. If the plume rises high enough into conditionally unstable air, it can develop into a full thunderstorm. The resulting lightning may ignite new fires (so-called "dry lightning"), and downdrafts and outflows can cause the parent fire to spread erratically. PyroCb events also generate their own weather, including fire tornadoes formed within the plume.

Stratospheric injection and climate effects

The most powerful pyroCb events push smoke through the tropopause into the stratosphere, where it can persist for months and spread globally, with measurable effects on surface temperature and ozone chemistry comparable in scale to a moderate volcanic eruption. During the Australian bushfires of 2019–20 ("Black Summer"), a series of pyroCb outbreaks injected an estimated one million tonnes of smoke into the stratosphere; the smoke plume circled the Southern Hemisphere and rose to record altitudes near 35 km.

Historical events

Notable pyroCb events occurred during the Black Saturday fires in Australia (2009), the Fort McMurray wildfire in Canada (2016), the British Columbia fires of 2017 — which produced a months-long smoke layer — and the 2020 fires in Australia, California, and Siberia. Similar clouds were documented over the firestorms of Hamburg and Hiroshima in the 1940s, and volcanic analogues accompany large eruptions.
