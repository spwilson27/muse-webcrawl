Edge effects

In ecology, edge effects are the changes in population or community structures that occur at the boundary of two or more habitats. Areas with small habitat fragments exhibit especially pronounced edge effects that may extend throughout the range of the fragment. Increased availability of light at the edge of a forest, for example, promotes the growth of certain plant species and alters predator–prey relationships.

Types

Edge effects arise where contrasting environments meet. Inherent edges are natural and stable, such as a lakeshore; induced edges result from disturbance, such as logging or fire, and may shift over time as vegetation recovers. Narrow edges occur where one habitat ends abruptly, while wide edges (ecotones) grade gradually between habitats.

Effects on organisms

Edges experience greater variation in light, temperature, wind, and humidity than habitat interiors. Some "edge species" thrive in these conditions, while interior specialists decline. Edges can facilitate predation, nest parasitism (for example by brown-headed cowbirds), and the spread of invasive species and disease. Long-term studies in Amazonian forest fragments, such as the Biological Dynamics of Forest Fragments Project, have shown that edge effects can penetrate hundreds of metres into a fragment and increase tree mortality.

Conservation implications

As habitat fragmentation increases, the proportion of edge habitat rises. Conservation planners therefore favour large, compact reserves with a low perimeter-to-area ratio, and buffer zones that soften the contrast between a reserve and surrounding land.
