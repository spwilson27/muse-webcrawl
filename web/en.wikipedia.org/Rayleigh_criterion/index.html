Rayleigh criterion

Two Airy patterns separated at the Rayleigh criterion distance

The Rayleigh criterion is a rule proposed by the British physicist Lord Rayleigh for the minimum resolvable separation between two light sources in an optical system, limited by diffraction. Two point sources are regarded as just resolved when the centre of one source's diffraction pattern (the maximum of its Airy disk) coincides with the first minimum of the other's pattern.

Angular resolution

For a circular aperture of diameter D and light of wavelength λ, the Rayleigh criterion gives a minimum angular separation θ ≈ 1.22 λ / D, measured in radians. A telescope with a larger aperture therefore resolves finer detail. For the human eye, with a pupil of a few millimetres, the criterion predicts a resolution of roughly one arcminute in visible light, consistent with typical visual acuity.

Applications

The criterion is used in the design and specification of telescopes, microscopes, cameras and radar systems. It is a convention rather than a hard physical limit: with favourable conditions and image processing, objects closer than the Rayleigh separation can sometimes be distinguished, a fact exploited by super-resolution microscopy techniques.
