Woodrow Wilson

Thomas Woodrow Wilson (December 28, 1856 – February 3, 1924) was the 28th president of the United States, serving from 1913 to 1921. An academic and member of the Democratic Party, Wilson served as president of Princeton University (1902–1910) and as governor of New Jersey (1911–1913) before winning the presidency. He led the United States into the First World War in 1917 and was the architect of the League of Nations, for which he was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1919.

Early life and academia

Wilson was born in Staunton, Virginia, the son of a Presbyterian minister, and grew up in Georgia and South Carolina during the Civil War and Reconstruction. He studied at Princeton, read law at the University of Virginia, and earned a doctorate in history and political science from Johns Hopkins University — the only U.S. president to hold a PhD. His dissertation, Congressional Government (1885), became a classic of American political science. He taught at Bryn Mawr and Wesleyan before joining Princeton's faculty, becoming its president in 1902.

Presidency

Wilson won the 1912 election amid a split in the Republican Party between William Howard Taft and Theodore Roosevelt. His domestic program, the "New Freedom", produced the Federal Reserve Act (1913), the Federal Trade Commission, the Clayton Antitrust Act, tariff reduction and a federal income tax under the new Sixteenth Amendment. His administration also resegregated many federal workplaces, a record for which he has been sharply criticised.

Re-elected in 1916 on the slogan "He kept us out of war", Wilson asked Congress for a declaration of war against Germany in April 1917 following unrestricted submarine warfare and the Zimmermann Telegram. He articulated his war aims in the Fourteen Points (January 1918) and personally negotiated at the Paris Peace Conference, securing the Covenant of the League of Nations within the Treaty of Versailles.

Final years and legacy

On a national tour to build support for the treaty, Wilson suffered a severe stroke in October 1919; the Senate ultimately rejected U.S. membership of the League. Incapacitated during his final months in office, he retired to Washington, where he died in 1924. Historians generally rank Wilson among the upper tier of presidents for his legislative leadership and international vision, while faulting his record on civil liberties and race.
