Hill climbing

In numerical analysis and artificial intelligence, hill climbing is a mathematical optimization technique which belongs to the family of local search. It is an iterative algorithm that starts with an arbitrary solution to a problem and attempts to find a better solution by making incremental changes; if a change produces a better solution, it is accepted and the process repeats until no further improvement can be found.

Variants

In simple hill climbing, the first closer node is chosen, whereas in steepest-ascent hill climbing all successors are compared and the one closest to the goal (highest in value) is selected. Stochastic hill climbing chooses among uphill moves at random, often with probability proportional to the improvement. Random-restart hill climbing runs the search repeatedly from random starting points to escape poor local optima.

Problems

Hill climbing can get stuck: on a local maximum (a peak higher than its neighbours but lower than the global maximum), on a plateau (a flat region where neighbours have equal value), or on a ridge (a slope requiring a combination of moves that individually look unpromising). Because it keeps no memory of past states and never makes a downhill move, the basic algorithm is not guaranteed to find the global optimum.

Applications

Hill climbing underlies many optimization methods and is used, in variations, in problems from the travelling salesman problem to neural network training and operations research. Related techniques include simulated annealing, which occasionally accepts worse moves to escape local optima, and gradient descent, which follows the direction of steepest improvement in continuous spaces.
