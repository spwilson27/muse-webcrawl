Pleura

The pleura and pleural cavity of the right lung
Pleura
SystemRespiratory system
LocationSurrounding the lungs within the thoracic cavity
FluidPleural fluid (serous)

The pleurae (singular pleura) are the two membranes surrounding the lungs and lining the thoracic cavity. Each pleura is a serous membrane of mesothelium arranged as a closed sac with two layers: the visceral pleura, which adheres intimately to the surface of the lung and extends into its fissures, and the parietal pleura, which lines the inner chest wall, diaphragm and mediastinum. Between the two layers lies the pleural cavity, a potential space containing a thin film of pleural fluid — normally only a few millilitres.

Function

The pleural fluid lubricates the surfaces so that the lungs can glide almost frictionlessly during breathing. More importantly, surface tension of the fluid couples the lung to the chest wall: when the thorax expands, the lung is pulled open with it, and the subatmospheric pressure in the pleural space keeps the lung inflated against its natural elastic recoil. This hydraulic linkage is why a breach of the pleura, admitting air into the cavity, can cause the lung to collapse — a pneumothorax.

Structure and regions

The parietal pleura is named by region: costal pleura against the ribs, diaphragmatic pleura over the diaphragm, mediastinal pleura facing the midline, and the cervical pleura or cupola rising into the neck above the first rib. The visceral pleura is insensitive to pain, whereas the parietal pleura is richly supplied with sensory fibres — intercostal nerves for the costal part and the phrenic nerve for the diaphragmatic part — which is why pleural inflammation is painful and why diaphragmatic pleural pain may be referred to the shoulder tip.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the pleura, pleurisy (pleuritis), produces sharp chest pain worsened by breathing and coughing, and a friction rub may be heard with a stethoscope. Excess fluid in the pleural cavity is a pleural effusion, caused by heart failure, pneumonia, cancer or pulmonary embolism; fluid may be sampled or drained by thoracentesis. Blood in the cavity is a haemothorax and pus an empyema. Exposure to asbestos can cause pleural plaques, diffuse pleural thickening and the malignant tumour mesothelioma decades later. A pleurectomy or pleurodesis — deliberately scarring the layers together — is used to treat recurrent pneumothorax or effusion.
