Fixed-term election

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A fixed-term election is an election whose date is determined by law or constitution in advance, rather than being called at the discretion of the government or head of state. In systems with fixed terms, the legislature and executive serve for a set period — commonly four or five years — and elections occur on a known schedule, except sometimes where an early dissolution mechanism exists for exceptional circumstances.

Examples

The United States is the archetypal fixed-term system: federal elections are held every two years for the House of Representatives, every four years for the presidency, on the Tuesday after the first Monday in November, and no vote of no confidence can shorten a term. Many presidential and semi-presidential systems follow similar patterns. Among parliamentary systems, Norway's Storting cannot be dissolved at all during its four-year term, and Germany's Basic Law makes early dissolution of the Bundestag difficult, requiring a failed chancellor election or a deliberately lost confidence vote. Australia and several Canadian provinces have adopted fixed election dates by statute, although the constitutional power of dissolution usually remains.

Arguments

Supporters argue that fixed terms remove the incumbent government's ability to time elections for partisan advantage, allow better planning by electoral administrators, businesses, and voters, and make campaigning fairer. Critics argue that fixed terms can prolong deadlocked or incompetent governments, produce long, expensive permanent campaigns, and create "lame duck" periods; and that flexible-term parliamentary systems discipline governments through the constant possibility of an election. Hybrid arrangements — fixed dates subject to early dissolution on a supermajority or a constructive vote of no confidence — attempt to combine stability with accountability.

Fixed-term legislation in the United Kingdom

The United Kingdom historically left election timing to the prime minister's advice to the monarch, subject to a maximum five-year Parliament. The Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 set five-year terms and specified conditions for early elections, but disputes over its operation during the Brexit deadlock led to its repeal by the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022, which restored the previous prerogative-based arrangement.
