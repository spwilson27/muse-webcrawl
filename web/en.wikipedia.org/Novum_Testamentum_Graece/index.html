Novum Testamentum Graece (Latin: "New Testament in Greek") is a critical edition of the Greek New Testament, first published in 1898 by Eberhard Nestle and continued by his son Erwin Nestle and by Kurt Aland. Commonly called the Nestle–Aland text after its editors, it is the standard scholarly edition used worldwide for New Testament study and as the base text for most modern Bible translations.

Method

Nestle's original method was mechanical: he compared the editions of Tischendorf, Westcott and Hort, and Weymouth, printing the reading supported by two of the three, with variants recorded in the apparatus. Later editions, prepared at the Institute for New Testament Textual Research in Münster, have applied internal and external evidence directly to the manuscript tradition. The 28th edition (2012) incorporated the Editio Critica Maior for the Catholic Epistles, revising the text in 34 passages, and is identical in text with the 5th edition of the United Bible Societies' Greek New Testament, though their apparatuses differ.

Apparatus

The edition's compact critical apparatus, written in Latin abbreviations and symbols, cites variant readings from Greek manuscripts (papyri, uncials, minuscules, and lectionaries), early versions, and Church Fathers, allowing scholars to weigh the evidence for the printed text. Marginal symbols mark passages of textual uncertainty, and cross-references and Old Testament quotations are also noted.
