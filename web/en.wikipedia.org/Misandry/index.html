Misandry

Misandry is the hatred of, contempt for, or prejudice against men. It is the counterpart of misogyny, the hatred of or prejudice against women. The word derives from the Greek misos ("hatred") and anēr, andros ("man"); the term is attested in English from the late 19th century.

Misandry can manifest as disparagement of men, negative stereotyping of masculinity, or discrimination. The concept is invoked in academic gender studies, in literature, and in debates involving the men's rights movement and men's studies, as well as by critics of certain strands of feminism. Other commentators argue that, whatever individual attitudes exist, misandry lacks the institutional and historical weight of misogyny, and some distinguish sharply between prejudice against men and structural oppression.

In scholarship

Sociologist Allan G. Johnson discusses misandry in The Gender Knot (2005), distinguishing culturally propagated contempt for men from a system of male privilege. Anthony Synnott's Re-Thinking Men (2009) catalogues representations of men as villains, victims, and inferiors. Religious-studies scholars Paul Nathanson and Katherine K. Young argued in Spreading Misandry (2001) and Legalizing Misandry (2006) that popular culture and law institutionalise a worldview of men as dangerous or deficient — claims that have drawn both support and criticism.

In literature and media

Examples cited in scholarship range from man-hating characters in classical literature to modern popular-culture tropes in which male characters are portrayed as incompetent ("doofus dad" advertising and sitcom conventions). Valerie Solanas's SCUM Manifesto (1967) is frequently cited as an extreme literary instance, though its status as satire is disputed.

Debates

Usage of the term is contentious. Men's rights advocates argue misandry is widespread and under-acknowledged; many feminist scholars respond that isolated or ironic expressions of contempt for men do not constitute a system comparable to misogyny. Surveys measuring hostile attitudes toward men (e.g., the Ambivalence toward Men Inventory developed by Peter Glick and Susan Fiske) find that hostile and benevolent sexism toward men coexist in many societies.
