Impressionism was a 19th-century art movement characterised by relatively small, thin, yet visible brush strokes, open composition, emphasis on accurate depiction of light in its changing qualities (often accentuating the effects of the passage of time), ordinary subject matter, unusual visual angles, and inclusion of movement as a crucial element of human perception and experience. Impressionism originated with a group of Paris-based artists whose independent exhibitions brought them to prominence during the 1870s and 1880s.

The Impressionists faced harsh opposition from the conventional art establishment in France. The name of the style derives from the title of a Claude Monet work, Impression, soleil levant (Impression, Sunrise), which provoked the critic Louis Leroy to coin the term in a satirical review of the first Impressionist exhibition in 1874. The artists adopted the name defiantly.

Overview

Radical in their early work, the Impressionists broke many of the rules of earlier painting. Where academic painters built images with smoothly blended tones and drew on classical and historical subjects, the Impressionists used short, broken brushstrokes of pure, unmixed colour, letting the viewer's eye blend the hues. They painted outdoors (en plein air), capturing transient effects of sunlight and atmosphere, and favoured scenes of modern Parisian life — boulevards, cafés, dance halls, railways, and leisure in the countryside.

Techniques

  • Short, thick strokes of paint applied quickly to capture the essence of the subject rather than its details
  • Colours applied side by side with little mixing, creating vibrant surface effects
  • Avoidance of black paint; shadows rendered in complementary colours
  • Wet paint applied on wet paint, producing softer edges
  • Emphasis on natural light and the time of day, sometimes through series paintings of the same subject

History

The movement developed in the 1860s among young artists including Monet, Renoir, Sisley, and Frédéric Bazille, who met at the studio of Charles Gleyre. Rejected repeatedly by the official Salon, they held their first group exhibition in 1874 at the studio of the photographer Nadar, exhibiting as the Société Anonyme des artistes peintres, sculpteurs et graveurs. Eight Impressionist exhibitions were held between 1874 and 1886. By the late 1880s the methods of Impressionism had spread across Europe and America, and its artists had achieved commercial success through dealers such as Paul Durand-Ruel.

Legacy

Impressionism paved the way for Post-Impressionism and modern art generally, legitimising direct observation, colour experiment, and personal expression over academic convention. Its influence extends to music (Debussy and Ravel were labelled Impressionist composers) and literature.
