Ridesharing company

A ridesharing company (also known as a transportation network company, ride-hailing company, or mobility service provider) is a company that, via websites and mobile apps, matches passengers with drivers of local vehicles for hire and provides other transportation services. The industry is sometimes also known as the "peer-to-peer ridesharing" or "on-demand transportation" sector.

Unlike traditional taxi services, ridesharing drivers typically use their own personal vehicles and are legally classified in most jurisdictions as independent contractors rather than employees, a model that has generated significant regulatory controversy around the world.

History

The modern ridesharing industry began in the late 2000s and early 2010s. Uber was founded in San Francisco in 2009 as UberCab, initially offering a luxury black-car service booked through a smartphone app. Lyft launched in 2012 with a model built around ordinary drivers using their own cars, distinguished by pink moustaches attached to vehicles. In China, Didi Chuxing grew rapidly after its 2012 founding and eventually acquired Uber's China operations in 2016. Other major companies include Grab and Gojek in Southeast Asia, Ola in India, Bolt in Europe and Africa, and Cabify in Spain and Latin America.

Business model

Riders request a trip through an app, are matched with a nearby driver, and pay electronically, with the company taking a commission (often 20–30%) and the remainder going to the driver. Fares are computed by algorithms that may include "surge pricing" or "dynamic pricing" during periods of high demand. Driver earnings and availability are managed partly through incentive schemes, ratings systems, and algorithmic assignment.

Regulation and criticism

Ridesharing companies have faced legal challenges over driver classification, insurance, safety screening, accessibility, congestion, and effects on public transit and the taxi industry. California's Assembly Bill 5 (2019) sought to reclassify drivers as employees; the industry responded with Proposition 22 (2020), a ballot measure that exempted app-based drivers while granting some benefits. In London, Transport for London repeatedly contested Uber's operating licence. Supporters argue the services increase mobility, reduce drunk driving, and supplement transit, while critics point to increased vehicle miles travelled and precarious labour conditions.
