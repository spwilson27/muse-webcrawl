Thrombus

A thrombus (plural thrombi), colloquially called a blood clot, is the final product of the blood coagulation step in haemostasis that forms inside a blood vessel or the heart during life. It consists of aggregated platelets, red blood cells, and a mesh of cross-linked fibrin protein. A clot that breaks free and travels through the bloodstream is called an embolus.

Formation

Thrombosis is promoted by the factors summarized in Virchow's triad: endothelial injury, abnormal blood flow (stasis or turbulence), and hypercoagulability. Arterial thrombi, formed under high flow, are rich in platelets and appear pale; venous thrombi form in sluggish flow, trap many red cells, and appear dark red.

Outcomes and complications

A thrombus may resolve by fibrinolysis, become organized into the vessel wall, propagate, or embolize. Thrombosis underlies myocardial infarction, most strokes, deep vein thrombosis, and pulmonary embolism. Treatment includes anticoagulants, antiplatelet agents, and thrombolytic drugs.
