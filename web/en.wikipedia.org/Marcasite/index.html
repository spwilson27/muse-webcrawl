Marcasite

The mineral marcasite, sometimes called "white iron pyrite", is iron sulfide with the chemical formula FeS2. It is a dimorph of pyrite: the two minerals share the same composition but have different crystal structures — marcasite is orthorhombic, pyrite is cubic. Marcasite tends to form in low-temperature, highly acidic sedimentary environments and is lighter and more brittle than pyrite.

Occurrence and formation

Marcasite commonly occurs in sedimentary rocks such as shales, limestones and low-rank coals, where it grows as nodules, stalactitic masses, radiating "cockscomb" aggregates, and thin tabular crystals. It forms under acidic, low-temperature conditions, often from the activity of sulfate-reducing bacteria, and is common in the chalk deposits of the English Channel coasts and in coal mines of Illinois and Wisconsin.

Alteration and "pyrite decay"

Marcasite is unstable in humid air: it oxidises to iron sulfate and sulfuric acid, a process known in museums as "marcasite disease" or pyrite decay, which can crumble specimens and fossils and damage adjacent materials. Collections therefore keep marcasite specimens in dry conditions. In mines, oxidising marcasite and pyrite generate acid mine drainage, and spontaneous heating of sulfides in coal waste has ignited underground fires.

Uses

Historically, "marcasite jewellery" — fashionable in the Victorian era and revived in Art Deco designs — was usually made of cut steel or of pyrite rather than marcasite itself, the name being used loosely for faceted iron pyrite set in silver. True marcasite is too fragile and unstable for most lapidary use, though polished nodules are sold to collectors, sometimes under the trade name "pyrite dollars" for radiating discs from Illinois coal mines.
