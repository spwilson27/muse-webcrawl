The Second Party System is a periodisation used by historians and political scientists to describe the system of political parties in the United States from roughly 1828 to 1854, with important antecedents in the First Party System (1790s–1820s). The system was characterised by a strong two-party contest between the Democratic Party, organised around Andrew Jackson and Martin Van Buren, and the Whig Party, formed in opposition to Jackson under leaders such as Henry Clay, Daniel Webster and later William Henry Harrison.

Characteristics

The Second Party System saw the rapid expansion of the electorate to nearly all white adult men, mass campaign organisation (conventions, partisan newspapers, parades and rallies), and the highest voter turnouts in American history — presidential elections from 1840 onwards routinely mobilised around 80% of eligible voters. The parties were remarkably evenly matched nationally, which encouraged disciplined organisation and programmatic (if often deliberately ambiguous) platforms. Key issues included the national bank — Jackson's veto of the recharter of the Second Bank of the United States in 1832 and the ensuing Bank War — internal improvements, the tariff, public lands, and the spoils system. Whigs promoted Clay's American System of bank, tariff and infrastructure; Democrats championed limited government, hard money and states' rights while opposing monopoly privilege.

Collapse

The system disintegrated in the early 1850s as slavery — an issue both parties had sought to suppress — destroyed the Whigs after the Compromise of 1850 and the Kansas–Nebraska Act of 1854. The Whig Party collapsed, nativist Know Nothing sentiment flared briefly, and the new antislavery Republican Party rose in the North, inaugurating the Third Party System.
