Polar orbit

A polar orbit is one in which a satellite passes above or nearly above both poles of the body it orbits (usually a planet such as the Earth, but possibly another body such as the Moon or Sun) on each revolution. It therefore has an inclination of 90 degrees or close to it with respect to the body's equator.

Because the planet rotates beneath the satellite while the orbital plane remains fixed in inertial space, a satellite in a low polar orbit passes over a different strip of the surface on each revolution, eventually scanning the entire planet. This makes polar orbits ideal for mapping, Earth observation, environmental monitoring, and reconnaissance.

Uses

Polar orbits are commonly used for weather satellites (such as the NOAA and MetOp series), Earth-imaging satellites (such as Landsat and Sentinel), and scientific missions measuring the atmosphere, ice caps, and oceans. The Iridium communications constellation uses near-polar orbits to provide coverage at high latitudes, including the poles, which geostationary satellites cannot serve.

Sun-synchronous orbit

A special case is the Sun-synchronous orbit, a slightly retrograde near-polar orbit (typical inclination about 98–99 degrees) in which the orbital plane precesses at the same rate as the planet's motion around the Sun. A satellite in such an orbit passes over any given point on the surface at the same local solar time on every pass, giving consistent lighting conditions for imaging.
