Pinot noir is a red wine grape variety of the species Vitis vinifera. The name derives from the French words for "pine" and "black", referring to the grape's tightly clustered, pinecone-shaped bunches of dark fruit. It is chiefly associated with the Burgundy region of France, where it produces some of the most sought-after and expensive red wines in the world.

Characteristics

Pinot noir is notoriously difficult to grow and vinify. The thin-skinned berries are susceptible to rot, mildew and heat, and the variety thrives only in cool to moderate climates with well-drained, often limestone-rich soils. In the glass, Pinot noir wines are typically light to medium in body and color, with aromas of red cherry, raspberry and strawberry, developing earthy, forest-floor and mushroom notes with age. The grape is also one of the three classic varieties of Champagne, where it contributes body and structure to sparkling blends, and is pressed gently to make white and rosé sparkling wines.

History and clones

The variety is ancient — likely over a thousand years old — and highly prone to mutation. Hundreds of clones are officially recognized, and related varieties such as Pinot blanc, Pinot gris and Pinot Meunier are color mutations of Pinot noir. Burgundy's Côte d'Or remains its spiritual home, but the grape has achieved notable success in Oregon's Willamette Valley, California's Sonoma Coast and Carneros, New Zealand's Central Otago and Martinborough, and Germany, where it is known as Spätburgunder.

Reputation

Pinot noir is often described as the most romantic and expressive of grapes, prized for its transparency to terroir — the sense that wines from neighboring vineyards can taste strikingly different. Its following expanded dramatically after the 2004 film Sideways, in which the protagonist's devotion to Pinot noir (and disdain for Merlot) measurably shifted American wine sales.
