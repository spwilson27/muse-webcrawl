Prairie School

Prairie School is a late 19th- and early 20th-century architectural style, most common in the Midwestern United States. The style is usually marked by horizontal lines, flat or hipped roofs with broad overhanging eaves, windows grouped in horizontal bands, integration with the landscape, solid construction, craftsmanship, and discipline in the use of ornament. Horizontal lines were thought to evoke and relate to the native prairie landscape. The term "Prairie School" was not used by these architects to describe themselves; the term was coined by H. Allen Brooks, one of the architectural historians to study the field.

Characteristics and architects

The most famous proponent of the style was Frank Lloyd Wright, whose Prairie houses—including the Robie House (1910) in Chicago and the Willits House (1901)—feature low-pitched roofs, open floor plans organized around a central fireplace, art glass windows, and a deliberate blending of building and site. Other prominent figures included Louis Sullivan (a mentor to many), George Grant Elmslie, William Gray Purcell, Marion Mahony Griffin, Walter Burley Griffin, George Washington Maher, and the firm of Purcell and Elmslie, whose work spread the style to banks, courthouses, and smaller homes across the Midwest.

Development and decline

The Prairie School emerged from Chicago's architectural ferment after the Great Fire of 1871 and the World's Columbian Exposition of 1893, whose classical revivalism the younger architects resisted in seeking an indigenous American architecture. Wright's studio in Oak Park became its epicentre. The style waned after about 1914–1920 as tastes shifted to period revivals and as its leaders dispersed—Griffin to Australia and India, for example—though Wright's later Usonian homes extended its principles. Prairie School ideas influenced European modernism, notably the De Stijl movement and early Bauhaus designers who studied Wright's published Wasmuth Portfolio.
