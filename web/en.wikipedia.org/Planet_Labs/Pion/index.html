Pion

In particle physics, a pion or pi meson, denoted π, is a subatomic particle and the lightest meson. Pions exist in three states, with charges +1, 0 and −1, written π+, π0 and π−. They are composite particles made of a quark and an antiquark, and they play an important role in explaining the low-energy properties of the strong nuclear force.

Properties

The charged pions have a mass of about 139.6 MeV/c² and a mean lifetime of about 2.6 × 10−8 seconds, decaying mainly into a muon and a muon neutrino. The neutral pion has a slightly smaller mass, about 135.0 MeV/c², and a much shorter lifetime, decaying mainly into two photons.

History

Pions were predicted theoretically in 1935 by Hideki Yukawa as carriers of the nuclear force, and the charged pion was discovered experimentally in 1947 by a group led by Cecil Powell at the University of Bristol using photographic emulsions exposed to cosmic rays. Yukawa and Powell both received Nobel Prizes for their work.

Role in nuclear physics

Pion exchange is a major component of the residual strong force that binds protons and neutrons together in atomic nuclei. Virtual pions are exchanged between nucleons, and pion production is common in high-energy particle collisions.
