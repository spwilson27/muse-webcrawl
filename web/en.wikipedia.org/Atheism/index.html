Atheism
[Image placeholder: atheism symbol]
DefinitionAbsence of belief in the existence of deities
Related conceptsAgnosticism, ignosticism, antitheism, religious scepticism
EtymologyGreek atheos (ἄθεος), "without god(s)"

Atheism, in the broadest sense, is an absence of belief in the existence of deities. Less broadly, atheism is a rejection of the belief that any deities exist. In an even narrower sense, atheism is specifically the position that there are no deities. Atheism is contrasted with theism, which affirms the reality of at least one deity. "Strong" atheism asserts that no gods exist; "weak" atheism denotes merely lacking a belief in gods, a category that overlaps with agnosticism.

History

Atheistic thought appears in ancient Indian philosophy (the Cārvāka school), in Greek atomists such as Democritus and Epicurus, and in Lucretius's De rerum natura. The term "atheist" was long a label of abuse: Socrates was accused of not believing in the city's gods, and early Christians were called atheists for rejecting the Roman gods. Atheism became a self-description in the Enlightenment; Baron d'Holbach's System of Nature (1770) openly defended it, and the French Revolution briefly promoted a Cult of Reason. In the 20th century, state atheism characterised the Soviet Union and other communist states, while in the 2000s the "New Atheism" of Richard Dawkins, Christopher Hitchens, Sam Harris and Daniel Dennett brought atheist arguments to a mass readership.

Demographics and arguments

Global estimates of atheists vary widely by definition and survey method, from roughly 200 million to several hundred million, with the highest proportions in East Asia and parts of Europe; a larger share of humanity is religiously unaffiliated. Philosophical arguments for atheism include the problem of evil, arguments from inconsistent revelations and nonbelief, and appeals to parsimony (Occam's razor); Russell's teapot illustrates arguments about the burden of proof.
