Judiciary

The judiciary (also known as the judicial system or court system) is the system of courts that interprets, defends, and applies the law in the name of the state. Under the doctrine of the separation of powers, the judiciary is one of the three branches of government, alongside the legislature and the executive, and generally does not make law but interprets and applies it to the facts of each case.

Functions

Courts resolve disputes between private parties, hear criminal prosecutions, review administrative action, and in many systems exercise judicial review — the power to declare legislation or executive acts unconstitutional. Judges decide cases based on statutes, precedent (in common law systems), codes (in civil law systems), and constitutional provisions. Court structures typically include trial courts, appellate courts, and a supreme or constitutional court at the apex.

Judicial independence

The effectiveness of the rule of law depends on judicial independence: judges must decide free from interference by the other branches, litigants, or public pressure. Safeguards include secure tenure, protected salaries, and appointment procedures insulated from politics, though systems vary widely — judges are appointed, elected, or selected by mixed commissions in different jurisdictions.
