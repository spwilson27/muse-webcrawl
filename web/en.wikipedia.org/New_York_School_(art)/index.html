The New York School was an informal group of American poets, painters, dancers, and musicians active in the 1950s and 1960s in New York City. In the visual arts the term refers primarily to the painters of Abstract Expressionism and the related Color Field painting that made New York, rather than Paris, the centre of the Western art world after the Second World War.

[ Image: Jackson Pollock at work on a drip painting in his Long Island studio, 1950 ]

First generation

The first generation included Jackson Pollock, Willem de Kooning, Arshile Gorky, Mark Rothko, Clyfford Still, Barnett Newman, Ad Reinhardt, and Robert Motherwell. Many had been shaped by the Federal Art Project of the Works Progress Administration and by European émigré teachers such as Hans Hofmann. Critics Clement Greenberg and Harold Rosenberg promoted the movement; Rosenberg's 1952 essay "The American Action Painters" coined the term "action painting" for its gestural wing. The artists socialised at the Cedar Tavern and exhibited at galleries such as Betty Parsons and the Art of This Century.

Second generation and after

A second generation — including Helen Frankenthaler, Joan Mitchell, Grace Hartigan, Larry Rivers, and Jasper Johns as a transitional figure — moved toward Color Field painting, lyrical abstraction, and eventually Pop art, moving the label "New York School" into the broader postwar avant-garde that also embraced the poets of the New York School of poetry.
