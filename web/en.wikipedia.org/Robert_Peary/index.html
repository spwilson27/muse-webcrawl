Robert Edwin Peary (1856–1920) was an American explorer and United States Navy officer who claimed to have reached the geographic North Pole on 6 April 1909, accompanied by Matthew Henson and four Inughuit men — Ootah, Ooqueah, Seeglo and Egingwah. His claim was widely credited for much of the 20th century, though it has since been seriously questioned.

Greenland expeditions

A civil engineer in the Navy, Peary began Arctic work in 1886 and made repeated expeditions to northern Greenland through the 1890s, proving Greenland was an island and learning Inuit techniques of dress, diet and dog-sledging. He lost eight toes to frostbite. Expeditions in 1900 reached Cape Morris Jesup, Greenland's northern tip, and in 1906 his party set a farthest-north record of about 87°06′N.

The 1908–09 polar dash

Peary's final expedition sailed aboard the specially built Roosevelt, wintering at Cape Sheridan on Ellesmere Island. Using a staged system of supporting sledge parties ("the Peary system"), Peary, Henson and the four Inughuit made the last dash from Bartlett's furthest camp, claiming to reach the Pole on 6 April 1909. Within days of his return, a rival claim emerged from Frederick Cook, who said he had reached the Pole in 1908; the controversy embittered both camps for decades.

The disputed claim

Peary's critics — notably explorer Wally Herbert, who retraced the route — argued his improbably fast final-leg speeds, sparse navigational records and missing diary pages cast doubt on whether he reached the Pole exactly, though the National Geographic Society stood by him. Modern reassessments generally hold that Peary came close, possibly within a few tens of kilometres, while crediting Henson and the Inughuit as indispensable partners long under-acknowledged. Peary was promoted to rear admiral and thanked by Congress in 1911. He is often contrasted with Robert Falcon Scott, the South Pole explorer, and should not be confused with engineer Robert Stephenson.
