Julian day

Julian day
EpochNoon, 24 November 4714 BCE (proleptic Gregorian) / 1 January 4713 BCE (Julian)
Proposed byJoseph Justus Scaliger (1583)
Used inAstronomy, chronology, computing

The Julian day is the continuous count of days since the beginning of the Julian Period, used primarily by astronomers to record dates without the complications of months, leap years and calendar reforms. The Julian day number (JDN) is the integer assigned to a whole solar day; the Julian date (JD) adds the fraction of the day elapsed, with the day beginning at noon Universal Time.

The Julian Period

The system was proposed by the scholar Joseph Justus Scaliger in 1583. Its epoch is 1 January 4713 BCE in the Julian calendar (24 November 4714 BCE Gregorian), chosen because it was the most recent year in which three cycles used in chronology — the 28-year solar cycle, the 19-year lunar (Metonic) cycle and the 15-year indiction cycle — all began together; the full Julian Period lasts 7,980 years. The name refers to Scaliger's father, Julius Caesar Scaliger, not (as often assumed) to the Julian calendar, though the coincidence is convenient.

Use and variants

Because any date converts to a single number, intervals are found by simple subtraction, which makes the system ideal for astronomy, variable-star observing and software date arithmetic. Variants include the Modified Julian Date (MJD = JD − 2400000.5, starting at midnight), used by NASA and satellite operators, and the Lilian day number, counted from the introduction of the Gregorian calendar in 1582. The Julian date should not be confused with the Julian calendar or with ordinal ("Julian") dates used in some industries to mean the day-of-year.
