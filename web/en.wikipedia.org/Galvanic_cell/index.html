A galvanic cell or voltaic cell, named after Luigi Galvani and Alessandro Volta respectively, is an electrochemical cell in which a spontaneous redox reaction generates an electric current. It consists of two different metals (electrodes) connected by a conductive electrolyte and an external circuit: oxidation occurs at the anode and reduction at the cathode, and electrons flow through the external circuit from anode to cathode.

The Daniell cell

The classic example is the Daniell cell (1836), with a zinc anode in zinc sulfate solution and a copper cathode in copper sulfate solution, the two half-cells joined by a salt bridge or porous barrier that completes the circuit while limiting mixing. Zinc dissolves (Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻) and copper plates out (Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu), giving a standard cell potential of about 1.10 volts under standard conditions.

Thermodynamics

The maximum voltage a galvanic cell can deliver equals the difference of the standard electrode potentials of its half-reactions (the electromotive force), and the maximum electrical work is given by ΔG = −nFE, where n is the number of moles of electrons transferred and F is the Faraday constant. A cell based on non-spontaneous chemistry, by contrast, consumes electricity; such a device operated in reverse is an electrolytic cell, and rechargeable batteries switch between the two modes.

Applications

Galvanic cells underlie all primary batteries — zinc–carbon, alkaline, and lithium cells — while the galvanic series of metals in seawater governs corrosion engineering and cathodic protection, in which a sacrificial anode (often zinc or aluminium) corrodes preferentially to protect a steel hull or pipeline. Galvanic corrosion between dissimilar metals is the destructive counterpart of the same principle.
