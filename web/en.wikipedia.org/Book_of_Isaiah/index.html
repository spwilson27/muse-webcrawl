The Book of Isaiah is the first of the Latter Prophets in the Hebrew Bible and the first of the Major Prophets in the Christian Old Testament. It is identified by a superscription as the work of the 8th-century BCE prophet Isaiah ben Amoz, though modern scholars divide it into at least two or three major parts.

Structure

  • Proto-Isaiah (chapters 1–39): oracles against Judah and the nations from the reigns of Uzziah to Hezekiah, including the Immanuel prophecy and the narrative of Sennacherib's siege of Jerusalem.
  • Deutero-Isaiah (40–55): exilic consolation — "Comfort ye my people" — with the Servant Songs and the naming of Cyrus as God's anointed.
  • Trito-Isaiah (56–66): post-exilic material envisioning a new heavens and a new earth.

Themes

Isaiah stresses the holiness of God, judgement on injustice, the remnant of Israel, and the hope of a coming era of peace in which nations "shall beat their swords into plowshares". The book is among the most quoted in the New Testament, and Handel's Messiah draws heavily on it. The Great Isaiah Scroll, a complete copy found among the Dead Sea Scrolls, dates to about 335–120 BCE and is one of the oldest biblical manuscripts known.
