An intraocular lens (IOL) is a small artificial lens implanted in the eye to replace the natural crystalline lens when it has been removed during cataract surgery, or, less commonly, to correct refractive errors in a procedure called refractive lens exchange. Most IOLs consist of a small plastic optic with plastic side struts, called haptics, that hold the lens in place within the capsular bag of the eye.

History

British ophthalmologist Harold Ridley implanted the first IOL on 29 November 1949 at St Thomas' Hospital, London, after observing that fragments of acrylic canopy from Spitfire aircraft were tolerated inside pilots' eyes. His idea was initially met with strong professional resistance, but by the 1980s improved designs and surgical technique made IOL implantation the standard of care worldwide; tens of millions are now implanted each year.

Types

Monofocal IOLs focus at a single distance and are the most common. Multifocal and extended depth of focus (EDOF) lenses aim to provide both distance and near vision, reducing dependence on spectacles, sometimes at the cost of glare or halos. Toric IOLs correct corneal astigmatism. Phakic IOLs are implanted in front of the natural lens to correct high myopia in patients who retain their own lens; eyes without a natural lens are termed pseudophakic or aphakic (if no IOL is placed).

Power calculation

The required lens power is calculated pre-operatively from corneal curvature and axial length measurements (biometry) using formulas such as SRK/T, Holladay and Barrett, targeting the desired post-operative refraction, usually emmetropia for distance.
