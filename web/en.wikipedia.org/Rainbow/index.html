Rainbow

A rainbow is an optical phenomenon caused by reflection, refraction, and dispersion of light in water droplets, resulting in a spectrum of light appearing in the sky. It takes the form of a circular arc: a primary rainbow shows red on the outer part and violet on the inner side, spanning about 42 degrees around the antisolar point.

A double rainbow, with a fainter secondary bow whose colors are reversed.

Formation

Sunlight entering a raindrop is refracted, reflected once inside the drop, and refracted again on leaving. Because water disperses light, different colors leave at slightly different angles: red light at about 42° and violet at about 40° from the antisolar direction. A secondary rainbow, formed by two internal reflections, appears at about 51° with its colors reversed. The region between the bows is noticeably darker and is known as Alexander's band.

Rainbows can be seen whenever water droplets are airborne and the sun is low behind the observer; they can also be produced by spray, dew (dewbows), fog (fog bows, usually white), and moonlight (moonbows). A full circle rainbow can be observed from aircraft.

Scientific explanation

René Descartes gave the first quantitative account of the rainbow's angle in 1637, and Isaac Newton demonstrated that white light is composed of the colors of the spectrum. Supernumerary bows—narrow pastel bands inside the primary bow—are explained by wave interference and were accounted for in the 19th century by the Airy theory.

Culture and symbolism

Rainbows appear in the mythologies of many cultures: as the biblical sign of God's covenant with Noah, as the Norse bridge Bifröst, and as the path to a leprechaun's pot of gold in Irish folklore. In modern times the rainbow flag has become a symbol of LGBTQ pride and of diversity generally.
