Amazon river dolphin

Amazon river dolphin
Scientific name: Inia geoffrensis
Other names: boto, pink river dolphin
Conservation status: Endangered (IUCN)

The Amazon river dolphin (Inia geoffrensis), also known as the boto or pink river dolphin, is a species of toothed whale endemic to the Amazon and Orinoco river basins of South America. It is the largest species of river dolphin, with males reaching about 2.5 m in length, and is famous for its colouration: adults, especially males, acquire a striking pink hue, thought to result from scar tissue and flushed blood vessels.

Description and behaviour

Unlike marine dolphins, the boto has unfused neck vertebrae, letting it turn its head sharply, and a long beak with molar-like teeth suited to catching fish and crushing turtles and crabs in flooded forests. It has a prominent melon used in echolocation, small eyes, and broad flippers that let it manoeuvre among submerged trees during the flood season. Botos are generally solitary or found in small groups, and feed on dozens of fish species.

Conservation and folklore

The species is classified as Endangered, threatened by dam construction fragmenting rivers, mercury pollution from gold mining, entanglement in fishing gear and deliberate killing for use as catfish bait. In Amazonian folklore the boto is a shape-shifter (an encantado) that emerges from the river at festivals in human form — a belief that traditionally protected the animals from being hunted.
