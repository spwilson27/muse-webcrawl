Cerebral achromatopsia

Cerebral achromatopsia is a type of colour blindness caused not by the eyes but by damage to the cerebral cortex of the brain, typically the ventral occipitotemporal cortex including area V4. Affected people see the world in shades of grey despite having normal retinal function.

Causes and symptoms

The condition usually follows stroke, trauma or other lesions of the lingual and fusiform gyri. Unlike congenital achromatopsia, patients retain normal visual acuity and have no photophobia; their deficit is specifically in colour perception, and they may also show prosopagnosia or visual field defects depending on the lesion.

Research

Studies of cerebral achromatopsia have been important in identifying the brain's colour-processing regions. A well-known case was described by neurologist Oliver Sacks in "The Case of the Colorblind Painter".
