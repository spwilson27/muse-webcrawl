Forum shopping

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Forum shopping is a colloquial term for the practice of litigants having their legal case heard in the court thought most likely to provide a favorable judgment. Some jurisdictions have become known as "plaintiff-friendly" and attract litigation even when there is little or no connection between the legal issues and the jurisdiction in which suit is filed.

Overview

Where the same dispute can be litigated in more than one competent forum — different states, countries, or courts within one system — a claimant may select the forum whose substantive law, procedure, limitation period, remedies (such as damages calculation), or judiciary is most advantageous. Defendants may respond by asking the court to decline jurisdiction, for example through the doctrine of forum non conveniens, or by filing a competing action first in another forum.

Examples

United States

Within the United States, forum shopping occurs between state and federal courts and among federal districts. Patent plaintiffs for a time concentrated filings in the Eastern District of Texas, perceived as plaintiff-friendly, until the Supreme Court's 2017 decision in TC Heartland LLC v. Kraft Foods Group Brands LLC tightened venue rules. Bankruptcy venue shopping by large companies, defamation "libel tourism" to London courts, and divorce forum selection are other frequently cited examples.

International

Internationally, claimants may choose among national courts under rules such as the European Union's Brussels I Regulation, which largely gives priority to the court first seised and has limited the ability of courts to refuse cases because another forum is more appropriate.

Reforms and criticism

Critics argue forum shopping undermines fairness, wastes resources and lets parties distort outcomes. Defenders respond that choice among lawful forums is a legitimate part of advocacy and can discipline inefficient courts. Legislatures and courts have responded with venue statutes, transfer rules, anti-suit injunctions and choice-of-law doctrines that reduce, but do not eliminate, the practice.
