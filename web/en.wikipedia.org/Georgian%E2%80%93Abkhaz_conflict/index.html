The Georgian–Abkhaz conflict is a territorial and ethnic conflict between Georgia and the Abkhaz separatists of Abkhazia, a region in the northwest of Georgia on the eastern coast of the Black Sea. It is one of the post-Soviet "frozen conflicts", and it emerged as the Soviet Union collapsed in the late 1980s and early 1990s.

Background

Tensions between ethnic Georgians and Abkhaz over the status of Abkhazia within Georgia have roots in the Soviet period, when Abkhazia was made an autonomous republic (ASSR) within the Georgian Soviet Socialist Republic despite the Abkhaz forming a minority of its population. Disputes over university admissions, cultural policy, and demographic change fuelled periodic protests.

War of 1992–1993

In August 1992 Georgian government forces entered Abkhazia, beginning a bitter thirteen-month war. Abkhaz forces, supported by volunteers from the North Caucasus and, decisively, by Russian assistance, drove Georgian troops out of the region by September 1993. The war was accompanied by ethnic cleansing of Georgians from Abkhazia; more than 200,000 ethnic Georgians fled or were expelled, and thousands of people on both sides were killed.

Aftermath

A 1994 ceasefire agreement placed Commonwealth of Independent States (mostly Russian) peacekeepers along the Inguri River. Sporadic violence continued, notably fighting in the Kodori Valley and the Gali district in 1998. During the Russo-Georgian War of August 2008, Abkhaz forces took the Kodori Gorge, and Russia formally recognised Abkhazia as an independent state — a step Georgia and most of the international community reject, regarding Abkhazia as occupied Georgian territory.
