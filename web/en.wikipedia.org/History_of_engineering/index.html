The history of engineering is as old as civilisation: the word "engineer" derives from the Latin ingenium (cleverness) via the builders of military "engines", but the practice — applying mathematics, materials and ingenuity to build works that serve society — runs from the pyramids to the semiconductor fab.

Ancient and classical engineering

Imhotep, architect of Egypt's Step Pyramid (c. 2650 BCE), is the first engineer known by name. Mesopotamian irrigation, Roman roads, aqueducts and concrete vaults, Chinese canals, bridges and seismoscope, Indian metallurgy (the rust-resistant Iron Pillar of Delhi) and the water-lifting devices of Hellenistic Alexandria display mature engineering traditions. Medieval master builders raised Gothic cathedrals and windmills; Islamic engineers such as the Banu Musa brothers and al-Jazari advanced automata and water machinery.

The profession forms

Renaissance figures like Brunelleschi and Leonardo united art, science and craft. In the 18th century engineering emerged as a distinct profession: John Smeaton called himself the first "civil engineer", France founded the École des Ponts et Chaussées (1747), and the steam engine of Boulton and Watt powered factories, pumps and locomotives. Britain's Institution of Civil Engineers (1818) set the professional model.

Industrial and modern eras

The 19th century added iron and steel structures, railways, telegraphy, sanitation systems and chemical and electrical engineering — the latter splitting into its own discipline, as did mechanical engineering. The 20th century brought aviation, electronics, nuclear power, computing, spaceflight and software engineering; systems engineering tied them together for projects like Apollo. Today's frontiers — renewable energy, biotechnology, robotics, nanotechnology and AI — continue the same trajectory: engineering as the deliberate shaping of the material world.
