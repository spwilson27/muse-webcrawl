Scottish clan

A Scottish clan (from Gaelic clann, "children") is a kinship group among the Scottish people, historically centred on the Highlands, whose members identified with a common surname, territory and chief. Clans gave their members a sense of shared identity and descent, though in practice membership also included unrelated dependents, tenants and allied families (septs) who accepted the chief's protection and authority.

Structure

The head of a clan is its chief, whose authority was traditionally hereditary and whose office is now regulated, so far as armorial matters are concerned, by the Lord Lyon King of Arms; a chief recognised by Lyon is styled "The Much Honoured" and is entitled to supporters and a clan crest badge. Below the chief stood the daoine uaisle (gentlemen of the clan), including tacksmen who held land leases and organised military service. Clan identity is expressed through a surname, a tartan pattern, a crest badge and plant badge, a motto or war-cry (slogan), clan lands, and sometimes a gathering place.

History

Clan society emerged in the medieval Highlands, where royal authority was weak and kin-based organisation filled the gap; great kindreds such as Clan Donald dominated the west and the Isles under the Lordship of the Isles until its forfeiture in 1493. Clans feuded, raided cattle and formed shifting alliances; many took part in the Jacobite risings of 1715 and 1745. After the defeat at Culloden in 1746, the British government dismantled the military and judicial foundations of the clan system through the Disarming Act and the Heritable Jurisdictions Act of 1746. During the 18th and 19th centuries many chiefs became commercial landlords, and the Highland Clearances saw clan lands emptied for sheep farming, driving extensive emigration to North America and Australasia.

Modern clans

The Romantic revival of the 19th century — popularised by Sir Walter Scott and the royal patronage of tartan — fixed much of the modern imagery of clans, including many "clan tartans" whose antiquity is doubtful. Today clan societies flourish worldwide, especially in the Scottish diaspora: they maintain chiefs where lines survive, hold gatherings and Highland games, and preserve genealogy and heritage. Some clans are "armigerous," having arms registered but no recognised chief.
