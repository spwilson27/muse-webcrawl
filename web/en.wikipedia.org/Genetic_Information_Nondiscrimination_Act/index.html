Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act

The Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008 (GINA) is an Act of the United States Congress designed to prohibit some types of genetic discrimination. The act prohibits the use of genetic information in health insurance and employment: it bars health insurers from using genetic information to determine eligibility or premiums, and employers from using it in hiring, firing, or promotion decisions.

Background

As genetic testing became cheaper and more informative in the 1990s and 2000s, surveys showed that fear of discrimination discouraged people from participating in genetic research and from taking clinically useful tests. Earlier protections were piecemeal: the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act of 1996 offered limited protection in group health plans, and many states had enacted their own genetic nondiscrimination laws of varying scope.

Provisions

Title I of GINA prohibits health insurers from requesting or requiring genetic testing, adjusting premiums, or denying coverage based on genetic information, including family medical history. Title II prohibits employers with 15 or more employees, employment agencies, and labour unions from discriminating on the basis of genetic information, and restricts their acquisition of such information to narrow exceptions.

Limitations

GINA does not apply to life insurance, disability insurance, or long-term care insurance, nor to the United States military's health system or certain federal programmes. Genetic information about a manifested disease is treated differently from predictive test results, and conditions an individual already has are addressed by other laws such as the Americans with Disabilities Act and the Affordable Care Act.
