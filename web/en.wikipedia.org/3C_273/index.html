3C 273 is a quasar located in the constellation of Virgo. It was the first quasar ever identified and remains the optically brightest quasar in the sky from Earth, with an apparent magnitude of about 12.9 — visible in amateur telescopes despite lying roughly 2.4 billion light-years away.

Discovery

The object was catalogued as the 273rd source in the Third Cambridge Catalogue of Radio Sources (3C). In 1963, Dutch astronomer Maarten Schmidt measured its spectrum at the Palomar Observatory and recognised that its hydrogen emission lines were shifted by a redshift of 0.158, implying an enormous distance and luminosity — about four trillion times that of the Sun. The discovery of 3C 273's redshift revealed that quasars are extremely distant, highly luminous active galactic nuclei powered by accretion onto supermassive black holes.

Properties

At the heart of 3C 273 lies a supermassive black hole estimated at roughly 900 million solar masses. The quasar emits a prominent relativistic jet, extending some 200,000 light-years, visible in radio, optical and X-ray images. Because of its brightness and relative proximity, 3C 273 serves as a calibration source for many astronomical instruments and a reference for studying quasar structure.
