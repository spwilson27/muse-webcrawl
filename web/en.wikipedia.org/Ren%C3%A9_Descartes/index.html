René Descartes (1596–1650) was a French philosopher, mathematician and scientist, widely regarded as the founder of modern philosophy. His statement "Cogito, ergo sum" ("I think, therefore I am"), first written in French as "Je pense, donc je suis" in the Discourse on the Method (1637), became a foundational element of Western rationalism.

Philosophy

Descartes employed methodical doubt, rejecting any belief that could conceivably be doubted, until he reached the indubitable fact of his own thinking existence. From this foundation he attempted to rebuild knowledge, arguing for the existence of God and of a real material world. He proposed a dualism of mind (res cogitans) and body (res extensa), and his Meditations on First Philosophy (1641) remain a standard text in philosophy.

Mathematics and science

Descartes invented analytic geometry, linking algebra and geometry through the coordinate system that now bears his name (Cartesian coordinates). He also contributed to optics, formulating a law of refraction, and proposed a mechanistic, vortex-based physics in his Principles of Philosophy (1644).

Life

After education at the Jesuit college of La Flèche and service as a soldier, Descartes lived mostly in the Dutch Republic from 1628. In 1649 he moved to Stockholm as tutor to Queen Christina of Sweden, where he died of pneumonia in February 1650.
