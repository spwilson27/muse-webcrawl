Palinopsia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Palinopsia (from Greek palin, "again", and opsia, "seeing") is the pathological persistence or recurrence of a visual image after the stimulus has been removed. It is a disturbance of visual processing in the brain rather than of the eye, and is distinct from the normal brief afterimage everyone experiences after looking at a bright light.

Types

Palinopsia is commonly divided into illusory palinopsia — vague, indistinct afterimages influenced by ambient light and motion, including prolonged afterimages, light streaking, and visual trailing (moving objects leaving a series of frozen copies behind them) — and hallucinatory palinopsia, in which formed, high-resolution images or scenes persist or recur, sometimes long after viewing, and can be inserted into the visual field at any location.

Causes

Illusory palinopsia is associated with migraine, hallucinogen persisting perception disorder (HPPD), head trauma, and certain medications such as trazodone, nefazodone, topiramate, and clomiphene; it overlaps with the symptom complex of visual snow syndrome. Hallucinatory palinopsia typically signals a structural lesion of the posterior cerebral cortex — from stroke, tumour, seizure, or infection — and warrants neuroimaging, as it localises to the occipital and parieto-occipital regions, often of the non-dominant hemisphere.

Mechanisms and treatment

Proposed mechanisms include cortical hyperexcitability and dysfunction of visual motion and memory processing areas. Treatment targets the underlying cause; various agents (acetazolamide, verapamil, lamotrigine, clonazepam and others) have been reported to help in case series, but evidence is anecdotal and symptoms may persist.
