Georges-Eugène Haussmann (27 March 1809 – 11 January 1891), commonly known as Baron Haussmann, was a French official who served as prefect of the Seine (1853–1870) under Emperor Napoleon III. He directed the immense public-works programme known as Haussmann's renovation of Paris, which transformed the medieval city into the modern capital of broad boulevards, parks and uniform stone apartment blocks.

The boulevards and stone-fronted apartment buildings of Haussmann's Paris remain the city's defining image.

The renovation

Appointed prefect in 1853 with a mandate to modernise Paris, Haussmann demolished large tracts of crowded, insanitary old Paris and cut some 137 km of new avenues through the city, many radiating from focal monuments such as the Arc de Triomphe at the Étoile. The works rebuilt sewers and water supply, created great parks (the Bois de Boulogne and Bois de Vincennes, the Parc des Buttes-Chaumont), added markets, schools, hospitals and the Opéra (Palais Garnier), and imposed strict rules on building height and façades that produced the classic cream-coloured "Haussmann building".

The renovation improved circulation, sanitation and light, but displaced hundreds of thousands of poorer Parisians to the periphery, drove up rents, and was financed by debt schemes that drew fierce criticism — Jules Ferry attacked his "fantastic accounts". Whether the boulevards were designed partly to prevent barricade-building and ease military movement remains debated. Haussmann was dismissed in January 1870 amid financial scandal, shortly before the fall of the Second Empire.

Legacy

Most of central Paris still wears Haussmann's imprint, and his name became a byword for sweeping urban renewal ("Haussmannisation") — admired for beauty and functionality, criticised for social cleansing and authoritarian methods. He later served as a deputy and senator, and wrote memoirs defending his work.
