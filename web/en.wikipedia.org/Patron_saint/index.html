Patron saint

TraditionsRoman Catholicism, Eastern Orthodoxy, Oriental Orthodoxy, some Anglican and Lutheran churches
RoleHeavenly advocate and intercessor for a nation, place, craft, activity, or person
ExamplesSt George (England), St Nicholas (children, sailors), St Francis of Assisi (animals, ecology)

A patron saint is a saint who in Catholicism, Anglicanism, or Eastern Orthodoxy is regarded as the heavenly advocate of a nation, place, craft or activity, class, clan, family, or person. Believers ask for the patron saint's intercession and often observe the saint's feast day with special celebrations.

Origins

The practice grew out of the early Christian veneration of martyrs, whose relics were enshrined in churches dedicated to them. As the cult of saints developed, individual churches, towns, and guilds adopted particular saints as protectors, often chosen because of a connection with the place — a birthplace, a burial site, or an episode of the saint's life or martyrdom. The patronage of professions frequently derives from an attribute of the saint's life or death: Saint Barbara protects miners and artillerymen because her father was struck by lightning; Saint Lucy, depicted carrying her eyes, protects the blind; Saint Joseph, a carpenter, protects workers.

Examples

Many countries have national patron saints: Saint George for England and Georgia, Saint Andrew for Scotland and Greece, Saint Patrick for Ireland, Saint Denis for France, and Saint James for Spain. Cities likewise have patrons, such as Saint Mark for Venice and Saint Januarius for Naples, whose feast day draws crowds to witness the liquefaction of his preserved blood. Professions, activities, and causes also have patrons: Saint Francis of Assisi is patron of animals and ecology, Saint Thomas More of lawyers and politicians, and Saint Jude of lost or desperate causes.

In other traditions

Protestant churches generally abandoned the invocation of saints, though some Anglican and Lutheran calendars retain commemorations and church dedications. Analogous protective figures exist in other religions, such as patron deities in Hinduism and folk traditions.
