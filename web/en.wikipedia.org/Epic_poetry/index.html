Epic poetry

An epic poem, or simply an epic, is a lengthy narrative poem typically about extraordinary deeds of extraordinary characters who, in dealings with gods or other superhuman forces, gave shape to the moral universe of their descendants. Epics are among the oldest surviving forms of literature and were originally composed and transmitted orally.

Characteristics

Classical epics typically begin in medias res, invoke a Muse, employ elevated diction and catalogue-like passages, and centre on a hero of national or cosmic significance. The Homeric poems — the Iliad and the Odyssey — are the defining examples in the Western tradition, composed in dactylic hexameter. Milman Parry and Albert Lord showed that their formulaic style reflects composition in oral performance.

World epics

The genre is worldwide: the Mesopotamian Epic of Gilgamesh, the Sanskrit Mahābhārata and Rāmāyaṇa (the longest poems ever written), Virgil's Aeneid, the Old English Beowulf, the Finnish Kalevala, the Persian Shahnameh, the Mali Epic of Sundiata and the Kyrgyz Epic of Manas. Later literary epics, from Dante's Divine Comedy to Milton's Paradise Lost, consciously emulated the classical models.

Mock epic

The elevation of trivial subjects in epic form produced the mock epic, exemplified by Alexander Pope's The Rape of the Lock.
