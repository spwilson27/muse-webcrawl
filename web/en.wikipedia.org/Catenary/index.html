Catenary

In physics and geometry, a catenary is the curve that an idealised hanging chain or cable assumes under its own weight when supported only at its ends. The curve has a U-like shape and is described mathematically by the hyperbolic cosine function: y = a·cosh(x/a), where a is a constant determined by the chain's length, weight and the span.

History

Galileo believed the hanging chain formed a parabola, but Jungius showed this was false, and in 1691 Leibniz, Huygens and Johann Bernoulli independently derived the true curve in response to a challenge from Jacob Bernoulli. The name comes from the Latin catena, "chain"; Thomas Jefferson coined the English word "catenary." Christiaan Huygens had earlier proved that the catenary is the curve of uniform strength.

Properties and applications

A parabola, by contrast, is the shape of a cable supporting a load distributed uniformly horizontally — as in a suspension bridge deck. Inverted, the catenary gives the ideal arch shape that stands with purely compressive forces, a principle used from Gaudí's models to the Gateway Arch in St. Louis, whose profile is a weighted catenary. Catenaries also appear in mooring lines, overhead power and railway lines (where the supporting system is itself called "catenary"), and in the design of hanging structures and kilns.
