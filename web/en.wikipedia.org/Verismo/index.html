Verismo

OriginItaly, late 19th century
FieldsLiterature, opera
Key literary figuresGiovanni Verga, Luigi Capuana
Key operasCavalleria rusticana, Pagliacci

Verismo (Italian for "realism", from vero, "true") was an Italian literary and operatic movement of the late nineteenth century that sought to portray everyday life — particularly the lives of the poor and of the rural South — with objectivity and without romantic idealisation. It was the Italian counterpart of French naturalism, drawing inspiration from Émile Zola and the positivist philosophy of the period.

Literature

The movement's leading theorist and practitioner was the Sicilian writer Giovanni Verga, whose short stories in Vita dei campi (1880) and novels I Malavoglia (1881) and Mastro-don Gesualdo (1889) depicted Sicilian fishermen and peasants in an impersonal, free-indirect style that lets the narration adopt the characters' own speech and worldview. Luigi Capuana provided the movement's theoretical manifestos and his own naturalist novels.

Opera

In opera, verismo is associated with the giovane scuola ("young school") of Italian composers — Pietro Mascagni, Ruggero Leoncavallo, Umberto Giordano, Francesco Cilea, and the early Giacomo Puccini. Mascagni's Cavalleria rusticana (1890), adapted from Verga's story, and Leoncavallo's Pagliacci (1892) established the model: short, intense works set among ordinary people, driven by passion and violence, and sung in a declamatory, emotionally charged style. The two one-act operas are still routinely performed together as "Cav/Pag". Though the movement's vogue faded after about 1910, verismo singing and staging permanently changed operatic performance toward greater realism.
