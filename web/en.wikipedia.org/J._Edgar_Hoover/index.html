J. Edgar Hoover

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

John Edgar Hoover (January 1, 1895 – May 2, 1972) was an American law-enforcement administrator who served as the first Director of the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI). Appointed director of the Bureau of Investigation in 1924, he was instrumental in founding the FBI in 1935 and remained its director until his death in 1972, serving under eight presidents.

Career

Hoover built the FBI into a large, professional crime-fighting agency, introducing centralized fingerprint files, forensic laboratories, and scientific investigative methods. He presided over campaigns against gangsters in the 1930s, Nazi spies in World War II, and suspected communists during the Cold War.

Controversy and legacy

Late in life and after his death, Hoover became a controversial figure: evidence emerged that he had secretly amassed political intelligence, used the FBI to harass political dissenters and civil rights leaders — notably through the COINTELPRO programs — and kept files on elected officials. The FBI headquarters in Washington, D.C., the J. Edgar Hoover Building, bears his name. His long tenure led Congress to limit FBI directors to a single ten-year term.
