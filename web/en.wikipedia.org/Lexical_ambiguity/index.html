Lexical ambiguity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The word "bat" is lexically ambiguous between an animal and a piece of sports equipment.

Lexical ambiguity is the presence of two or more possible meanings in a single word (a lexeme). It is distinguished from syntactic (structural) ambiguity, in which a phrase or sentence can be parsed in more than one way. A word is lexically ambiguous if its meanings are distinct enough to allow different interpretations in context; the classic example is bank, which can mean a financial institution or the side of a river.

Homonymy and polysemy

Lexical ambiguity arises from homonymy, where two unrelated words happen to share a form (e.g., bat the animal and bat the club, which have different historical origins), and from polysemy, where a single word has developed several related senses (e.g., head of a body, of a company, or of a beer). Dictionaries treat homonyms as separate entries and polysemes as sub-senses of one entry.

Processing and disambiguation

In language comprehension, listeners and readers rapidly use context to select the intended sense. Psycholinguistic experiments—such as cross-modal priming studies—suggest that multiple meanings of an ambiguous word are briefly activated before context settles on one. In natural language processing, choosing the correct sense is the task of word-sense disambiguation, important for machine translation, information retrieval, and question answering.

Examples

  • crane: a bird; a machine for lifting; to stretch one's neck.
  • light: not heavy; not dark; a source of illumination.
  • right: correct; a direction; a legal entitlement.
