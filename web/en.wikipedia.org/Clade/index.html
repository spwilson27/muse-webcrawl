Clade

A clade comprises an ancestor and all of its descendants.

A clade (from Ancient Greek kládos, "branch"), also called a monophyletic group, is a grouping of organisms that consists of a common ancestor and all of its direct descendants. Clades are the fundamental units of cladistics and of modern phylogenetic classification: a valid clade can be delimited on a phylogenetic tree by a single "cut", separating one branch and everything that grows from it.

Definition and terminology

The concept was formalised by the entomologist Willi Hennig, founder of phylogenetic systematics, and the word "clade" was coined by Julian Huxley in 1957. A group that includes a common ancestor but excludes some of its descendants is called paraphyletic (for example, "reptiles" excluding birds), while a group assembled from descendants of several ancestors on the basis of convergent features is polyphyletic (for example, "flying animals" combining insects, birds and bats). Modern classification aims to name only monophyletic groups.

Naming clades

Under rank-based systems such as the Linnaean hierarchy, clades are assigned ranks (phylum, class, order, family and so on), but a clade's membership does not depend on its rank. The PhyloCode provides an alternative, rank-free system of phylogenetic nomenclature in which clade names are defined directly by ancestry — for example as the least inclusive clade containing two specified species (a node-based definition) or as all organisms sharing a particular derived trait (an apomorphy-based definition).

Examples

Well-known clades include Mammalia (all mammals and their most recent common ancestor), Aves (birds), Tetrapoda (four-limbed vertebrates and their descendants, including snakes), and Amniota. Clades are nested within clades: primates are a clade within mammals, which are a clade within tetrapods, which are a clade within vertebrates, and so on back to the last universal common ancestor of all life.
