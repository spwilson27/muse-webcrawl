Sodium channel

Sodium channels are integral membrane proteins that form ion channels conducting sodium ions (Na+) through a cell's plasma membrane. They are classified by their gating: voltage-gated sodium channels (NaV), which open in response to membrane depolarization and generate the upstroke of the action potential in neurons, muscle, and heart; ligand-gated channels such as the epithelial sodium channel (ENaC); and others responsive to intracellular messengers.

Structure and gating

The voltage-gated channel's pore-forming α subunit is a single large polypeptide of four homologous domains (DI–DIV), each with six transmembrane segments; the S4 segments act as voltage sensors and the loops between S5 and S6 form the selectivity filter, whose DEKA motif confers sodium selectivity. Auxiliary β subunits modulate gating and localization. On depolarization the channel activates within fractions of a millisecond, then rapidly inactivates — an "ball-and-chain"-like hinged lid (the IFM motif) occludes the pore — producing the brief sodium current and the absolute refractory period. In mammals there are nine α-subunit isoforms, NaV1.1–1.9, encoded by SCN1A–SCN11A genes, with distinct tissue distributions.

Pharmacology and toxins

Sodium channels are targets of local anesthetics, many antiarrhythmic and antiepileptic drugs, and powerful natural toxins: tetrodotoxin (TTX) and saxitoxin block the outer pore; batrachotoxin and various scorpion and sea-anemone toxins modify gating and are research tools. Insecticides such as pyrethroids act on insect sodium channels.

Disease

Mutations cause channelopathies: SCN1A mutations in Dravet syndrome and other epilepsies; SCN4A in periodic paralyses and myotonias; SCN5A in long QT syndrome type 3 and Brugada syndrome; SCN9A (NaV1.7) in inherited pain disorders — loss of function causes congenital insensitivity to pain, gain of function causes erythromelalgia — making NaV1.7 a major analgesic drug target.
