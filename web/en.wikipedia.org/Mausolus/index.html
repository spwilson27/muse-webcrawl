Mausolus

Mausolus (Ancient Greek: Μαύσωλος; Carian: 𐊪𐊠𐊥𐊱𐊩𐊵 Mauśoλ) was a ruler of Caria (377–353 BCE), nominally a satrap of the Achaemenid Empire. He enjoyed the status of a king or dynast by virtue of the powerful position created by his father Hecatomnus, who had succeeded the assassinated Persian satrap Tissaphernes in the Carian satrapy and founded the hereditary dynasty of the Hecatomnids.

Mausolus, in conjunction with his sister and queen Artemisia, conquered a number of cities and islands in the region and moved his capital from Mylasa to Halicarnassus, which he refortified and rebuilt on a grand scale. He is best remembered for the monumental shrine tomb erected for him, the Mausoleum at Halicarnassus, from which the word "mausoleum" derives.

Reign

Mausolus ruled for 24 years. He participated in the Revolt of the Satraps against the Persian king Artaxerxes II, supported the anti-Athenian side in the Social War, and expanded his territory along the Lycian and coastal regions. Despite his nominal loyalty to Persia, he conducted himself as an independent Hellenizing monarch, founding Greek-style cities and institutions.

Mausoleum at Halicarnassus

The tomb Mausolus planned for himself and his wife at Halicarnassus (modern Bodrum, Turkey) was designed by the Greek architects Satyros and Pythius of Priene and adorned by the sculptors Scopas, Leochares, Bryaxis, and Timotheus. Completed around 350 BCE, it stood approximately 45 metres (148 ft) high and was counted among the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. It survived largely intact into the medieval period before being destroyed by earthquakes and quarried by the Knights Hospitaller. Colossal statues identified as Mausolus and Artemisia are now in the British Museum.
