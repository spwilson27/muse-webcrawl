Die Brücke

Die Brücke (German for "The Bridge") was a group of German artists founded in Dresden in 1905 by architecture students Ernst Ludwig Kirchner, Fritz Bleyl, Karl Schmidt-Rottluff and Erich Heckel. Together with Der Blaue Reiter in Munich, Die Brücke formed the nucleus of Expressionism, one of the founding movements of modern art, distinguished by raw emotion, vigorous brushwork, intense non-naturalistic colour and a revival of the woodcut print.

Kirchner's woodcut imagery became emblematic of Die Brücke.

History

The group's name, drawn from Nietzsche's Thus Spoke Zarathustra, symbolised their ambition to build a bridge to the art of the future. Emil Nolde and Max Pechstein joined in 1906, and Otto Mueller in 1910. From 1911 the group worked in Berlin, where their depictions of metropolitan life reached a climax. Disputes over Kirchner's chronicle of the group led to its dissolution in 1913.

Style and legacy

Die Brücke artists painted landscapes, nudes and street scenes with jagged contours, clashing colours and deliberately crude forms, influenced by Fauvism, van Gogh, Munch and the tribal art they saw in ethnographic museums. The Nazis condemned their work as "degenerate" in 1937. The Brücke Museum in Berlin, opened in 1967, holds the largest collection of their art.
