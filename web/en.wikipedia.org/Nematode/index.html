Nematode

Nematoda
Phylum: Nematoda
Common names: Roundworms, eelworms
Diversity: ~25,000+ described species; total possibly ~1 million
Fossil record: Early Devonian – present

The nematodes (phylum Nematoda), also called roundworms, are one of the most diverse and abundant groups of animals on Earth. They occur in virtually every ecosystem — marine, freshwater, and terrestrial — from polar regions to ocean trenches, and in enormous numbers: a few square metres of soil may contain millions of individuals. Nathan Cobb famously remarked that if all matter except nematodes disappeared, the world would still be dimly recognizable by its nematode outline. Most species are microscopic, though a few parasites reach several metres in length.

Anatomy and life cycle

Nematodes are unsegmented, bilaterally symmetric worms with a tough collagenous cuticle that is moulted four times during growth. They lack a circulatory and respiratory system; digestion runs through a simple straight gut, and locomotion is by sinusoidal undulation using longitudinal muscles only. Species may be free-living (feeding on bacteria, fungi, or other organisms) or parasitic on plants, animals, and humans. Reproduction is usually sexual, with separate sexes, though hermaphroditism and parthenogenesis occur.

Importance

Free-living nematodes are vital to nutrient cycling and soil health. Plant-parasitic species (such as root-knot and cyst nematodes) cause major agricultural losses, while animal parasites include human pathogens such as Ascaris, hookworms, pinworms, and the filarial worms causing elephantiasis and river blindness. The free-living species Caenorhabditis elegans is one of biology's principal model organisms, central to developmental biology and the first multicellular organism to have its genome fully sequenced (1998).
