Kresy, or Kresy Wschodnie ("Eastern Borderlands"), is the Polish name for the eastern territories of the interwar Second Polish Republic, lying east of the Curzon Line. The region encompassed the voivodeships of Lwów, Wilno, Nowogródek, Polesie, Tarnopol, Stanisławów, Wołyń and Białystok, and had a mixed population of Poles, Ukrainians, Belarusians, Jews, Lithuanians and others.

History

The territories had belonged for centuries to the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth before passing to the Russian and Austrian empires in the partitions of the late 18th century. They were incorporated into the reborn Polish state after the Polish–Ukrainian and Polish–Soviet wars, confirmed by the Treaty of Riga (1921).

Second World War and aftermath

Following the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, the Soviet Union occupied and annexed the Kresy in September 1939. Nazi Germany occupied the region from 1941 to 1944, during which the Jewish population was largely murdered in the Holocaust and ethnic violence, notably in Volhynia, claimed tens of thousands of Polish lives. After the war the Potsdam settlement confirmed Soviet annexation; most ethnic Poles were "repatriated" westward, many to the formerly German territories Poland received in compensation. The Kresy remain a potent symbol in Polish cultural memory and literature.
