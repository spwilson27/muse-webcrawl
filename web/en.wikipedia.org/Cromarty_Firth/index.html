Cromarty Firth

Location: Ross and Cromarty, Highland, Scotland
Coordinates: 57°41′N 4°07′W
Ocean/sea sources: Moray Firth, North Sea

The Cromarty Firth (Scottish Gaelic: Caolas Chrombaidh) is an arm of the Moray Firth in north-east Scotland. It is a deep, sheltered inlet of the North Sea, extending inland from between the two Sutors — headlands known as the Sutors of Cromarty — near the town of Cromarty on the Black Isle, northwest past Dingwall to the valley of the River Conon.

The Cromarty Firth viewed from the Black Isle.

Geography

The firth is a classic ria — a drowned glacial valley — and provides one of the finest natural deep-water harbours in Europe. Its entrance, barely a mile wide between the North and South Sutors, opens into a basin that extends some 20 miles (30 km) inland. Towns and villages on its shores include Cromarty, Invergordon, Alness, Dingwall, and Conon Bridge. The Cromarty Bridge, opened in 1979, carries the A9 road across the firth near its southwestern end.

History

The firth's sheltered anchorage made it an important naval base during both World Wars; Invergordon was a major Royal Navy base and the scene of the Invergordon Mutiny of 1931. From the 1970s, the firth became a centre of the North Sea oil industry: the yard at Nigg built production platforms, and the deep water of the firth was long used for the mooring and maintenance of oil rigs and, more recently, structures for offshore wind farms.

Nature

The Cromarty Firth is designated a Site of Special Scientific Interest and a Special Protection Area for its intertidal mudflats, which support nationally important numbers of wintering wildfowl and waders. The firth is also well known for its population of bottlenose dolphins, among the most northerly in the world.
