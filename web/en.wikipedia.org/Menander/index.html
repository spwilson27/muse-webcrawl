Menander (c. 342–c. 290 BC) was a Greek dramatist and the best-known representative of Athenian New Comedy. He wrote more than a hundred plays, of which only one, Dyskolos (The Grouch), survives complete, along with substantial fragments of others recovered from Egyptian papyri in the 20th century.

Work

Unlike the political satire of Aristophanes' Old Comedy, Menander's plays concern ordinary citizens — lovers, misers, soldiers, slaves, and stern fathers — in domestic plots of mistaken identity, blocked romance, and reconciliation. His refined verse, stock characters, and maxim-like lines were enormously popular in antiquity; he was ranked second only to Homer by some critics.

Legacy

Menander profoundly influenced Roman comedy: Plautus and Terence adapted his plots, and through them his conventions passed into European theatre — the young lovers, clever slave, and five-act structure became templates for later comedy from Shakespeare to the modern sitcom. Famous quotations attributed to him include "Whom the gods love dies young".
