Distributive politics

Distributive politics is the study, within political science, of how governments allocate resources — public spending, infrastructure, subsidies, grants and regulatory benefits — among geographic districts, groups and individuals. Because benefits can be targeted while costs are spread across all taxpayers, distributive allocations create distinctive incentives for legislators and executives, and are central to theories of coalition building in legislatures.

Concepts

Pork barrel spending is the classic example: projects directed at a legislator's district, such as dams, roads or military bases. Theodore Lowi's policy typology distinguished distributive policies (which allocate divisible benefits to particular subgroups with little visible cost) from redistributive and regulatory policies. David Mayhew argued that US members of Congress are motivated by re-election and engage in "credit claiming" for particularised benefits.

Theories

Weingast, Shepsle and Johnsen modelled distributive policy as a collective action problem: each district demands projects whose local benefits exceed its share of taxes, producing oversized budgets — the "Law of 1/n". Party-based theories propose that governing parties target either core supporters (Cox and McCubbins) or swing voters (Lindbeck and Weibull; Dixit and Londregan) with transfers. Empirical studies of US federal spending, EU structural funds and grant allocation in many countries find systematic partisan and electoral patterns.

Normative debates

Targeted spending can compensate disadvantaged regions and smooth regional inequality, but it can also reward political loyalty, distort investment, and undermine programmatic policymaking. Formula-based allocation and independent grant bodies are often proposed as correctives.
