Syllabic

Syllabic refers to things relating to a syllable. The term is used in several technical senses: in linguistics and phonetics, a syllabic consonant is a consonant that forms the nucleus of a syllable; in writing systems, a syllabic script or syllabary is one whose characters represent syllables; and in music, syllabic text setting assigns one note to each syllable of text, as opposed to melismatic setting.

Syllabic writing

A syllabary is a set of written symbols representing syllables, typically a consonant followed by a vowel. Examples include Japanese kana (hiragana and katakana), the Cherokee syllabary devised by Sequoyah in the 1820s, the Vai syllabary of Liberia, and the Canadian Aboriginal syllabics used for Cree, Inuktitut and other languages. Ancient scripts such as Linear B and the Cypriot syllabary were also syllabic.

Syllabic consonants

In phonetics, syllabic consonants — most often nasals and liquids such as [n̩] and [l̩] — can serve as the syllable nucleus in place of a vowel, as in the English words button and bottle in many pronunciations. They are marked in the International Phonetic Alphabet with a vertical stroke below the symbol.

Music

In chant and song, syllabic style sets one pitch per syllable, in contrast to neumatic (a few notes per syllable) and melismatic (many notes per syllable) styles — a distinction of particular importance in the analysis of Gregorian chant.
