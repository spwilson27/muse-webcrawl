Indian philosophy refers to the philosophical traditions originating in the Indian subcontinent, some of which date back more than two and a half millennia. Its central concerns include the nature of reality, knowledge, the self, liberation from suffering, and right conduct. Indian philosophical systems are traditionally divided into orthodox (āstika) schools, which accept the authority of the Vedas, and heterodox (nāstika) schools, which do not.

Orthodox schools

The six classical āstika schools (darśanas) are Nyāya (logic and epistemology), Vaiśeṣika (atomism and metaphysics), Sāṃkhya (dualism of puruṣa and prakṟti), Yoga (meditative discipline, systematized by Patañjali), Mīmāṃsā (Vedic exegesis), and Vedānta (the philosophy of the Upanishads), the last of which includes Advaita (non-dualism), Viśiṣṭādvaita, and Dvaita traditions.

Heterodox schools

Nāstika traditions include Buddhism, Jainism, and the materialist Cārvāka school. Buddhism developed sophisticated theories of impermanence, no-self (anātman), dependent origination, and emptiness (śūnyatā), while Jain philosophy is known for its doctrines of non-violence (ahiṃsā), many-sidedness (anekāntavāda), and non-absolutism (syādvāda).

Common themes

Across many schools run shared concepts: karma (action and its consequences), saṃsāra (the cycle of rebirth), and mokṣa or nirvāṇa (liberation from that cycle). Philosophical debate was conducted through commentaries, sub-commentaries, and formal disputation, producing a rich literature in Sanskrit, Pali, and Prakrit.

Modern period

In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, thinkers such as Swami Vivekananda, Sri Aurobindo, Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan, and Jiddu Krishnamurti brought Indian philosophy into dialogue with Western thought, and academic philosophy in India today studies both classical and contemporary analytic and continental approaches.
