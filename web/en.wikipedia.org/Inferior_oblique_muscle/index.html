Inferior oblique muscle

OriginOrbital surface of the maxilla
InsertionSclera, posterolateral eyeball
NerveOculomotor nerve (CN III), inferior division
ActionsExtorsion, elevation, abduction of the eye

The inferior oblique muscle is one of the six extraocular muscles that move the eye. It is the only extraocular muscle to originate at the front of the orbit, arising from the orbital surface of the maxilla just lateral to the nasolacrimal canal, and passing backward beneath the inferior rectus to insert on the posterolateral sclera.

Action

The inferior oblique's primary action is extorsion (outward rotation of the top of the eye), with secondary elevation and tertiary abduction. Its elevating action is greatest when the eye is adducted (turned toward the nose), a fact used in clinical testing of its function. It is supplied by the inferior division of the oculomotor nerve (cranial nerve III), which enters its posterior surface — the same surface where the parasympathetic fibres to the ciliary ganglion travel.

Clinical significance

Overaction of the inferior oblique is common in congenital esotropia and dissociated vertical deviation, causing the eye to shoot upward in adduction; it is treated surgically by weakening procedures such as recession, myectomy or anterior transposition. Palsy of the muscle is comparatively rare and produces vertical diplopia. The muscle's proximity to the inferior rectus and Lockwood's ligament is important in orbital floor fracture repair and lower eyelid surgery.

Comparative anatomy

Unlike the superior oblique, which acts through the trochlear pulley, the inferior oblique pulls directly from its anterior origin, giving it a short course of about 37 mm — the shortest of the extraocular muscles.
