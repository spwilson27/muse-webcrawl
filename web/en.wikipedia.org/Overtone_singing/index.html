Overtone singing

Overtone singing — also known as overtone chanting, harmonic singing or throat singing in some traditions — is a vocal technique in which the singer manipulates the resonances of the vocal tract to make individual overtones of a fundamental note stand out as separate pitches, so that one voice seems to produce two notes at once.

Traditions

The best-known styles are those of Central Asia: Tuvan khoomei (with substyles such as sygyt and kargyraa), Mongolian khöömii, and the related Tibetan Buddhist chant of the Gelug tradition, in which monks produce very low fundamentals with prominent harmonics. Overtone techniques also occur in Sardinian cantu a tenore, South African traditions, and the Inuit vocal games of Canada and Alaska, though these differ in method.

Technique

By shaping the tongue, lips and jaw, the singer tunes a resonance of the vocal tract to coincide with a single harmonic of the voice, amplifying it far above its neighbours. Western practitioners since the 1960s and 1970s — including the vocal ensemble experiments of Karlheinz Stockhausen's circle and singers such as David Hykes — have brought the technique into contemporary and meditative music.
