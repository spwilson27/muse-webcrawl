Biological Weapons Convention

The Biological Weapons Convention (BWC), formally the Convention on the Prohibition of the Development, Production and Stockpiling of Bacteriological (Biological) and Toxin Weapons and on their Destruction, is a disarmament treaty that effectively bans biological weapons. It was the first multilateral treaty to prohibit an entire category of weapons of mass destruction. The convention was opened for signature on 10 April 1972 and entered into force on 26 March 1975.

Provisions

Article I prohibits states parties from developing, producing, stockpiling, acquiring, or retaining microbial or other biological agents and toxins "of types and in quantities that have no justification for prophylactic, protective or other peaceful purposes", as well as weapons and delivery equipment designed to use them. This "general purpose criterion" means the ban covers future agents as well as known ones. Other articles oblige parties to destroy existing stocks, forbid transferring the weapons or assisting others to acquire them, require national implementing legislation, and provide for consultation and complaints to the UN Security Council.

History

The treaty built on the 1925 Geneva Protocol, which banned the use of biological and chemical weapons but not their possession. Impetus for a full ban grew during the Cold War; the United Kingdom proposed separating biological from chemical weapons, and in 1969 US President Richard Nixon unilaterally renounced offensive biological weapons, renouncing them before the treaty was concluded. Negotiations in Geneva produced the text adopted by the UN General Assembly in 1971.

Compliance and limitations

The BWC has no formal verification organisation comparable to the OPCW for chemical weapons, relying instead on review conferences held roughly every five years and voluntary confidence-building measures. Attempts in the 1990s to negotiate a verification protocol collapsed in 2001. Historical violations — most notably the Soviet Union's vast clandestine Biopreparat programme, which continued after the USSR ratified the treaty, and revelations about Iraq's programme — have fuelled debate about the treaty's effectiveness, though its normative prohibition is widely regarded as strong: no state openly claims a right to possess biological weapons.
