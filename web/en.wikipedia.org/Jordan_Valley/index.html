The Jordan Valley forms part of the larger Jordan Rift Valley. It is a long, narrow depression in the Levant, running roughly from the sources of the Jordan River in the north, through the Sea of Galilee and the Jordan River, down to the Dead Sea in the south, and continuing toward the Red Sea. Much of the valley lies below sea level, making it one of the lowest land regions on Earth.

Geography

The valley is bounded by steep escarpments on both sides: the hills of Galilee, Samaria and Judea to the west, and the plateaus of the Golan Heights, Ajloun and Moab to the east. Its floor is divided into an upper floodplain (the Zor) through which the river meanders, and a wider, flatter terrace (the Ghor) used extensively for agriculture.

The northern section, the Hula Valley, was once a lake and marshland; it was drained in the 1950s and is now farmland and a nature reserve important for migrating birds. South of the Sea of Galilee the valley widens, and near the Dead Sea it reaches its lowest elevations.

Climate and agriculture

The valley has a hot, arid to semi-arid climate, with mild winters that make it a natural greenhouse. Irrigated agriculture is extensive, producing bananas, dates, citrus fruit, vegetables and Medjool dates. Water is drawn from the Jordan River, side wadis and groundwater, and water allocation has long been a sensitive political issue in the region.

History

The Jordan Valley is one of the oldest continuously inhabited regions in the world. Jericho, near the northern shore of the Dead Sea, is among the oldest known cities, with settlement layers reaching back to the Natufian period. The valley was a corridor for trade and conquest throughout antiquity, and sites such as Beth Shean, Pella and Gerasa flourished in the Roman and Byzantine periods. In the modern era the valley forms the border between Jordan to the east and Israel and the West Bank to the west along much of its length.

Geology

The valley lies along the Dead Sea Transform, a major fault system marking the boundary between the Arabian Plate and the Sinai–Levantine subplate. Movement along this transform created the depression and continues to produce earthquakes, including the destructive Jericho earthquake of 1927.
