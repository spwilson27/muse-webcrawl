Entente Cordiale

The Entente Cordiale (French pronunciation: [ɑ̃tɑ̃t kɔʁdjal]; roughly "cordial agreement") comprised a series of agreements signed on 8 April 1904 between the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland and the French Third Republic, which saw a significant improvement in Anglo-French relations. The agreements settled long-standing colonial disputes and ended a millennium of intermittent conflict and rivalry between the two states, laying the groundwork for their diplomatic and military cooperation against the growing power of the German Empire.

Background

France and Britain had been rivals for centuries — through the Hundred Years' War, the colonial struggles of the eighteenth century, and the Napoleonic Wars — and remained competitors for empire in Africa and Asia. Their rivalry nearly produced war during the Fashoda Incident of 1898, when French and British expeditions confronted each other in the Sudan; France's climb-down left resentment but also clarified that accommodation was preferable. Britain, meanwhile, was abandoning "splendid isolation" as German naval expansion and the burdens of the Boer War exposed its overstretch. The 1903 visit of Edward VII to Paris, and President Loubet's return visit to London, thawed public opinion in both countries.

Terms

The core agreements, negotiated by French foreign minister Théophile Delcassé and British foreign secretary Lord Lansdowne, were a classic colonial bargain. France recognised Britain's position in Egypt; Britain recognised France's pre-eminence in Morocco. Other clauses settled disputes over the Newfoundland fisheries and French shore rights, the Nigeria–Niger frontier, Siam (Thailand), Madagascar, and the New Hebrides condominium. Crucially, the Entente was not a formal alliance: Britain undertook no obligation to support France in a war. But the settlements removed the main sources of friction, and from 1905–06 the two countries' military staffs began informal conversations about cooperation in a possible war with Germany.

Consequences

Germany tested the Entente in the First Moroccan Crisis of 1905, provoking the opposite of its intended effect: Britain backed France at the Algeciras Conference, and Anglo-French military planning deepened. A parallel Anglo-Russian Convention in 1907 completed the emerging Triple Entente of Britain, France, and Russia, facing the Triple Alliance of Germany, Austria-Hungary, and Italy. In 1914, Britain's freedom of action was formally intact, but the Entente had created strong moral and strategic expectations; Britain declared war on Germany in August 1914 after the invasion of Belgium, and the two countries fought the First World War as allies. The phrase entente cordiale has since been used for the enduring, sometimes strained, Franco-British relationship, and its centenary was celebrated by both states in 2004.
