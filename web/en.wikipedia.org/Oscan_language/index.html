Oscan language

RegionSouth-central Italy (Samnium, Campania, Lucania, Bruttium)

Oscan is an extinct Indo-European language of ancient Italy, belonging with Umbrian to the Osco-Umbrian (Sabellic) branch of the Italic languages. It was spoken by the Samnites and neighbouring peoples across south-central Italy from at least the 7th century BC until it was displaced by Latin in the 1st century AD, in the aftermath of the Social War.

Attestation

Oscan is known from around 800 inscriptions: coin legends, dedications, legal texts and curse tablets. Major documents include the Cippus Abellanus, a treaty inscribed on stone, and the Tabula Bantina, the longest Oscan text, a municipal statute from Bantia. Inscriptions are written in a native alphabet derived from Etruscan, and also in Greek and Latin scripts.

Features

Oscan differs from Latin in characteristic sound changes: Indo-European *kʷ becomes p (Oscan pis versus Latin quis, "who"), and voiced aspirates become fricatives. Its grammar closely resembles Latin in structure, with a comparable case system.

Legacy

Although Oscan died out in antiquity, it left traces in local Latin usage and place names, and a few words may survive in southern Italian dialects. The last known inscriptions, graffiti from Pompeii, date to the city's final decades before AD 79.
