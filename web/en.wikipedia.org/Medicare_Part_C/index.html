Medicare Part C

Medicare Part C, known as Medicare Advantage (MA), is a programme of the United States federal government under which Medicare beneficiaries receive their Medicare benefits through private health insurance plans approved by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS), instead of through the traditional fee-for-service Parts A and B.

How it works

CMS pays each participating insurer a monthly capitated amount per enrollee, adjusted for health status through risk scores. Plans must cover everything Original Medicare covers (except hospice, which remains under Part A) and typically combine hospital, medical, and often prescription-drug (Part D) coverage in a single plan, with a required annual out-of-pocket maximum. Most plans use provider networks — HMO and PPO structures — and many offer extra benefits not covered by Original Medicare, such as dental, vision, hearing, and fitness programmes.

History and enrolment

Private-plan options date to the 1970s and were formalised as "Medicare+Choice" by the Balanced Budget Act of 1997, then renamed Medicare Advantage by the Medicare Modernization Act of 2003. Enrolment has grown steadily; by 2024 more than half of eligible Medicare beneficiaries — over 30 million people — were enrolled in Medicare Advantage plans.

Criticism and debate

Analyses by the Medicare Payment Advisory Commission have repeatedly found that Medicare pays more per MA enrollee than it would spend on comparable beneficiaries in traditional Medicare, driven partly by coding practices that raise risk scores ("upcoding"). Supporters cite care coordination, extra benefits, and high enrollee satisfaction.
