Ontology

Ontology is the branch of metaphysics concerned with the nature of being and existence: what entities exist, how they can be grouped into categories, and how they relate to one another. The word derives from the Greek on (ὄν, "being") and logia ("study"). Ontology asks questions such as: What is it for something to exist? Do abstract objects such as numbers exist? What are the fundamental categories of reality?

History

The subject begins with Parmenides and Plato and was systematised by Aristotle, whose Categories and Metaphysics distinguished substance from accident and form from matter. Medieval scholastics such as Aquinas debated the distinction between essence and existence and the status of universals. The term "ontology" itself was coined in the early 17th century and popularised by Christian Wolff. In the 20th century, W. V. O. Quine made ontological commitment — measured by what a theory's quantifiers range over — central to analytic philosophy, while Heidegger recast ontology as the question of the meaning of Being itself.

Key debates

Ontologists dispute whether universals exist independently (realism) or only as names (nominalism); whether possible but non-actual objects have being; whether material objects persist through change as the same objects; and whether social entities such as money and nations are real. Meta-ontology asks whether these questions have substantive answers at all or are merely verbal disputes.

Applied ontology

In computer science and information science, an "ontology" is a formal specification of the concepts and relationships in a domain — used in the Semantic Web, biomedical databases such as the Gene Ontology, and knowledge graphs.
