The British Motor Corporation (BMC) was a British vehicle manufacturer formed in 1952 by the merger of the Austin Motor Company and Morris Motors Limited. At its creation it was the largest British motor company of its day and briefly the fourth-largest car maker in the world, employing some 40,000 workers.

History

The merger brought together the rival Austin and Morris ranges, although the two dealer networks and much of the model line-up remained separate, a policy of badge engineering that defined BMC throughout its life. Leonard Lord, the company's dominant figure, famously declared that the merged firm would keep the competition "in their place". Alec Issigonis, recruited from Alvis, led the engineering of a new generation of cars.

The company's most celebrated product was the Mini, launched in 1959. Designed by Issigonis in response to the fuel shortages caused by the Suez Crisis, its transverse engine and front-wheel-drive layout created a packaging template copied across the industry. Other notable models included the Morris Minor, the MG MGB, the Austin-Healey 3000, and the ADO16 range (Austin/Morris 1100 and 1300), which was Britain's best-selling car for much of the 1960s.

Decline and merger

Despite strong engineering, BMC suffered from an over-complex range, duplicated models, weak cost control and labour disputes. In 1966 it merged with Jaguar Cars to form British Motor Holdings, and in 1968 that group combined with Leyland Motors to create the British Leyland Motor Corporation.
