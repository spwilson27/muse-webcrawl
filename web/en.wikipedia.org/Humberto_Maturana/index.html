Humberto Maturana Romesín (14 September 1928 – 6 May 2021) was a Chilean biologist and philosopher. Together with his student Francisco Varela he developed the concept of autopoiesis — the self-producing organization that defines living systems — and a theory of cognition in which perceiving and knowing are the effective actions of the organism rather than representations of an external world.

Life and work

Maturana studied medicine at the University of Chile and biology at University College London and Harvard, receiving his PhD in 1958. His early neurophysiological work included the influential 1959 paper "What the Frog's Eye Tells the Frog's Brain," written with Jerome Lettvin and others at MIT. Returning to Chile, he built a laboratory at the University of Chile and turned increasingly to theoretical questions about the organization of living beings and the biology of perception.

Autopoiesis and cognition

In Autopoiesis and Cognition (1980) and The Tree of Knowledge (1987, with Varela), Maturana argued that living systems are autonomous, self-referential networks of processes that continually regenerate themselves, and that the nervous system is operationally closed: it does not take in information about the world but undergoes structural changes triggered by interactions. His dictum "everything said is said by an observer" expresses his radical constructivist epistemology, which influenced systems theory, cybernetics, family therapy, and enactive approaches to cognitive science.

Later life

Maturana received Chile's National Prize for Natural Sciences in 1994 and co-founded the Matríztica institute in Santiago, applying his biology of love and language to education and organizations. He died in Santiago in 2021.
