African American culture

African American culture, also known as Black American culture, refers to the contributions of African Americans to the culture of the United States, either as part of or distinct from mainstream American culture. It developed from the experiences of enslaved Africans and their descendants, blending West African traditions with European and Native American influences, and has profoundly shaped American music, language, religion, food, and art.

History

Enslaved Africans from diverse ethnic groups preserved elements of their heritage — oral traditions, call-and-response patterns, rhythmic structures, and spiritual beliefs — under conditions of oppression. After emancipation, institutions such as the Black church, historically Black colleges and universities (HBCUs), and Black-owned businesses sustained and developed the culture through the Jim Crow era and the Great Migration.

Music

African American music is arguably the culture's most influential export: spirituals, gospel, blues, ragtime, jazz, rhythm and blues, soul, funk, and hip hop all originated in Black communities. Hip hop, born in the Bronx in the 1970s, became a global cultural movement encompassing rap, DJing, breakdancing, and graffiti art.

Language and literature

African American Vernacular English (AAVE) is a distinct dialect with its own grammar, rooted in the languages of West Africa and the rural South. The literary tradition runs from slave narratives by Frederick Douglass and Harriet Jacobs through the Harlem Renaissance (Langston Hughes, Zora Neale Hurston), to James Baldwin, Toni Morrison, and Maya Angelou.

Religion and family

The Black church has been a centre of community, education, and political organisation since slavery. Extended family networks, "fictive kin," and traditions such as Watch Night services, Juneteenth celebrations, and historically Black fraternities and sororities remain central cultural institutions.
