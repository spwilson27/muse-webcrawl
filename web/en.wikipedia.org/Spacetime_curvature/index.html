Spacetime curvature
TheoryGeneral relativity (Einstein, 1915)

Spacetime curvature is the central idea of Einstein's theory of general relativity: gravity is not a force acting in space but the effect of mass and energy bending the four-dimensional geometry of spacetime itself. Objects under no other force follow geodesics — the straightest possible paths through this curved geometry — which appear to us as curved orbits. John Archibald Wheeler summarised it: "Spacetime tells matter how to move; matter tells spacetime how to curve."

Mathematical description

Curvature is encoded in the Riemann curvature tensor, built from the spacetime metric. The Einstein field equations relate the Einstein tensor (a measure of curvature) to the stress–energy tensor of matter and radiation: Gμν + Λgμν = (8πG/c4) Tμν. Solutions include the Schwarzschild geometry around a spherical mass, the Kerr geometry of rotating black holes, and the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric for the expanding universe.

Evidence

Curvature explains Mercury's anomalous perihelion precession (43 arcseconds per century) and the bending of starlight near the Sun, confirmed by Eddington's 1919 eclipse expedition. It predicts gravitational redshift, time dilation (measured daily by GPS clocks), gravitational lenses, frame-dragging (measured by Gravity Probe B) and gravitational waves, directly detected by LIGO in 2015. Extreme curvature produces black holes, where the curvature at the central singularity becomes infinite — a signal that the classical theory breaks down.

Intuition and limits

The popular rubber-sheet analogy captures geodesics qualitatively but misleads: most of the curvature felt on Earth is in the time direction. In the weak-field, slow-motion limit, general relativity reduces to Newton's law of gravitation.
