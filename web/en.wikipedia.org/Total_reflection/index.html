Total internal reflection

Light totally internally reflected inside a glass block

Total internal reflection (TIR) is an optical phenomenon in which a wave striking the boundary between two media at a sufficiently oblique angle is completely reflected back into the first medium instead of being refracted into the second. It occurs when the wave travels from a medium with a higher refractive index to one with a lower index, and the angle of incidence exceeds the critical angle.

Explanation

By Snell's law, light passing from a denser to a rarer medium bends away from the normal. As the incident angle increases, the refracted angle approaches 90 degrees; at the critical angle the refracted ray skims along the surface, and beyond it no refracted ray exists — all the energy is reflected. An evanescent wave still penetrates a short distance into the second medium, a fact exploited in techniques such as attenuated total reflectance spectroscopy.

Applications

Total internal reflection is the operating principle of optical fibres, which guide light over long distances with low loss and form the backbone of modern telecommunications. It is also used in binoculars and prism systems, fingerprint scanners, rain sensors, and explains natural effects such as the sparkle of diamonds, whose high refractive index gives a small critical angle, and the reflectivity of a fish's view of the water surface (Snell's window).
