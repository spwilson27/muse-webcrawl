Abyssal plain

An abyssal plain is an underwater plain on the deep ocean floor, usually found at depths between 3,000 and 6,000 metres (9,800 and 19,700 ft). Lying generally between the foot of a continental rise and a mid-ocean ridge, abyssal plains cover more than 50% of the Earth's surface. They are among the flattest, smoothest, and least explored regions on Earth.

Abyssal plains result from the blanketing of an originally uneven surface of oceanic crust by fine-grained sediments, mainly clay and silt. Much of this sediment is deposited by turbidity currents that flow down from the continental margins, filling in topographic lows. Between turbidity events, fine pelagic sediment rains down slowly from the surface waters.

Formation and features

The flat surface of an abyssal plain is interrupted in places by abyssal hills, seamounts, and guyots that rise above the sediment cover. Because the Pacific Ocean has fewer large rivers delivering sediment and more deep-sea trenches that trap it, Pacific abyssal plains are less extensive and less smooth than those of the Atlantic and Indian oceans, where thick sediment deposits from rivers such as the Ganges have built enormous plains and deep-sea fans.

Life and resources

Although once thought to be nearly lifeless deserts, abyssal plains support a diverse community of organisms adapted to darkness, near-freezing temperatures and high pressure, sustained largely by marine snow falling from above. Abyssal plains are also of interest for their polymetallic (manganese) nodules, potato-sized concretions rich in manganese, nickel, copper and cobalt scattered over the sediment surface, and as potential sites for sub-seabed carbon storage and deep-sea mining.
