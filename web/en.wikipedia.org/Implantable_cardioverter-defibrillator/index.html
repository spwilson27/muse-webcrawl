Implantable cardioverter-defibrillator

An implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) is a small battery-powered device placed in the body of patients at risk of sudden cardiac death from dangerous heart rhythm disturbances. Like a pacemaker, it is implanted under the skin, usually below the collarbone, but unlike a simple pacemaker it can deliver an electric shock to restore a normal rhythm.

How it works

Thin wires called leads pass through a vein into the heart chambers. The device continuously monitors the heart rhythm; if it detects ventricular fibrillation or a very rapid ventricular tachycardia, it delivers a defibrillating shock. Many ICDs also pace the heart if it beats too slowly.

Indications

ICDs are recommended for survivors of cardiac arrest, patients with severely reduced ejection fraction after a heart attack or from cardiomyopathy, and people with certain inherited arrhythmia syndromes.

Risks and follow-up

Risks include infection, lead displacement, and inappropriate shocks. Patients attend regular follow-up checks, and modern devices can transmit data remotely to the care team.
