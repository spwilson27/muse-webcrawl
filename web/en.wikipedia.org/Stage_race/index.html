Stage race

A stage race is a multi-day competition, most commonly in road bicycle racing, in which competitors race a series of individual races ("stages") on consecutive days, and the overall winner is the rider with the lowest cumulative time. Stage races are among the most prestigious events in professional cycling; the three-week "Grand Tours" — the Tour de France, Giro d'Italia and Vuelta a España — are the most famous.

Format

A stage race typically combines flat mass-start road stages, mountain stages, and one or more time trials (individual or team). Distances vary from two or three days for short stage races to 21 stages over 23 days for a Grand Tour. Riders compete both for individual stage victories and for the overall "general classification" (GC). Time bonuses may be awarded at stage finishes and intermediate sprints.

Classifications and jerseys

Besides the general classification — whose leader wears a distinctive jersey such as the Tour de France's maillot jaune — most stage races award points classifications for sprinters, mountains classifications for climbers, young rider classifications, and a team classification based on the combined times of a squad's best riders.

History

The first stage races grew out of the promotional efforts of French newspapers in the early twentieth century; the Tour de France was founded in 1903 by L'Auto to boost its circulation. Races such as Paris–Nice (1933) and the Critérium du Dauphiné became key preparation events for the Grand Tours.

Other sports

The term is also used for multi-day events in mountain biking, rallying (where the component races are "special stages"), and in some multi-day running events.
