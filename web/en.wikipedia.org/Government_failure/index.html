Government failure

Government failure, in public economics, is an economic inefficiency caused by government intervention in an economy, in parallel to the concept of market failure. The term describes situations in which policies intended to correct market failures—or to achieve other public goals—instead create new distortions whose costs exceed any benefits.

Causes

Public choice theory, associated with economists such as James Buchanan and Gordon Tullock, explains government failure through the incentives facing political actors:

  • Information problems: officials may lack the knowledge needed to design effective interventions, a difficulty highlighted by Friedrich Hayek.
  • Rent-seeking: firms and interest groups invest resources in lobbying for subsidies, tariffs, or regulations that benefit them at public expense.
  • Regulatory capture: agencies may come to serve the industries they regulate rather than the public.
  • Short electoral horizons: politicians may favour policies with immediate visible benefits and deferred costs, financed through government debt.
  • Bureaucratic incentives: agencies without competitors or profit signals may expand budgets and resist reform.

Examples

Commonly cited examples include price controls that create shortages, subsidies that keep unviable producers in business, poorly designed licensing regimes that restrict competition, and large public projects that run far over budget. Economists such as Charles Wolf systematized the analysis of "nonmarket failure" in the 1970s and 1980s.

Relationship to market failure

The existence of government failure does not by itself settle policy questions: the relevant comparison is between imperfect markets and imperfect governments in each specific case. Analysts therefore weigh the expected costs of intervention against the costs of leaving a market failure uncorrected.
