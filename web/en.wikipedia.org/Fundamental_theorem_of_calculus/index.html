Fundamental theorem of calculus

The fundamental theorem of calculus is a theorem that links the concept of differentiating a function with the concept of integrating a function. It establishes that differentiation and definite integration are, in a precise sense, inverse operations of one another.

First part

The first part of the theorem states that if f is a continuous real-valued function on [a, b], and F is defined by F(x) = ∫ₐˣ f(t) dt, then F is differentiable on (a, b) and F′(x) = f(x). Thus the accumulation function of f is an antiderivative of f.

Second part

The second part (the Newton–Leibniz theorem) states that if F is any antiderivative of a continuous function f on [a, b], then ∫ₐᵇ f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a). This gives a practical method for evaluating definite integrals.

Intuition

Informally, the theorem says that the total change in a quantity equals the integral of its rate of change — just as the distance travelled equals the integral of speed over time.

History

Elements of the theorem appear in the work of James Gregory and Isaac Barrow in the 17th century; Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz developed it as the cornerstone of their calculus. Rigorous proofs awaited the 19th-century formalisation of the integral by Cauchy and Riemann.

Generalisations

The theorem generalises to the Lebesgue integral, to line and surface integrals (the gradient theorem, Stokes' theorem, and the divergence theorem), and to calculus on manifolds.
