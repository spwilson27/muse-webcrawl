Pope John XXIII (Latin: Ioannes XXIII; born Angelo Giuseppe Roncalli, 25 November 1881 – 3 June 1963) was head of the Catholic Church and sovereign of the Vatican City State from 1958 until his death. Affectionately known as the "Good Pope", he is best remembered for convening the Second Vatican Council, which modernised the Church. He was canonised in 2014.

Early life and career

Born to a family of sharecroppers in Lombardy, Roncalli was ordained in 1904, served as an army chaplain and medical orderly in the First World War, and entered the Vatican diplomatic service. As apostolic delegate in Bulgaria and then in Turkey and Greece, he aided refugees and — during the Second World War — helped save thousands of Jews through transit visas and baptismal certificates. He became nuncio to France in 1944 and Patriarch of Venice in 1953.

Papacy

Elected at 76, John was widely expected to be a transitional pope. Instead, in January 1959 he announced an ecumenical council — the Second Vatican Council — which opened in October 1962 and set the Church on a path of aggiornamento (updating), liturgical reform and dialogue with other Christians, other religions and the modern world. His encyclicals included Mater et magistra (1961) and Pacem in terris (1963), addressed "to all men of good will" amid Cold War tensions; he also quietly mediated during the Cuban Missile Crisis. He improved relations with the Jewish community, removing the phrase "perfidious Jews" from the Good Friday liturgy.

Death and canonisation

John died of stomach cancer on 3 June 1963, during the council, which his successor Paul VI completed. He was beatified by John Paul II in 2000 and canonised by Pope Francis on 27 April 2014, together with John Paul II. His feast day, 11 October, marks the opening of Vatican II.
