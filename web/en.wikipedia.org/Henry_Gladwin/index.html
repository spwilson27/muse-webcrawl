Henry Gladwin (c. 1729/30 – 22 June 1791) was a British Army officer best known for commanding Fort Detroit during its siege by the Odawa chief Pontiac in 1763, the best-known episode of Pontiac's War. His successful defence of the post against a much larger Indigenous alliance made him a celebrated figure in Britain and North America.

Early career

Little is recorded of Gladwin's early life. He entered the army and served in North America during the French and Indian War, was wounded at the Battle of the Monongahela (Braddock's defeat) in 1755, and by 1760 held the rank of major. After the British takeover of the French posts in the Great Lakes, he was appointed commandant of Detroit in 1762.

Siege of Detroit, 1763

In the spring of 1763 Pontiac, angered by British policies after the conquest of New France, organised an attack on Detroit. According to the famous — and possibly embellished — account, Gladwin was warned of Pontiac's plan to surprise the fort during a council by an Indigenous woman, sometimes identified as Catherine, who had noticed the warriors' shortened muskets hidden under their blankets. When Pontiac entered the fort on 7 May 1763 he found the garrison armed and waiting, and withdrew. He then besieged the fort for months. Gladwin, with only about 120 soldiers plus armed traders, held out through the summer, repelled Pontiac's attacks, survived the ambush of a relief force at the Battle of Bloody Run (31 July 1763), and kept the post supplied by armed vessels on the Detroit River until reinforcements and the approach of winter led Pontiac to abandon the siege in October 1763.

Later life

Gladwin was promoted lieutenant-colonel and later, after returning to England, rose to the rank of major-general. He retired to Stubton Hall in Lincolnshire, where he died in 1791. Gladwin County, Michigan, and the city of Gladwin within it, are named in his honour, and the defence of Detroit became a staple of early American frontier literature.
