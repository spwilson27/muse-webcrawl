Sumerian (native name: eme-gir15, "native tongue") is the language of ancient Sumer, spoken in southern Mesopotamia (modern-day southern Iraq) from at least the 4th millennium BCE. It is the earliest language known to have been written down, preserved in the cuneiform script on clay tablets. Sumerian is a language isolate — no relationship with any other known language has been demonstrated despite many attempts.

History

Sumerian was progressively replaced as a spoken language by Akkadian, a Semitic language, around 2000 BCE, but it continued to be used as a sacred, ceremonial, and scholarly language in Mesopotamia for nearly two thousand more years, much as Latin was used in medieval Europe. The last dated cuneiform texts are from the 1st century CE.

Grammar

Sumerian is an agglutinative, ergative–absolutive language with subject–object–verb word order. Verbs are built from long chains of prefixes and suffixes marking mood, aspect, and agreement with subject and object, while nouns take case suffixes such as ergative -e, genitive -ak, and dative -ra. Nouns belong to two genders, human (animate) and non-human, which govern pronoun and agreement choices.

Corpus and decipherment

The Sumerian corpus includes royal inscriptions, laws, administrative and economic records (which form the great majority of tablets), hymns, myths such as the Sumerian flood story, proverbs, and lexical lists used by scribal schools (edubba). Sumerian was recovered in the 19th century once Akkadian cuneiform had been deciphered, largely through bilingual tablets and ancient dictionaries compiled by Babylonian scribes.
