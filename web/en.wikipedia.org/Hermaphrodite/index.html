Hermaphrodite

A hermaphrodite is a sexually reproducing organism that produces both male and female gametes. Animal species in which individuals are of different sexes, either male or female but not both, are gonochoric, which is the opposite of hermaphroditic. The individuals of many taxonomic groups of animals, primarily invertebrates, are hermaphrodites, capable of producing both sperm and ova. In the great majority of tunicates, mollusks, and earthworms, hermaphroditism is a normal condition, enabling a form of sexual reproduction in which either partner can act as the female or male.

Terminology

The term derives from the Latin hermaphroditus, from the Ancient Greek Hermaphroditos, the son of Hermes and Aphrodite in Greek mythology who, according to Ovid, fused with the nymph Salmacis, resulting in one individual possessing physical traits of both sexes. Historically, the term was also used for humans with ambiguous sexual characteristics, but this usage has fallen out of favor in medicine, where the term intersex is used for humans; true hermaphroditism in the biological sense does not occur in mammals in the way it does in many invertebrates.

Simultaneous hermaphroditism

Simultaneous hermaphrodites possess both male and female reproductive organs at the same time. Earthworms are a classic example: although an individual produces both sperm and eggs, it cannot fertilize itself and must exchange sperm with a partner during mating. Many snails and slugs are simultaneous hermaphrodites, and some species engage in elaborate courtship behaviors, such as the shooting of "love darts" in land snails, before reciprocal insemination.

Sequential hermaphroditism

Sequential hermaphrodites begin life as one sex and change to the other later in life. When an individual is male first and then becomes female, this is called protandry, as seen in clownfish, where the dominant individual of a group is female and the largest male becomes female when she dies. When an individual is female first and then becomes male, this is protogyny, common in wrasses and groupers, where large dominant individuals are male.

In plants

Most flowering plants are hermaphroditic in the sense that a single flower produces both pollen and ovules; such plants are often described as having "perfect" flowers. Many plants have mechanisms, such as self-incompatibility or separation of male and female phases in time (dichogamy), that promote cross-pollination and reduce self-fertilization.

Evolutionary considerations

Hermaphroditism is thought to be advantageous when mates are scarce or mobility is low, since any encounter between two individuals can lead to reproduction. The size-advantage model explains sequential sex change: if reproductive success increases with size more steeply for one sex than the other, selection favors changing sex at the size where the benefit switches.
