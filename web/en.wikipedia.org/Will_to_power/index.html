Will to power

The will to power (German: der Wille zur Macht) is a concept in the philosophy of Friedrich Nietzsche. Nietzsche asserted that the will to power is the main driving force in humans — stronger than the will to live or the will to pleasure — a striving not merely to survive but to grow, expand, overcome resistance, and impose form and value on the world.

Development of the concept

The idea appears in published works such as Thus Spoke Zarathustra (1883–85), Beyond Good and Evil (1886), and On the Genealogy of Morality (1887), and pervades the notebooks (the Nachlass) of his final productive years. After Nietzsche's collapse, his sister Elisabeth Förster-Nietzsche compiled selections from those notebooks into a volume published as The Will to Power (1901, expanded 1906); modern scholarship treats that book as an editorial construction rather than a work Nietzsche completed.

Interpretations

Scholars disagree widely: some read the will to power as a psychological thesis about motivation, others as a metaphysical claim that reality itself consists of centers of force striving against one another, and still others as a purely interpretive tool. Martin Heidegger treated it as the culmination of Western metaphysics, while Gilles Deleuze read it as an affirmative, creative principle. The concept — never about mere political domination in Nietzsche's own usage — has nonetheless often been misappropriated in popular culture.
