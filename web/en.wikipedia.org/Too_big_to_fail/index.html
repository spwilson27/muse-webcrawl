Too big to fail

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Headquarters of a systemically important bank during the 2008 financial crisis]

"Too big to fail" (TBTF) is a theory in banking and finance that asserts that certain corporations, particularly financial institutions, are so large and interconnected that their failure would be disastrous to the wider economy, and therefore governments must bail them out when they face potential failure. The phrase entered public discourse during the 1984 rescue of Continental Illinois and became the organising idea — and the central controversy — of government responses to the 2008 financial crisis.

History

The term was popularised by U.S. Congressman Stewart McKinney in 1984 congressional hearings on the FDIC's rescue of Continental Illinois, then the largest bank failure in U.S. history. During the 2008 crisis the doctrine shaped the rescue of Bear Stearns and AIG and the $700 billion Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP); its apparent limits were shown when Lehman Brothers was allowed to fail in September 2008, triggering a global panic that defenders of the doctrine cite as proof of its premise and critics as proof of inconsistent policy.

Criticism and reform

Critics argue that TBTF creates moral hazard: an implicit public guarantee lets giant banks borrow more cheaply and take greater risks, privatising gains and socialising losses. Post-crisis reforms — the Dodd–Frank Act in the United States, higher capital requirements and "living will" resolution plans for systemically important banks, and the Basel III framework internationally — aimed to make failure orderly rather than impossible to contemplate. Debate continues over whether the largest banks are bigger, relative to the economy, than before the crisis.
