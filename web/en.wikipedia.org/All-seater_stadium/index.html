All-seater stadium

An all-seater stadium is a sports stadium in which every spectator is provided with a seat, as opposed to older grounds that included areas of terracing where spectators stood. The term is used most commonly in the context of association football stadiums in the United Kingdom and Europe.

A modern all-seater stadium, with individual seats in every stand.

History

Standing terraces were the norm in British football grounds for most of the twentieth century, allowing large capacities at low ticket prices. A series of stadium disasters, culminating in the Hillsborough disaster of April 1989 in which 97 Liverpool supporters died, led to the Taylor Report, which recommended that all stadiums in the top divisions of English football be converted to all-seater designs. The Football Spectators Act and subsequent licensing requirements made this mandatory for the top two tiers, and most major grounds were rebuilt or renovated during the 1990s.

Effects

Conversion to all-seater stadiums generally reduced capacities but increased revenue per spectator through higher ticket prices and improved hospitality facilities. Supporters' groups have debated the effect on atmosphere, and in recent decades campaigns for "safe standing" — typically using rail seating that can be locked upright — have led several countries, including Germany (which long retained standing areas) and later England, Scotland, and Wales, to permit licensed standing accommodation once again.

Around the world

Many stadiums in North America were built as all-seater venues from the start. In continental Europe the picture is mixed: Germany's Bundesliga grounds famously include large standing terraces such as the Yellow Wall at Dortmund's Westfalenstadion, while stadiums hosting UEFA finals must be able to operate in all-seater configuration.
