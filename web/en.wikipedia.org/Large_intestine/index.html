The large intestine, also known as the large bowel, is the last part of the gastrointestinal tract and the digestive system in tetrapods. It includes the cecum, colon, rectum and anal canal. Its main functions are to absorb water and electrolytes from the liquid chyme delivered by the small intestine, to ferment some undigested material with the help of the gut microbiota, and to form, store and eliminate feces.

Structure

About 1.5 metres long, the large intestine frames the coils of the small intestine. It begins at the cecum in the right iliac fossa, from which the vermiform appendix hangs. The colon proper is divided into ascending, transverse, descending and sigmoid sections, recognisable by their sacculations (haustra) and by the three longitudinal bands of muscle called taeniae coli. The sigmoid colon leads to the rectum and the anal canal. Unlike the small intestine, the colon has no villi; its mucosa is lined with deep crypts rich in mucus-secreting goblet cells.

Function and microbiota

Roughly 1–1.5 litres of liquid enter the colon each day; all but about 100–200 mL of water is reabsorbed. The colon hosts the densest microbial community in the body — tens of trillions of bacteria — which ferment dietary fibre into short-chain fatty acids that nourish the colonic lining, and produce vitamin K and some B vitamins. Slow haustral churning mixes the contents, while mass movements a few times a day drive feces towards the rectum.

Clinical significance

Disorders of the large intestine include appendicitis, diverticular disease, the inflammatory bowel diseases ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease, irritable bowel syndrome, and colorectal cancer — among the most common cancers worldwide, and the target of screening programmes based on stool tests and colonoscopy. Surgical removal of part or all of the colon (colectomy) may be required for cancer, severe colitis or obstruction.
