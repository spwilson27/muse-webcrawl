Byzantine architecture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Byzantine architecture is the architecture of the Byzantine Empire, or East Roman Empire, which flourished from the 5th century until the fall of Constantinople in 1453. It continued earlier Roman and early Christian traditions but was profoundly shaped by the needs of the Orthodox Church and the imperial court, and is characterised above all by the development of the dome on pendentives, the use of brick and decorative mosaic, and increasingly complex centralised church plans.

Early Byzantine architecture

Early Byzantine architecture built on Roman engineering: concrete and brick construction, barrel and groin vaults, and the basilican plan adapted for Christian worship. The reign of Justinian I (527–565) produced its greatest monuments, including the Hagia Sophia in Constantinople (532–537), designed by Anthemius of Tralles and Isidore of Miletus, whose vast dome on pendentives remained the largest cathedral in the world for nearly a thousand years. Other Justinianic works include the church of San Vitale in Ravenna, famous for its mosaics of Justinian and Theodora, and the Hagia Irene in Constantinople.

Middle and Late Byzantine architecture

After the disruptions of the 7th and 8th centuries, middle Byzantine churches were typically smaller, often built on the cross-in-square plan, with a central dome raised on a drum and rich brick patterning on the exterior. Monasteries such as Hosios Loukas and Daphni in Greece preserve fine examples and celebrated mosaic programmes. Late Byzantine (Palaiologan) architecture added elaborate brickwork, multiple domes, and tall drums, as in the Chora Church in Constantinople.

Influence

Byzantine architecture strongly influenced the architecture of the Islamic world, particularly Ottoman mosque design, and spread through the Balkans and Russia, where it shaped Orthodox church building for centuries. In the West it contributed to Romanesque and, through Venice (St Mark's Basilica), to Italian medieval architecture.
