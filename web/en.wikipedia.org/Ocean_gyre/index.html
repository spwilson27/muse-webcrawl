The five major ocean gyres

An ocean gyre is a large system of circulating ocean currents, typically formed by global wind patterns and forces created by Earth's rotation (the Coriolis effect). Gyres occur in each of the major ocean basins and are central to the ocean's surface circulation, heat transport, and the accumulation of floating debris.

The five major subtropical gyres

  • North Atlantic Gyre — bounded by the Gulf Stream, North Atlantic Current, Canary Current, and North Equatorial Current; contains the Sargasso Sea.
  • South Atlantic Gyre — Brazil Current, South Atlantic Current, Benguela Current, and South Equatorial Current.
  • North Pacific Gyre — Kuroshio, North Pacific Current, California Current, and North Equatorial Current.
  • South Pacific Gyre — East Australian Current, South Pacific Current, Humboldt Current, and South Equatorial Current; its center is the oceanic pole of inaccessibility ("Point Nemo").
  • Indian Ocean Gyre — Agulhas Current, South Indian Current, West Australian Current, and South Equatorial Current.

Wind-driven Ekman transport pushes surface water toward gyre centers, creating a slight mound of water and downward flow (downwelling), which makes subtropical gyre interiors nutrient-poor "ocean deserts" with low biological productivity. Subpolar gyres, by contrast, tend toward upwelling and richer ecosystems.

Marine debris

The slowly circulating interiors of gyres collect floating plastic and other debris, most famously the Great Pacific garbage patch in the North Pacific Gyre. Similar accumulation zones exist in the other gyres, spurring research and cleanup efforts.
