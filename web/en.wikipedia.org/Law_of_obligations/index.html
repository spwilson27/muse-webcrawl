Law of obligations

The law of obligations is one of the major branches of private law, concerned with legal duties (obligations) that one person owes to another and the remedies available when those duties are breached. In civil law systems it is codified as a distinct part of the civil code, while in common law systems the same ground is covered by the separate subjects of contract, tort and unjust enrichment. The concept derives from Roman law, where an obligatio was a legal bond between a creditor and a debtor.

Divisions

Contract

Obligations arising voluntarily from agreement. A binding contract generally requires offer, acceptance, consideration (in common law) and an intention to create legal relations. Breach entitles the innocent party to damages or, in some cases, specific performance.

Delict and tort

Obligations imposed by law to compensate for wrongful acts — for example negligence causing personal injury or property damage. Civil law systems speak of delict; common law systems of torts.

Unjust enrichment

Obligations to restore a benefit unjustly obtained at another's expense, historically handled under the heading of quasi-contract.

Performance and extinction

Obligations are normally extinguished by performance, but may also end by release, set-off, novation, prescription (limitation) or impossibility. Non-performance gives rise to secondary obligations, chiefly the duty to pay damages.

Comparative law

France (Code civil, Book III), Germany (BGB, Book 2: Schuldrecht), Switzerland and many other codified systems treat obligations systematically, while the law of England and Wales reaches comparable results without a single code. Harmonisation projects such as the Principles of European Contract Law seek common ground.
