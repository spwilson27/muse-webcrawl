Dii Consentes

The Dii Consentes, also known as the Dii Complices, are a list of twelve major deities in the Roman pantheon, roughly equivalent to the Twelve Olympians of Greek religion. Their gilded statues stood in the Forum Romanum in the Porticus Deorum Consentium, and were later described by the poet Ausonius and others. The historian Livy lists them arranged in six male–female pairs.

The twelve

The six pairs given by the poet Ennius, in a fragment preserved by Apuleius, are: Juno and Vesta; Minerva and Ceres; Diana and Venus; Mars and Mercury; Neptune and Jupiter. Other arrangements order the twelve as Jupiter, Juno, Neptune, Minerva, Mars, Venus, Apollo, Diana, Vulcan, Vesta, Mercury, and Ceres.

Interpretation

The phrase consentes suggests gods who act "in agreement" or as a council. The grouping is thought to derive from Etruscan religion, which had a council of twelve gods, and to reflect the organisation of the Roman state, in which the twelve may have been imagined as a divine senate advising Jupiter. Their collective worship was centred on the Capitoline Hill and the Forum, where the Porticus Deorum Consentium was restored in the late fourth century AD, one of the last acts of public pagan restoration in Rome.

Legacy

The Roman twelve correspond closely to the Greek Olympians, with Roman names substituted: Zeus/Jupiter, Hera/Juno, Poseidon/Neptune, Demeter/Ceres, Apollo, Artemis/Diana, Ares/Mars, Aphrodite/Venus, Hephaestus/Vulcan, Athena/Minerva, Hermes/Mercury, and Hestia/Vesta (with Dionysus/Bacchus sometimes replacing Vesta).
