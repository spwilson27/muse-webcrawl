Italian Renaissance sculpture

Renaissance marble sculpture

Italian Renaissance sculpture is the sculpture produced in Italy between roughly the early 15th and late 16th centuries, forming a central part of the art of the Italian Renaissance. Drawing inspiration from classical antiquity, sculptors revived naturalism, anatomical accuracy and the free-standing nude, working principally in marble and bronze.

Early Renaissance

The conventional starting point is the 1401 competition for the bronze doors of the Florence Baptistery, contested by Lorenzo Ghiberti and Filippo Brunelleschi and won by Ghiberti, whose Gates of Paradise (1425–1452) remain a landmark. Donatello revolutionized the medium with works such as his bronze David (c. 1440s) — the first free-standing nude since antiquity — the equestrian monument to Gattamelata in Padua, and his expressive late wood sculptures. Other leading figures include Jacopo della Quercia, Luca della Robbia (known for glazed terracotta), Desiderio da Settignano and the Rossellino workshop.

High Renaissance and Michelangelo

The era culminated in Michelangelo, who considered himself first a sculptor. His Pietà (1498–99), David (1501–04), the figures of the Medici Chapel in Florence and the unfinished Slaves set a standard of monumental, dynamic anatomy that dominated the century.

Late Renaissance and Mannerism

Later sculptors such as Benvenuto Cellini, Bartolomeo Ammannati, and Giambologna developed a virtuoso, serpentine (figura serpentinata) Mannerist style, producing multi-viewpoint bronze groups and elaborate fountains across Italy and for courts beyond the Alps.
