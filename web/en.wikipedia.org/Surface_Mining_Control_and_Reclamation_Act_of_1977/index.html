Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act of 1977

The Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act of 1977 (SMCRA) is the primary federal law in the United States that regulates the environmental effects of coal mining. Signed by President Jimmy Carter on 3 August 1977 after earlier versions had been vetoed twice by President Gerald Ford, the Act created the Office of Surface Mining Reclamation and Enforcement (OSMRE) within the Department of the Interior.

Provisions

SMCRA establishes a permitting programme for surface coal mining and the surface effects of underground mining. Operators must obtain a permit before mining, post a reclamation bond sufficient to cover the cost of restoring the land if they default, and restore mined land to its approximate original contour and a condition capable of supporting its pre-mining uses. The Act sets performance standards governing topsoil handling, hydrologic balance, blasting, and the protection of streams and other environmentally sensitive areas.

Abandoned Mine Land fund

Title IV of the Act created the Abandoned Mine Land (AML) Reclamation Fund, financed by a fee on each ton of coal produced. The fund pays for the reclamation of lands mined and abandoned before 1977 — an estimated two million acres affected by unreclaimed highwalls, acid mine drainage, subsidence and dangerous openings. States and tribes with approved programmes receive grants from the fund; the fee, originally 35 cents per ton of surface-mined coal, has been repeatedly reauthorized, most recently at reduced rates under the Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act of 2021.

State primacy

Under Title V, states may assume primary regulatory authority ("primacy") if their programmes are at least as stringent as the federal standards and are approved by the Secretary of the Interior. Most major coal-producing states, including West Virginia, Kentucky, Pennsylvania and Wyoming, run their own SMCRA programmes subject to federal oversight.
