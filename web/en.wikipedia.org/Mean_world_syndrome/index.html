Mean world syndrome

Mean world syndrome is a cognitive bias wherein those who consume media (particularly television) perceive the world to be more dangerous and violent than it really is. The term was coined by communications professor George Gerbner, a pioneer in cultivation theory, who found that heavy television viewers were more likely to believe that the world is a mean and scary place, that people cannot be trusted, and that crime is more prevalent than statistics show.

Cultivation theory

Gerbner's Cultural Indicators research programme at the University of Pennsylvania compared light and heavy viewers: heavy viewers gave "television answers" that exaggerated personal risk of involvement in violence, mistrusted strangers, and estimated inflated numbers of people employed in law enforcement. Gerbner summarized: "If you grow up in a home where there is more fear in the air... you are likely to be more afraid than you would be otherwise."

Criticism and later research

Subsequent studies have debated effect sizes and whether television itself or selective exposure drives the association; cultivation research has since been extended to news media, video games, and social media. Meta-analyses generally find a small but consistent cultivation effect on fear of crime and perceived social reality.
