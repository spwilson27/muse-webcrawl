Enemy of the state

An enemy of the state is a person accused of crimes against the state, such as treason, espionage, or terrorism, or more broadly someone a government designates as a threat to its authority. The term has often been applied to political dissidents and opponents of authoritarian regimes.

Historical usage

The phrase derives from the Latin hostis publicus, "public enemy," used in the Roman Republic for enemies declared by the Senate. In the Soviet Union, the label "enemy of the people" was applied to large numbers of people during the Great Purge and other repressions. Similar designations were used in Nazi Germany and in other twentieth-century states to justify imprisonment, execution, or exile.

Legal status

In constitutional democracies, the category has no general legal standing outside specific offences such as treason or sedition; critics note that the label is readily turned against journalists, protesters, and opposition figures. Human rights organizations monitor its use as an indicator of political repression.
