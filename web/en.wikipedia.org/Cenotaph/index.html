Cenotaph
From Greek kenos (empty) + taphos (tomb): an empty tomb or monument honouring a person or group buried elsewhere.

A cenotaph is an empty tomb or a monument erected in honour of a person or group of people whose remains are elsewhere, or whose remains cannot be recovered. The word derives from the Greek kenotaphion, "empty tomb".

War memorials

The most famous cenotaph is the Cenotaph in Whitehall, London, designed by Edwin Lutyens and unveiled in 1920. A temporary wood-and-plaster version was built for the 1919 peace parade; public demand led to a permanent structure in Portland stone, inscribed "The Glorious Dead". It is the focus of the United Kingdom's National Service of Remembrance each Remembrance Sunday. Lutyens designed many other cenotaphs, including those in Manchester and Southampton, and the form was adopted across the Commonwealth.

Other cenotaphs

Cenotaphs also commemorate individuals: the tomb of Dante in Florence is a cenotaph because his remains are in Ravenna; statues and monuments mark figures from Shakespeare (buried in Stratford) to astronauts whose remains were lost. In Hiroshima, the Memorial Cenotaph in Peace Memorial Park holds the names of victims of the atomic bombing. In Hong Kong, India, Canada, Australia, and elsewhere, cenotaphs anchor annual remembrance ceremonies.
