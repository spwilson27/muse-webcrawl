Stress (psychology)

In psychology, stress is a feeling of emotional strain and pressure. It is a psychological and biological response experienced on encountering a threat that we feel we do not have the resources to deal with. A stressor is any stimulus — such as a major life event, work pressure, illness, or environmental demand — that causes the body and mind to be stressed.

Biology

The stress response is coordinated by the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis and the sympathetic nervous system, releasing hormones including adrenaline and cortisol. In the short term these changes support the "fight or flight" response and can be adaptive; chronic activation, however, contributes to wear and tear on the body known as allostatic load. Hans Selye's general adaptation syndrome described the response in stages of alarm, resistance, and exhaustion.

Types and measurement

Positive stress that motivates performance is sometimes called eustress, while harmful stress is distress. Common sources measured by life-event scales such as the Holmes and Rahe stress scale include bereavement, divorce, job loss, and financial problems. Psychologists distinguish acute stress, episodic acute stress, and chronic stress, and study coping strategies such as problem-focused and emotion-focused coping, following the transactional model of Richard Lazarus, which emphasises appraisal of demands and resources.

Health effects

Chronic stress is associated with cardiovascular disease, weakened immune function, depression, anxiety disorders, and other conditions. Stress management techniques include exercise, relaxation training, cognitive behavioural therapy, mindfulness, and social support.
