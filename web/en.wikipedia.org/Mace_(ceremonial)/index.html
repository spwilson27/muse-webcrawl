Mace (ceremonial)

A ceremonial mace is a highly ornamented staff or club, typically made of metal and often carried before a sovereign, a presiding officer, or another high official as a symbol of authority. Unlike the mace used as a medieval weapon, the ceremonial mace is a symbolic object, designed to embody the power and jurisdiction of the institution or person it represents.

History

The mace originated as a weapon of war in the medieval period. Over time, particularly in England, it evolved from a practical arm into a mark of royal authority. Sergeants-at-arms carried maces on behalf of the monarch, and these gradually became decorated with crowns, coats of arms, and precious metals. By the early modern period the form had inverted in many traditions: the heavy striking head shrank while the butt end, once used to display a seal or arms, grew large and ornate.

Use in legislatures

Ceremonial maces are used in many parliaments descended from the Westminster tradition, including those of the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, and numerous Commonwealth legislatures. In such chambers the mace usually rests on the table while the house is in session, indicating that the sitting is properly constituted under the authority it represents. In the United States House of Representatives, the Mace of the House, made of ebony and silver and dating to 1842, is placed on a pedestal whenever the House is in session and is presented by the Sergeant at Arms to restore order when members become unruly.

Universities

Many universities possess ceremonial maces carried in academic processions at graduations and other formal occasions. These often commemorate the institution's founding and incorporate its symbols, seals, and heraldry.
