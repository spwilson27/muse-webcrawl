Printing press

Printing press
Inventor (movable type, Europe)Johannes Gutenberg
Datec. 1440
PredecessorsWoodblock printing; movable type in China and Korea

A printing press is a mechanical device for applying pressure to an inked surface resting upon a print medium, thereby transferring the ink. It marked a dramatic improvement on earlier methods of printing and spread rapidly, revolutionising the dissemination of information in Europe and, later, the world.

History

Woodblock printing originated in China, where the oldest dated printed book is the Diamond Sutra of 868 CE. Movable type was invented there by Bi Sheng around the 1040s using ceramic pieces, and metal type was in use in Korea by the 13th century (the Jikji, 1377, is the oldest extant book printed with movable metal type). Independently, around 1440 the German goldsmith Johannes Gutenberg developed a system in Mainz combining durable metal movable type, oil-based ink, paper and a screw press adapted from wine and paper making. His first major product was the 42-line Bible (c. 1455).

Impact

Within decades printing shops operated in hundreds of European towns; by 1500 presses had produced millions of books (the incunabula), more than all previous centuries combined. Cheap print fuelled the Renaissance, the Reformation, the Scientific Revolution and the growth of vernacular literatures and newspapers. Later innovations included the iron hand press, the steam-powered press (1814, used for The Times), the rotary press, and offset printing in the 20th century, before digital printing largely displaced letterpress from the 1980s onward.
