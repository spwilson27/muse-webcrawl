Pythagoras of Samos (Greek: Πυθαγόρας; c. 570 – c. 495 BC) was an ancient Ionian Greek philosopher, polymath, and the eponymous founder of Pythagoreanism. His political and religious teachings were well known in Magna Graecia and influenced the philosophies of Plato, Aristotle, and, through them, the West in general. Pythagoras is best known today for the Pythagorean theorem in mathematics, though whether he personally proved it is disputed, since the result was known earlier in Babylon and India.

Life

Reliable details of Pythagoras's life are scarce; the fullest accounts — by Diogenes Laërtius, Porphyry, and Iamblichus — were written centuries later and mix history with legend. He was born on the island of Samos, son of Mnesarchus, a gem-engraver or merchant. Tradition says he travelled widely in his youth, studying in Egypt and Babylon. Around 530 BC he emigrated to Croton in southern Italy, where he founded a school or religious community whose members followed strict rules of diet, dress, and silence, believed in the transmigration of souls (metempsychosis), and held certain foods — most famously beans — to be forbidden. The Pythagoreans became politically powerful in Croton and other cities, provoking a violent backlash; Pythagoras fled to Metapontum, where he died.

Teachings

Pythagoras wrote nothing, so his own doctrines are hard to separate from those of his followers. Core Pythagorean beliefs included: the soul is immortal and passes through cycles of rebirth; kinship exists between all living beings, implying vegetarianism; and, most consequentially, that number is the principle of all things. The Pythagoreans studied the ratios of musical harmony — discovering that consonant intervals correspond to simple whole-number ratios such as the octave (2:1), fifth (3:2), and fourth (4:3) — and extended number symbolism throughout nature, developing the doctrine of the "harmony of the spheres" (musica universalis).

Mathematics

The theorem bearing his name states that in a right-angled triangle, the square of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the other two sides (a² + b² = c²). Pythagorean tradition also grappled with irrational numbers: the discovery that √2 cannot be expressed as a ratio of integers, attributed to Hippasus, was said to have shaken the school's faith that all is number. The Pythagoreans classified numbers (odd/even, prime, perfect, triangular, square), studied the tetraktys (1+2+3+4=10), and are credited with early work on amicable numbers and geometric constructions. In astronomy, Pythagoreans such as Philolaus proposed a cosmos with the Earth in motion around a "central fire", an early step away from geocentrism.

Legacy

Pythagorean ideas flowed into Plato's Timaeus and the Platonic tradition, into Neopythagoreanism and Neoplatonism in late antiquity, and from there into Renaissance thought; Copernicus and Kepler cited Pythagorean harmony as inspiration. Pythagoras remains a figure poised between mathematics and mysticism — honoured both as a founder of deductive science and as a sage of legend.
