Bryce Canyon National Park

Bryce Canyon National Park is a national park of the United States located in southwestern Utah. Its major feature is Bryce Canyon, which despite its name is not a canyon but a collection of giant natural amphitheaters along the eastern side of the Paunsaugunt Plateau, famous for its hoodoos — tall, thin spires of rock eroded from the Claron Formation.

Geology

The amphitheaters expose sedimentary rock deposited in an ancient lake system between about 60 and 40 million years ago. Iron oxides colour the strata in shades of red, orange and white. At elevations of 2,400 to 2,700 metres, frost wedging — water freezing and expanding in cracks for some 200 nights a year — erodes the plateau edge and sculpts the hoodoos, fins, arches and mazes of the canyon.

History

Southern Paiute people inhabited the region for centuries. Mormon settlers arrived in the 1870s; Ebenezer Bryce, a homesteader, gave the canyon his name, remarking it was "a hell of a place to lose a cow". President Warren G. Harding proclaimed Bryce Canyon a national monument in 1923, and Congress redesignated it a national park in 1928.

Flora and fauna

The park's elevation gradient supports ponderosa pine forest on the rim, Douglas fir and white fir at mid-elevations, and pinyon-juniper woodland below. Wildlife includes mule deer, pronghorn, prairie dogs, the California condor and the threatened Utah prairie dog. The park is also noted for exceptionally dark night skies.

Recreation

Visitors drive the 29-km rim road to viewpoints such as Sunrise, Sunset, Inspiration and Bryce Points, and hike trails including the Navajo Loop and Queen's Garden. The park receives more than two million visitors annually.
