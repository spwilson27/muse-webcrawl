Peter Stuyvesant

Peter Stuyvesant (Dutch: Pieter Stuyvesant; c. 1592 – August 1672) was the last Dutch director-general of the colony of New Netherland, serving from 1647 until it was provisionally ceded to the English in 1664. He was a major figure in the early history of New York City, and his name is preserved in streets, neighbourhoods, schools and towns across the region.

Early life and career

Stuyvesant was born in Friesland, the son of a Reformed minister. He joined the Dutch West India Company and served in the Caribbean, becoming director of the colonies of Curaçao, Aruba and Bonaire. In 1644, during an attack on the Spanish fort at Saint Martin, his right leg was shattered by a cannonball and amputated below the knee; he thereafter wore a wooden leg ornamented with silver, earning the nicknames "Peg Leg Pete" and "Silver Leg".

Director-general of New Netherland

Arriving in New Amsterdam in 1647, Stuyvesant governed autocratically but energetically: he regulated taverns and trade, reorganised the council, built a defensive wall along what is now Wall Street, and extended Dutch authority — conquering New Sweden on the Delaware River in 1655. His relations with the colonists were frequently strained, and his religious intolerance — against Lutherans, Quakers, Catholics and Jews — drew rebukes from the Company; his attempt to expel Jewish refugees from Brazil in 1654 was overruled, an episode linked to the Flushing Remonstrance of 1657.

Surrender and later life

In 1664 an English expedition under Richard Nicolls demanded the colony's surrender. Stuyvesant wished to resist, but leading citizens, noting the colony's weak defences, pressed him to capitulate; the Articles of Capitulation guaranteed the Dutch their property and religious practice. Stuyvesant defended his conduct in Amsterdam, then returned to spend his last years on his farm, the Great Bouwerie, in Manhattan. He died in 1672 and was buried in St. Mark's Church in-the-Bowery, built on his family chapel's site.

Legacy

Stuyvesant's name survives in Stuyvesant Town, Bedford–Stuyvesant, Stuyvesant High School and Stuyvesant Square in New York, and the town of Stuyvesant in Columbia County. His legacy is debated: an able administrator of a diverse colony, but also an authoritarian ruler responsible for policies of religious persecution and for conflict with Native American peoples.
