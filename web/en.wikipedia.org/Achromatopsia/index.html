Achromatopsia

Medical information
Specialty:Ophthalmology, neurology
Symptoms:Colour blindness (total), photophobia, nystagmus, reduced visual acuity
Frequency:~1 in 30,000 (complete form)

Achromatopsia, also known as rod monochromacy, is a medical syndrome that exhibits symptoms relating to five conditions: photophobia, colour blindness, nystagmus, and hemeralopia. Achromatopsia is characterised by a partial or total absence of colour vision. People with complete achromatopsia cannot perceive any colours; they see only black, white, and shades of grey. Incomplete achromatopsia is a milder form of the condition that allows some colour discrimination.

Causes

Congenital achromatopsia is usually inherited in an autosomal recessive manner and results from mutations in genes encoding components of the cone phototransduction cascade, including CNGA3, CNGB3, GNAT2, PDE6C, PDE6H and ATF6. Because the cone photoreceptors do not function, affected individuals rely on rod vision, which explains the reduced acuity, extreme light sensitivity, and involuntary eye movements. The condition is notably common on the Micronesian island of Pingelap, where a founder effect following a typhoon in the 18th century left a large fraction of the population affected — a case documented in Oliver Sacks' book The Island of the Colorblind.

Acquired achromatopsia

Acquired, or cerebral, achromatopsia results from damage to the ventral occipitotemporal cortex (particularly area V4) from stroke, trauma, or other neurological injury. Unlike the congenital form, colour perception is lost while form vision and acuity may remain largely normal, because the retina and cones are intact but the cortical processing of colour is disrupted.

Management

There is no cure, but symptoms can be managed with dark or red-tinted lenses to reduce photophobia, low-vision aids, and tinted contacts. Gene therapy trials targeting CNGA3 and CNGB3 mutations have been investigated in early clinical studies.
