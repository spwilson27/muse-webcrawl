Steady-state economy

Field: Ecological economics
Key figures: Herman Daly, John Stuart Mill
Related concepts: Degrowth, limits to growth

A steady-state economy is an economy made up of a constant stock of physical wealth (capital) and a constant population size. In effect, such an economy does not grow in the course of time. The term usually refers to the national economy of a particular country, but it is also applicable to the economy of a city, a region, or the whole world.

Concept

The idea has roots in classical economics: John Stuart Mill wrote favourably of the "stationary state" in his Principles of Political Economy (1848), seeing in it the possibility of improved human welfare without material growth. In the 20th century the concept was developed by ecological economist Herman Daly, who defined the steady state by constant stocks of people and artefacts, maintained by the lowest feasible flows of matter and energy from depletion of resources to pollution.

Policy proposals

Proposed institutions for a steady-state economy include stabilising population, limiting resource throughput with caps and quotas, income redistribution to guarantee a minimum and maximum income, and shifting taxation onto resource use. The concept is closely related to, but distinct from, degrowth, which concerns a planned reduction of throughput in wealthy economies.
