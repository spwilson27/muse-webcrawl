The Captive Mind


First English-language edition (1953).

The Captive Mind (Polish: Zniewolony umysł) is a 1953 work of non-fiction by the Polish writer and Nobel laureate Czesław Miłosz. Written in exile in Paris after Miłosz defected from the Polish People's Republic, where he had served as a diplomat, the book analyzes how intellectuals in the Soviet bloc came to accept, collaborate with, or resist Stalinist ideology. It was published in English in 1953 and became one of the classic works of Cold War literature.

Contents

Miłosz explains the appeal of the communist "New Faith" to writers and thinkers in Eastern Europe after the devastation of the Second World War, using concepts he coined or popularized. The most famous is the "Pill of Murti-Bing", borrowed from Stanisław Ignacy Witkiewicz's novel Insatiability, describing the intellectual's resort to ideological doublethink to resolve internal contradictions. Another is "Ketman", a practice (named after a concept in Islamic tradition) of outward conformity concealing private dissent, which Miłosz classifies into several varieties. The second half of the book presents four portraits of Polish writers, disguised by Greek letters — Alpha, Beta, Gamma, and Delta — who submitted to or were destroyed by the regime in different ways; their identities (Jerzy Andrzejewski, Tadeusz Borowski, Jerzy Putrament, and Konstanty Ildefons Gałczyński) became widely known.

Reception

The book was published with support from the émigré journal Kultura and was smuggled into Poland, where it circulated in samizdat. It established Miłosz's reputation in the West and remains a standard reference on intellectual life under totalitarianism, frequently compared with the works of Arthur Koestler and George Orwell.
