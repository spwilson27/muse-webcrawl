Quantitative genetics is the branch of genetics concerned with phenotypes that vary continuously — such as height, weight, or milk yield — rather than falling into discrete categories. Such traits are typically influenced by many genes of small effect (polygenes) together with environmental factors, so their inheritance cannot be analysed with simple Mendelian ratios.

History

The field was founded in the aftermath of the rediscovery of Mendel's work, when a dispute arose between the Mendelians and the biometricians, who studied continuous variation statistically. In 1918 Ronald Fisher published "The Correlation Between Relatives on the Supposition of Mendelian Inheritance", showing that continuous variation was fully consistent with many Mendelian factors of small effect. Fisher, Sewall Wright and J. B. S. Haldane went on to build the mathematical theory during the modern synthesis.

Basic concepts

The phenotypic value of an individual is decomposed as P = G + E, a genotypic value plus an environmental deviation. The genotypic value is further divided into additive, dominance and interaction (epistatic) components. The proportion of phenotypic variance attributable to additive genetic variance is the narrow-sense heritability (h² = VA/VP), which predicts the response to selection through the breeder's equation R = h²S.

Methods

Quantitative geneticists estimate variance components from resemblance between relatives, selection experiments, and, in the genomic era, from genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and quantitative trait locus (QTL) mapping. Animal and plant breeders use estimated breeding values, increasingly computed with genomic selection, to improve livestock and crops.
