Electric vehicle

An electric vehicle (EV) is a vehicle that uses one or more electric motors for propulsion. Electric vehicles can be powered by a collector system from off-vehicle sources, by a self-contained battery, by solar panels, or by an electric generator. The term most commonly refers to battery electric cars, but also covers electric trains, trucks, buses, bicycles, motorcycles, boats and aircraft.

History

Electric cars were among the earliest automobiles. Robert Anderson is credited with inventing a crude electric carriage in Scotland around 1832–1839, and practical rechargeable batteries made electric cars viable in the late 19th century; by 1900, electric cars accounted for a significant share of vehicles in the United States. They declined as internal combustion engines became cheaper and gasoline infrastructure spread.

Interest revived during the oil crises of the 1970s and again in the 1990s with models such as the General Motors EV1. The launch of the Tesla Roadster in 2008 and mass-market cars such as the Nissan Leaf (2010) and Tesla Model S (2012) marked the modern era. Global sales passed ten million per year in the early 2020s, with China the largest market.

Technology

Most modern electric cars use rechargeable lithium-ion battery packs and one or more AC induction or permanent-magnet motors. Electric motors deliver high torque from standstill, are far more efficient than combustion engines, and permit regenerative braking, which recovers energy when slowing down. Range depends on battery capacity; charging may be slow (AC, hours) or fast (DC, tens of minutes).

Types

  • Battery electric vehicles (BEVs) — powered solely by batteries.
  • Plug-in hybrid electric vehicles (PHEVs) — combine a battery that can be charged from the grid with a combustion engine.
  • Hybrid electric vehicles (HEVs) — combine an engine with a small battery charged by the engine and braking.
  • Fuel cell vehicles (FCEVs) — generate electricity from hydrogen.

Environmental impact

EVs produce no tailpipe emissions, and lifecycle studies generally find them cleaner than comparable petrol or diesel cars, with the advantage growing as electricity grids decarbonise. Points of debate include emissions and environmental damage from battery mineral mining, battery recycling, and the carbon intensity of manufacturing.
