Infectious mononucleosis

Other namesGlandular fever, mono, kissing disease
CauseEpstein–Barr virus (EBV) in most cases
SymptomsFatigue, fever, sore throat, swollen lymph nodes, enlarged spleen
Usual duration2–4 weeks; fatigue may persist longer

Infectious mononucleosis (IM), also known as glandular fever, is an infectious disease most often caused by the Epstein–Barr virus (EBV), a member of the herpesvirus family. It is characterised by fever, sore throat, swollen lymph nodes, and profound fatigue, and it occurs most commonly in adolescents and young adults. Because the virus spreads through saliva, it is sometimes called the "kissing disease".

Signs and symptoms

After an incubation period of roughly four to six weeks, symptoms typically develop gradually: extreme tiredness, fever, a severe sore throat that may not respond to antibiotics, swollen glands in the neck, and in many cases an enlarged spleen and sometimes liver. A rash can appear, particularly in people given amoxicillin or ampicillin. Most symptoms resolve within two to four weeks, though fatigue can linger for months.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is suggested by the clinical picture and a blood count showing atypical lymphocytes, and it is confirmed by the heterophile antibody (Monospot) test or by specific EBV antibody tests. Cytomegalovirus and a few other infections can produce a similar mononucleosis-like syndrome.

Treatment and prognosis

There is no specific antiviral treatment; care is supportive, with rest, fluids, and analgesics. Because of the risk of splenic rupture, contact sports are usually avoided for at least the first month of illness. Most people recover fully, and the virus thereafter persists latently in B lymphocytes for life, as with other herpesviruses.
