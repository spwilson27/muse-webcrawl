Opacity (optics)

Opacity is the measure of impenetrability of a material to electromagnetic radiation, especially visible light. An opaque object allows no light to pass through it: incident light is either absorbed or reflected (or both). The opposite property is transparency (and partial transmission is translucency). Opacity depends on the frequency of the radiation, the material's composition, and its thickness and temperature.

Quantitative measures

Several related quantities describe opacity. The attenuation (extinction) coefficient measures how strongly a beam loses intensity per unit path length through absorption and scattering. Absorbance is defined logarithmically from the ratio of incident to transmitted intensity. In astrophysics, opacity (often symbol κ) expresses the resistance of stellar material to the flow of radiation and enters models of stellar structure; it is commonly expressed per unit mass and depends strongly on temperature and density.

Mechanisms

A material opaque at one wavelength may be transparent at another: glass is opaque to some ultraviolet frequencies but transparent to visible light; radio waves pass through walls that block light. Absorption occurs when photon energy matches transitions in atoms or molecules, or excites free electrons, while scattering by particles and inhomogeneities also blocks transmission.

Applications

Opacity is a key property in paint and pigment selection (hiding power), radiation shielding, atmospheric science, and the modeling of stars and planetary atmospheres. In computer graphics, opacity is the inverse of the transparency (alpha) value used in compositing.
