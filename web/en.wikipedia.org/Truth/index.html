Truth

[Image: "Veritas" (truth) allegorically represented in art]

Truth is most often used to mean being in accord with fact or reality, or fidelity to an original or standard. Truth is also often ascribed to things that aim to represent reality or otherwise correspond to it, such as beliefs, propositions, and declarative sentences. Beliefs that are not true are considered to be false. The concept of truth is discussed and debated in various contexts, including philosophy, art, theology, law, and science.

Theories of truth

Philosophers have offered competing theories. The correspondence theory holds that a statement is true if it corresponds to the way the world actually is — a view associated with Aristotle. The coherence theory treats truth as coherence within a consistent system of beliefs. Pragmatism identifies truth with what works in practice or what inquiry would converge upon. Deflationary and minimalist theories deny that "truth" names a substantive property, while the semantic theory of truth developed by Alfred Tarski defines truth formally for structured languages and gave rise to truth-conditional semantics.

Truth in context

In science, truth is approached through evidence, reproducibility, and the provisional nature of theories. In law, truth is established by evidence and testimony within procedural rules. In religion, truth may be revealed or doctrinal. Questions of truth, lies, and deception — including bullshit, self-deception, and the paradoxes of self-reference such as the liar paradox — remain active subjects in epistemology, logic, and philosophy of language.
