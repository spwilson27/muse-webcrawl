1998 Russian financial crisis

Date17 August 1998 (devaluation and default announced)
CountryRussia
CausesFiscal deficits, fixed exchange rate, falling oil prices, Asian financial crisis contagion, GKO debt pyramid
EffectsRuble devaluation, default on domestic debt (GKOs), inflation of 84% in 1998, banking collapse, resignation of the Kiriyenko government

The Russian financial crisis of 1998, also called the ruble crisis, struck Russia on 17 August 1998, when the government of President Boris Yeltsin and Prime Minister Sergei Kiriyenko devalued the ruble, defaulted on domestic government debt (the short-term GKO bonds), and declared a 90-day moratorium on payments by commercial banks to foreign creditors. It was one of the most dramatic financial collapses of the post-Cold War era.

Background

Through the mid-1990s the Russian government financed chronic budget deficits by issuing short-term treasury bills (GKOs) at ever-higher yields, creating a pyramid of debt. Tax collection was weak, capital flight was heavy, and the central bank defended a fixed "corridor" for the ruble. The Asian financial crisis of 1997 drove investors out of emerging markets, while the collapse of oil prices — Russia's main export — to around $11 per barrel gutted state revenues. In July 1998 the IMF and World Bank assembled a $22.6 billion support package, but confidence continued to drain away.

Collapse

On 17 August 1998 the government simultaneously widened the exchange-rate band (effectively devaluing the ruble), restructured GKO debt on punitive terms, and froze private foreign-debt payments. The ruble fell from about 6 per U.S. dollar to 21 by early 1999. Major banks such as Inkombank, Menatep and SBS-Agro collapsed, wiping out the savings of many households. Inflation reached 84 per cent in 1998, and GDP — after a brief rally — contracted sharply. Yeltsin dismissed Kiriyenko's government within a week and appointed Yevgeny Primakov.

Aftermath

Paradoxically, the devaluation powered a rapid recovery: imports collapsed, domestic industry revived, and rising oil prices restored state finances after 1999. The default also shook global markets, contributing to the near-collapse of the U.S. hedge fund Long-Term Capital Management in September 1998. Politically, the crisis discredited the liberal reformers of the 1990s and helped pave the way for the more state-centred order that followed under Vladimir Putin.
