Psychoacoustics

Psychoacoustics is the branch of psychophysics involving the scientific study of how the human auditory system perceives various sounds — more specifically, the branch of science studying the psychological responses associated with sound (including noise, speech, and music). Psychoacoustics is a branch of sound science that bridges physical acoustics and psychology.

Key phenomena

Important topics include the perception of pitch, loudness, and timbre; the absolute threshold of hearing; and equal-loudness contours (originally measured by Fletcher and Munson), which show that the ear's sensitivity varies with frequency. The phon and sone are units of perceived loudness derived from such measurements.

Masking and critical bands

Auditory masking occurs when the perception of one sound is affected by the presence of another. Frequency selectivity is described by the concept of critical bands — bandwidths within which sounds interact in masking. Masking phenomena are exploited in perceptual audio coding formats such as MP3 and AAC, which discard sound components predicted to be inaudible.

Localization and binaural hearing

Humans localize sound using interaural time and level differences, spectral cues from the pinnae, and head movements. The precedence effect causes the first-arriving sound to dominate perceived location. Binaural hearing also enables the "cocktail party effect" — focusing on one voice among many.

Applications

Psychoacoustics underlies audio compression, hearing aid and cochlear implant design, sound quality evaluation in product design (e.g., automotive acoustics), noise annoyance assessment, and concert hall acoustics. Metrics such as loudness (in sones), sharpness, roughness, and fluctuation strength are used to quantify product sound.
