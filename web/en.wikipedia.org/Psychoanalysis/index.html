Psychoanalysis

Psychoanalysis is a set of theories and therapeutic techniques that partly deal with the unconscious mind, and which together form a method of treatment for mental disorders. The discipline was established in the early 1890s by the Austrian neurologist Sigmund Freud, who developed it from his work with patients suffering from hysteria. The term also refers to Freud's theory of personality and mind, which profoundly influenced 20th-century psychology, psychiatry, and the humanities.

Core ideas

Central psychoanalytic concepts include: the unconscious—a reservoir of thoughts, memories, and drives outside awareness that shapes behaviour; repression, the defence by which unacceptable wishes are kept unconscious; the structural model of the psyche as id, ego, and superego (1923); the Oedipus complex and stages of psychosexual development; and therapeutic methods of free association, dream interpretation, and the analysis of transference, in which patients redirect feelings about important figures onto the analyst. Symptoms, slips of the tongue ("Freudian slips"), and dreams are treated as meaningful compromise formations of unconscious conflict.

History and development

Freud's Studies on Hysteria (1895, with Josef Breuer) and The Interpretation of Dreams (1899) founded the field. A circle formed in Vienna, followed by schisms: Alfred Adler founded individual psychology, and Carl Jung founded analytical psychology. Later developments included ego psychology (Anna Freud, Heinz Hartmann), object relations theory (Melanie Klein, Donald Winnicutt), self psychology (Heinz Kohut), and the structuralist reformulations of Jacques Lacan. The International Psychoanalytical Association, founded in 1910, remains the main accrediting body.

Criticism and evidence

Psychoanalysis has been criticised—most famously by Karl Popper—for unfalsifiability, and its classical technique (multiple sessions per week over years) has largely been supplemented or replaced by shorter psychodynamic therapies. Critics also dispute Freud's emphasis on sexuality and the historical handling of some cases. Defenders cite growing empirical support for psychodynamic therapy's effectiveness for several disorders and the lasting influence of concepts such as unconscious processing, defence mechanisms, and attachment in mainstream psychology.

Cultural influence

Beyond the clinic, psychoanalysis shaped literary criticism, film theory, philosophy, and everyday language, contributing terms such as "repression", "the unconscious", "Oedipus complex", and "death drive" to the wider culture.
