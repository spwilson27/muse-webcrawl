Supplemental Security Income

Supplemental Security Income (SSI) is a United States federal program administered by the Social Security Administration that provides monthly payments to people with limited income and resources who are aged 65 or older, blind, or disabled. Created by the Social Security Amendments of 1972 and begun in 1974, SSI replaced a patchwork of state programs for the aged, blind, and disabled.

Eligibility and payments

Unlike Social Security retirement or disability insurance (SSDI), SSI is means-tested and funded from general tax revenues, not payroll taxes; eligibility does not depend on work history. Applicants must meet strict income and asset limits — countable resources of $2,000 for an individual and $3,000 for a couple, a figure unchanged since 1989. The federal benefit rate is adjusted annually for inflation; most states add a supplementary payment. In most states, SSI receipt automatically confers Medicaid eligibility.

Recipients

About 7–8 million people receive SSI, including children with severe disabilities and working-age adults unable to engage in substantial gainful activity. The program is a safety net of last resort, and its rules on income, in-kind support, and living arrangements are a frequent subject of policy debate.
