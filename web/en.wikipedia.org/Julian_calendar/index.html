Julian calendar
Introduced: 45 BC by Julius Caesar
Average year: 365.25 days
Leap rule: Every 4th year
Superseded by: Gregorian calendar (from 1582)

The Julian calendar is a calendar of 365 days divided into twelve months, with a leap day added to February every four years, giving an average year of 365.25 days. Proposed by the Alexandrian astronomer Sosigenes and proclaimed by Julius Caesar in 46 BC, it took effect on January 1, 45 BC and became the predominant calendar of the Roman world and most of Europe for more than 1,600 years.

Structure

The Julian reform regularized the previously chaotic Roman calendar. Months took the lengths they retain today, and the year began on January 1. To align the calendar with the seasons, Caesar's first year of the reform — the "year of confusion," 46 BC — was extended to 445 days. The month Quintilis was renamed July in Caesar's honor, and Sextilis became August under Augustus.

Drift and replacement

The Julian year of 365.25 days exceeds the tropical year (about 365.2422 days) by roughly 11 minutes, causing the calendar to gain about one day every 128 years. By the 16th century the spring equinox had drifted some ten days from its position at the Council of Nicaea (325). Pope Gregory XIII's reform of 1582 produced the Gregorian calendar, which dropped ten days and refined the leap rule; Catholic countries adopted it at once, while Protestant and Orthodox regions followed over the following centuries — Britain in 1752 and Russia only in 1918.

Present use

The Julian calendar is still used by some Eastern Orthodox churches to compute liturgical dates, by the Berbers of North Africa, and in modified (Revised Julian) form elsewhere. Dates recorded in it are conventionally labeled "Old Style."
