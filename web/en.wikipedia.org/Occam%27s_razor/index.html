Occam's razor (also Ockham's razor) is the problem-solving principle that, when presented with competing hypotheses about the same prediction, one should prefer the hypothesis that requires the fewest assumptions. The principle is attributed to William of Ockham, a 14th-century English friar and scholastic philosopher, who frequently invoked parsimony in his writings, although the idea appears earlier in Aristotle, Ptolemy, and other scholastics.

Formulations

The best-known Latin formulation, Entia non sunt multiplicanda praeter necessitatem ("Entities must not be multiplied beyond necessity"), was actually coined by later writers summarizing Ockham's approach. Ockham's own phrasing was closer to: "It is vain to do with more what can be done with fewer."

Use in science and philosophy

The razor functions as a heuristic for theory choice: among empirically equivalent theories, the simpler is preferred as a starting point. It has been invoked in debates over the luminiferous ether, phlogiston, and the interpretation of quantum mechanics. Formal counterparts include minimum description length, the Akaike information criterion, and Solomonoff's theory of inductive inference, which ground parsimony in probability theory. Critics note that simplicity is ambiguous — syntactic, ontological, and parametric simplicity can pull in different directions — and that the razor is a guide to inquiry, not a guarantee of truth.
