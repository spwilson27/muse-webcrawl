Leslie B. Lamport (born February 7, 1941) is an American computer scientist and mathematician. He is best known for his foundational work in distributed systems and concurrency, for creating the LaTeX document preparation system, and for the development of temporal logic and its application to the specification and verification of concurrent systems. In 2013 he received the ACM A.M. Turing Award for his contributions to the theory and practice of distributed and concurrent systems.

Early life and education

Lamport was born in New York City. He received a B.S. in mathematics from the Massachusetts Institute of Technology in 1960, and M.A. and Ph.D. degrees in mathematics from Brandeis University in 1963 and 1972, respectively. His dissertation concerned singularities in analytic partial differential equations.

Career

Lamport worked at Massachusetts Computer Associates from 1970 to 1977, at SRI International from 1977 to 1985, and at Digital Equipment Corporation (later Compaq) from 1985 to 2001. In 2001 he joined Microsoft Research, where he continued work on formal methods and distributed algorithms.

Contributions

Distributed systems

Lamport's 1978 paper "Time, Clocks, and the Ordering of Events in a Distributed System" introduced the concept of logical clocks and "happened-before" ordering, and is one of the most cited papers in computer science. He also gave the classic formulation of the Byzantine generals problem (with Robert Shostak and Marshall Pease, 1982), showing how groups of processes can reach agreement despite faulty or malicious participants. His Paxos family of consensus algorithms, presented in "The Part-Time Parliament" (1989, published 1998), became a cornerstone of reliable distributed computing and influenced systems such as Google's Chubby and Apache ZooKeeper's Zab protocol.

Concurrency and mutual exclusion

Lamport devised the bakery algorithm for mutual exclusion, devised the first known correct solution concepts for concurrent read/write registers (the "atomic register" hierarchy), and developed the notion of sequential consistency as a correctness condition for shared-memory multiprocessors.

LaTeX and formal specification

In the early 1980s Lamport created LaTeX, a set of macros for Donald Knuth's TeX typesetting system that greatly simplified document preparation; it remains the standard tool for scientific publishing in mathematics, physics, and computer science. He later developed the Temporal Logic of Actions (TLA) and the TLA+ specification language, together with the PlusCal algorithm language, for formally specifying and model-checking concurrent and distributed systems, presented in his book Specifying Systems (2002).

Awards and honors

Lamport received the IEEE Emanuel R. Piore Award in 2004, the Edsger W. Dijkstra Memorial Award (Dijkstra Prize) in distributed computing in 2000 and again in 2005, the IEEE John von Neumann Medal in 2008, and the ACM Turing Award in 2013 "for fundamental contributions to the theory and practice of distributed and concurrent systems." He is a Fellow of the ACM and a member of the U.S. National Academy of Sciences and National Academy of Engineering. He also received five honorary doctorates from European universities.
