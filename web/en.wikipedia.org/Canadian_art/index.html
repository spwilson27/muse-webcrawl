Canadian art encompasses the visual arts produced in the area now known as Canada from Indigenous traditions thousands of years old through the colonial period to the contemporary era. It reflects the country's landscapes, its Indigenous peoples, and successive waves of European settlement and immigration.

History

Indigenous peoples created sophisticated carving, weaving, beadwork and graphic traditions long before European contact; Inuit sculpture and prints from Cape Dorset became internationally celebrated in the mid-twentieth century. Under French and British rule, art served religious, military and topographical purposes. The late nineteenth century brought academic landscape painting, and Tom Thomson and the Group of Seven (1920–1933) forged a nationalist school of rugged northern landscape painting.

Modern and contemporary

Post-war movements included Montreal's Automatistes, led by Paul-Émile Borduas, and Toronto's Painters Eleven abstractionists. Contemporary Canadian artists such as Jeff Wall, Stan Douglas, Kent Monkman and Shary Boyle work across photography, installation and painting, while the National Gallery of Canada in Ottawa holds the country's principal collection.
