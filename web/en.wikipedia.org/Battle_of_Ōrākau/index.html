Battle of Ōrākau

The Battle of Ōrākau was a battle fought at Ōrākau in the Waikato region of New Zealand from 31 March to 2 April 1864, during the Invasion of the Waikato phase of the New Zealand Wars. Around 300 Māori defenders, led by Rewi Maniapoto of Ngāti Maniapoto, held a fortified pā against a much larger force of British and colonial troops under Brigadier-General George Carey. The battle is remembered for the defenders' defiant refusal to surrender, summed up in the famous reply attributed to them: "Ka whawhai tonu mātou, ake! ake! ake!" — "We will fight on forever, forever, forever!"

Background

Following the fall of Rangiriri and other engagements in 1863–64, Kīngitanga forces retreated south. Rewi Maniapoto began building a pā at Ōrākau, near present-day Kihikihi, though the site was criticised by other chiefs as poorly chosen: it lacked a water supply and an easy line of retreat, and stood in open country where it could be surrounded. The garrison included men, women and children from several iwi, including Ngāti Maniapoto, Ngāti Raukawa, Waikato and Tūhoe, alongside a small number of Pākehā Māori.

The battle

Carey's force of roughly 1,400–1,800 soldiers and militia surrounded the pā on 31 March 1864. The defenders' earthworks resisted artillery bombardment, and repeated assaults were driven back. When called on to surrender, the garrison refused, reportedly also rejecting an offer for the women and children to leave. On 2 April, after two days without water, the defenders made a sudden breakout through the British lines to the south. Many were killed in the pursuit; Māori casualties are usually estimated at around 160, while British losses were about 50 killed and wounded.

Legacy

Ōrākau became a symbol of Māori resistance and of the phrase "ake, ake, ake". The exact wording of the defenders' reply has been debated by historians, but the stand itself is widely commemorated, and the battle site is preserved near Kihikihi. The battle was effectively the last major engagement of the Waikato campaign.
