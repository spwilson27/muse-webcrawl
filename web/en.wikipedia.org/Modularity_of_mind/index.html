Modularity of mind

Modularity of mind is the notion, in cognitive science and philosophy of mind, that a mind may be composed of innate, functionally specialised neural structures or processes — modules — that have distinct, established functions. The idea is associated primarily with Jerry Fodor, who proposed it in his 1983 monograph The Modularity of Mind, drawing partly on Noam Chomsky's notion of a language organ and on Franz Joseph Gall's earlier faculty psychology.

Fodor's thesis

Fodor argued that input systems (perception and language comprehension) are modular, where modules are characterised by properties such as domain specificity, mandatory operation, speed, informational encapsulation, shallow outputs, fixed neural architecture, and characteristic breakdown patterns. In contrast, "central systems" responsible for belief fixation and reasoning were, for Fodor, non-modular and holistic — a consequence he later developed pessimistically in The Mind Doesn't Work That Way (2000).

Massive modularity

Evolutionary psychologists such as Leda Cosmides, John Tooby, and Steven Pinker extended modularity far beyond input systems, proposing massive modularity: that the mind consists largely or entirely of special-purpose modules shaped by natural selection to solve adaptive problems (e.g., cheater detection in social exchange, theory of mind, face recognition). Peter Carruthers has defended versions of massive modularity philosophically.

Criticism

Critics question whether modules can be individuated cleanly, whether encapsulation is as strict as claimed, and whether neuroplasticity and developmental evidence fit a strongly nativist picture. Alternative approaches include connectionism, embodied cognition, and predictive processing, which emphasise distributed, flexible processing over dedicated modules.
