Regional anaesthesia

Regional anaesthesia is the loss of sensation (anaesthesia) in a specific region of the body achieved by the injection or application of a local anaesthetic agent near nerves that supply that region. Unlike general anaesthesia, the patient remains conscious, or may be lightly sedated, while the targeted area is rendered insensate and immobile.

Techniques

Regional techniques are broadly divided into neuraxial (central) blocks and peripheral nerve blocks. Neuraxial techniques include spinal anaesthesia, in which a small dose of local anaesthetic is injected into the cerebrospinal fluid of the subarachnoid space, and epidural anaesthesia, in which a larger volume is placed in the epidural space, often through a catheter allowing continuous infusion. Caudal anaesthesia, a variant of the epidural approach through the sacral hiatus, is widely used in children.

Peripheral nerve blocks target individual nerves or plexuses, such as the brachial plexus for arm surgery, the femoral or sciatic nerves for leg surgery, and the transversus abdominis plane (TAP) block for abdominal procedures. Ultrasound guidance has become standard for many peripheral blocks, improving accuracy and reducing complications.

Advantages

Regional anaesthesia avoids the airway manipulation and systemic drug effects of general anaesthesia, provides excellent postoperative analgesia, reduces opioid requirements, and may decrease nausea, vomiting and the incidence of chronic post-surgical pain. It is particularly valuable in patients with significant cardiopulmonary disease.

Complications

Possible complications include failure of the block, post-dural-puncture headache, hypotension from sympathetic blockade, local anaesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST), nerve injury, haematoma and infection. Contraindications include patient refusal, infection at the injection site, coagulopathy and, for neuraxial techniques, severe hypovolaemia or raised intracranial pressure.
