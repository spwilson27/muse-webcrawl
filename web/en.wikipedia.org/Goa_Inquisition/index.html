Goa Inquisition

The Goa Inquisition operated in Portuguese India from 1560 to 1812.

The Goa Inquisition was an extension of the Portuguese Inquisition established in the Portuguese colony of Goa and other possessions in Asia. Formally founded in 1560 at the urging of missionaries including Francis Xavier, it was directed principally at Hindus, Muslims and newly converted Christians suspected of secretly practising their former religions, as well as at New Christians from Portugal accused of Judaizing.

History

Operating from the Inquisitorial palace in Old Goa, the tribunal conducted trials using secret accusations, imprisonment and, in some cases, torture. Convicted relapsed heretics could be executed at autos-da-fé; records indicate roughly 57 people were executed in person and many more in effigy, while thousands were tried and punished over the tribunal's existence. The Inquisition also censored books and suppressed Hindu and Muslim rites in the Old Conquests, at times enforcing policies — such as bans on public non-Christian worship — that accompanied temple destruction and discriminatory laws.

Abolition

The Marquis of Pombal's reforms curtailed the tribunal in the 1770s; it was briefly restored, then finally abolished in 1812. Its memory remains a subject of historical debate and controversy in Goa and in Portugal, with estimates of its severity varying among historians.
