Brigade

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


NATO map symbol for an infantry brigade

A brigade is a major tactical military formation, typically composed of three to six battalions plus supporting elements. It is smaller than a division and larger than a regiment or group, usually numbering between 2,500 and 8,000 personnel, and is commanded by a brigadier or brigadier general. Brigades may be independent formations combining infantry, armour, artillery and support arms, or subordinate units within a division.

History

The term derives from the French brigade, from the Italian brigata (troop or company). The formation was systematised in the army of Gustavus Adolphus of Sweden in the Thirty Years' War and was widely adopted in European armies. During the two world wars, brigades in British and Commonwealth armies typically grouped three infantry battalions, while the United States reorganised its divisions around combat commands and later brigades. After the Cold War, many armies restructured around modular, self-contained brigade combat teams — in the US Army, infantry, Stryker and armored brigade combat teams became the service's principal deployable units.

Organisation

A modern brigade has a headquarters and usually combines manoeuvre battalions with fires (artillery), reconnaissance, engineer, air defence and logistics units, making it the smallest formation capable of sustained independent operations. In some armies the brigade replaced the division as the primary formation; in others, such as the British Army, brigades remain deployable headquarters that command battlegroups assembled from across the army.
