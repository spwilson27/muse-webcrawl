Imminent lawless action
SourceBrandenburg v. Ohio, 395 U.S. 444 (1969)
JurisdictionUnited States
DoctrineFirst Amendment; incitement

"Imminent lawless action" is a standard under United States constitutional law that the Supreme Court of the United States established in Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969) for determining when a state may constitutionally limit an individual's free speech rights under the First Amendment.

The Brandenburg test

Under the test, speech advocating the use of force or violation of the law is protected unless it is (1) "directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action" and (2) "likely to incite or produce such action". Abstract advocacy of violence or illegality, however offensive, remains protected.

Background and application

The standard replaced the older "clear and present danger" test of Schenck v. United States (1919). It was applied to protect a Ku Klux Klan leader's speech in Brandenburg itself and later speakers in cases such as Hess v. Indiana (1973).
