Phở (Vietnamese: [fəː] ⓘ) is a Vietnamese soup dish consisting of broth, rice noodles (bánh phở), herbs, and meat — usually beef (phở bò) or sometimes chicken (phở gà). It originated in northern Vietnam in the early 20th century and is widely considered the national dish of Vietnam. Phở is served in restaurants worldwide and is a popular street food at home.

History

Phở most likely developed in Hanoi and Nam Định province in the early 1900s, influenced by both Chinese noodle soups and French colonial demand for beef, which made beef bones and scraps available for stock. After the partition of Vietnam in 1954, refugees carried phở south, where the Saigon style added more herbs, bean sprouts, and hoisin and chilli sauces. Following the Vietnam War, the diaspora spread phở across North America, Australia, and Europe.

Preparation

The broth — the soul of the dish — is made by simmering beef or chicken bones with charred onion and ginger and spices such as star anise, cloves, cinnamon, cardamom, and fennel seed, then seasoning with fish sauce and rock sugar. Flat rice noodles are blanched, topped with slices of raw or cooked beef (which cook in the hot broth) or chicken, and garnished with scallions, onion, and herbs. Southern-style servings add Thai basil, sawtooth herb, bean sprouts, lime, and jalapeño or bird's eye chilli on the side.

Styles

Northern (Hanoi) phở is plainer and broth-focused, with wider noodles and fewer garnishes, while southern (Saigon) phở is sweeter and served with a generous plate of herbs. Regional variants include rolled phở (phở cuốn) and stir-fried phở (phở xào).
