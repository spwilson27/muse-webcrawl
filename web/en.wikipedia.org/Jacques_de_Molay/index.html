Jacques de Molay (c. 1240–1250 – 18 March 1314) was the 23rd and last Grand Master of the Knights Templar, leading the order from 1292 until its dissolution by Pope Clement V in 1312. Arrested in Paris in 1307 on the orders of King Philip IV of France, he was tortured into confessing to heresy, later retracted his confession, and was burned at the stake in 1314. His dramatic end, and the legend that he cursed the king and the pope from the flames, have made him one of the most famous Templars in history.

Early life and career

De Molay was born in Molay, in the Franche-Comté, then part of the Holy Roman Empire, probably to a minor noble family. He joined the Knights Templar in 1265 at Beaune and served in the Holy Land, rising through the order's ranks. After the fall of Acre in 1291 ended the Crusader presence on the mainland, de Molay was based in Cyprus. In 1292 he was elected Grand Master, succeeding Thibaud Gaudin, and spent the following years seeking support in Europe for a new crusade and defending the order against proposals to merge it with the Knights Hospitaller.

Arrest and trial

Philip IV, deeply indebted to the order and covetous of its wealth, moved against the Templars in 1307. On Friday, 13 October 1307, royal agents arrested de Molay and hundreds of French Templars simultaneously. Charged with heresy, blasphemy, idolatry, and obscene rituals, de Molay confessed under torture to some charges, including denying Christ. In 1309–1310 he retracted his confession before papal commissioners, but after the Council of Vienne, Pope Clement V dissolved the order by the bull Vox in excelso (1312) and de Molay's fate was left to a royal commission.

Execution and the curse legend

In March 1314, de Molay and the Templar preceptor of Normandy, Geoffroi de Charney, publicly retracted their confessions before a crowd at Notre-Dame. Philip IV, furious, ordered them burned as relapsed heretics. They were executed by slow fire on an island in the Seine (the Île aux Juifs, near the Pont Neuf) on 18 March 1314. According to legend, de Molay called out from the flames, summoning Pope Clement and King Philip to meet him before God's tribunal within a year — and indeed, Clement died a month later and Philip within the year, a coincidence that cemented the story of "the curse of Jacques de Molay" in popular culture. Modern historians regard the charges against the Templars as fabricated and de Molay's trial as a judicial outrage driven by royal finance and politics.
