Epiglottis

Epiglottis
Part ofLarynx
SystemRespiratory system; digestive system
FunctionSeals the windpipe during swallowing

The epiglottis is a leaf-shaped flap of elastic cartilage covered with a mucous membrane, located at the entrance of the larynx in the throat. Its principal function is to fold over the windpipe (trachea) during swallowing, preventing food and liquid from entering the airway and lungs.

Structure

The epiglottis is attached to the thyroid cartilage by the thyroepiglottic ligament and to the hyoid bone by the hyoepiglottic ligament. Its free upper end projects upward behind the tongue. The epiglottic cartilage is elastic cartilage, allowing it to flex and spring back. Taste buds are present on its laryngeal surface.

Function

During normal breathing the epiglottis stands upright, allowing air to pass into the larynx and lungs. During the pharyngeal stage of swallowing, elevation of the larynx and folding of the epiglottis redirect the bolus into the oesophagus. Failure of this protective mechanism can lead to aspiration.

Clinical significance

Inflammation of the epiglottis, epiglottitis, is a medical emergency most often caused by Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib), historically in children; vaccination has greatly reduced its incidence. It presents with drooling, sore throat, stridor and respiratory distress, and the swollen epiglottis may appear as a "thumbprint" on a lateral neck X-ray.
