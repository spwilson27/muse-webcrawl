Democratic peace theory

Democratic peace theory posits that democracies are hesitant to engage in armed conflict with other identified democracies. This seemingly empirical regularity has been described by proponents as being as close as anything we have to an empirical law in international relations. The theory traces its intellectual roots to Immanuel Kant's 1795 essay Perpetual Peace, which argued that states with republican constitutions and a federation of free states would tend toward peace.

Explanations

Two broad families of explanation are offered. Normative explanations hold that democratic leaders internalize norms of compromise and nonviolent dispute resolution and externalize them in dealings with other democracies. Institutional (or structural) explanations hold that checks and balances, the need for public consent, and the slow mobilization of democracies make war between them less likely, and that democracies signal their intentions more transparently, reducing miscalculation.

Criticism

Critics dispute the definitions of "democracy" and "war" used in the literature, note that the finding may be confounded by alliances, wealth, trade, and the Cold War alignment, and point out that democracies frequently wage war against non-democracies and have used covert operations against democratically elected governments. The "monadic" version of the theory — that democracies are generally more peaceful — is much less supported than the "dyadic" version concerning pairs of democracies.
