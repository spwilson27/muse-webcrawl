Enolate

An enolate anion: the conjugate base of a carbonyl compound, with negative charge delocalised between carbon and oxygen.

An enolate is an alkene (ene) combined with an alcohol (olate), more precisely the anion formed by deprotonation of the alpha position of a carbonyl compound. Enolates are ambident nucleophiles: the negative charge is delocalised over the alpha carbon and the carbonyl oxygen, and they can react at either site, although carbon attack usually predominates with alkyl halides and other typical electrophiles.

Formation

Enolates are formed by removing an alpha proton from a ketone, aldehyde, ester or related carbonyl compound with a base. Because the alpha C–H bond is appreciably acidic (pKa around 19–25 for simple ketones in water terms), bases such as lithium diisopropylamide (LDA), sodium hydride, alkoxides or hydroxide can be used. The choice of base, solvent and temperature determines whether the kinetic or the thermodynamic enolate is produced. Deprotonation with LDA in tetrahydrofuran at −78 °C generally gives the kinetic enolate derived from the less substituted alpha position, whereas equilibrating conditions favour the more substituted, more stable enolate.

Geometry and structure

Metal enolates of ketones exist as either (E)- or (Z)-isomers about the C=C double bond of the enolate. The geometry of the enolate controls the stereochemical outcome of subsequent reactions, most famously the syn/anti selectivity of the aldol reaction. Ireland's model relates enolate geometry to the diastereoselectivity observed in ester enolate aldol reactions. Enolates are typically aggregated in solution, and counterion and solvent strongly influence their reactivity and selectivity.

Reactions

Enolates are among the most important nucleophiles in carbon–carbon bond formation. Their reactions include:

  • Alkylation with alkyl halides (forming a new C–C bond at the alpha position)
  • The aldol reaction with aldehydes and ketones
  • Michael addition to alpha,beta-unsaturated carbonyl compounds
  • The Claisen condensation (ester enolates)
  • Acylation, halogenation (as in the haloform reaction) and alpha-hydroxylation

Enamines and silyl enol ethers

Related nucleophiles include enamines, formed from ketones and secondary amines, and silyl enol ethers, formed by trapping an enolate with a silyl chloride such as trimethylsilyl chloride. Silyl enol ethers serve as neutral enolate equivalents in the Mukaiyama aldol reaction and other Lewis-acid-promoted additions.
