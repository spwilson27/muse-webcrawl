Ottoman architecture is the architecture of the Ottoman Empire, developed in Bursa and Edirne in the 14th and 15th centuries from earlier Seljuk Turkish architecture and influenced by Byzantine, Persian, and Islamic traditions. Its classical period, in the 16th and 17th centuries, produced some of the most celebrated monuments of Islamic architecture, above all the mosques of Mimar Sinan.

Early and classical periods

Early Ottoman mosques adapted Seljuk forms; the conquest of Constantinople in 1453 exposed Ottoman builders to the great dome of Hagia Sophia, which profoundly influenced the classical style. The imperial mosque complex (külliye) typically combined a domed prayer hall with minarets, a courtyard, madrasas, a hospital, kitchens, and a mausoleum. Sinan, chief royal architect from 1538 to 1588, perfected the centralized domed structure in the Şehzade Mosque (1548), the Süleymaniye Mosque (1557), and his masterpiece, the Selimiye Mosque in Edirne (1575), whose dome rests on eight piers.

Later periods

The 18th century brought the Tulip Period and an Ottoman Baroque style, seen in the Nuruosmaniye Mosque (1755) and in fountains and pavilions. The 19th century added eclectic palaces such as Dolmabahçe (1856), mixing Baroque, Rococo, and Neoclassical elements. A First National Architectural Movement in the early 20th century revived Seljuk and Ottoman motifs before the modern Republic adopted international styles.

Features

Characteristic elements include vast central domes on pendentives flanked by semi-domes, slender pencil minarets, extensive Iznik tile revetment, calligraphic decoration, muqarnas vaulting, and carefully landscaped courtyards. Secular Ottoman architecture survives in caravanserais, bridges, aqueducts, baths (hammams), and waterfront wooden houses (yalı).
