Wood pasture

Wood pasture is a historical European land management system in which livestock are grazed in woodland or among scattered trees, combining grazing land with trees that are managed for timber, wood products, and fodder. It is one of the oldest land-use systems in Europe and is recognised today as a habitat of high conservation value.

Description

In wood pasture, trees — often pollarded, that is, cut above the reach of browsing animals so they regrow a crown of branches — stand at low density in grassland or heath grazed by cattle, sheep, pigs, or deer. Pollarding supplied firewood, poles, and leaf fodder while extending the tree's life, and acorns and beech mast provided pannage for pigs. Classic surviving examples include the New Forest and Epping Forest in England and the dehesas of Spain and montados of Portugal, where cork and holm oaks shade grazing land.

Ecology and history

Dutch ecologist Frans Vera argued that the park-like wood pasture landscape resembles the mosaic of groves and grassland maintained by wild large herbivores in prehistoric Europe, a hypothesis that has reshaped debates about natural vegetation. Whether or not that theory holds, ancient wood pastures are rich in veteran trees supporting rare fungi, lichens, invertebrates such as stag beetles, and hole-nesting birds.

Decline and revival

The system declined with enclosure, changes in forestry that separated grazing from timber production, and the loss of common rights during the 18th and 19th centuries. Many remaining sites are now protected as Sites of Special Scientific Interest, national nature reserves, or Natura 2000 areas, and conservation grazing has been reintroduced to restore them.
