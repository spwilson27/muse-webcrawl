In mathematics, Pascal's triangle is a triangular array of numbers in which each number is the sum of the two numbers directly above it. The rows correspond to the coefficients of the binomial expansion: the entries of row n are the binomial coefficients C(n, k). The triangle is named after the French mathematician Blaise Pascal, who studied it in his Traité du triangle arithmétique (published 1665), though it had been known for centuries to mathematicians in China (Yang Hui), Persia (Omar Khayyam) and India.

Construction and properties

The triangle begins with a single 1 at the apex; each subsequent row starts and ends with 1, and every interior entry is the sum of its two neighbours in the row above — Pascal's rule. Consequences include: the sum of row n equals 2n; the rows give the expansions of (a + b)n; shallow diagonals sum to the Fibonacci numbers; and the triangle contains the natural numbers, triangular numbers and tetrahedral numbers along its diagonals. Highlighting odd entries reveals an approximation of the Sierpiński triangle fractal.

Applications

Binomial coefficients from Pascal's triangle appear throughout combinatorics and probability — for example in counting combinations, the binomial distribution, and the expansion of powers of binomials. The triangle is also used in algebra, number theory (for example in testing primality via divisibility patterns) and in the hockey-stick identity, which sums entries along a diagonal.

History

The arithmetical triangle appeared in the work of the Indian prosodist Pingala, was developed by Persian mathematicians including Al-Karaji and Omar Khayyam (hence "Khayyam's triangle"), and was described in China by Jia Xian and Yang Hui ("Yang Hui's triangle"). Pascal's treatise systematised its properties and applied them to probability theory, developed in his correspondence with Pierre de Fermat.
