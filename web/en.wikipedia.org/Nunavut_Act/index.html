The Nunavut Act (French: Loi sur le Nunavut) is an act of the Parliament of Canada that established the territory of Nunavut and its government. Passed in 1993 alongside the Nunavut Land Claims Agreement—the largest Indigenous land claim settlement in Canadian history—the Act provided for the division of the Northwest Territories, with the eastern Arctic becoming Nunavut ("Our Land" in Inuktitut) on 1 April 1999.

Background

Inuit organisations had sought a separate, Inuit-majority territory since the 1970s. A 1992 plebiscite in the Northwest Territories approved the division, and Inuit voters ratified the land claims agreement the same year. The Nunavut Land Claims Agreement transferred title to about 350,000 square kilometres of land to Inuit, established wildlife and land management boards, and provided capital transfers exceeding one billion dollars.

Provisions

The Act constitutes Nunavut as a territory with a Legislative Assembly elected by all residents (Inuit and non-Inuit alike) and a government operating largely by consensus rather than political parties. It provides for a Commissioner as the federal representative, continues existing Northwest Territories laws until amended, and recognises the territory's official languages—Inuktitut, Inuinnaqtun, English, and French. Iqaluit was chosen as the capital by plebiscite in 1995.

Significance

Nunavut was the first major change to Canada's political map since Newfoundland joined Confederation in 1949, and the first territory created as the result of an Indigenous land claim. Because roughly 85 percent of its population is Inuit, the territory functions as a form of Inuit self-government within Canada.
