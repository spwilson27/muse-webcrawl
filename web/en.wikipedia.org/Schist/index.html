Schist

Mica schist showing characteristic foliation.

Schist is a medium-grained metamorphic rock showing pronounced schistosity — a foliation in which platy mineral grains, mainly micas, are aligned in parallel layers visible to the naked eye. Schist forms under higher temperatures and pressures than slate or phyllite, representing a moderate to high grade of metamorphism. The name derives from the Greek schistos, "split", referring to the rock's tendency to split along its foliation planes.

Formation and composition

Schists form when sedimentary rocks such as mudstone and shale, or fine-grained igneous rocks, are subjected to directed pressure and heat during regional metamorphism, typically in mountain-building events. Recrystallisation produces abundant mica (muscovite and biotite) together with quartz and feldspar; index minerals such as garnet, staurolite, kyanite, and sillimanite may be present and record the metamorphic grade.

Types

Schists are named for their dominant minerals, as in mica schist, garnet schist, chlorite schist, talc schist, and graphite schist. Blueschist, containing the blue amphibole glaucophane, forms in high-pressure, low-temperature conditions characteristic of subduction zones and is important evidence for plate tectonics.

Uses and engineering

Schist is used locally as a building and decorative stone, though its foliation makes it less durable than massive rock. The Manhattan schist bedrock underlies New York City and provides firm foundations for skyscrapers. In engineering, schistose rock can be weak along its foliation and requires care in tunnelling and slope design.
