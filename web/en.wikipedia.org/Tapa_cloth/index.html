Tapa cloth

Tapa cloth (or simply tapa) is a barkcloth made across the islands of the Pacific Ocean, primarily from the inner bark of the paper mulberry tree (Broussonetia papyrifera). It is known by many local names: siapo in Samoa, ngatu in Tonga, ahu in Tahiti, kapa in Hawaii, and masi in Fiji. Tapa was once the principal material for clothing and ceremonial goods throughout Polynesia and parts of Melanesia, and remains culturally important today.

Production

Strips of inner bark are soaked, then beaten with wooden mallets on an anvil to spread the fibres into thin sheets. Sheets are joined edge to edge with natural adhesives or by felting to produce large cloths. Decoration is applied by stencils, freehand painting with plant dyes in browns and blacks, rubbing over pattern boards, or printing with carved stamps. Geometric motifs, often repeated in grids, are characteristic of many traditions.

Uses

Historically tapa served as clothing, bedding, room dividers, burial wrappings, and ceremonial dress. Large, finely made cloths are prestige valuables presented at weddings, funerals, and chiefly occasions — in Tonga and Samoa, presentations of tapa and fine mats remain central to ceremonial exchange. Tongan ngatu can reach enormous sizes for royal events.

Modern revival

Although displaced for everyday wear by imported textiles, tapa making persists, particularly in Tonga, Samoa, Fiji, and Hawaii, supported by cultural festivals, the art market, and contemporary Pacific artists who use barkcloth as a medium connecting their work to ancestral practice.
