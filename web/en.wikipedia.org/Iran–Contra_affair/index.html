Iran–Contra affair

Date1985–1987 (operations); exposed November 1986

Central figuresOliver North, John Poindexter, William Casey, Robert McFarlane

OutcomeSeveral convictions, most later vacated or pardoned

The Iran–Contra affair, also referred to as the Iran–Contra scandal or Irangate, was a political scandal in the United States that came to light in November 1986. Senior officials in the administration of President Ronald Reagan had secretly facilitated arms sales to Iran — then under an arms embargo and at war with Iraq — and used part of the proceeds to fund the Contras, anti-communist rebels fighting Nicaragua's Sandinista government.

The operations

The arms-for-hostages deals, arranged with Israeli intermediaries beginning in 1985, were intended to secure the release of American hostages held by Hezbollah-linked groups in Lebanon and to open a channel to Iranian moderates. National Security Council staff member Lieutenant Colonel Oliver North then diverted profits from the sales to the Contras at a time when the Boland Amendments prohibited US government funding of the Contra war effort.

Exposure and investigations

The scheme unravelled after a supply plane was shot down over Nicaragua in October 1986 and a Lebanese magazine disclosed the arms deals in November. Attorney General Edwin Meese revealed the diversion on 25 November 1986. The Tower Commission criticized Reagan's management style but found no proof he knew of the diversion. Televised congressional hearings in 1987 made North a household name; Independent Counsel Lawrence Walsh's investigation produced indictments and several convictions, though key convictions were overturned on appeal, and President George H. W. Bush pardoned six officials in 1992.

Significance

The affair badly damaged the Reagan administration's credibility, contradicted its stated policies of never negotiating with terrorists and opposing the Sandinistas openly, and led to reforms of National Security Council procedures.
