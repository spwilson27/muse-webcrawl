Libel

Libel
Area of lawTort law (defamation)
FormWritten or otherwise permanent defamatory statement
Contrasted withSlander (spoken defamation)
Key elementsFalse statement of fact, publication to a third party, fault, damage to reputation

Libel is a method of defamation expressed by print, writing, pictures, signs, effigies, or any communication embodied in physical form that is injurious to a person's reputation, exposes a person to public hatred, contempt or ridicule, or injures a person in their business or profession. In common law jurisdictions, defamation in a permanent form is libel, while defamation in a transient form — typically spoken words or gestures — is slander.

Elements

A claimant in a libel action generally must show that the defendant published a false statement of fact about the claimant to a third party, that the statement harmed the claimant's reputation, and that the defendant acted with the requisite degree of fault. Libel is often actionable per se, meaning damage is presumed, reflecting the lasting harm that a permanent publication can cause.

Defences

  • Truth — a substantially true statement is not defamatory (called "justification" in some jurisdictions).
  • Honest opinion / fair comment — recognisable opinion based on true facts on a matter of public interest.
  • Privilege — absolute privilege (e.g. statements in parliament or in court) and qualified privilege (e.g. fair and accurate reports of public proceedings).
  • Innocent dissemination — for distributors who neither knew nor ought to have known of the defamatory content.

By jurisdiction

In the United States, the First Amendment sharply limits libel: under New York Times Co. v. Sullivan (1964), public officials — later extended to public figures — must prove "actual malice", meaning the statement was made with knowledge of its falsity or reckless disregard for the truth. In England and Wales, the Defamation Act 2013 introduced a "serious harm" threshold and reformed several defences. Historically, criminal libel existed in many systems, and blasphemous and seditious libel were prosecuted in England into the modern era before abolition.
