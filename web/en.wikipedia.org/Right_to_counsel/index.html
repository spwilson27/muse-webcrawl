Right to counsel

In criminal law, the right to counsel means a defendant has a legal right to have the assistance of counsel (i.e. lawyers) and, if the defendant cannot afford a lawyer, requires that one be appointed to them. The right to counsel forms part of the right to a fair trial in many jurisdictions.

United States

In the United States, the Sixth Amendment to the Constitution guarantees that "in all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right ... to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence." In Gideon v. Wainwright (1963), the Supreme Court held that states are required under the Fourteenth Amendment to provide counsel in criminal cases to represent defendants who are unable to afford to pay their own attorneys. The right was later extended to misdemeanour cases carrying a risk of imprisonment in Argersinger v. Hamlin (1972).

The right to counsel also underlies the Miranda warnings given to suspects in police custody, following Miranda v. Arizona (1966). The right attaches at critical stages of criminal proceedings, and its denial may require reversal of a conviction. Under Strickland v. Washington (1984), a defendant may also claim ineffective assistance of counsel where the lawyer's performance fell below an objective standard of reasonableness and prejudiced the defence.

Other jurisdictions

Article 6 of the European Convention on Human Rights guarantees the right of a person charged with a criminal offence to defend themselves through legal assistance of their own choosing or, if they have not sufficient means to pay, to be given it free when the interests of justice so require. Many national constitutions contain equivalent guarantees.
