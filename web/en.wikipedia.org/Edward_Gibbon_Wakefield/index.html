Edward Gibbon Wakefield

Edward Gibbon Wakefield (20 March 1796 – 16 May 1862) was a British politician and colonial theorist whose ideas of "systematic colonisation" shaped settlement in South Australia, New Zealand, and Canada. Karl Marx devoted the final chapter of Das Kapital to attacking his theory.

Edward Gibbon Wakefield

Early life and imprisonment

Born in London, Wakefield worked as a diplomatic courier and twice married heiresses by elopement; the second abduction — of 15-year-old Ellen Turner in 1826 — led to his trial and three years in Newgate Prison. There he turned to penal reform and colonial questions, writing his influential Letter from Sydney (1829) from prison, presenting it as the work of an emigrant settler.

Systematic colonisation

Wakefield argued that colonies failed when land was given away too cheaply, allowing labourers to become landowners at once and leaving no workforce. He proposed selling Crown land at a "sufficient price" high enough to delay that transition, and using the proceeds to fund assisted emigration — balancing land, labour, and capital in a self-financing system. His ideas drove the colonisation of South Australia (founded 1836) and the New Zealand Company settlements at Wellington, Nelson, New Plymouth, and Canterbury from 1840.

Later career

Wakefield accompanied Lord Durham to Canada in 1838 and influenced the Durham Report on responsible government. He served briefly in the Canadian assembly and later emigrated to New Zealand in 1853, entering politics in Wellington, but illness ended his public life. He died in Wellington in 1862. His colonisation schemes, while transformative for settler societies, facilitated the dispossession of Māori land and remain controversial.
