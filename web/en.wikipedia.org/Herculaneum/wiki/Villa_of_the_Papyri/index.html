Villa of the Papyri

The Villa of the Papyri (Italian: Villa dei Papiri) was an ancient Roman villa in Herculaneum, in what is now Ercolano in southern Italy. It is named after its unique library of papyrus scrolls, which was carbonised by the heat of pyroclastic flows during the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 AD. It is also thought to have been the residence of Julius Caesar's father-in-law, Lucius Calpurnius Piso Caesoninus.

Excavation

The villa was rediscovered in 1750 when workmen digging a well shaft struck its remains. Karl Weber directed tunnelling excavations during the 1750s and 1760s, recovering a large collection of bronze and marble statues, busts and busts of Greek philosophers and writers, as well as the villa's remarkable library. Most of the villa remains underground today.

The scrolls

The library contained some 1,800 scrolls, the only surviving library from the Greco-Roman world. Most texts are works of Epicurean philosophy, especially by Philodemus of Gadara, along with parts of Epicurus' On Nature. Carbonised by the eruption, the scrolls are extremely fragile; modern multispectral imaging and, more recently, X-ray tomography and machine-learning "virtual unwrapping" have allowed scholars to read texts without physically unrolling them.

Legacy

The J. Paul Getty Museum's Getty Villa in Malibu, California, is modelled on the Villa of the Papyri, using Weber's excavation plans as a template.
