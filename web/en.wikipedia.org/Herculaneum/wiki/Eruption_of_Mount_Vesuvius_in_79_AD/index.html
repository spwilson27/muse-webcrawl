Eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 AD

The eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 AD was one of the most catastrophic and deadliest volcanic eruptions in European history. The eruption ejected a cloud of stones, ashes and volcanic gases to a height of 33 km (21 mi), spewing molten rock and pulverized pumice at a rate of 600,000 cubic metres per second. More than 1,000 people are thought to have died in the eruption, though the exact toll is unknown. The only surviving eyewitness account of the event consists of two letters by Pliny the Younger to the historian Tacitus.

Course of the eruption

The eruption began around midday on the first day with a tall Plinian column of ash and pumice that drifted southeast over Pompeii. During the following night and early morning, pyroclastic surges and flows descended the mountain, destroying Herculaneum and reaching Pompeii. Pliny the Elder, commanding the Roman fleet at Misenum, sailed to rescue survivors and died at Stabiae.

Victims and rediscovery

At Pompeii, many died from thermal shock caused by pyroclastic surges; the hollows left by decomposed bodies in the hardened ash allowed archaeologists to make the famous plaster casts of victims. Pompeii and Herculaneum were lost until their rediscovery in the 18th century, beginning with Herculaneum in 1738 and Pompeii in 1748.
