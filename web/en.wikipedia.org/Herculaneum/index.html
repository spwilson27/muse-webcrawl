Herculaneum (Italian: Ercolano) was an ancient Roman town located in the modern-day comune of Ercolano, Campania, Italy. Herculaneum was destroyed and buried under volcanic ash and pumice in the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in AD 79. Like the nearby city of Pompeii, Herculaneum is famous as one of the few ancient cities to be preserved nearly intact, as the solidified material that encased the city protected it against looting and the elements.

Destruction

Unlike Pompeii, which was buried by falling ash and pumice, the seaside resort town of Herculaneum — wealthier and smaller, with perhaps 4,000–5,000 inhabitants — was overwhelmed in the early hours of the eruption by superheated pyroclastic surges and flows that carbonized wood and organic matter instantly and killed the population. At the ancient shoreline, more than 300 skeletons were found in boat chambers in the 1980s, showing that many residents had fled to the beach awaiting rescue by sea.

Excavation and preservation

Because the volcanic material hardened into a rock-like mass, Herculaneum's multi-storey buildings survived with upper floors, wooden doors, furniture, foodstuffs, and even a carbonized scroll library intact: the Villa of the Papyri, the largest known private house from the Roman world, contained some 1,800 papyrus scrolls, many now being read using X-ray phase-contrast imaging and virtual unwrapping. Only a fraction of the site has been excavated; the rest lies beneath the modern town of Ercolano.
