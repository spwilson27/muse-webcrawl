Centrifugal force

Centrifugal force is a fictitious force in Newtonian mechanics (also called an "inertial" or "pseudo" force) that appears to act on all objects when viewed in a rotating frame of reference. It is directed away from an axis of rotation which passes through the coordinate system's origin. If the axis passes through the origin of the coordinate system, the centrifugal force is directed radially outwards from that axis. Its magnitude is proportional to the mass of the object, the distance from the origin, and the square of the angular velocity of the frame.

Formula

F = m ω² r

where m is the mass of the object, ω is the angular velocity of the rotating frame, and r is the distance of the object from the axis of rotation. Equivalently, for an object moving at tangential speed v in a circle of radius r, the outward acceleration is v²/r, the same magnitude as the centripetal acceleration required to keep it in circular motion in an inertial frame.

Introduction

The concept arose in the seventeenth century: Christiaan Huygens used the term vis centrifuga in his work on circular motion, published in Horologium Oscillatorium (1673). Isaac Newton, in the Principia (1687), analysed the tension in a cord whirling a stone and treated the outward tendency as a reaction to the inward (centripetal) force exerted by the cord. In modern treatments, centrifugal force is not a "real" interaction force but a consequence of describing motion from a non-inertial, rotating viewpoint: within that frame it must be included for Newton's laws to give correct results.

Examples

Passengers in a turning car feel pressed against the outer door; in the car's rotating frame this is attributed to centrifugal force. Centrifuges separate substances by density by spinning samples at high speed. The Earth's rotation produces a centrifugal effect that slightly reduces effective gravity at the equator and contributes to the planet's equatorial bulge. Banked roadways and the artificial gravity proposed for rotating space stations both exploit the effect.

Relation to centripetal force

Centripetal force is the real, inward force (tension, gravity, friction, normal force) that causes circular motion in an inertial frame. Centrifugal force is its counterpart in the rotating frame, equal in magnitude and opposite in direction, but arising from the frame's rotation rather than from any physical interaction. Confusion between the two is a common source of error in elementary mechanics.
