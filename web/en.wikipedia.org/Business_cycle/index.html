Business cycle

The business cycle, also known as the economic cycle, is the pattern of expansion and contraction in economic activity around a long-term growth trend. It consists of alternating periods of growth (expansions or booms) and decline (contractions, culminating in recessions), measured by fluctuations in GDP, employment, income, and industrial production.

Phases

The cycle is conventionally divided into four phases: expansion, peak, contraction, and trough. In the United States, the National Bureau of Economic Research dates turning points; a popular rule of thumb defines a recession as two consecutive quarters of falling real GDP, though official dating considers a broader range of indicators.

Theories

Explanations of the cycle have included monetary and credit theories, Keynesian aggregate-demand fluctuations, real business cycle theory emphasising technology shocks, and financial theories such as Hyman Minsky's financial instability hypothesis. Cycles of differing length have been proposed, including the short Kitchin cycle, the Juglar investment cycle, and the long Kondratieff wave.

Policy

Governments and central banks attempt to smooth the cycle through fiscal policy and monetary policy, and automatic stabilisers such as unemployment insurance dampen fluctuations.
