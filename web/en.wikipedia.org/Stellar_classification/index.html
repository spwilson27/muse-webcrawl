Stellar classification

In astronomy, stellar classification is the classification of stars based on their spectral characteristics. Electromagnetic radiation from the star is analysed by splitting it with a prism or diffraction grating into a spectrum exhibiting the rainbow of colours interspersed with spectral lines. Each line indicates a particular chemical element or molecule, with the line strength reflecting its abundance and the star's temperature.

The modern Morgan–Keenan classification orders classes O, B, A, F, G, K, M from hottest to coolest (mnemonic: "Oh, Be A Fine Guy/Girl, Kiss Me"), subdivided by digits 0–9. Classes L, T, and Y were added for brown dwarfs. A luminosity class (I supergiants through V main sequence dwarfs) is appended, e.g. the Sun is G2 V.

History

The system grew from the Harvard classification developed by Edward Pickering and the "Harvard Computers", notably Annie Jump Cannon, who classified hundreds of thousands of stars for the Henry Draper Catalogue. Antonia Maury's finer scheme influenced the later luminosity classes. In 1943 W. W. Morgan, Philip Keenan, and Edith Kellman published the MKK atlas that standardised the two-dimensional system.

Relation to stellar properties

Spectral class correlates with position on the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram: class determines temperature and colour, while luminosity class reflects surface gravity and radius. Peculiar stars receive suffixes (e.g. "e" for emission lines, "m" for metallic lines).
