Lake Bonneville

Lake Bonneville was a vast prehistoric pluvial lake that occupied much of the eastern Great Basin of western North America during the late Pleistocene epoch. At its maximum, around 18,000 years ago, it covered approximately 51,000 square kilometres — an area comparable to modern Lake Michigan — and reached depths of about 300 metres. It was the largest of the many pluvial lakes formed in the region because of the cooler, wetter climate of the last ice age.

History

The lake rose and fell through several cycles as precipitation and evaporation changed. Distinct ancient shorelines are preserved on the surrounding mountainsides as wave-cut terraces; the best known are the Bonneville shoreline (the highest), the Provo shoreline, and the lower Stansbury and Gilbert shorelines. Around 14,500 years ago the lake, ponded behind an alluvial dam at Red Rock Pass in present-day Idaho, overtopped and catastrophically drained in the Bonneville flood — one of the largest known floods in the geologic record — which carved features along the Snake River in Idaho. The lake level dropped about 100 metres to the Provo shoreline, after which it continued to shrink as the climate warmed and dried.

Remnants

The modern remnants of Lake Bonneville include the Great Salt Lake, Utah Lake, and Sevier Lake in Utah. The famous Bonneville Salt Flats, used for land-speed records, are dried lakebed sediments of the ancient lake. The lake was named for U.S. Army officer Benjamin Bonneville, an early explorer of the American West, and its shorelines were systematically mapped by geologist Grove Karl Gilbert in the late 19th century.
