Pancoast tumor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Pancoast tumor, also called a superior sulcus tumor, is a tumor arising at the apex (top) of the lung, in the superior pulmonary sulcus. Most Pancoast tumors are non-small-cell lung cancers, particularly squamous cell carcinoma and adenocarcinoma. Because of their location, they invade nearby structures — the parietal pleura, ribs, vertebrae, nerves and the subclavian vessels — and produce a characteristic set of symptoms known as Pancoast syndrome (or Tobias syndrome).

Signs and symptoms

The typical presentation includes severe pain in the shoulder, radiating along the arm in the distribution of the ulnar nerve, together with atrophy of hand and arm muscles, destruction of the first and second ribs, and Horner's syndrome (ptosis, miosis, anhidrosis and apparent enophthalmos) caused by involvement of the sympathetic trunk. Compression of the subclavian vessels may cause swelling of the arm. Unlike many lung cancers, cough and breathlessness may be absent early on because the tumors arise in the periphery of the lung apex.

History

The syndrome was described in detail by the American radiologist Henry Pancoast, who published accounts of tumors of the superior pulmonary sulcus in 1924 and 1932; the tumors bear his name.

Treatment

Historically, Pancoast tumors were considered inoperable and fatal. Modern management typically combines chemotherapy and radiotherapy followed by surgical resection (trimodality therapy), which has substantially improved survival in patients whose tumors can be completely removed. Treatment planning requires careful imaging to assess invasion of the spine, brachial plexus and great vessels.
