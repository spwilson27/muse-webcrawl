Comparative psychology

Comparative psychology is the scientific study of the behavior and mental processes of non-human animals, especially as they relate to the phylogenetic history, adaptive significance, and development of behavior. It involves comparing species — including humans — to illuminate evolutionary continuities and differences in perception, learning, cognition, and social behavior.

History

The field traces its origins to Charles Darwin's work on evolution, particularly The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals (1872), and to George John Romanes's Animal Intelligence (1882). In the 20th century, comparative psychology in North America was strongly influenced by behaviorism and laboratory studies of learning in rats and pigeons, while European ethology, led by Konrad Lorenz and Nikolaas Tinbergen, emphasized observing animals in natural settings.

Research topics

Comparative psychologists study learning and conditioning, communication, tool use, problem solving, numerical and spatial cognition, self-recognition, theory of mind, emotions, and social organization across a wide range of species, from insects to primates. Well-known studies include Wolfgang Köhler's work on insight in chimpanzees, Irene Pepperberg's studies of parrot cognition, and research on the waggle dance of honeybees.

Relation to other fields

The discipline overlaps with ethology, behavioral ecology, evolutionary psychology, animal cognition, and neuroscience. Researchers caution against both anthropomorphism — attributing human traits to animals without evidence — and "anthropodenial," the a priori rejection of shared characteristics between humans and other animals.
