Four Asian Tigers

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Four Asian Tigers (also known as the Four Asian Dragons or Four Little Dragons in Chinese and Korean) are the developed East Asian economies of Hong Kong, Singapore, South Korea, and Taiwan. Between the early 1960s and 1990s, they underwent rapid industrialisation and maintained exceptionally high growth rates of more than 7 per cent a year, transforming themselves from low-income to high-income economies.

Development model

The Tigers pursued export-oriented industrialisation, moving from light manufactures such as textiles into steel, shipbuilding, electronics, and eventually high-technology and financial services. Shared features included heavy investment in education, high savings rates, land reform (in South Korea and Taiwan), macroeconomic stability, openness to foreign technology, and — in South Korea, Singapore, and Taiwan — active state guidance of industry through credit direction, state-owned enterprises, and industrial policy. All four benefited from favourable geography, disciplined labour forces, and access to the United States market during the Cold War.

Crisis and maturity

The 1997 Asian financial crisis hit South Korea severely (it required an IMF bailout) and affected Hong Kong and Singapore, while Taiwan weathered it comparatively well. All four economies subsequently returned to growth, though at the slower rates typical of advanced economies. Today all four are classified as advanced, high-income economies with world-leading positions in semiconductors (Taiwan and South Korea), finance and logistics (Hong Kong and Singapore), and consumer electronics.

Legacy

The Tigers' success made them models — and subjects of debate — in development economics, challenging both dependency theory and laissez-faire orthodoxy. A second tier of Southeast Asian economies, the "Tiger Cub Economies" (Indonesia, Malaysia, the Philippines, Thailand, and Vietnam), later attempted to follow the same path.
