Modified atmosphere


Application: Food packaging and storage
Common gases: Nitrogen, carbon dioxide, oxygen

Modified atmosphere packaging (MAP) is the practice of modifying the composition of the internal atmosphere of a package — usually of food, pharmaceuticals, or perishable goods — in order to improve shelf life. The atmosphere inside the package is altered by reducing oxygen and replacing it with gases such as nitrogen or carbon dioxide, slowing oxidation and the growth of aerobic micro-organisms.

Methods

In passive modified atmosphere, the product's own respiration and the permeability of the packaging film gradually establish an equilibrium atmosphere. In active MAP, the package is flushed with a chosen gas mixture or vacuum before sealing. Gas mixtures are tailored to the product: red meat is often packed in high-oxygen mixtures to maintain its colour, while bread and coffee are packed in nitrogen or carbon dioxide to inhibit mould.

History and uses

Controlled-atmosphere storage of fruit dates to research in the 1920s and 1930s in Britain, and modified-atmosphere shipping of apples and meat followed mid-century. MAP expanded rapidly from the 1970s with ready meals, fresh pasta, salads, and coffee. Oxygen scavengers and carbon dioxide emitters in sachets or labels can further adjust the atmosphere after packing.

Limitations

Modified atmosphere does not sterilize food; temperature control remains essential, and reducing oxygen can favour anaerobic pathogens such as Clostridium botulinum if other controls fail, so MAP is typically combined with refrigeration and other "hurdle" measures.
