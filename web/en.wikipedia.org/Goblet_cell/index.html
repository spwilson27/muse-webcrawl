Goblet cell

Goblet cells are simple columnar epithelial cells whose function is to secrete gel-forming mucins, the main component of mucus. They are named for their distinctive goblet shape: a narrow base containing the nucleus and a swollen apical portion (the theca) packed with mucin granules. Goblet cells are found scattered among other cells in the epithelia of the respiratory, gastrointestinal and reproductive tracts and in the conjunctiva of the eye.

Distribution and secretion

In the intestine, goblet cells increase in number from the small intestine toward the colon, where mucus forms a two-layered barrier separating gut bacteria from the epithelium. In the airways they contribute to the mucus blanket that traps inhaled particles and is swept away by cilia (the mucociliary escalator). Their principal product is the mucin MUC2 in the gut and MUC5AC and MUC5B in the airways, stored in granules and released by exocytosis in response to irritants, neurotransmitters and inflammatory signals, with a massive compound exocytosis available as an emergency flush.

Clinical significance

Goblet cell hyperplasia and excess mucus production are features of chronic bronchitis, asthma and cystic fibrosis. Conversely, loss of goblet cells and thinning of the mucus layer are seen in ulcerative colitis. Intestinal metaplasia of the stomach and oesophagus (Barrett's oesophagus) is defined partly by the appearance of goblet cells where they do not belong. Rare tumours called goblet cell adenocarcinomas arise mainly in the appendix.
