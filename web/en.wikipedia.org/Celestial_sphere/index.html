Celestial sphere

In astronomy and navigation, the celestial sphere is an abstract sphere that has an arbitrarily large radius and is concentric to Earth. All objects in the sky can be conceived as being projected upon the inner surface of the celestial sphere, which may be centred on Earth or the observer.

Features

The sphere is divided by the extensions of Earth's own geometry: the celestial equator is the projection of Earth's equator, and the north and south celestial poles extend Earth's axis of rotation. The ecliptic — the Sun's apparent annual path — is tilted about 23.4° to the celestial equator. Positions on the sphere are given by coordinates such as right ascension and declination (equatorial system) or altitude and azimuth (horizontal system).

History

Ancient astronomers, from the Babylonians and Greeks onward, modelled the heavens as a literal rotating sphere carrying the fixed stars, with the planets moving on nested spheres. Though replaced as physical reality by the heliocentric model, the celestial sphere remains an indispensable practical tool in positional astronomy and celestial navigation.

Use

Star charts, planispheres, and armillary spheres are all representations of the celestial sphere. Because the sphere rotates about the celestial poles once per sidereal day as seen from Earth, the visibility of objects depends on the observer's latitude and the time of year.
