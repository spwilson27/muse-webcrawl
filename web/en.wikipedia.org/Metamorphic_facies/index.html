Metamorphic facies

A metamorphic facies is a set of mineral assemblages in metamorphic rocks that formed under similar pressures and temperatures. The concept, developed by the Finnish geologist Pentti Eskola in the early twentieth century, allows geologists to infer the conditions under which a rock was metamorphosed from the minerals it contains, whatever its bulk composition.

The facies series

The principal facies, in order of increasing temperature and (roughly) pressure, include the zeolite facies (lowest grade), prehnite–pumpellyite facies, greenschist facies, amphibolite facies, granulite facies, blueschist facies, eclogite facies, and the hornfels facies of contact metamorphism. Each is named for the characteristic minerals formed in a basaltic rock under those conditions: a basalt metamorphosed in the greenschist facies becomes a rock rich in chlorite, actinolite and epidote, while in the amphibolite facies it becomes hornblende plus plagioclase, and in the eclogite facies the dense pair garnet plus omphacite.

Pressure–temperature significance

Different tectonic settings produce characteristic sequences of facies, called facies series. Subduction zones, where cold oceanic crust descends rapidly, produce the high-pressure, low-temperature blueschist–eclogite series; mountain belts show the medium-pressure Barrovian series; and zones of high heat flow produce low-pressure series. The paired metamorphic belts of Japan, where high-pressure and low-pressure series occur side by side, were key evidence for plate tectonics.

Index minerals

Diagnostic (index) minerals let geologists map metamorphic grade in the field. The appearance of minerals such as staurolite, kyanite or sillimanite in pelitic rocks marks increasing grade, while the change from kyanite to sillimanite marks crossing into higher temperatures.
