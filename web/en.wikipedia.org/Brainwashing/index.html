Brainwashing

Brainwashing (also known as mind control, coercive persuasion or thought reform) is the concept that the human mind can be altered or controlled by certain psychological techniques such that the subject adopts beliefs and behaviours imposed by an agent. The term was popularised in English in the 1950s by journalist Edward Hunter, translating the Chinese xǐnǎo ("wash brain"), in reporting on the treatment of prisoners during and after the Korean War.

Origins and study

Western alarm grew when some American prisoners of war in Korea issued confessions and propaganda statements, and a small number initially refused repatriation. Psychiatrists Robert Jay Lifton and Edgar Schein studied re-education programmes in Maoist China and described processes of milieu control, confession, guilt induction and identity disruption. During the Cold War, intelligence agencies also experimented with coercive techniques, most notoriously in the CIA's MKUltra programme.

Scientific and legal debates

Mainstream psychology is divided over whether "brainwashing" constitutes a distinct, powerful form of influence beyond ordinary persuasion, coercion and socialisation. Lifton's criteria of thought reform have been applied to high-demand groups and cults, while critics such as Dick Anthony argued that the concept lacks scientific support; courts have often excluded brainwashing testimony, and the American Psychological Association declined to endorse a 1980s task-force report on "deceptive and indirect methods of persuasion and control."

In popular culture

The idea has a lasting hold on the public imagination, from The Manchurian Candidate to "deprogramming" controversies of the 1970s, and the word is now used loosely for any strong indoctrination or propaganda.
