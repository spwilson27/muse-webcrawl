A water clock or clepsydra (from Greek κλεψύδρα, "water thief") is a timepiece by which time is measured by the regulated flow of liquid into (inflow type) or out of (outflow type) a vessel, where the amount is then marked against a scale. Water clocks are among the earliest time-measuring instruments, independent of the observation of the Sun, and were used in Egypt, Mesopotamia, Persia, Greece, Rome, India, China and Korea for over two thousand years.

History

The oldest surviving water clock, found in the tomb precinct of Amenhotep I and dated to about 1500 BC, is an alabaster outflow vessel from Karnak whose interior markings divided the night into hours. Babylonian and Egyptian astronomers used water clocks to time night observations. Greek and Roman courts used clepsydrae to limit speeches; the word became proverbial for a timed allowance.

The Hellenistic engineer Ctesibius of Alexandria (3rd century BC) greatly improved accuracy with a constant-head regulator and added automata, dials and pointers. Roman water clocks were described by Vitruvius. In China, water clocks were combined with escapement mechanisms by Yi Xing and Zhang Sixun in the Tang and Song dynasties, culminating in Su Song's great astronomical clock tower at Kaifeng (1094), driven by a waterwheel with an escapement. Elaborate water clocks were also built in the Islamic world, notably by Al-Jazari in the 13th century.

Operation and limits

Flow rate varies with water pressure, so sophisticated designs used overflow regulators to keep a constant head, floats with racks and pinions, or conical vessels compensating for falling pressure. Temperature changes, viscosity and evaporation limited precision, and water clocks were eventually superseded by verge-and-foliot mechanical clocks in 14th-century Europe and by pendulum clocks in the 17th century. In Korea, Jang Yeong-sil's self-striking clepsydra (1434) remained a national standard timekeeper for centuries.
