Can-can

The can-can (also spelled cancan) is a high-energy, physically demanding dance that became a popular music-hall entertainment in the 1840s and remains a fixture of French cabaret. It is traditionally performed by a chorus line of female dancers in long skirts, petticoats, and black stockings, executing high kicks, splits, and skirt manipulations to a fast 2/4 tempo. The dance is indelibly associated with Parisian venues such as the Moulin Rouge and with the music of Jacques Offenbach, particularly the "Galop infernal" from Orpheus in the Underworld.

History

The can-can emerged in the working-class dance halls of Paris in the 1830s and 1840s, evolving from the final figure of the quadrille, in which dancers improvised exuberant, acrobatic steps. At first danced by both men and women, it scandalised respectable society with its raised skirts and provocative gestures; celebrated performers such as La Goulue and Jane Avril, immortalised in the posters of Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec, became stars of the fin-de-siècle cabaret. Male can-can dancers also enjoyed vogues of popularity, notably the acrobatic Valentin le Désossé.

Performance

The classic can-can is performed to a gallop, usually Offenbach's. Choreography features the high kick (battement), the rond de jambe, cartwheels, jump splits, and the port d'armes, in which a dancer grasps her ankle and rotates. Costumes — long, layered skirts lifted to display ruffled underwear and stockings — are central to the effect. Despite its risqué reputation, the can-can in its classic venues was carefully choreographed revue entertainment, and it survives today at the Moulin Rouge and in revues and films worldwide.
