Stereotype

In social psychology, a stereotype is a generalised belief about a particular category of people. It is an expectation that people might have about every person of a particular group. The type of expectation can vary; it can be, for example, an expectation about the group's personality, preferences, appearance or ability. Stereotypes are sometimes overgeneralised, inaccurate, and resistant to new information, but can sometimes be accurate.

Origin of the term

The word comes from the French stéréotype, from Greek στερεός (stereos), "firm, solid" and τύπος (typos), "impression" — originally a printing plate that duplicated text. Journalist Walter Lippmann popularised the psychological sense in Public Opinion (1922), calling stereotypes "pictures in our heads".

Relation to prejudice and discrimination

Stereotypes (beliefs), prejudice (feelings), and discrimination (behaviour) are related but distinct components of intergroup attitudes. Research distinguishes explicit stereotypes, consciously endorsed, from implicit stereotypes measured by tools such as the Implicit Association Test.

Content and function

The stereotype content model (Fiske and colleagues) organises group stereotypes along two dimensions — warmth and competence — producing admiration, pity, envy, or contempt. Stereotypes serve cognitive economy, help justify existing hierarchies (system justification), and can shape the targets' behaviour through stereotype threat and self-fulfilling prophecies.

Accuracy debate

Some researchers argue stereotypes often contain a "kernel of truth" and rank among social psychology's more replicable effects; others emphasise their role in sustaining inequality and the harms of applying group generalisations to individuals.
