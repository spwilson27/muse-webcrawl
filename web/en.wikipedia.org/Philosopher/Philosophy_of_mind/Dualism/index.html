Dualism

In the philosophy of mind, dualism is a family of views holding that the mental and the physical — mind and body, or mind and brain — are fundamentally distinct kinds of things. Its most famous form is the substance dualism of René Descartes, according to which the mind is a non-physical, thinking substance (res cogitans) and the body an extended physical substance (res extensa).

Types of dualism

Substance dualism holds that mind and matter are two distinct substances. Property dualism holds that there is only one kind of substance, but that mental properties are irreducible to physical properties; versions include epiphenomenalism, in which mental states are caused by physical events but cause nothing themselves, and the emergentism associated with David Chalmers's "naturalistic dualism," which treats consciousness as a fundamental feature of the world.

Arguments and problems

Dualists argue from the apparent privacy and qualia of experience, the conceivability of minds without bodies, and the "hard problem of consciousness." The classic objection is the interaction problem, pressed on Descartes by Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia: how can a non-spatial, non-physical mind causally affect a physical body? Critics also cite the dependence of mental life on the brain — shown by injury, drugs, and development — and the causal closure of physics. Despite being a minority view among contemporary philosophers, dualism continues to be developed and debated.

History

Ideas of a soul distinct from the body appear in Plato and in many religious traditions. Descartes gave dualism its canonical modern form in the Meditations (1641), locating interaction at the pineal gland. Later defenders and critics include Malebranche, Leibniz, Gilbert Ryle — whose phrase "the ghost in the machine" attacked the Cartesian picture — and modern proponents such as Richard Swinburne.
