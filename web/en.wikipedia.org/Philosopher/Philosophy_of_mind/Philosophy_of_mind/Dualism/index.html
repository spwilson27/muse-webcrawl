Dualism (philosophy of mind)

In the philosophy of mind, dualism is the theory that the mental and the physical – or mind and body – are, in some sense, radically different kinds of thing. Its most famous form is the substance dualism of René Descartes, who held that the mind is a non-extended, thinking substance (res cogitans) distinct from the extended material substance of the body (res extensa).

Varieties

Substance dualism posits two kinds of substance, mental and physical. Property dualism holds that there is one kind of substance but two kinds of property, with mental properties not reducible to physical ones; it is associated with arguments such as the "hard problem" of consciousness and philosophical zombies. Predicate and epistemological dualisms make weaker claims about language or knowledge.

Criticism

The classic objection to interactionist dualism is the problem of mental causation: if mind and body are wholly distinct, it is unclear how either can causally affect the other, as they seem to do. Descartes proposed the pineal gland as the point of interaction, a suggestion widely ridiculed even in his lifetime. Most contemporary philosophers of mind favour forms of physicalism, though dualist positions continue to be defended.
