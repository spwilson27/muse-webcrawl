René Descartes

René Descartes (31 March 1596 – 11 February 1650) was a French philosopher, scientist, and mathematician, widely considered a seminal figure in the emergence of modern philosophy and science. Often called the father of modern Western philosophy, Descartes laid the foundation for 17th-century continental rationalism, later advocated by Spinoza and Leibniz, and opposed by the empiricist school of thought consisting of Hobbes, Locke, Berkeley, and Hume.

Philosophy

Descartes is best known for his statement Cogito, ergo sum ("I think, therefore I am"), which appears in his Discourse on the Method (1637) and Principles of Philosophy (1644). Seeking a foundation of knowledge immune to doubt, he subjected all his beliefs to methodical doubt, finding that the very act of doubting proved his existence as a thinking thing.

Mind–body dualism

In the Meditations on First Philosophy (1641), Descartes argued that the mind is a non-extended, thinking substance, while the body is an extended, non-thinking substance. This substance dualism raised the enduring problem of how the two substances interact.

Mathematics and science

Descartes developed the Cartesian coordinate system, allowing geometric shapes to be expressed algebraically, and contributed to optics, including the law of refraction. His philosophical and scientific ideas shaped the course of Western thought for centuries.
