Philosophy of mind

Philosophy of mind is the branch of philosophy that studies the nature of the mind, mental events, mental functions, mental properties, and consciousness, and their relationship to the physical body — the mind–body problem. It asks what it is for a system to have experiences, beliefs, and intentions, and how these relate to neurons and behavior.

Central problems

The field is organized around several hard questions. The mind–body problem concerns whether mental states are identical to, reducible to, or distinct from physical states. The hard problem of consciousness, named by David Chalmers, asks why physical processing is accompanied by subjective experience — qualia — at all. The problem of intentionality concerns how thoughts can be about things, and the problem of mental causation asks how mental states can cause physical actions if the physical world is causally closed.

Major positions

Dualism separates mind and matter, in Cartesian substance form or in modern property-dualist form. Physicalism holds that everything mental is physical; its variants include behaviorism, the identity theory of Place and Smart, functionalism — which defines mental states by their causal roles and underlies much of cognitive science — and eliminative materialism. Other positions include panpsychism, neutral monism, and idealism. Arguments that structure the debate include the knowledge argument (Mary the color scientist), the conceivability of philosophical zombies, and Nagel's question of what it is like to be a bat.

Relations to science

Philosophy of mind interacts closely with psychology, neuroscience, linguistics, and artificial intelligence, informing debates about animal consciousness, machine minds, personal identity, free will, and the self.
