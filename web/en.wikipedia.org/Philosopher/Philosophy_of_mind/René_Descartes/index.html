René Descartes (31 March 1596 – 11 February 1650) was a French philosopher, mathematician, and scientist, widely regarded as a founder of modern philosophy and of analytic geometry. His statement cogito, ergo sum ("I think, therefore I am") and his dualism of mind and body set the agenda for philosophy of mind for centuries.

Life

Descartes was educated by the Jesuits at La Flèche and studied law at Poitiers before traveling and serving briefly as a gentleman soldier. From 1628 he lived in the Dutch Republic, where in quiet retirement he wrote his major works, including the Discourse on the Method (1637) and the Meditations on First Philosophy (1641). In 1649 he accepted an invitation to the court of Queen Christina of Sweden; the cold climate and early-morning lessons undermined his health, and he died in Stockholm in February 1650.

Philosophy

Seeking certainty, Descartes subjected all his beliefs to methodical doubt, finding an indubitable foundation in the thinking self. From the cogito he argued for the existence of God and the external world, and distinguished the thinking substance of mind from the extended substance of body — mind–body dualism — while proposing the pineal gland as the point of their interaction. His mechanistic physics treated the material world, including animal bodies, as machinery.

Mathematics and science

Descartes's La Géométrie united algebra and geometry through the coordinate system that bears his name (Cartesian coordinates), making modern analytic geometry possible. He also contributed to optics, formulating the law of refraction independently, and to the theory of the rainbow.
