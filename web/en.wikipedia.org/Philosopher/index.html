Philosopher

A philosopher is a person who practices philosophy: the study of general and fundamental questions concerning existence, reason, knowledge, values, mind, and language. The word derives from the ancient Greek philosophos (φιλόσοφος), "lover of wisdom", a term traditionally credited to Pythagoras, who is said to have called himself not a sage (sophos) but merely a lover of wisdom.

History

In ancient Greece, philosophers such as Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle investigated nature, ethics, and logic in ways that laid the foundations of Western thought; comparable traditions arose independently in India (the Upanishadic thinkers, the Buddha, Mahavira) and China (Confucius, Laozi, Mozi). In the medieval period, philosophy was largely pursued within religious institutions — by figures such as Augustine, Aquinas, Avicenna, Averroes, and Maimonides — while the early modern period saw figures like René Descartes, Locke, Hume, and Kant define new approaches to knowledge and the mind.

Modern practice

Since the nineteenth century, philosophy has been professionalised within universities, and "philosopher" most commonly denotes an academic who researches and teaches in fields such as metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, logic, aesthetics, and the philosophy of mind. Philosophers employ methods including conceptual analysis, thought experiments, and argument, and their work informs law, science, and public life, though the label is also applied to influential thinkers outside the academy.
