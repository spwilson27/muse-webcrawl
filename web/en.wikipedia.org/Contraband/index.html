Contraband

Contraband (from Medieval French contrebande, “smuggling”) is any good that is illegal to possess, trade, or transport. The term covers goods that are inherently unlawful (such as narcotics or unlicensed weapons) and goods that are lawful in themselves but traded in defiance of taxes, embargoes or blockades.

Contraband of war

In international law, contraband of war is cargo that a belligerent may seize when carried to an enemy in wartime. The Declaration of Paris (1856) and the Hague Conventions distinguished absolute contraband (arms and munitions) from conditional contraband (goods such as food and fuel usable for either war or peace). Neutral shipping carrying contraband could be stopped, searched and its cargo confiscated by prize courts.

American Civil War usage

During the American Civil War, enslaved people who escaped to Union lines were declared “contraband of war” by General Benjamin Butler at Fort Monroe in 1861 — a legal fiction that freed them from return to their owners and foreshadowed emancipation.

Modern usage

Today the word is most often used for smuggled goods in prisons, at borders, and in customs law, where contraband is subject to seizure and forfeiture.
