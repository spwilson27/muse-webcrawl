Oxy-fuel welding and cutting

Oxy-fuel welding (commonly called oxyacetylene welding) and oxy-fuel cutting are processes that use fuel gases combined with oxygen to weld or cut metals. French engineers Edmond Fouché and Charles Picard developed oxygen-acetylene welding in 1903.

Equipment

The apparatus consists of pressurised cylinders of oxygen and a fuel gas (usually acetylene), regulators, hoses, check valves, and a torch. Acetylene burned in pure oxygen produces a flame of about 3,500 °C (6,330 °F), hot enough to melt steel.

Welding

In welding, the flame melts the edges of the workpieces and, usually, a filler rod, forming a weld pool that solidifies into a joint. Flux may be used to prevent oxidation.

Cutting

In oxy-fuel cutting, the metal is heated to ignition temperature and then a high-pressure jet of oxygen oxidises it rapidly, blowing the molten oxide away and severing the material. The process is widely used for cutting steel plate and for demolition, though it is largely restricted to ferrous metals.

Safety

Hazards include flashbacks into hoses and cylinders, which are mitigated by flashback arrestors, and the instability of acetylene at high pressures, which limits its storage conditions.
