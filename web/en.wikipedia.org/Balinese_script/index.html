The Balinese script, natively known as Aksara Bali and Hanacaraka, is an abugida used in the island of Bali, Indonesia, to write the Balinese language, Old Javanese, and Sanskrit. It descends, through the Old Kawi script of Java, from the Pallava and ultimately Brahmi scripts of India. Its flowing, ornamental letterforms traditionally were incised with a knife on prepared palm leaves (lontar), which were then blackened with soot to make the text legible.

Structure

Like other Brahmic scripts, each consonant letter carries an inherent vowel /a/; other vowels are written with dependent signs placed above, below, before or after the consonant, and the inherent vowel is suppressed by a killer sign (adeg-adeg). The basic inventory comprises 18 consonant letters (wyanjana), vowel letters (swara) and modified forms (warga) with diacritics (pangangge). A fuller, more elaborate set of characters, including aspirated and retroflex letters, is used for writing Sanskrit and Old Javanese loanwords. Numerals, punctuation and special sacred symbols (modre) also form part of the system.

Use

Balinese script remains in use for religious texts, ritual calendars, traditional literature, genealogies and signage, and it is taught in schools in Bali, though the Latin alphabet dominates everyday writing. The script was encoded in the Unicode Standard in 2006 (version 5.0).
