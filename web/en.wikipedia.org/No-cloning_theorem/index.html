No-cloning theorem

In physics, the no-cloning theorem states that it is impossible to create an independent and identical copy of an arbitrary unknown quantum state. It was stated by William Wootters and Wojciech Zurek in 1982 (and independently by Dennis Dieks), and follows directly from the linearity of quantum mechanics.

Proof sketch

Suppose a unitary operation U could copy any state: U|ψ⟩|0⟩ = |ψ⟩|ψ⟩ for every |ψ⟩. Applying this requirement to two states |ψ⟩ and |φ⟩ and taking inner products forces ⟨ψ|φ⟩ = ⟨ψ|φ⟩², which holds only if the states are identical or orthogonal. Hence no single device can clone arbitrary states; cloning is possible only for a fixed set of mutually orthogonal states, as with classical information.

Implications

The theorem rules out faster-than-light communication via entanglement, constrains quantum error correction (which must avoid copying data directly), and underpins the security of quantum key distribution, since an eavesdropper cannot copy the transmitted quantum states without disturbing them. Approximate and probabilistic cloning machines remain possible within limits — a theme developed in the related no-broadcast theorem.
