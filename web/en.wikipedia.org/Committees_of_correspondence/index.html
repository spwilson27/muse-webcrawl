Committees of correspondence

The committees of correspondence were a network of committees established in the American colonies in the years before and during the American Revolution to coordinate opposition to British policies. By exchanging letters and pamphlets, the committees spread news of British actions, rallied public opinion, and helped unify the Thirteen Colonies.

History

Early committees formed in response to the Stamp Act crisis of 1764–1765. In November 1772 Samuel Adams and Joseph Warren persuaded the Boston town meeting to create a committee of correspondence that published the rights of the colonists and grievances against Britain; more than 260 Massachusetts towns formed similar committees. In March 1773 the Virginia House of Burgesses, acting on a proposal associated with Dabney Carr, established the first intercolonial standing committee, and the other colonies soon followed.

By 1774 the committees were coordinating the election of delegates to the First Continental Congress, enforcing boycotts of British goods, and, through committees of inspection and safety, gradually displacing royal government in much of America.
