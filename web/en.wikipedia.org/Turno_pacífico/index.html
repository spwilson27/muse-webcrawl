Turno pacífico

The turno pacífico alternated power between Antonio Cánovas del Castillo's Conservatives and Práxedes Mateo Sagasta's Liberals.

The turno pacífico ("peaceful turn" or "peaceful alternation") was the political system of Restoration Spain, operating roughly from 1881 (and formalised by the Pact of El Pardo in 1885) until the collapse of the regime in the 1920s, by which the two main dynastic parties — the Conservative Party of Antonio Cánovas del Castillo and the Liberal Party of Práxedes Mateo Sagasta — agreed to alternate in government, taking turns in power rather than contesting elections genuinely.

How it worked

Under the turno, the monarch would entrust the formation of a government to the leader of one of the two parties. The incoming prime minister would then dissolve the Cortes and call elections — which his government would arrange to win. Results were managed from the Ministry of the Interior through the encasillado (the pre-arranged slate of approved candidates) and implemented in the provinces by local political bosses, the caciques, who delivered the required majorities through patronage, coercion and falsification, especially in rural districts. Elections therefore ratified a change of government already decided in Madrid, rather than causing it.

Purpose and effects

Cánovas designed the system after the instability of the preceding decades — pronunciamientos, the First Republic and the Carlist wars — to give Spain governmental stability, reconcile the army and the former moderates and progressives to the restored Bourbon monarchy of Alfonso XII, and keep revolutionary and anti-dynastic forces out of power. It succeeded in stabilising the regime for a generation, but at the cost of hollowing out representative politics: turnout and genuine competition were low, regional and working-class movements (Catalan and Basque nationalism, socialism, anarchism) were excluded, and public cynicism about "la farsa" (the farce) grew.

Decline

After the deaths of Cánovas (assassinated 1897) and Sagasta (1903), the parties fragmented into personal factions, making the smooth rotation harder to manage. The disaster of 1898, the crisis of 1917, and the rise of mass politics progressively destroyed the turno's workability; the coup of Miguel Primo de Rivera in 1923 ended the Restoration system altogether.
