Panathenaea

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Panathenaea (Ancient Greek: Παναθήναια) was the most important religious festival of ancient Athens, held in honour of the city's patron goddess Athena. Celebrated annually in the month of Hekatombaion (roughly July–August), it was held with greatly enlarged splendour every fourth year as the Great Panathenaea.

The festival

The centrepiece was a grand procession through the city — along the Panathenaic Way, from the Dipylon Gate through the Agora to the Acropolis — in which citizens, metics, colonists and allies' representatives carried offerings to Athena. A new embroidered robe (peplos), woven over nine months by Athenian women and girls and depicting the Gigantomachy, was presented to the ancient olive-wood cult image of Athena Polias; a great hecatomb of cattle at the altar was followed by the distribution of sacrificial meat.

Games and contests

According to tradition reorganised by Peisistratus in 566/5 BC, the Great Panathenaea included musical and rhapsodic contests (recitations of Homer), gymnastic events in the Panathenaic Stadium, equestrian races, and tribal team events such as the torch race and the euandria. Athletic victors received Panathenaic amphorae — distinctive black-figure jars filled with olive oil from the sacred trees of Athena, bearing the goddess's image and the inscription "from the games at Athens"; these prize amphorae continued to be made in the black-figure technique long after it fell from general use.

Significance

The festival displayed Athens' wealth, piety and imperial reach to the Greek world. Its procession is generally taken to be the subject of the Parthenon frieze. The Panathenaea continued into late antiquity, fading in the 4th–5th centuries AD as the city Christianised.
