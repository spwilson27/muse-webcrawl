Timbre

Timbre (pronounced roughly "TAM-ber"), also known as tone colour or tone quality, is the perceived sound quality of a musical note, sound or tone. It is what distinguishes two sounds that have the same pitch and loudness — for instance, a violin and a flute playing the same note — and is often described with words such as bright, dark, warm, harsh or mellow. The American Standards Association defined timbre negatively, as that attribute of auditory sensation by which a listener can judge two sounds of equal pitch and loudness to be dissimilar.

Physical basis

Timbre arises principally from the spectrum of a sound and how it changes over time. Different instruments produce different sets of harmonics (overtones) with different relative strengths, and the amplitude envelope — the attack, decay, sustain and release of the note — strongly affects perception: removing the attack transient of a piano tone, for example, can make it hard to identify as a piano. Noise components (breath, bow friction, hammer strikes), vibrato, and inharmonicity in stiff strings also contribute.

Perception and analysis

Psychoacoustic research represents timbre as a multidimensional perceptual space. Studies using multidimensional scaling, from John Grey's work in the 1970s onwards, find dimensions related to spectral centroid (brightness), attack time, and spectral flux — how the spectrum varies during the sound. Timbre is central to orchestration, instrument recognition, singing and speech (vowel quality depends on formants), and to electronic music, where synthesizers and samplers manipulate spectra and envelopes to design new tone colours. In German music theory the idea was developed as Klangfarbe, and works such as Schoenberg's speculated on melodies of tone colour (Klangfarbenmelodie).
