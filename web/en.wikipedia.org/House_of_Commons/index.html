House of Commons

The House of Commons is the name for the elected lower house of a bicameral parliament. In some countries it is the more powerful house, able to override the upper house and form governments. The term originates in England, where the "commons" — communities and boroughs — sent representatives to Parliament, distinct from the lords spiritual and temporal who formed the House of Lords.

The House of Commons chamber at the Palace of Westminster, London.

Houses of Commons around the world

United Kingdom: The House of Commons of the United Kingdom is the lower house of the Parliament of the United Kingdom, consisting of 650 Members of Parliament (MPs) elected by the first-past-the-post system in single-member constituencies. The government is drawn from, and accountable to, the Commons, and the prime minister must retain its confidence.

Canada: The House of Commons of Canada is the lower house of the Parliament of Canada, with 338 members (increased to 343 following redistribution) elected in ridings. It sits in Ottawa and, as in the United Kingdom, is the dominant house in legislation and supply.

Ireland: Historically, the Irish House of Commons existed until 1801; the modern lower house of the Oireachtas is Dáil Éireann.

Title of members

Members of a House of Commons are typically styled "Member of Parliament" (MP). The presiding officer is the Speaker, who maintains order and interprets the rules of the house. In the British tradition, members address each other indirectly through the Speaker and refer to one another as "the honourable member" for a constituency.
