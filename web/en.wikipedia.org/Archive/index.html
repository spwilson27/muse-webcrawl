Archive

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An archive is an accumulation of historical records or materials — in any medium — or the physical facility in which they are located. Archives contain primary source documents that have accumulated over the course of an individual or organisation's lifetime, and are kept to show the history and function of that person or organisation. In general, archives consist of records that have been selected for permanent or long-term preservation on the grounds of their enduring cultural, historical, or evidentiary value. Archival records are normally unpublished and almost always unique, unlike books or magazines, for which many identical copies may exist.

Terminology and scope

The word derives from the Greek arkheîon (the office of the archons, where official records were kept). Archivists distinguish archives from libraries: library materials are usually published and catalogued item-by-item, whereas archives are unpublished records arranged according to their origin — the principles of provenance and original order (respect des fonds) — and described hierarchically from fonds to series to file to item. A person who works in archives is called an archivist. The discipline concerned with the appraisal, arrangement, description, preservation, and provision of access to archives is archival science.

Types

Archives are maintained by governments (national archives such as the UK National Archives and the US National Archives and Records Administration), municipalities, universities, religious bodies, businesses, charities, families, and individuals. Specialised archives preserve particular formats — film and sound archives, photographic archives, and increasingly born-digital records and web archives. Community archives, often created by marginalised groups, have challenged traditional collecting priorities.

Functions and practice

Archival work includes appraisal (selecting the small fraction of records with permanent value), acquisition, arrangement and description (finding aids, standards such as ISAD(G) and Encoded Archival Description), preservation (environmental control, conservation, digitisation, and digital preservation against format obsolescence), and reference and outreach. Archivists also manage access restrictions arising from privacy, data protection, and national security law. Digital-age challenges include the volume of email and records in cloud systems, authenticity and fixity of digital objects, and the appraisal of social media.

History

Systematic state archives date from ancient administrations; the modern archival profession emerged in the 19th century with institutions such as France's Archives nationales (1790, during the Revolution, when archives were declared a public resource open to citizens) and the École des Chartes. The Dutch Manual of Muller, Feith, and Fruin (1898) and the work of Sir Hilary Jenkinson and T. R. Schellenberg codified modern theory in the 20th century. In computing, the word "archive" has broadened to cover any stored collection of data, including web archiving projects such as the Internet Archive (founded 1996).
