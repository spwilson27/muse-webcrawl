Microarchitecture

In computer engineering, microarchitecture, also called computer organization and sometimes abbreviated as µarch or uarch, is the way a given instruction set architecture (ISA) is implemented in a particular processor. A given ISA may be implemented with different microarchitectures; implementations may vary due to different goals of a given design or due to shifts in technology.

Relation to instruction set architecture

The ISA is roughly the programmer's view of a computer, whereas microarchitecture describes the internal structure that realises it: datapaths, execution units, pipelines, caches, buffers, registers and control logic. For example, Intel's Skylake, AMD's Zen, and Apple's Firestorm are distinct microarchitectures implementing the x86-64 and ARM64 instruction sets, respectively. The same microarchitecture may be fabricated in different process technologies or reused across product families with different cache sizes and clock speeds.

Elements

  • Pipelining — overlapping the fetch, decode, execute, memory access and writeback stages of successive instructions.
  • Superscalar execution — issuing multiple instructions per clock cycle to parallel execution units.
  • Out-of-order execution — reordering instructions dynamically (e.g. with register renaming and reservation stations) to keep units busy.
  • Branch prediction — guessing the outcome of conditional branches to keep the pipeline filled, including speculative execution, whose side channels enabled the Spectre vulnerabilities disclosed in 2018.
  • Cache hierarchy — levels of SRAM (L1, L2, L3) buffering main memory, plus translation lookaside buffers for virtual memory.
  • Simultaneous multithreading — sharing one core's resources between two or more hardware threads.

Modern microarchitecture design is driven by performance, power efficiency and transistor budgets as automatic gains from process scaling have slowed, leading to domain-specific accelerators and heterogeneous designs.
