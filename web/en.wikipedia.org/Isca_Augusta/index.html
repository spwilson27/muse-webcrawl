Isca Augusta, also known as Isca Silurum, was a Roman legionary fortress in Britannia, founded around AD 74–75 on the River Usk in what is now Caerleon, near Newport in south Wales. It was the permanent base of the Legio II Augusta for over two centuries and is one of the most important Roman military sites in Britain.

History

The fortress was established during the campaigns of governor Sextus Julius Frontinus against the Silures tribe. Initially built in timber, it was rebuilt in stone from about AD 100, enclosing roughly 50 acres with barracks, headquarters (principia), commanding officer's house, granaries, workshops, a military bath-house and a large amphitheatre outside the walls. The legion also operated a port on the Usk and drew supplies through a network of forts in south Wales.

Legio II Augusta contributed detachments to the building of Hadrian's Wall, and part of the legion may have left Isca in the later third century as the fortress declined. Occupation dwindled in the early fourth century. In Welsh tradition Caerleon — "fortress of the legion" — became associated with King Arthur, notably in Geoffrey of Monmouth's writings.

Archaeology

Excavations have revealed the only fully visible Roman legionary barracks in Europe (Prysg Field), one of the best-preserved amphitheatres in Britain, and extensive bath remains displayed at the National Roman Legion Museum, established in 1850. The site is managed by Cadw and Amgueddfa Cymru.
