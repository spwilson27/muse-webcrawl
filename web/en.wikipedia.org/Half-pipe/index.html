A half-pipe is a structure used in gravity extreme sports such as snowboarding, skateboarding, skiing, freestyle BMX, skating, and scooter riding. The structure resembles a cross-section of a swimming pool: essentially half a pipe cut lengthwise, with two concave ramps (transitions) topped by vertical sections (vert) facing each other across a flat bottom. Riders traverse back and forth, gaining speed and performing aerial tricks at each wall.

History

Half-pipes originated in 1970s skateboarding, when Californian skaters rode empty swimming pools and drainage ditches; the first purpose-built wooden half-pipes appeared by the late 1970s. The form transferred to snow in the 1980s as snowboarders dug snow halfpipes, and machine-shaped pipes groomed with specialised equipment (such as the Zaugg Pipe Monster) standardised the modern snow superpipe. Snowboard halfpipe became an Olympic event in 1998 and freestyle skiing halfpipe in 2014; skateboarding's Olympic debut in 2020 used the related park (bowl) format.

Structure and riding

The transitions ("trannies") curve from the flat bottom to vertical lips; the brief vertical section lets riders launch straight up ("air") and re-enter cleanly. By pumping through the transitions riders convert potential to kinetic energy and can increase height with each wall. Tricks include airs, grabs, spins (such as the McTwist and the 1080/1260 rotations), inverts and handplants on the coping, and lip tricks. Competition snow superpipes are 22 feet (6.7 m) high — larger than standard 18-foot pipes — while classic wooden skate ramps are usually 11–13 feet.
