Nabis (art)

The Nabis (French: les nabis, from the Hebrew word for "prophets") were a group of young French artists active in Paris in the 1890s who played a major role in the transition from Impressionism and academic art towards abstract art, symbolism, and the early modern movements.

Origins

The group formed around Paul Sérusier, who in 1888 painted a small landscape at Pont-Aven under the guidance of Paul Gauguin, using bold, unnatural colour. Back in Paris he showed the work — later called The Talisman — to fellow students at the Académie Julian, including Pierre Bonnard, Édouard Vuillard, Maurice Denis, Ker-Xavier Roussel, and Paul Ranson.

Style and work

Influenced by Gauguin, Japanese prints, and medieval art, the Nabis favoured flat areas of colour, expressive distortion, and decorative composition. Denis summed up their creed in 1890: a painting is "essentially a flat surface covered with colours assembled in a certain order." They worked across easel painting, posters, book illustration, stained glass, and theatre design, collaborating with the Théâtre de l'Œuvre.

Later years

The group exhibited together through the 1890s and dissolved around 1900, though Bonnard and Vuillard went on to long, celebrated careers.
