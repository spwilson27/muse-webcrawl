Powhatan Confederacy

Indigenous name: Tsenacommacah

Location: Virginia Tidewater, North America

Paramount chief: Wahunsenacawh (Chief Powhatan), d. 1618

Languages: Virginia Algonquian

The Powhatan Confederacy, called Tsenacommacah by its people, was a political alliance of Algonquian-speaking Native American peoples in the Tidewater region of present-day Virginia. At the arrival of English colonists in 1607 it comprised some thirty tribes and perhaps 14,000–21,000 people, ruled from the capital at Werowocomoco by the paramount chief Wahunsenacawh, known to the English simply as Powhatan.

The confederacy had been built in the late sixteenth century by inheritance and conquest, uniting towns along the James, York, Rappahannock and Potomac rivers and the Eastern Shore. Member chiefs (werowances) paid tribute in corn, hides, copper and pearls in exchange for military protection.

Contact and conflict

Relations with the Jamestown colony were tense from the start, punctuated by trade, kidnapping of Powhatan's daughter Pocahontas, her marriage to John Rolfe, and a fragile peace. After Wahunsenacawh's death, his brother Opechancanough led mass attacks on the colony in 1622 and 1644. Defeat in the ensuing wars, followed by the treaties of 1646 and 1677, confined the Powhatan peoples to reservations and ended the confederacy as a political power.

Descendants

Descendant communities persist today, including the Pamunkey and Mattaponi, whose reservations are among the oldest in the United States, and several tribes received federal recognition in 2018.
