Pyrrhonism

Pyrrhonism is an ancient Greek school of philosophical skepticism founded by Pyrrho of Elis in the fourth century BCE; it is best known through the surviving works of Sextus Empiricus, written in the late second century or early third century CE. Pyrrhonists practice suspension of judgment (epoché) about all non-evident matters, holding that for every argument an equal and opposite argument can be advanced. The aim is ataraxia — freedom from disturbance and anxiety — which the Pyrrhonist claims follows suspension of judgment "as a shadow follows a body".

The modes

The Pyrrhonists developed systematic argumentative techniques to induce suspension of judgment. The Ten Modes of Aenesidemus exploit conflicts in perception arising from differences between animals, senses, circumstances, and so on. The Five Modes of Agrippa — dispute, regress, relativity, hypothesis, and circularity — challenge any purported justification. Sextus's Outlines of Pyrrhonism is the principal surviving handbook of the school.

Life and practice

Rejecting belief does not mean inaction: the Pyrrhonist lives by appearances, following a fourfold observance of the guidance of nature, the necessity of feelings, the tradition of laws and customs, and instruction in the arts. The school flourished into the Roman Empire and counted physicians among its adherents, who applied skeptical suspension to medical theory.

Legacy

Rediscovered in the Renaissance through Latin translations of Sextus, Pyrrhonism provoked the "Pyrrhonian crisis" in early modern philosophy and deeply influenced Montaigne, Descartes's methodical doubt, and Hume's skepticism. Its name survives in the usage "Pyrrhic victory" being unrelated — that derives from King Pyrrhus of Epirus instead.
