Hermann Schwarz

Karl Hermann Amandus Schwarz (25 January 1843 – 30 November 1921) was a German mathematician, known for his work in complex analysis.

Life

Schwarz was born in Hermsdorf, Silesia (now Jerzmanowa, Poland). Originally he studied chemistry at the Gewerbeinstitut in Berlin, but he switched to mathematics under the influence of Ernst Kummer and Karl Weierstrass, receiving his doctorate from the University of Berlin in 1864. He taught at the University of Halle from 1867, then at ETH Zurich from 1869, and finally at the University of Göttingen from 1875, where he held the chair previously held by Bernhard Riemann. In 1892 he moved to the University of Berlin, succeeding Weierstrass, and remained there until his death.

In 1864, he married Marie Kummer, the daughter of his teacher Ernst Kummer; they had six children. Among Schwarz's doctoral students were Lipót Fejér, Paul Koebe, and Ernst Zermelo.

Work

Schwarz made fundamental contributions to complex function theory and the calculus of variations. His name is attached to many results, including:

  • The Cauchy–Schwarz inequality in its integral form, which he published in 1888 (building on work of Cauchy and Bunyakovsky)
  • The Schwarz lemma, a cornerstone result on holomorphic maps of the unit disk
  • The Schwarz reflection principle for extending holomorphic functions across the real axis
  • The Schwarzian derivative, central to conformal mapping and later to Teichmüller theory
  • The Schwarz–Christoffel mapping for conformal maps onto polygons
  • The alternating method (Schwarz alternating method) for solving boundary-value problems on overlapping domains — a precursor of domain-decomposition methods in numerical analysis
  • Work on minimal surfaces, including the Schwarz minimal surface and solutions related to Plateau's problem
  • The Pappus centroid theorem's rigorous treatment and early work on surface area, including the "Schwarz lantern" example showing that naive polyhedral approximations of surface area can fail
