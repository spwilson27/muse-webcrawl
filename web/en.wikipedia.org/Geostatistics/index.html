Geostatistics

Geostatistics is a branch of statistics concerned with spatial or spatiotemporal data. Originally developed to estimate ore reserves in mining, it is now used in petroleum geology, hydrology, environmental science, epidemiology, agriculture and many other fields where measurements are taken at locations in space and are correlated with one another.

History

The field grew out of the work of South African mining engineer Danie Krige in the 1950s, who sought better estimates of gold grades in the Witwatersrand mines, and was formalised by the French mathematician Georges Matheron, who founded the Centre de Géostatistique at Fontainebleau in 1968. Matheron's theory of regionalised variables provided the mathematical foundation.

Core concepts

The central idea is that values measured near each other tend to be more alike than values far apart. This dependence is modelled with the variogram (or semivariogram), which plots the average squared difference between pairs of observations against the distance between them. The variogram's features include the nugget effect (variation at very small distances), the sill (the level at which correlation effectively vanishes) and the range (the distance at which the sill is reached).

Methods

The best-known geostatistical technique is kriging, a family of interpolation methods that produce the best linear unbiased prediction of a variable at an unmeasured location, together with an estimate of prediction uncertainty. Variants include ordinary, universal, indicator and co-kriging. Geostatistical simulation methods instead generate many equally probable maps of a variable, which is useful for quantifying uncertainty in resource estimates and environmental risk.
