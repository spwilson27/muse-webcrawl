Humanistic psychology
Key figuresAbraham Maslow, Carl Rogers, Rollo May
Emergence1950s–1960s, United States
Core conceptsSelf-actualization, free will, person-centred therapy

Humanistic psychology is a psychological perspective that emphasises the study of the whole person, free will, personal growth and self-actualisation. Emerging in the mid-20th century as a "third force" alongside behaviourism and psychoanalysis, it criticised behaviourism's determinism and reductionism and psychoanalysis's focus on pathology and the unconscious.

Key ideas

Abraham Maslow proposed a hierarchy of needs culminating in self-actualization and later studied "peak experiences". Carl Rogers developed person-centred (client-centred) therapy, based on unconditional positive regard, empathy and congruence, arguing that people have an inherent tendency toward growth when given the right conditions. Rollo May brought European existential thought into the movement.

Influence and criticism

Humanistic ideas reshaped counselling, education and management, and seeded positive psychology in the 1990s. Critics have faulted the school for vague, hard-to-test concepts and reliance on subjective experience rather than controlled experiment, though its therapeutic emphasis on the relationship between therapist and client remains influential.
