Antigenic shift

Reassortment of gene segments between two influenza viruses, producing a novel subtype

Antigenic shift is the process by which two or more different strains of a virus — most famously influenza viruses — combine to form a new subtype with a mixture of surface antigens from the original strains. It is an abrupt, major change, in contrast to antigenic drift, the slow accumulation of small mutations. Because populations have little or no immunity to the new subtype, antigenic shift is the mechanism responsible for influenza pandemics.

Mechanism

Influenza A viruses have genomes of eight separate RNA segments. When a single host cell — for example in a pig simultaneously infected with avian and human influenza viruses — is co-infected by two different strains, the segments can be packaged in mixed combinations (reassortment), yielding progeny viruses carrying a novel haemagglutinin (H) or neuraminidase (N) subtype to which humans are immunologically naive.

Pandemics

Documented shift events underlie the great 20th- and 21st-century pandemics: the 1957 "Asian flu" (H2N2), the 1968 "Hong Kong flu" (H3N2) and the 2009 "swine flu" pandemic (H1N1pdm09), whose virus was a triple-reassortant of swine, avian and human lineages. The 1918 "Spanish flu" H1N1 virus is thought to have emerged through related avian-to-human adaptation. Aquatic birds are the natural reservoir of influenza A diversity, providing the gene pool from which shifted viruses emerge.

Contrast with antigenic drift

Antigenic drift occurs constantly in both influenza A and B and is why seasonal flu vaccine composition is reviewed every year; shift occurs only occasionally and only in influenza A, but with far greater consequences because of the population's lack of immunity.
