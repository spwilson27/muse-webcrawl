Anglophobia

Anglophobia is dislike of, or prejudice against, England or English people. The term combines "Anglo-" (referring to England or the English) with "-phobia" (fear or aversion). Its opposite is Anglophilia. Anglophobia has arisen historically from political conflicts, colonial grievances, sporting rivalries, and cultural resentment, and has been expressed in countries as varied as France, Ireland, Scotland, Wales, Spain, Argentina, and the United States.

Historical contexts

Centuries of Anglo-French rivalry, from the Hundred Years' War to the Napoleonic Wars, entrenched mutual stereotypes. In Ireland, English (later British) rule, land confiscation, and the handling of the Great Famine left a lasting legacy reflected in nationalist rhetoric. During the American Revolution and the War of 1812, anti-English sentiment was a significant strand of American political discourse, and episodes such as "Anglophobia" in late-19th-century American politics influenced disputes over Venezuela's boundary and fisheries.

Within the United Kingdom

Anti-English sentiment also occurs within the UK itself, in Scotland and Wales, where it may be linked to debates about devolution, independence, and the dominance of England within the Union. English people living in Scotland and Wales have reported instances of hostility, and police statistics record racially aggravated incidents directed at English people, though scholars and commentators debate the scale and nature of the phenomenon.

Other settings

In Argentina, Anglophobia intensified after the 1982 Falklands War (Guerra de las Malvinas). Anti-English feeling has also surfaced in Spain in connection with the Gibraltar dispute, and in various postcolonial settings where it often overlaps with broader anti-British or anti-imperial sentiment.
