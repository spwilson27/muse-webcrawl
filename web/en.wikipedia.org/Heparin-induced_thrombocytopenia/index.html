Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT) is the development of thrombocytopenia (a low platelet count) due to the administration of the anticoagulant heparin. HIT predisposes to thrombosis (the abnormal formation of blood clots in blood vessels) because platelets are activated; when thrombosis occurs the condition is called heparin-induced thrombocytopenia and thrombosis (HITT). Paradoxically, although the platelet count falls, bleeding is rare and clotting is the main danger.

Mechanism

HIT is caused by the formation of abnormal antibodies that activate platelets. Heparin binds platelet factor 4 (PF4), a protein released from platelet granules; in susceptible individuals, IgG antibodies form against the heparin–PF4 complex. These immune complexes bind platelet Fc receptors, causing platelet activation, release of procoagulant microparticles, and generation of thrombin. The resulting consumption of platelets lowers the count, typically by more than 50%, while the prothrombotic state can cause venous and arterial thrombosis.

Signs and symptoms

The platelet count typically falls 5 to 10 days after heparin is started (sooner if the patient was recently exposed). New or worsening thrombosis — deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, myocardial infarction, stroke, or limb artery occlusion — develops in a substantial fraction of untreated patients. Skin necrosis at heparin injection sites and acute systemic reactions after intravenous heparin may also occur.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis combines clinical assessment and laboratory testing. The 4T score estimates pretest probability from the degree of Thrombocytopenia, Timing of the platelet fall, presence of Thrombosis, and absence of oTher causes. Laboratory tests include immunoassays for anti-PF4/heparin antibodies (ELISA) and functional assays such as the serotonin-release assay, which confirm platelet-activating antibodies.

Treatment

All forms of heparin must be stopped immediately, including flushes and heparin-coated catheters. Because the thrombotic risk remains high, anticoagulation is switched to a non-heparin agent such as argatroban, bivalirudin, danaparoid, or fondaparinux. Warfarin must not be started alone during acute HIT, as it can precipitate venous limb gangrene; it is introduced only after the platelet count recovers, with overlapping cover. Platelet transfusion is generally avoided.

Types

An older terminology distinguished HIT type I — a mild, non-immune, transient fall in platelets early in therapy — from immune HIT type II, the serious disorder described here. The non-immune form is now usually termed heparin-associated thrombocytopenia.
