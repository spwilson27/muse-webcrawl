Luteinizing hormone

Luteinizing hormone (LH)
Also called: Lutropin
Secreted by: Gonadotroph cells, anterior pituitary gland
Regulated by: Gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH)
Targets: Ovaries and testes (gonads)
[Image placeholder: Diagram of the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis showing LH secretion]

Luteinizing hormone (LH, also known as lutropin) is a hormone produced by gonadotropic cells in the anterior pituitary gland. Together with follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) it is a gonadotropin, released in pulses in response to gonadotropin-releasing hormone from the hypothalamus, and it is essential to the functioning of the reproductive system in both sexes.

Function in females

In women, a sharp mid-cycle rise — the "LH surge" — triggers ovulation and converts the ruptured follicle into the corpus luteum, which secretes progesterone. Home ovulation predictor kits detect this surge. LH also stimulates thecal cells to produce androgens that are converted to oestrogen.

Function in males

In men, LH stimulates the Leydig cells of the testes to produce testosterone; it was historically called interstitial cell-stimulating hormone (ICSH) in males.

Clinical significance

Abnormal LH levels feature in disorders such as polycystic ovary syndrome, hypogonadism and precocious or delayed puberty. Recombinant LH and related gonadotropins are used in fertility treatment.
