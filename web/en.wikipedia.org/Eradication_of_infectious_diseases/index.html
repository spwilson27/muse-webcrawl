Eradication of infectious diseases

Eradication is the reduction of the worldwide incidence of a disease to zero through deliberate efforts, with no further need for control measures once eradication is achieved. It is the ultimate goal of disease control and differs from elimination, which is the reduction of incidence to zero in a defined geographical area, and from extinction, in which the infectious agent no longer exists anywhere, including in laboratories.

Criteria

For a disease to be eradicable, several biological and technical criteria must generally be met: humans should be essential to the life cycle of the agent (with no animal or environmental reservoir), sensitive and practical diagnostic tools must exist to detect levels of infection that can interrupt transmission, and an effective intervention — usually a vaccine — must be available to interrupt transmission. Political and social factors, including sustained funding and public commitment, are equally decisive in practice.

Successful eradications

Only two diseases have been eradicated globally to date. Smallpox, a human disease caused by the variola virus, was declared eradicated by the World Health Organization in 1980, following an intensified campaign of surveillance and ring vaccination led by figures such as William Foege and D. A. Henderson. Rinderpest, a disease of cattle and other even-toed ungulates, was declared eradicated in 2011 — the first and so far only animal disease to be eradicated.

Ongoing efforts

The Global Polio Eradication Initiative, launched in 1988, has reduced poliomyelitis cases by over 99 per cent, but wild poliovirus transmission persists endemically in Afghanistan and Pakistan. Dracunculiasis (Guinea worm disease), targeted by the Carter Center and partners, has been brought from an estimated 3.5 million cases in 1986 to only a handful of human cases annually, although infections in animals have complicated the final stages. Campaigns or proposals have also addressed measles, rubella, yaws, malaria, and hookworm.

Challenges

Eradication campaigns face biological obstacles such as asymptomatic transmission and animal reservoirs, logistical difficulties in conflict zones and remote regions, misinformation and vaccine hesitancy, and the escalating cost per case as incidence approaches zero — the so-called "last mile" problem.
