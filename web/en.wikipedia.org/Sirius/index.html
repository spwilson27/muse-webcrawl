Sirius is the brightest star in the night sky. Its name is derived from the Greek Seirios, meaning "glowing" or "scorching". Designated α Canis Majoris (Latinised to Alpha Canis Majoris), the system is a binary star in the constellation Canis Major, consisting of a main-sequence star (Sirius A) and a faint white dwarf companion (Sirius B).

Observational history

Sirius was recorded in the earliest astronomical records. In ancient Egypt its heliacal rising coincided with the annual flooding of the Nile and marked the new year. Friedrich Bessel inferred the existence of an unseen companion in 1844 from irregularities in Sirius's proper motion; Alvan Graham Clark first observed Sirius B in 1862. In 1915 Walter Adams found Sirius B to be a white dwarf—the first identified after the planet-like dwarfs of the era.

Properties

Sirius A is about twice the mass of the Sun and roughly 25 times more luminous, with a surface temperature near 9,940 K. Sirius B, once the more massive star of the pair, has collapsed into a white dwarf of about one solar mass compressed into a volume similar to Earth's. The system lies 8.6 light-years away, making it one of the Sun's closest neighbours, and it is slowly approaching the Solar System.

Cultural significance

Known colloquially as the "Dog Star", Sirius features in the mythology and calendars of many cultures, including Egyptian, Greek, Polynesian, and Indigenous North American traditions. The phrase "dog days of summer" derives from the period when Sirius rose with the Sun.
