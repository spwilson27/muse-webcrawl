Gault Millau

Gault et Millau is a French restaurant guide founded by the food critics Henri Gault (1929–2000) and Christian Millau (1928–2017). Launched in 1969, it became one of the most influential gastronomic guides in France and a principal rival to the Michelin Guide, championing the movement that became known as nouvelle cuisine.

History

Henri Gault and Christian Millau, journalists who had worked together at Paris-Presse, began publishing restaurant criticism in the late 1960s. Their annual guide, first issued in 1969, quickly gained a reputation for wit, independence and iconoclasm. In 1973 Millau published in the Gault Millau magazine the famous "ten commandments" of nouvelle cuisine, codifying the lighter, more inventive style associated with chefs such as Paul Bocuse, Michel Guérard and the Troisgros brothers.

Rating system

Unlike Michelin's stars, Gault Millau rates restaurants on a scale of 20 points and awards toques (chef's hats): one toque for 11–12.5 points, rising to five toques for the very highest scores of 19 or 19.5. Ratings are accompanied by full written reviews that also assess the welcome, the wine list and the setting. The guide is known for never awarding a perfect 20, on the principle that perfection belongs to God alone.

Influence and expansion

The guide's advocacy of young, creative chefs helped reshape French gastronomy in the 1970s and 1980s. Editions were later published for numerous countries in Europe and beyond, and the brand passed through several owners; since 2019 it has been owned by Russian investors associated with the Zakharov family, with expansion into new markets including the Middle East and Asia.
