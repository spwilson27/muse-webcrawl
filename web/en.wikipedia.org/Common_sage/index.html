Common sage

Scientific nameSalvia officinalis
FamilyLamiaceae
Native toMediterranean region

Common sage (Salvia officinalis), also called garden sage, culinary sage, or simply sage, is a perennial evergreen subshrub of the mint family Lamiaceae, native to the Mediterranean region. It has been cultivated since antiquity for its strongly flavoured leaves, used as a culinary herb and in traditional medicine; its species name officinalis refers to its historic place in the official pharmacopoeia.

Description

Sage grows to about 60 cm tall and wide, with woody stems and oblong, wrinkled, grey-green leaves that are soft and hairy to the touch. In late spring or summer it produces spikes of violet-blue, pink, or white flowers that attract bees. Numerous cultivars exist, including purple-leaved, variegated, and dwarf forms grown for ornament.

Uses

In the kitchen, sage is a classic flavouring for fatty meats, sausages, stuffings (notably sage-and-onion stuffing), and the Italian dish saltimbocca; its leaves may also be fried in butter for pasta. Because of its robust flavour it is used sparingly. Historically, sage enjoyed a reputation as a near-panacea — its Latin name derives from salvare, "to save" — and was prescribed for memory, digestion, and sore throats. The essential oil contains thujone and camphor, and the plant is a source of rosmarinic acid and other antioxidants. The Dalmatian coast of Croatia is a noted centre of commercial sage production and of sage honey.
