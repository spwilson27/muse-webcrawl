The West Germanic languages are centred on north-western Europe.

The West Germanic languages constitute the largest of the three branches of the Germanic family of languages (the others being the North Germanic and the extinct East Germanic languages). The West Germanic branch is classically subdivided into Anglo-Frisian and continental West Germanic groups, and includes English — by far the most widely spoken Germanic language — as well as German and Dutch.

History

West Germanic developed from Proto-Germanic dialects spoken along the North Sea coast and in the Elbe–Weser region in the last centuries BCE. Its unity is debated: characteristic shared features include the loss of final -z, the monophthongisation of ai and au in some contexts, and the development of the Ingvaeonic nasal spirant law in the coastal (Anglo-Frisian and Low German) varieties. Anglo-Saxon settlers carried the Anglo-Frisian varieties to Britain in the 5th century, giving rise to English and Scots.

Classification

The principal modern West Germanic languages are English, German (High German), Dutch, Afrikaans, Frisian, Low German, Luxembourgish, Yiddish, and Scots. Together they have well over 500 million native speakers, and English adds more than a billion second-language speakers worldwide.
