Conscientious objector

A conscientious objector is an individual who has claimed the right to refuse to perform military service on the grounds of freedom of conscience — religious, ethical or philosophical opposition to war or to participation in armed conflict. Such objectors are sometimes assigned to alternative civilian service; in some systems they have faced imprisonment or other penalties.

History

Religious objectors from the historic peace churches — Quakers, Mennonites and Brethren — refused service long before modern conscription. During the First World War, Britain's 1916 Military Service Act became the first to give statutory recognition to conscientious objection, though many objectors were still imprisoned. In the Second World War, American objectors served in the Civilian Public Service, working in forestry, hospitals and medical experiments; Desmond Doss, a medic who refused to carry a weapon, received the Medal of Honor.

Modern status

The Vietnam War era saw large numbers of draft objectors in the United States, and the Supreme Court broadened the legal definition beyond orthodox religious belief. The right to conscientious objection is now recognised in the European Convention on Human Rights jurisprudence and by many national constitutions, though its scope — for example, objection to particular wars or to military taxes — remains contested.
