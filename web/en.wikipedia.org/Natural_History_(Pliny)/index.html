The Natural History (Latin: Naturalis Historia) is a work by Pliny the Elder, the largest single work to have survived from the Roman Empire to the modern day. Comprising 37 books, it purports to cover the entire field of ancient knowledge, from astronomy and geography to zoology, botany, mineralogy, agriculture, and art history.

Overview

Pliny claimed in his preface to have examined some 2,000 volumes by 100 authors, compiling roughly 20,000 facts. The books move from the cosmos and the natural world through mankind to animals, plants, medicines, metals, and stones, concluding with precious gems. Pliny died in the eruption of Vesuvius in 79 CE while attempting to observe it — an event described by his nephew, Pliny the Younger, who published the work.

Influence

The Natural History became the model for later encyclopedias and remained a standard authority on the natural world through the Middle Ages and Renaissance, valued both for genuine observations and for the marvellous creatures and remedies it transmitted.
