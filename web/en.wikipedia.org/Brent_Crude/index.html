Brent Crude

Brent Crude is a major trading classification of sweet light crude oil that serves as one of the two principal benchmark prices for purchases of oil worldwide, the other being the American West Texas Intermediate (WTI). Originally the designation for oil extracted from the Brent oilfield in the North Sea off Scotland, opened by Shell in 1976, "Brent" now refers to a basket of North Sea grades — Brent Blend, Forties, Oseberg, Ekofisk and, since 2018, Troll (the BFOET complex), with the US grade WTI Midland added to the basket in 2023. Brent futures traded on the Intercontinental Exchange (ICE) underpin the pricing of roughly two-thirds to four-fifths of the world's internationally traded crude oil supplies.

Characteristics

Brent is a light crude — API gravity around 38 — and sweet, with low sulfur (about 0.37%), making it straightforward to refine into gasoline and middle distillates. Production from the original Brent field, which peaked in the 1980s, declined until the field was decommissioned in the early 2020s; the benchmark survived by successively broadening the basket of deliverable grades to sustain liquidity, assessed daily as "Dated Brent" by price-reporting agencies such as Platts (S&P Global).

Role as a benchmark

Because Brent is waterborne and traded globally, its price is set by worldwide supply and demand and is used as the reference for crude produced in Europe, Africa and much of the Middle East sold into Europe and Asia, as well as for price formulas in long-term contracts. Brent futures and options on ICE are among the world's most liquid energy contracts. The Brent–WTI spread, normally a few dollars reflecting transport and quality differences, widened dramatically in 2011–2014 when US shale output glutted the landlocked WTI hub at Cushing, Oklahoma. Brent spiked to an intraday record near $147 per barrel in July 2008 and collapsed below $20 in April 2020 during the COVID-19 demand shock, before surging again above $120 after Russia's 2022 invasion of Ukraine.
