FG 42

The FG 42 (German: Fallschirmjägergewehr 42, "paratrooper rifle 42") is a selective-fire automatic rifle produced in Nazi Germany during World War II. It was developed specifically for the Fallschirmjäger airborne infantry and combined the firepower of a light machine gun with a weapon light and compact enough to be carried during a parachute jump.

The FG 42 (late model)

Development

After the Battle of Crete in 1941, German paratroopers, who jumped with only pistols and submachine guns, demanded a versatile automatic weapon. Rheinmetall-Borsig's design by Louis Stange fired the full-power 7.92×57mm Mauser cartridge from a 20-round side-mounted magazine, with a straight-line stock, bipod, and integral bayonet to control recoil. Two main variants were produced: the early model with a sharply raked grip and stamped construction, and the later, heavier model with a wooden stock and revised gas system intended to improve reliability.

Service

Only about 7,000 were built before the war ended, but the FG 42 saw action in notable operations, including the rescue of Benito Mussolini at Gran Sasso in 1943. Its gas system and design influenced post-war weapons, notably the American M60 machine gun.
