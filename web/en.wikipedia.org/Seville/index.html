Seville

Plaza de España, built for the Ibero-American Exposition of 1929.

Seville (Spanish: Sevilla) is the capital and largest city of the autonomous community of Andalusia in southern Spain, and the fourth-largest city in the country, with a population of roughly 690,000 in the city and about 1.5 million in the metropolitan area. It lies on the Guadalquivir river, whose navigable course made Seville a major trading port despite being some 80 kilometres inland.

History

Founded as the Roman city of Hispalis near the earlier settlement of Italica, Seville became an important centre of Islamic al-Andalus under the Umayyads and the Abbadid taifa, and later under the Almohads, who built the minaret now known as the Giralda. Ferdinand III of Castile conquered the city in 1248. After 1492 Seville held the monopoly of trade with Spain's American colonies through the Casa de Contratación, becoming one of Europe's richest cities in the sixteenth century before silting of the river and the transfer of trade to Cádiz diminished it. The Ibero-American Exposition of 1929 and Expo '92 marked its modern revivals.

Culture and landmarks

The historic centre contains three UNESCO World Heritage Sites: Seville Cathedral — among the largest Gothic cathedrals in the world, incorporating the Giralda bell tower and the tomb of Christopher Columbus — the Alcázar palace complex, still used by the Spanish royal family, and the General Archive of the Indies. Seville is renowned for flamenco, for the April Fair (Feria de Abril) and Holy Week processions, for bullfighting at the Maestranza, and for being the setting of operas including Carmen and The Barber of Seville. Its summers are among the hottest in Europe, with temperatures regularly exceeding 40 °C.
