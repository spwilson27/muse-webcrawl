Loggia

A Renaissance loggia

A loggia (Italian: [ˈlɔddʒa]) is an architectural feature that is a covered exterior gallery or corridor, usually on an upper level, but sometimes on the ground level of a building. The corridor is open to the elements on one side, supported by columns or arches, and open to the interior of the building. Unlike a porch, a loggia is integrated into the structure of the building rather than projected from it.

History

The loggia became a hallmark of Italian architecture from the medieval period onward. Civic loggias, such as the Loggia dei Lanzi in Florence (built 1376–1382), served as public ceremonial spaces. In Renaissance palaces and villas, loggias provided shaded outdoor rooms overlooking courtyards or gardens, and were often decorated with frescoes, as in Raphael's loggia in the Vatican Palace. The form spread throughout Europe and was revived in later styles, including the mansions of the Italianate and Mediterranean Revival movements.

Related forms

Loggias are related to, but distinct from, porticoes, verandas, balconies, colonnades and peristyles. In modern apartment buildings the term is often used for a recessed balcony enclosed on three sides by walls.
