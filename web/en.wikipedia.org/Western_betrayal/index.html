Western betrayal

Western betrayal (Polish: zdrada Zachodu) is the view that the United Kingdom and France broke their military alliances with Poland and Czechoslovakia, and more broadly that the Western powers failed Central and Eastern Europe, during the diplomatic crises of the Second World War and its aftermath. The concept remains a touchstone in Central European political memory.

Key episodes

The term is associated first with the Munich Agreement of 1938, in which Britain and France accepted the German annexation of the Sudetenland without Czechoslovak participation. In 1939 Britain and France declared war after Germany invaded Poland but mounted no significant offensive in the west (the "Phoney War"). Further episodes cited include the failure to support the Warsaw Uprising of 1944, the Tehran and Yalta conferences of 1943–45 — which placed Poland's postwar borders and government largely in Soviet hands — and the Western acceptance of the Soviet sphere in Eastern Europe during the early Cold War, including the lack of intervention in Hungary in 1956.

Legacy

Historians debate how far alternative Western policies were feasible, but the betrayal narrative has strongly influenced Polish and Czech attitudes toward alliances, contributing to post-1989 determination to join NATO and secure explicit American security commitments.
