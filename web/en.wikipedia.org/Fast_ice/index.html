Fast ice

Fast ice extending from the Antarctic coast, stationary and attached to the shore.

Fast ice (also called land-fast ice or shore-fast ice) is sea ice that is "fastened" to the coastline, to the sea floor along shoals, or to grounded icebergs. Unlike drift ice, which moves with winds and currents, fast ice remains stationary, attached to the shore or to other fixed objects.

Formation and characteristics

Fast ice grows from the freezing of seawater at the coast, or develops when floating pack ice becomes grounded or attached to the shore and is prevented from moving. It may extend only a few metres, or several hundred kilometres, from the coast. Because it does not move, fast ice tends to be smoother and more level than pack ice, though ridges can form where moving ice presses against its outer edge, creating a feature known as a stamukha zone in shallow water.

Ecology and use

Fast ice provides a stable platform used by wildlife — seals haul out and pup on it, and polar bears hunt along its edges. Antarctic research stations and Arctic communities use fast ice as a working surface and travel route, and its breakout late in the season is an important seasonal event. Because it forms and disappears in place, fast ice thickness and duration are monitored as indicators of polar climate change; in Antarctica, fast ice also protects coastlines and ice shelves from ocean swell.
