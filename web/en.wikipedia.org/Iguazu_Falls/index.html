Iguazu Falls (Spanish: Cataratas del Iguazú; Portuguese: Cataratas do Iguaçu) are waterfalls of the Iguazu River on the border between the Argentine province of Misiones and the Brazilian state of Paraná. Together they make up one of the largest waterfall systems in the world, extending over about 2.7 kilometres of the river above its confluence with the Paraná.

Description

The falls are arranged in a long horseshoe of roughly 275 separate cascades divided by rocky, forested islands. The most spectacular section is the U-shaped Devil's Throat (Garganta del Diablo), about 2,700 metres around its rim and 82 metres high — taller than Niagara Falls and wider than Victoria Falls. Walkways and a narrow-gauge train give access on the Argentine side, which holds about 80% of the falls, while the Brazilian side offers panoramic views. The name derives from the Guaraní words y (water) and ûasú (big).

History and protection

The first European to record the falls was the Spanish conquistador Álvar Núñez Cabeza de Vaca in 1541. The surrounding national parks — Iguazú National Park in Argentina (1934) and Iguaçu National Park in Brazil (1939) — were inscribed as UNESCO World Heritage Sites in 1984 and 1986 respectively, protecting subtropical rainforest with jaguars, toucans, and hundreds of butterfly species. In 2011 the falls were named one of the New7Wonders of Nature.
