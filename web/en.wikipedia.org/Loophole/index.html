Loophole

Arrowslits — the original "loopholes" — in a castle wall.

A loophole is an ambiguity or inadequacy in a system, such as a law or security, which can be used to circumvent or otherwise avoid the purpose, implied or explicitly intended, of the system. Originally, the word meant a narrow window or opening in a wall for looking through or for shooting arrows. It later developed the figurative sense of an evasion or escape.

Uses

Loopholes are searched for and used strategically in a variety of circumstances, including elections, politics, taxes, and in legal cases. Tax loopholes are ambiguities in tax law that allow taxpayers to legally reduce their tax burden, for example by shifting income to lower-tax jurisdictions. "Closing a loophole" is a common political phrase for reforming a law to remove such an ambiguity. In contracts, a loophole may allow a party to escape an obligation that the drafters intended to be binding.

Etymology

The term comes from the Dutch loopgat or from "loop" — narrow slits in castle walls and towers, also called arrowslits or murder holes, through which defenders could observe and shoot. The figurative meaning, "a means of escape", dates from the 17th century.
