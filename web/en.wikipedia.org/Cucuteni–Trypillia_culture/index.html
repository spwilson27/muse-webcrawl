The Cucuteni–Trypillia culture was a Neolithic–Eneolithic archaeological culture of eastern Europe, flourishing from about 5500 to 2750 BC across what is now Romania, Moldova and Ukraine. It is named for the villages of Cucuteni in Romania and Trypillia in Ukraine, and is regarded as one of the most advanced farming societies of its time, famous for its finely painted pottery and remarkably large settlements.

Settlements

In its later phases the culture built "mega-sites" such as Talianki, Maidanetske and Nebelivka in Ukraine — settlements of thousands of houses arranged in concentric rings that may have housed well over 10,000 people, making them among the largest settlements in the world at the time. Houses were regularly burned at the end of their use, a practice still debated by archaeologists.

Material culture

The culture produced elegant red, black and white painted ceramics, abundant female figurines, copper tools alongside stone and bone, and evidence of early wheeled vehicles in the form of animal figurines on wheels. Its economy rested on wheat, barley, cattle, pigs and sheep. The society appears to have been relatively egalitarian, without obvious palaces or elite burials, and it faded by the late 4th millennium BC as steppe cultures expanded from the east.
