Civil procedure

A civil courtroom.

Civil procedure is the body of law that sets out the rules and regulations along with the standards that courts follow when adjudicating civil lawsuits, as opposed to procedures in criminal law matters. These rules govern how a lawsuit may be commenced, what kind of service of process is required, the types of pleadings, motions and orders allowed, the conduct of discovery and trial, and the process for obtaining and enforcing a judgment.

Stages of a civil case

A typical civil action proceeds through several stages. It begins with pleadings, in which the plaintiff files a complaint stating a claim and the defendant answers or moves to dismiss. Discovery follows: the parties exchange information through interrogatories, depositions, requests for documents and requests for admission. Many cases are then resolved by settlement or by summary judgment, where the court decides that no genuine dispute of material fact exists. Remaining cases go to trial, before a judge (bench trial) or jury, and the losing party may appeal on questions of law. Finally, the prevailing party may use enforcement mechanisms — garnishment, liens, execution — to collect the judgment.

Systems compared

In the United States, federal civil cases are governed by the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure, adopted in 1938, which merged law and equity and introduced liberal notice pleading and broad discovery; most states have similar rules. England and Wales reformed their system through the Woolf reforms and the Civil Procedure Rules 1998, emphasising proportionality and case management. Civil law jurisdictions such as France and Germany give judges a more active, investigative role and typically lack juries in civil cases, a contrast often summarised as adversarial versus inquisitorial procedure.

Related concepts

Civil procedure interacts with jurisdiction (the court's power over persons and subject matter), venue, standing, res judicata and collateral estoppel (the preclusive effects of judgments), statutes of limitations, and alternative dispute resolution such as mediation and arbitration.
