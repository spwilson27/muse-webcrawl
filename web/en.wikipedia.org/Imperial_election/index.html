Imperial election

An imperial election was the formal election of the Holy Roman Emperor by the prince-electors of the Holy Roman Empire. From the thirteenth century the right to elect was restricted to a small college of the empire's most powerful princes, and the procedure was codified in the Golden Bull of 1356.

The electors

The Golden Bull fixed seven electors: the archbishops of Mainz, Trier, and Cologne; the King of Bohemia; the Count Palatine of the Rhine; the Duke of Saxony; and the Margrave of Brandenburg. Later additions included Bavaria (1623) and Hanover (1692). Elections were held at Frankfurt am Main, where the emperor-elect was traditionally crowned; coronation by the pope in Rome became rare after the early sixteenth century.

Procedure

The Archbishop of Mainz, as archchancellor, convened the college within thirty days of a vacancy. The electors voted by majority, swearing to choose without bribery — though in practice elections involved heavy negotiation, electoral capitulations (Wahlkapitulationen) limiting imperial power, and foreign subsidies. From 1438 the Habsburgs held the imperial title almost continuously, making elections largely confirmatory, though the 1742 election of the Wittelsbach Charles VII interrupted the succession during the War of the Austrian Succession.

End

The last imperial election was that of Francis II in 1792; the empire was dissolved in 1806.
