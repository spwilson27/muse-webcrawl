Altar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


An altar in a Christian church

An altar is a table or platform for the presentation of religious offerings, for sacrifices, or for other ritualistic purposes. Altars are found at shrines, temples, churches, and other places of worship. They are used particularly in paganism, Christianity, Buddhism, Hinduism, Judaism, modern paganism, and in certain Islamic communities around the Caucasus and Anatolia. Altars are also found in many other religions and in settings for honouring the dead.

Ancient religions

In the ancient world, altars were often raised structures of earth, stone or wood on which offerings — including animal sacrifices, grain, oil and wine — were burned or poured out. Greek and Roman temples had altars in front of the building where public sacrifices were performed, and Jewish worship centred on altars in the Tabernacle and the Temple in Jerusalem, including the altar of burnt offering and the altar of incense.

Christianity

In Christian churches, the altar is the table on which the Eucharist is consecrated. In the Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and many other traditions, the altar is a focal point of the sanctuary, often made of stone and containing relics. In many Protestant churches a simpler communion table is used.

Historically, altars in the West were often decorated with altarpieces — painted or carved panels such as triptychs — and dressed with altar cloths, candles, and a cross or crucifix.

Other traditions

In Hinduism, altars and shrines, both in temples and homes, receive offerings of flowers, food, lamps and incense before images of deities. In Buddhism, altars hold statues, candles and offerings before images of the Buddha. In Shinto, household altars (kamidana) honour the kami. Modern pagan and Wiccan practice uses personal altars as working spaces for ritual, furnished with candles, tools and seasonal items. In Mexico, home altars (ofrendas) are central to Day of the Dead observances honouring deceased relatives.
