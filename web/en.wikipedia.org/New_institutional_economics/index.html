New institutional economics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Key figures: Ronald Coase and Douglass North

New institutional economics (NIE) is an economic perspective that attempts to extend economics by focusing on the institutions — that is, the social and legal norms and rules — that underlie economic activity and with analysis beyond earlier institutional economics and neoclassical economics. It can be seen as a broadening step away from neoclassical economics in that it includes many of its ideas, but with less stringent assumptions than traditional microeconomics.

Origins

The roots of NIE lie in two articles by Ronald Coase: "The Nature of the Firm" (1937), which introduced transaction costs to explain the existence and boundaries of firms, and "The Problem of Social Cost" (1960), which gave rise to the Coase theorem on property rights and externalities. Oliver E. Williamson developed the field's transaction-cost framework and its analysis of governance structures, while Douglass North applied institutional analysis to long-run economic change, arguing that institutions are "the rules of the game in a society".

Key ideas

NIE analyses how institutions reduce uncertainty and transaction costs, how property rights shape incentives, and why inefficient institutions can persist. It studies hierarchies, markets, and hybrid arrangements as alternative governance forms, and applies institutional reasoning to development economics, economic history, law and economics, and political science. Coase, North, and later Elinor Ostrom and Oliver Williamson received Nobel Memorial Prizes for contributions associated with the field.

Unlike older institutional economics, NIE generally retains the analytical tools of mainstream economics — constrained optimisation and equilibrium analysis — while relaxing assumptions of perfect information and zero transaction costs.
