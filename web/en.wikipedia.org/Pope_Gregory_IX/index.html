Pope Gregory IX (Latin: Gregorius IX; born Ugolino di Conti, c. 1145 – 22 August 1241) was head of the Catholic Church and ruler of the Papal States from 19 March 1227 until his death. A nephew of Innocent III, he was educated at Paris and Bologna, became cardinal in 1198, and succeeded Honorius III at a very advanced age — traditionally said to be about 80. His pontificate was defined by his struggle with the Emperor Frederick II, his sponsorship of the mendicant orders, the founding of the Papal Inquisition, and a landmark codification of canon law.

Canon law and the Inquisition

In 1234 Gregory promulgated the Nova Compilatio Decretalium, known as the Liber Extra, a systematic collection of papal decretals compiled by Raymond of Peñafort that remained a foundation of canon law until 1917. Concerned by heresy and the shortcomings of episcopal courts, in 1231 he issued the bull Ille humani generis and entrusted inquisitions to the Dominicans, effectively founding the Papal (medieval) Inquisition; his appointee Conrad of Marburg conducted a notorious campaign in Germany. Gregory's bull Vox in Rama (1233), describing alleged heretical rites, also infamously associated cats with devil-worship.

Conflict with Frederick II

Gregory excommunicated Emperor Frederick II in 1227 for delaying his promised crusade; when Frederick then crusaded anyway (recovering Jerusalem by treaty in 1229) while excommunicate, tension deepened. The quarrel erupted into open war in Italy, with Gregory organising the Lombard League against imperial expansion, excommunicating Frederick again in 1239 and denouncing him as a heretic and precursor of Antichrist. Gregory died in August 1241 while Frederick's army threatened Rome and a fleet had captured many prelates travelling to a council Gregory had summoned to depose him.

Other achievements

A friend and protector of Saint Francis of Assisi — whose canonisation he proclaimed in 1228, one of the fastest in history — Gregory supported the Franciscans and Dominicans, canonised Saint Dominic (1234) and Saint Elizabeth of Hungary, issued the bull Parens scientiarum (1231) granting the University of Paris its charter of privileges, and supported missions and crusading in the Baltic and the Holy Land.
