Raman spectroscopy is a spectroscopic technique used to observe vibrational, rotational and other low-frequency modes in a system. It relies on inelastic scattering of monochromatic light — the Raman effect — in which photons exchange energy with molecular vibrations, shifting in wavelength by amounts that form a characteristic "fingerprint" of the material's chemical bonds.

Principle and history

When laser light illuminates a sample, most photons scatter elastically (Rayleigh scattering), but roughly one in ten million scatters inelastically: Stokes photons lose energy to vibrations, while rarer anti-Stokes photons gain it. The energy shifts, plotted against intensity, give the Raman spectrum. The effect was reported in 1928 by the Indian physicist C. V. Raman, working with K. S. Krishnan in Calcutta and using filtered sunlight; Raman received the 1930 Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery. The advent of lasers in the 1960s transformed a difficult experiment into a routine analytical tool.

Applications

Raman spectroscopy is non-destructive and requires little sample preparation, so it is used for chemical identification in pharmaceuticals and forensics, mineralogy and gemmology, carbon materials (graphene, nanotubes), semiconductor quality control, art authentication, and even planetary exploration — Raman instruments fly on Mars rovers. Variants such as surface-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SERS), resonance Raman and coherent anti-Stokes Raman spectroscopy (CARS) boost sensitivity for trace detection and microscopy. Its chief limitation is interference from sample fluorescence.
