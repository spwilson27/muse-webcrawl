Retablo

A retablo is a framed surface — typically a structure of wood, stone, or other material — placed behind and above the altar of a church, decorated with paintings and/or sculpture representing religious subjects. The term derives from the Latin retro tabulum altaris, "behind the altar table." Retablos range from simple panels to enormous architectural constructions covering the entire east wall of a church, and they are especially characteristic of late medieval and early modern Spanish and Spanish colonial art.

In Latin American folk tradition, the word retablo also denotes small devotional paintings on tin or wood, and in Peru a portable box-altar containing figurines of saints and religious scenes; these folk forms developed from the same colonial religious culture.

Spanish retablos

Retablos developed in Spain from the 14th century onward, growing in scale and complexity through the Gothic, Renaissance, and Baroque periods. Monumental Spanish retablos were typically organized in a grid of compartments (called encasamientos) arranged in horizontal registers (bodies) and vertical divisions (streets, or calles), crowned by an attic. Each compartment held a painting or a carved, polychromed and gilded wooden sculpture of a saint or a scene from the life of Christ or the Virgin.

Notable examples include the great retablo mayor of Seville Cathedral, one of the largest in Christendom, begun in 1482 and worked on for decades, and the retablos of Toledo, Burgos, and León cathedrals. Specialized craftsmen — architects of the wooden structure, sculptors, painters, and gilders — collaborated on these commissions under detailed contracts.

Construction and style

Wooden retablos were assembled from timber frameworks, carved in pine or other local woods, then finished with gesso (yeso), gilding (dorado), and painted surfaces. The technique of estofado — scratching patterns through paint applied over gold leaf — produced rich brocade effects. Architectural framing evolved from Gothic pinnacles and tracery through Renaissance classical orders to the exuberant twisted Solomonic columns and estípite supports of the Spanish Baroque (Churrigueresque) style.

Colonial Latin America

Spanish colonial churches in Mexico, Peru, and elsewhere adopted the monumental retablo, often executed by indigenous and mestizo craftsmen who blended European iconography with local materials and motifs. Gilded Baroque retablos survive in great numbers in Mexican churches, and the style reached extreme ornamental density in the Mexican ultra-Baroque.

Folk devotional retablos

In 19th- and early 20th-century Mexico, small votive paintings on tinplate — also called ex-votos — depicted miracles and answered prayers, with a narrative scene above and a written account of the event below. In the Peruvian Andes, retablos are portable wooden boxes whose doors open to reveal tiers of small painted figurines made from pasta (a potato-and-plaster mixture), representing religious festivals, saints, and scenes of everyday life; the form is associated especially with the Ayacucho region and artisans such as Joaquín López Antay.
