Federally funded research and development centers

Federally funded research and development centers (FFRDCs) are research institutions in the United States that are owned by the federal government but operated by contractors — typically universities or nonprofit organizations — under long-term agreements. They were created to give government agencies access to specialized scientific and engineering expertise that could not be met as effectively by in-house staff or commercial firms.

Characteristics

FFRDCs operate in the public interest, are free of commercial conflicts of interest, and have special access to government data and facilities. In return they are restricted from competing with the private sector for commercial work and from manufacturing products. The National Science Foundation maintains the master list of FFRDCs; there are roughly forty, receiving on the order of billions of federal dollars per year.

Examples

  • National laboratories of the Department of Energy, such as Los Alamos, Lawrence Livermore, Sandia, Oak Ridge, and Argonne.
  • Defense centers, including MIT Lincoln Laboratory, the Jet Propulsion Laboratory (operated by Caltech for NASA), and centers run by RAND, MITRE, and the Aerospace Corporation.
  • Other agencies: the National Center for Atmospheric Research (NSF), the National Cancer Institute's Frederick National Laboratory, and the National Cybersecurity FFRDC.

History

The model grew out of World War II collaborations between the government and universities, such as the MIT Radiation Laboratory and the Manhattan Project laboratories. RAND, created in 1948 to continue wartime operations research for the Air Force, is often considered the prototypical FFRDC and the original "think tank." The arrangement was formalized in federal policy in the 1960s and in acquisition regulations in 1984.
