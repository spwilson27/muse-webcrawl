Phaistos (Greek: Φαιστός) is a Bronze Age archaeological site at modern Tymbaki in south-central Crete, Greece, and was one of the major centres of Minoan civilization. The palace of Phaistos was the second most important Minoan palace after Knossos, and the site overlooks the fertile Messara Plain.

History

The site was inhabited from about 3000 BC. The first palace was built around 2000 BC (Middle Minoan period) and destroyed by an earthquake around 1700 BC, after which a second, grander palace was constructed on its ruins. Like the other Minoan palaces, Phaistos was destroyed around 1450 BC, probably in connection with the Mycenaean takeover of Crete. In the historical period a Greek city named Phaistos stood nearby; it minted its own coins and survived into the Hellenistic era before being destroyed by Gortyn around 150 BC.

The palace

The palace complex, excavated by the Italian School of Archaeology under Federico Halbherr and Luigi Pernier from 1900, is organized around a central court and a west court, with a monumental staircase, royal apartments, storerooms with giant pithoi (storage jars), and a theatre area. Unlike Knossos, Phaistos was not controversially reconstructed, so visitors see the ruins much as excavators found them.

Phaistos Disc

The site's most famous find is the Phaistos Disc, a fired-clay disc stamped with 241 pictographic signs in a spiral, discovered by Luigi Pernier in 1908 in the palace complex. Dated to roughly the 2nd millennium BC, the disc remains undeciphered and the purpose and meaning of its unique script are subjects of continuing debate.
