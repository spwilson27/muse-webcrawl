The lunar south pole is the southernmost point of the Moon, lying near the rim of Shackleton Crater. The polar region — broadly, latitudes south of about 80°S — has become the most sought-after destination in 21st-century lunar exploration because it combines two rare resources: permanently shadowed regions (PSRs) inside deep craters, where water ice has accumulated over billions of years, and nearby high ground that receives near-continuous sunlight for solar power.

Environment

The Moon's axis is tilted only about 1.5° to the ecliptic, so at the poles the Sun never climbs high above the horizon. Floors of deep polar craters have not seen sunlight for billions of years and reach temperatures below 40 K (−233 °C), among the coldest measured in the Solar System (NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter measured −238 °C in Hermite Crater near the north pole). These cold traps preserve volatiles delivered by comets and asteroids or produced in situ. Meanwhile, points on crater rims such as those of Shackleton can be illuminated more than 80–90% of the year.

Evidence for ice

Radar and neutron data from Lunar Prospector (1998) first suggested hydrogen enrichment at both poles. In 2009 the LCROSS impactor struck Cabeus Crater and its shepherding spacecraft measured water at roughly 5.6% by mass in the ejected plume, along with other volatiles. India's Chandrayaan-1 Moon Mineralogy Mapper detected surface water/hydroxyl signatures, and LRO instruments mapped extensive shadowed cold traps; in 2018 researchers reported exposed surface water ice at both poles.

Exploration

No human has yet visited the lunar south pole. Robotic milestones include India's Chandrayaan-3, which on 23 August 2023 became the first mission to land in the south polar region (about 69°S), making India the fourth nation to soft-land on the Moon. Intuitive Machines' Odysseus lander (IM-1) reached about 80°S in February 2024, the southernmost lunar landing. NASA's Artemis programme plans crewed landings near the south pole (Artemis III, with candidate regions around craters including Amundsen and Nobile), and rovers such as the planned VIPER were designed to prospect polar ice. China likewise targets the region for its Chang'e-7 mission and a future international lunar research station.

Strategic significance

Polar water ice could be refined into drinking water, breathable oxygen and — by electrolysis — hydrogen/oxygen rocket propellant, making the region a potential refuelling depot for deep-space exploration. Scarce sunlit high ground and limited "safe" landing zones raise questions about coordination and resource rights under the Outer Space Treaty and the Artemis Accords.
