Fiat money

Fiat money is a type of currency that is not backed by a precious metal, such as gold or silver, or any other tangible asset or commodity. Fiat currency is typically declared by a government to be legal tender, and its value is determined by the law, economic policy, and the trust people place in the issuing authority. Since the end of the Bretton Woods system in 1971, when the United States ended the convertibility of the dollar into gold, most major world currencies — including the US dollar, euro, pound sterling, and yen — have been fiat currencies.

History

Fiat money was first used in China in the 11th century during the Song dynasty, when jiaozi notes circulated in Sichuan as state-issued paper money. In the West, paper money issued by colonial governments and by revolutionary France (the assignats) provided early examples, with mixed success and frequent inflation. The 20th century saw a gradual retreat from the gold standard: Britain suspended it in 1914 and left it in 1931; the United States restricted domestic gold convertibility in 1933; and President Nixon's 1971 suspension of international gold redemption made the system fully fiat.

Advantages and criticism

Supporters argue fiat money gives central banks greater control over the money supply, enabling them to manage recessions, act as lenders of last resort, and collect seigniorage. Critics, including proponents of the gold standard and of cryptocurrencies, argue that fiat money tempts governments into over-issuance, producing inflation and occasionally hyperinflation — as in Weimar Germany, Zimbabwe, and Venezuela. The value of a fiat currency ultimately rests on confidence in the issuing state's economy and institutions.
