Questioned document examination

Questioned document examination (QDE) is a forensic science discipline whose purpose is to answer questions about a disputed document using a variety of scientific processes and methods. Many examinations involve a comparison of the questioned document, or components of the document, to a set of known standards provided by an investigator. The most common type of examination involves handwriting, in which the examiner attempts to determine whether a signature or handwritten note was written by a particular person.

Scope

Examinations may address the authenticity of documents, alterations, obliterations, additions, the identification of writing instruments, inks, papers, typewriters, printers, photocopiers, and the detection of indented impressions. Questioned document examiners may also determine the sequence in which marks were made on a document and examine charred or water-damaged documents.

Methods

Examiners use stereomicroscopes, magnification, and various light sources, including infrared and ultraviolet illumination, to reveal differences in ink and erasures. Instruments such as the electrostatic detection apparatus (ESDA) recover indented writing from pages beneath the original. Ink and paper may be analysed chemically, for example by thin-layer chromatography, though destructive testing is undertaken only when necessary and permitted.

Standards and practitioners

Professional bodies include the American Society of Questioned Document Examiners (ASQDE), founded in 1942. Standards for the work are published by bodies such as ASTM International, and examiners may be certified by the American Board of Forensic Document Examiners. Courts evaluate such testimony under rules governing expert evidence, such as the Daubert standard in the United States.
