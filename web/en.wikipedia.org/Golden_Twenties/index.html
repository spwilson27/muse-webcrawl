Golden Twenties

The Golden Twenties (German: Goldene Zwanziger) refers to the period of relative economic stability, cultural dynamism, and artistic innovation in Germany — especially Berlin — between 1924 and 1929, during the middle years of the Weimar Republic. The term parallels the "Roaring Twenties" in the United States.

Background

The period began with the end of hyperinflation through the introduction of the Rentenmark and the Dawes Plan of 1924, which regularised reparations and brought American loans and investment. Under Foreign Minister Gustav Stresemann, Germany rejoined international diplomacy through the Locarno Treaties (1925) and entry into the League of Nations (1926).

Culture

Berlin became a byword for avant-garde culture: the Bauhaus school, Expressionist and New Objectivity painting, the cinema of Fritz Lang and F. W. Murnau, Brecht and Weill's theatre, cabaret, jazz, and a famously permissive nightlife. Mass media, radio, and illustrated magazines flourished. The era ended abruptly with the Wall Street Crash of 1929 and the onset of the Great Depression, which destabilised the Republic and paved the way for the rise of Nazism.
