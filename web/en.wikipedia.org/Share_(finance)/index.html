Share (finance)

A 17th-century Dutch share certificate

In financial markets, a share (sometimes referred to as stock) is a unit of equity ownership in the capital stock of a corporation. A person who owns shares in a company is called a shareholder (or stockholder) and may receive a portion of the company's profits in the form of dividends, as well as voting rights in proportion to the number of shares held. Shares may be bought and sold on stock exchanges or privately, and their prices reflect investors' expectations of the company's future earnings.

Terminology

"Stock" in general refers to the total equity capital of a company or to shares collectively, while a "share" is a single unit of that stock. A company's authorised share capital is divided into shares, each with a nominal or par value; the shares a company has actually issued are its issued share capital, and those in investors' hands are its shares outstanding (excluding any treasury shares the company has bought back).

Types of shares

Common shares

Common shares (ordinary shares) typically carry voting rights and a residual claim on profits and assets: common shareholders are paid last, after creditors and preferred shareholders, but enjoy unlimited participation in the company's growth. Most publicly traded shares are common shares.

Preferred shares

Preferred shares (preference shares) have priority over common shares in the payment of dividends and in liquidation, usually paying a fixed dividend, but typically carry limited or no voting rights. Many are cumulative (missed dividends accrue) or convertible into common shares.

Other classes

Companies may issue multiple classes of shares with different voting rights — for example, dual-class structures in which founders hold shares with multiple votes per share — as well as redeemable shares, bonus shares, and restricted shares. Historically, bearer shares were transferable by delivery alone; these have been largely abolished because of money-laundering concerns.

Rights of shareholders

Shareholders typically have the rights to vote at general meetings (including electing directors), receive dividends when declared, inspect certain company records, transfer their shares, and claim a share of surplus assets on winding up. Shareholders also benefit from limited liability: their losses are limited to the amount they paid for their shares. Pre-emptive rights, where they exist, allow existing shareholders to maintain their proportionate stake when new shares are issued.

History

Transferable shares emerged with the joint-stock companies of the 17th century; the Dutch East India Company (VOC) issued the first widely traded shares on the Amsterdam stock exchange in 1602, complete with the first recorded dividend payments and share certificates. Share trading spread to London coffee houses and later to formal exchanges worldwide, becoming the principal mechanism by which companies raise equity capital.

Valuation

Shares are valued by many methods, including discounted cash-flow models, dividend discount models, and comparisons of ratios such as the price-to-earnings ratio. A share's market price at any moment is set by supply and demand among buyers and sellers, and can deviate substantially from any computed intrinsic value.
