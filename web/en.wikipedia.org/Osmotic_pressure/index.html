Osmotic pressure


Definition: Minimum pressure needed to prevent inward flow of solvent across a semipermeable membrane
Governing relation: van 't Hoff equation, Π = icRT

Osmotic pressure is the minimum pressure which needs to be applied to a solution to prevent the inward flow of its pure solvent across a semipermeable membrane. It is also defined as the measure of the tendency of a solution to take in its pure solvent by osmosis. Osmotic pressure is a colligative property, determined by the molar concentration of dissolved particles rather than their chemical nature.

The van 't Hoff equation

Jacobus van 't Hoff showed in 1887 that dilute solutions obey an equation analogous to the ideal gas law: Π = icRT, where Π is osmotic pressure, i is the van 't Hoff factor (the number of particles into which a solute dissociates), c is molar concentration, R is the gas constant and T the absolute temperature. Wilhelm Pfeffer's measurements with semipermeable membranes of copper ferrocyanide provided the experimental data; Morse and Frazer later refined the theory.

Applications

Reverse osmosis applies pressure exceeding the osmotic pressure to force solvent through a membrane, a principle used in desalination of seawater and water purification. In biology, osmotic pressure drives water movement across cell membranes, maintains turgor in plant cells, and in blood vessels the oncotic pressure exerted by plasma proteins opposes filtration out of capillaries. Osmotic pressure measurements are also used to determine the molar masses of large molecules such as polymers and proteins.
