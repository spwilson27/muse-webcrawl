Freedom Summer

Freedom Summer, also known as the Mississippi Summer Project, was a volunteer campaign launched in June 1964 to register African-American voters in Mississippi, where discriminatory practices such as literacy tests and poll taxes — backed by violence — had kept Black registration at around 7 percent of those eligible. The project was organised by the Council of Federated Organizations (COFO), an umbrella of civil rights groups including SNCC, CORE, the NAACP, and the SCLC, and directed by Bob Moses.

The campaign

Roughly 1,000 volunteers, mostly white college students from the North, joined local Black activists. Alongside voter registration, the project established Freedom Schools — some 41 of them teaching literacy, civics, and Black history to about 3,000 students — community centres, and the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party (MFDP), which challenged the seating of the all-white regular delegation at the 1964 Democratic National Convention in Atlantic City.

Violence and the murders of Chaney, Goodman, and Schwerner

On 21 June 1964, three project workers — James Chaney, a Black Mississippian, and Andrew Goodman and Michael Schwerner, white New Yorkers — disappeared after investigating a church burning in Neshoba County. Their bodies were found buried in an earthen dam 44 days later; they had been murdered by Ku Klux Klan members, some of them local lawmen. Over the summer, volunteers were beaten and arrested, and dozens of Black churches and homes were bombed or burned.

Legacy

National attention to the campaign and its violence helped build support for the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965. Fannie Lou Hamer's televised testimony for the MFDP became one of the era's defining moments, and many volunteers went on to careers in law, politics, journalism, and academia.
