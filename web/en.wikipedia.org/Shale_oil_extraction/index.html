Shale oil extraction

Shale oil extraction is an industrial process for unconventional oil production. It converts kerogen, the solid organic matter in oil shale rock, into liquid shale oil, gas, and char by pyrolysis — heating the rock in the absence of oxygen — followed by hydrogenation or other upgrading. Shale oil extraction is distinct both from conventional petroleum production and from the extraction of tight oil trapped in shale formations by hydraulic fracturing, with which it is sometimes confused.

Process

Mined oil shale is crushed and heated in a retort to about 450–550 °C. Kerogen decomposes into oil vapour and gas, which are condensed and separated, while the mineral residue — spent shale — is discharged. Raw shale oil contains nitrogen, sulphur, oxygen compounds, and olefins, and must be upgraded before it can substitute for crude oil in refineries. Energy for the process is often generated by burning the residual char or the produced gas.

Technologies

Above-ground retorts include the Kiviter and Galoter (Enefit) processes of Estonia, the Fushun retort of China, and Brazil's Petrosix technology. In-situ methods heat the shale underground over months or years — for example Shell's freeze-wall process with downhole electric heaters — aiming to avoid mining and surface waste, though none has reached large commercial scale. Classification schemes distinguish processes by heating method, heat carrier (gas, solid, or volumetric), and whether retorting occurs above or below ground.

History and industry

Oil shale was worked for oil in France and Scotland from the mid-19th century — James Young's Scottish shale oil industry long predates the petroleum age — and boomed during the world wars. Estonia built a major industry on its kukersite deposits and still burns oil shale for electricity as well as producing shale oil. Modern production is concentrated in Estonia, China (Fushun basin), and Brazil. World resources of oil shale are enormous — the Green River Formation of the western United States alone is estimated to hold the equivalent of well over a trillion barrels of oil — but extraction is costly and water-intensive, so output remains a small fraction of global crude oil supply.

Environmental issues

Surface retorting produces large volumes of spent shale — the processed rock bulks up by roughly 15–30% — which is stockpiled in waste heaps, together with ash, process water, and air emissions. Lifecycle greenhouse-gas emissions of shale oil are generally higher than those of conventional oil. In-situ methods would reduce surface disturbance but raise questions about groundwater protection and long heating energy demand.
