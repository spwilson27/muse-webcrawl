New Journalism

New Journalism is a style of news writing and journalism, developed in the 1960s and 1970s, that uses literary techniques deemed unconventional at the time. It is characterised by a subjective perspective, a literary style reminiscent of long-form non-fiction, and a vivid, scene-based construction of events. The term was codified with its enduring meaning in a 1973 anthology of the same name edited by Tom Wolfe, who championed the style along with writers such as Truman Capote, Hunter S. Thompson, Joan Didion, Norman Mailer and Gay Talese.

Techniques

Wolfe identified four devices that New Journalists borrowed from realist fiction: scene-by-scene construction, extensive use of realistic dialogue, presenting scenes through the eyes of a particular character (third-person point of view), and the recording of everyday gestures, habits, manners and other "status details" that reveal the social standing of the people described. Writers often immersed themselves in their subjects for long periods, reporting through "saturation reporting".

History

Although Wolfe argued that earlier writers from Dickens and Balzac to Jack London and George Orwell anticipated the style, the movement coalesced in American magazine writing of the 1960s, in publications such as Esquire, New York, Harper's and Rolling Stone. Landmark works include Capote's In Cold Blood (1966), which he called a "nonfiction novel"; Mailer's The Armies of the Night (1968); Thompson's Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas (1971), an example of the related gonzo journalism; and Didion's essay collections Slouching Towards Bethlehem (1968) and The White Album (1979).

Criticism

Traditional journalists criticised the movement for blurring the line between fact and fiction, questioning the verifiability of reconstructed dialogue and interior thoughts. Wolfe responded that the techniques produced reporting that was more, not less, accurate, capturing the emotional reality of events that conventional inverted-pyramid reporting missed.

Legacy

New Journalism influenced later generations of long-form and literary journalists and remains a standard reference point in debates about narrative non-fiction, creative non-fiction and the boundaries of journalistic objectivity.
