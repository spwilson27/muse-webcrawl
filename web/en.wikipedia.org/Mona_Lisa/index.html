The Mona Lisa (Italian: Gioconda or Monna Lisa; French: Joconde) is a half-length portrait painting by Italian artist Leonardo da Vinci. Considered an archetypal masterpiece of the Italian Renaissance, it has been described as "the best known, the most visited, the most written about, the most sung about, the most parodied work of art in the world". The painting's novel qualities include the subject's enigmatic expression, the monumentality of the composition, the subtle modelling of forms, and the atmospheric illusionism.

The painting has been traditionally considered to depict the Italian noblewoman Lisa del Giocondo, the wife of the Florentine silk merchant Francesco del Giocondo. Leonardo painted it in Florence, probably beginning in 1503 or 1504, and continued working on it for years; he took it with him to France, where it was acquired after his death by King Francis I. It is now the property of the French Republic and has been on permanent display at the Louvre in Paris since 1797.

Description

The sitter is shown seated in a loggia, turned three-quarters toward the viewer, her hands folded and resting on the arm of the chair. Behind her stretches an imaginary landscape of winding paths, a bridge, and distant icy mountains rendered in aerial perspective. Leonardo's sfumato technique — soft, smoky transitions without lines or borders — is especially evident in the corners of the mouth and eyes, producing the famous ambiguous smile that seems to change as the viewer shifts focus. The portrait was innovative in its pose and composition and became a model for portraiture for centuries.

Theft and fame

The Mona Lisa was admired by connoisseurs but was not uniquely famous until the 19th century, when Romantic and Symbolist writers celebrated its mysterious femininity. Its theft from the Louvre on 21 August 1911 by Vincenzo Peruggia, an Italian handyman who believed it should be returned to Italy, made international headlines and transformed it into a global icon. It was recovered in Florence in December 1913. The painting has since survived vandalism attempts (acid in 1956, a thrown rock, red paint in Tokyo in 1974, a teacup in 2009, cake in 2022) and is displayed behind bulletproof glass in climate-controlled conditions.

Legacy

The work holds the Guinness World Record for the highest known insurance valuation in history, assessed at US$100 million in 1962 (equivalent to roughly $1 billion in recent terms). It has been endlessly copied, parodied — most famously by Marcel Duchamp's L.H.O.O.Q. (1919) — and referenced in popular culture, and it draws the largest crowds of any painting at the Louvre, where it hangs in the Salle des États.
