Edith Cavell

Edith Louisa Cavell (4 December 1865 – 12 October 1915) was a British nurse who worked in German-occupied Belgium during the First World War. She treated wounded soldiers from both sides at the Berkendael Medical Institute in Brussels, and helped some 200 Allied soldiers escape from occupied Belgium to the neutral Netherlands. She was arrested by the German authorities, tried by a military court for treason, and executed by firing squad. Her execution provoked international outrage and she became an iconic figure of wartime propaganda.

Early life

Cavell was born in Swardeston, Norfolk, the daughter of a vicar. She worked as a governess, including for a family in Brussels, before training as a nurse at the London Hospital under Matron Eva Luckes. In 1907 she returned to Brussels to become matron of the Berkendael Medical Institute, where she helped professionalise nursing in Belgium.

Wartime work and arrest

When Brussels fell to the Germans in 1914, Cavell's clinic was taken over by the Red Cross and treated casualties of all nationalities. She joined an underground network that sheltered Allied soldiers cut off behind the lines and guided them to the Dutch border. Betrayed and arrested in August 1915, she admitted her activities at her court-martial.

Execution and legacy

Despite diplomatic appeals from the United States and Spain, Cavell was shot at dawn on 12 October 1915 at the Tir national shooting range in Schaerbeek. Her reported last words included the statement that 'patriotism is not enough'. After the war her body was repatriated and buried at Norwich Cathedral; memorials to her stand in London, Brussels and elsewhere, and Mount Edith Cavell in Canada is named in her honour.
