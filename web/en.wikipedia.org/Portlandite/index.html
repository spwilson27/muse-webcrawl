Portlandite is a calcium hydroxide mineral with the chemical formula Ca(OH)2. It is the naturally occurring form of calcium hydroxide and an important phase in the chemistry of Portland cement, from which it takes its name. It was first described in 1933 from occurrences at Vesuvius in Italy and in Northern Ireland.

Occurrence

Portlandite occurs in volcanic and metamorphic environments, including in contact-metamorphosed limestones and in lavas. Notable localities include Mount Vesuvius, the Scawt Hill intrusion in County Antrim, Northern Ireland, and various mines in South Africa and the United States.

Role in cement and concrete

In hydrated Portland cement, portlandite forms as a hydration product of the calcium silicate phases (alite and belite). It typically makes up 20–25% of the mass of hydrated cement paste. Portlandite buffers the pore solution at a high pH, which protects embedded steel reinforcement from corrosion, but it is also relatively soft, soluble, and contributes little to strength. It reacts with atmospheric carbon dioxide in the carbonation of concrete, and with supplementary cementitious materials such as fly ash and silica fume in pozzolanic reactions to form additional calcium silicate hydrate.

Properties

Portlandite crystallizes in the trigonal system, commonly forming hexagonal plate-like crystals. It has a Mohs hardness of 2 and perfect cleavage. It is slightly soluble in water, forming the alkaline solution known as limewater.
