Analog Science Fiction and Fact


A classic issue of Astounding, the magazine's title until 1960.

Analog Science Fiction and Fact is an American science fiction magazine, the longest continuously published in the genre. It was founded in 1930 as Astounding Stories and for most of its history carried the title Astounding Science Fiction, changing its name to Analog in 1960.

History

Under editor John W. Campbell from 1937, Astounding became the centre of the so-called Golden Age of Science Fiction, launching or developing the careers of Isaac Asimov, Robert A. Heinlein, A. E. van Vogt, L. Sprague de Camp and many others. Campbell demanded plausible, idea-driven stories and published formative works including Asimov's Foundation and Robot stories and Heinlein's early Future History tales.

The magazine serialised classics such as Frank Herbert's Dune (as "Dune World" and "The Prophet of Dune") in the 1960s. Later editors included Ben Bova and Stanley Schmidt; since 2013 it has been edited by Trevor Quachri. Once a pulp, it survives today as a digest-format magazine, the field's senior publication.

Influence

Analog's emphasis on "hard" science fiction and problem-solving stories shaped the genre's mainstream for decades, and its letters column was an early hub of organised science fiction fandom.
