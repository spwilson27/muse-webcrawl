Disulfide

In chemistry, a disulfide (or disulphide in traditional British spelling) is a compound containing a disulfide functional group or the anion S22−. The structure of the disulfide anion is H−S−S−H. The anion is formed (mainly) from hydrogen sulfide. More generally the term may refer to a chemical compound containing an S−S bond, such as disulfur dichloride (S2Cl2).

In biochemistry, a disulfide bond (also called an S–S bond or disulfide bridge) is a covalent bond derived by the coupling of two thiol groups. Disulfide bonds are common in proteins, where they form between cysteine residues and stabilize tertiary and quaternary structure. The linkage is also called an SS-bond or sometimes a disulfide bridge and is usually derived by the coupling of two thiol groups.

Occurrence in proteins

Disulfide bonds play an important role in the folding and stability of many proteins, especially those secreted to the extracellular medium. Insulin was the first protein for which disulfide bonds were characterized; it contains two interchain and one intrachain disulfide bond. Hair and feathers owe much of their strength to the disulfide bonds of keratin, which are rearranged in permanent-wave treatments.

Chemistry

Disulfides are formed by oxidation of thiols, and can be cleaved by reduction:

2 RSH ⇌ RS−SR + 2 H+ + 2 e−

The S–S bond length is typically around 2.05 Å, and the bond tends to adopt dihedral angles near 90°. In organic synthesis, disulfides feature in vulcanization of rubber, in lipoic acid, and in drugs such as disulfiram.

Inorganic disulfides

Many metal ores contain disulfide units, including pyrite (FeS2), "fool's gold". Carbon disulfide (CS2) is an important industrial solvent, although strictly it contains C=S double bonds rather than an S–S linkage.
