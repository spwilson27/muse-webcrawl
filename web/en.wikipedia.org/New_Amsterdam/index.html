New Amsterdam (Dutch: Nieuw Amsterdam) was a 17th-century Dutch settlement established at the southern tip of Manhattan Island that served as the seat of the colonial government in New Netherland. The settlement, outside Fort Amsterdam on Manhattan Island in the New Netherland province, was situated between the Hudson River (then "North River") and the East River.

History

The Dutch West India Company founded the colony of New Netherland in 1624, with settlers arriving on Manhattan from 1624–1625. In 1626 Director Peter Minuit famously "purchased" Manhattan from the Lenape for goods valued at 60 guilders — the origin of the "$24" legend. New Amsterdam was formally established as the capital in 1626 and received municipal rights in 1653.

Under Director-General Peter Stuyvesant (from 1647) the town gained a defensive wall (along present-day Wall Street) and a canal (now Broad Street). The settlement was a cosmopolitan trading port of roughly 1,500–2,500 inhabitants by the 1660s, with perhaps 18 languages spoken. In 1664, during the Second Anglo-Dutch War, Stuyvesant surrendered the colony to an English fleet without a fight; New Amsterdam was renamed New York in honour of the Duke of York. The Dutch briefly retook it in 1673 (renaming it New Orange) before the Treaty of Westminster (1674) confirmed English possession.

Legacy

Traces of New Amsterdam survive in New York's street plan of Lower Manhattan, Dutch-derived place names (Harlem, Brooklyn, the Bronx, Staten Island), and institutions of religious tolerance and commerce that historians trace partly to Dutch precedent.
