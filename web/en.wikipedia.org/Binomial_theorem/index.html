Binomial theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In elementary algebra, the binomial theorem (or binomial expansion) describes the algebraic expansion of powers of a binomial. According to the theorem, it is possible to expand the polynomial (x + y)n into a sum involving terms of the form axbyc, where the exponents b and c are nonnegative integers with b + c = n, and the coefficient a of each term is a specific positive integer depending on n and b. For example,

(x + y)4 = x4 + 4x3y + 6x2y2 + 4xy3 + y4.

The coefficient a in the term of xbyc is known as the binomial coefficient, written C(n, b) or "n choose b", equal to n! / (b! (n−b)!). These coefficients can be arranged in Pascal's triangle, in which each entry is the sum of the two above it.

History

Special cases of the theorem were known since at least the fourth century BC, when Euclid mentioned the case n = 2. The coefficients were tabulated by the Indian mathematician Pingala (c. 3rd century BC), by Chinese mathematicians in the works culminating in Yang Hui's triangle (13th century), and by Persian mathematicians including al-Karaji and Omar Khayyam. Isaac Newton generalised the theorem to non-integer and negative exponents, giving the infinite binomial series, published in the 1670s.

Statement and examples

For any nonnegative integer n,

(x + y)n = Σk=0..n C(n, k) xn−k yk.

Thus (x + y)2 = x2 + 2xy + y2 and (x + y)3 = x3 + 3x2y + 3xy2 + y3. Substituting y = −y gives the expansion of (x − y)n with alternating signs. The theorem can be proved by mathematical induction, or combinatorially: each term corresponds to choosing which factors contribute a y, and C(n, k) counts those choices — the basis of its central role in combinatorics and probability, including the binomial distribution.

Generalisations

Newton's generalised binomial theorem extends the result to any real exponent via an infinite series convergent for |y/x| < 1. The multinomial theorem extends the expansion to sums of more than two terms, and analogues exist for binomial-type sequences, q-series, and non-commuting variables.
