Climate of India

India hosts a wide range of climate types, from alpine conditions in the Himalayas to tropical monsoon climates

The climate of India consists of a wide range of weather conditions across a vast geographic scale and varied topography. Based on the Köppen system, India hosts seven major climatic sub-types: arid desert in the west, alpine tundra and glaciers in the north, humid tropical regions supporting rainforests in the southwest and the island territories, and the monsoon climates of the interior.

The nation has four seasons: winter (January–February), summer (March–May), monsoon (June–September) and post-monsoon (October–December). India's unique geography and geology strongly influence its climate; the Himalayas act as a barrier to cold katabatic winds from Central Asia, keeping the subcontinent warmer than corresponding latitudes elsewhere. The Thar Desert attracts the moisture-laden southwest monsoon winds that provide most of India's rainfall.

Monsoon

The southwest summer monsoon, which brings over 75% of India's annual rainfall, arrives on the Kerala coast around the beginning of June and advances northward. The northeast or retreating monsoon brings rain to the southeastern peninsula, especially Tamil Nadu, in October and November. Rainfall averages about 1,250 mm nationally but varies enormously, from under 150 mm in western Rajasthan to over 11,000 mm at Mawsynram in Meghalaya, one of the wettest places on Earth.

Extremes and disasters

India experiences heat waves, floods, droughts and tropical cyclones, particularly along the Bay of Bengal coast. The highest temperature reliably recorded in India is 51.0 °C at Phalodi, Rajasthan, in May 2016. Changing precipitation patterns and rising temperatures, driven in part by the feedbacks of the global climate system, are major concerns for agriculture and water resources. Much of the population depends on monsoon-fed agriculture and on fisheries such as those served by Cochin in Kerala.
