Jati

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jāti (Devanagari: जाति, Bengali: জাতি, Telugu: జాతి, Kannada: ಜಾತಿ, Malayalam: ജാതി, Tamil: சாதி, also spelled Jati) is the term used for the thousands of clans, tribes, communities, and sub-communities in India. It is often translated as "caste", though it is more specific than that term. Jatis are generally ascribed by birth (hereditary social groups) within the Indian caste system.

Definition

There is no universally accepted definition or set of criteria for a jati. Over the centuries, jatis have formed through social and economic processes, often around occupations, regions, customs, and endogamous marriage practices. A person belongs to their jati by birth, and marriage has traditionally been within the jati (endogamy) or at least within a cluster of related jatis.

Relation to varna

The jati system is distinct from, though often mapped onto, the fourfold varna classification of Brahmins (priests and scholars), Kshatriyas (rulers and warriors), Vaishyas (merchants and landowners), and Shudras (labourers and service providers). Varna is a broad theoretical framework described in ancient texts such as the Rigveda and the Manusmriti, whereas jatis are the practical, lived units of social organisation, numbering in the thousands across the subcontinent. Many jatis may be assigned, or claim, a place within one of the varnas, though such placement is frequently contested.

Occupation and mobility

Historically, many jatis were associated with particular occupations or crafts — such as weaving, pottery, metalworking, agriculture, or trade — and hereditary occupation reinforced group identity. Over time, however, jatis have changed occupations, risen or fallen in local status, split into sub-groups, or merged with others. Processes such as Sanskritisation, in which a jati adopts the rituals and customs of a higher-status group, illustrate the dynamism of the system.

Modern India

In modern India, jati continues to play a role in marriage, kinship, community organisations, and politics, even as the Constitution of India prohibits caste discrimination. Government policies of reservation (affirmative action) classify many jatis within categories such as Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes, and Other Backward Classes, making jati identity administratively and politically significant.
