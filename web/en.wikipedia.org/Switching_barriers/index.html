Switching barriers

Switching barriers
Field: Marketing, economics
Related concept: Switching costs
Effect: Customer lock-in

Switching barriers (or switching costs) are the obstacles — financial, procedural, or relational — that a customer faces when changing from one supplier, product, or brand to another. They are a central concept in marketing and industrial economics because they give incumbent firms a degree of market power over their existing customers, a phenomenon known as lock-in.

Types

Switching barriers are commonly divided into three categories. Financial barriers include contract termination fees, loss of loyalty points or discounts, and the cost of buying new equipment. Procedural barriers involve the time and effort of learning a new system, transferring data, or evaluating alternatives. Relational barriers arise from personal relationships with staff and identification with a brand.

Implications

High switching barriers reduce competition for existing customers even where markets appear open, and firms often invest deliberately in creating them through loyalty programmes, proprietary formats, and long-term contracts. Regulators sometimes intervene to lower barriers — for example through number portability in telecommunications or data portability rules — to increase competition.
