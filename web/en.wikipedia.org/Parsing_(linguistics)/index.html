Parsing (linguistics)

In linguistics, parsing is the process by which a sentence is analysed into its component parts and their syntactic relationships, either by a human reader or listener in real time, or by a computational system. The word derives from the Latin pars orationis, "part of speech": traditionally, to parse a sentence in grammar teaching meant identifying the part of speech of each word and its function in the sentence. In psycholinguistics, parsing research asks how the human language processor builds structure incrementally and resolves temporary ambiguities; in computational linguistics, parsing is the task of assigning a syntactic structure — usually a parse tree or a set of dependency relations — to a sentence.

Human sentence processing

Listeners do not wait until the end of a sentence to interpret it: structure is built word by word, which occasionally leads the parser down a "garden path", as in "The horse raced past the barn fell", where the initially preferred analysis must be revised. Theories of human parsing debate how strongly syntax is guided by word frequency, plausibility, and discourse context. Eye-tracking and self-paced reading experiments, and brain responses such as the P600 event-related potential elicited by syntactic violations, are the main sources of evidence.

Computational parsing

Computational parsers differ in the representation they output. Constituency parsers produce phrase-structure trees; dependency parsers produce networks of head–dependent relations between words. Early systems were rule-based, driven by hand-written grammars such as the context-free grammars of the Chomsky hierarchy. Modern parsers are usually statistical or neural: trained on treebanks such as the Penn Treebank, they learn to predict the most probable structure, and neural parsers based on sequence models and transformers now achieve high accuracy on standard benchmarks, though they can still falter on rare constructions and long-distance dependencies.

Applications

Parsed structure underpins many language technologies, including machine translation, information extraction, question answering, and grammar checking, and treebanks have become fundamental resources for both linguistic theory and natural language processing.
