Dachau trials

The Dachau trials were a series of trials of war criminals conducted by the United States Army in the former Dachau concentration camp in occupied Germany between November 1945 and August 1948. Held by US military courts within the grounds of the camp, they were distinct from the better-known Nuremberg trials conducted by the International Military Tribunal.

Background

As American forces liberated camps across Germany in 1945, the Army established a war crimes programme to prosecute camp staff and those who had murdered Allied prisoners of war and downed airmen. Dachau, liberated on 29 April 1945, was chosen as the venue, and its former SS compound served as courtroom and internment centre.

Principal proceedings

The first and most prominent trial, United States v. Martin Gottfried Weiss et al. (November–December 1945), prosecuted 40 officials of the Dachau camp itself, including commandant Martin Gottfried Weiss. All were convicted; 36 were sentenced to death, and 28 were hanged at Landsberg Prison in May 1946. Subsequent mass trials covered the Mauthausen, Flossenbürg, Buchenwald, Mühldorf and Dora-Nordhausen camp systems, as well as the Malmedy massacre trial of Waffen-SS personnel for the murder of American prisoners during the Battle of the Bulge — the most controversial of the Dachau proceedings because of later allegations (investigated and largely discounted by US Senate inquiries) that confessions had been coerced.

Outcomes

Of roughly 1,672 defendants, about 1,416 were convicted and some 426 death sentences were pronounced, though many were commuted during reviews in the late 1940s and 1950s as Cold War politics shifted; the last Dachau-convicted prisoners were released by the mid-1950s. Legal historians note the trials' use of a "common design" theory of liability, a precursor of later joint-criminal-enterprise doctrine in international criminal law.
