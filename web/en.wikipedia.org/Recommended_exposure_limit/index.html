Recommended exposure limit

Personal air sampling is used to compare workers' exposures with the REL for a substance.

A recommended exposure limit (REL) is an occupational exposure limit recommended by the United States National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) to the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) for adoption as a legal permissible exposure limit (PEL). RELs are health-based recommendations derived from toxicological and epidemiological evidence; they are not themselves enforceable, but they inform OSHA rulemaking and are widely used by industrial hygienists as benchmarks for safe exposure.

Forms of RELs

Most RELs are expressed as time-weighted average (TWA) concentrations over a working shift of up to 10 hours a day during a 40-hour working week. For some substances NIOSH also recommends a short-term exposure limit (STEL), averaged over 15 minutes, and a ceiling limit that should not be exceeded at any time. Where a substance is a confirmed or potential occupational carcinogen, NIOSH policy has generally been to recommend the lowest feasible concentration, since no safe threshold can be demonstrated.

Development

NIOSH develops RELs through its criteria documents, which review the scientific literature on a substance's health effects and recommend exposure limits together with sampling methods, engineering controls and medical surveillance. NIOSH publishes the values in its Pocket Guide to Chemical Hazards, covering several hundred substances. Because OSHA's PELs were largely adopted in 1970 from 1968 consensus values and have been updated for only a minority of substances, RELs are frequently more protective than the corresponding legal limits; for example, the NIOSH REL for many solvents and dusts is a fraction of the OSHA PEL.

Comparison with other limits

RELs are one of three families of exposure limits commonly listed side by side: OSHA PELs, which are legal requirements; threshold limit values (TLVs) of the American Conference of Governmental Industrial Hygienists, which are professional recommendations; and RELs. Employers seeking to protect workers are generally advised to observe the most protective applicable value.

NIOSH also sets immediately dangerous to life or health (IDLH) values, a separate category used for respiratory protection decisions rather than routine exposure control.
