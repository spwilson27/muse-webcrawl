The Ambassadors (Holbein)

ArtistHans Holbein the Younger
Year1533
MediumOil on oak
Dimensions207 cm × 209.5 cm
LocationNational Gallery, London

The Ambassadors is a 1533 painting by Hans Holbein the Younger. Also known as Jean de Dinteville and Georges de Selve, it was created in the period of Henry VIII of England and portrays two French envoys to his court: Jean de Dinteville, the French ambassador to England (left), and his friend Georges de Selve, bishop of Lavaur (right). The work is renowned for its complex iconography and for the distorted skull that stretches across the bottom of the picture, the most famous example of anamorphosis in art.

Iconography

The two men stand before a two-tiered shelf laden with objects that evoke the quadrivium — arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy — including a celestial globe, sundials, a torquetum, a lute, a set of flutes, an arithmetic book, and a Lutheran hymnal. Several objects are subtly discordant: the lute has a broken string, one flute is missing from its case, and the hymn book is open at Martin Luther's hymns — details read as allusions to the religious and political disharmony of Europe on the eve of the English Reformation. A crucifix, half hidden by the curtain in the upper left, and a Persian rug and terrestrial globe complete the crowded symbolism of worldly learning, power, and death.

The skull

The elongated smear in the foreground resolves into a human skull only when viewed at an acute angle, from the lower right of the painting. As a memento mori, it contrasts with the sitters' wealth and learning; it may also pun on Holbein's name (a hohlbein is a hollow bone). The anamorphic skull has made the painting a central example in discussions of perspective and, famously, in Lacan's psychoanalytic account of the gaze.

History

The painting passed from Dinteville's château at Polisy through French collections and was acquired by the National Gallery in London in 1890, where it remains one of the museum's best-known works.
