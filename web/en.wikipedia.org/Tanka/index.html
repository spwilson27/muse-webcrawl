Tanka

Tanka (短歌, "short poem") is a classical form of Japanese poetry. A tanka consists of five phrases (ku) in a 5-7-5-7-7 pattern of sound units (on), totalling 31 — one line in Japanese, conventionally rendered as five lines in English translation. It is the oldest continuously practised major form of Japanese verse and the direct ancestor of the haiku.

History

Tanka (originally called waka) dominate the earliest Japanese poetry anthologies. The Man'yōshū (c. 759) contains more than 4,000 of them, and the imperial anthology Kokin Wakashū (c. 905) established the form's aesthetics for court poetry. For over a thousand years, skill in composing tanka was an essential accomplishment of courtiers, and poems were exchanged in courtship, diplomacy, and contests (uta-awase).

The upper three phrases (5-7-5) of a tanka could stand alone and eventually developed into the independent haiku, while the full form was linked in sequences (renga) when a second poet added the closing 7-7.

Modern tanka

In the Meiji era, poets such as Masaoka Shiki and Yosano Akiko modernized the tanka, urging personal expression over courtly convention. In the 20th century, poets like Ishikawa Takuboku and Tawara Machi (whose 1987 collection Salad Anniversary was a bestseller) brought contemporary language and subjects to the form. Tanka are now written worldwide, in Japanese and in many other languages, where the syllable pattern is usually kept at or under 31 syllables in five lines.
