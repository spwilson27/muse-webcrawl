Glaciology

Glaciology (from Latin glacies, "frost, ice") is the scientific study of glaciers and ice, and more broadly of the natural phenomena involving ice, including ice sheets, sea ice, snow, permafrost, and ice cores. It is an interdisciplinary field drawing on geology, geophysics, physical geography, climatology, meteorology, hydrology, and physics.

History

The scientific study of glaciers began in the Alps in the early 19th century. Louis Agassiz's work, including Études sur les glaciers (1840), popularised the theory that ice ages had once covered much of the Earth. In the 20th century the work of Hans Ahlmann, and later researchers such as John Nye and Walther Schwarzacher, placed glaciology on a physical footing, describing ice deformation and glacier flow mathematically.

Areas of study

Glaciologists study how glaciers form, move, and erode landscapes; the mass balance between snow accumulation and melting; and the record of past climates preserved in ice cores from Greenland and Antarctica, which extend back hundreds of thousands of years. Modern glaciology employs satellite remote sensing, radar sounding, GPS measurements, and numerical models to understand ice-sheet behaviour.

Importance

Understanding glaciers is central to predicting sea-level rise, managing freshwater resources fed by meltwater, and reconstructing past climate change. The retreat of most of the world's glaciers since the late 19th century is among the most visible indicators of global warming.
