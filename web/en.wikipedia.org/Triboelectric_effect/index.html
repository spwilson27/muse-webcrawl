Triboelectric effect

The triboelectric effect (also known as triboelectric charging) is a type of contact electrification in which certain materials become electrically charged after they are separated from another material with which they were in contact. Rubbing the materials together increases the contact surface and thus the effect. It is the mechanism behind most everyday static electricity.

Description

When two different materials are touched together and then pulled apart, one tends to gain electrons and become negatively charged while the other loses electrons and becomes positive. The triboelectric series ranks materials by this tendency: materials near the positive end (such as rabbit fur, glass, and human hair) readily give up electrons, while those near the negative end (such as amber, sulphur, ebonite, and Teflon) readily accept them. The word derives from the Greek tribos, "rubbing"; amber's attractive power after rubbing was known to Thales in antiquity and gave electricity its name, from elektron, Greek for amber.

Mechanism

Although long studied, the microscopic mechanism remains incompletely settled. Electron transfer driven by differences in work function explains many metal–insulator cases, but identical materials can also charge on contact, and ion and material transfer, surface states, and mechanical deformation all appear to contribute in different systems.

Consequences and applications

Triboelectric charging causes clothing static, shocks from car doors, dust attraction to screens, and hazardous sparks around fuel and grain dust. It is harnessed deliberately in xerography (photocopiers and laser printers), electrostatic precipitators, Van de Graaff generators, and triboelectric nanogenerators that harvest mechanical energy.
