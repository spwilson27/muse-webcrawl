Cordial (medicine)

A cordial is any invigorating and stimulating preparation that was originally intended for medicinal use, in particular to invigorate the heart and cheer the spirits. The term derives from the Latin cor, "heart".

Cordials were compounded from aromatic herbs, spices, and fruits steeped in alcohol or syrup, and were popular in Europe from the late Middle Ages through the nineteenth century. Apothecaries sold them as remedies for faintness, indigestion, and melancholy, and many liqueurs — including chartreuse, bénédictine, and various bitters — began life as medicinal cordials prepared by monks or druggists.

Usage

In modern usage the word survives in different senses by region: in Britain a cordial is a concentrated non-alcoholic fruit syrup diluted with water, while in the United States "cordial" is used as a synonym for a sweet liqueur. Historically famous medicinal cordials included Godfrey's Cordial, a preparation of opium and treacle given to infants in Victorian England, whose dangers eventually contributed to tighter regulation of patent medicines.
