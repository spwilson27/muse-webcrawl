Ollantaytambo (Quechua: Ullantaytampu) is a town and Inca archaeological site in southern Peru, about 72 kilometres northwest of Cusco, at the northwestern end of the Sacred Valley of the Incas. It is both a remarkably preserved Inca town and the site of one of the few victories of Inca forces over Spanish conquistadors.

History

In the mid-15th century the Inca emperor Pachacuti conquered the region and rebuilt the town as a royal estate serving the imperial court, constructing massive terraced fortifications on the hillside above it. During the Spanish conquest, the rebel Inca Manco Inca Yupanqui used Ollantaytambo as a stronghold and in 1537 defeated a Spanish expedition there, flooding the plain to hinder the conquistadors' cavalry — one of the rare major Inca victories — before retreating into the Vilcabamba region.

The site

The fortress-temple complex is dominated by immense agricultural terraces and the unfinished Temple of the Sun, built of huge, finely fitted pink granite blocks hauled from a quarry across the Urubamba River. Below lies the old town, whose Inca street grid of narrow stone canals and canchas (walled block compounds) remains inhabited today, making Ollantaytambo one of the last living Inca towns.

Modern role

Ollantaytambo is a principal rail gateway to Machu Picchu, with trains departing for Aguas Calientes, and a major stop on the Sacred Valley tourist circuit.
