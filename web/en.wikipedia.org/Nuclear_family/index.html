Nuclear family

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A nuclear family (also called an elementary family, atomic family, or conjugal family in its strict form) is a family group consisting of two parents and their children living together in a single household. It is distinguished from the extended family, in which grandparents, aunts, uncles, and other relatives share or closely surround the household. The term uses "nuclear" in the sense of a core or nucleus, not atomic energy.

History of the concept

Although households of parents and children are ancient, the term nuclear family entered common use in the mid-20th century, popularised by anthropologists and sociologists such as George Peter Murdock, who argued in his 1949 study Social Structure that the nuclear family is a universal human social grouping performing essential functions for society. Talcott Parsons later presented the isolated nuclear family as the form best adapted to industrial, geographically mobile societies.

Historians such as Peter Laslett have shown that small, nuclear households were in fact common in north-western Europe for centuries before industrialisation, challenging the idea that the extended family was everywhere the pre-industrial norm. In many other cultures, multigenerational and extended households were and remain the dominant arrangement.

Usage and variation

In modern demography the definition is often broadened: a single parent with children may be counted as a nuclear family, and the term "immediate family" covers parents, children, siblings, and spouses regardless of co-residence. Since the late 20th century, falling marriage rates, rising divorce, cohabitation, blended families, and same-sex parenting have diversified household forms in Western countries, and the share of children living with two married parents has declined, though the nuclear family remains a widely held social ideal and a common lived arrangement.

Criticism and debate

Critics have questioned Murdock's universality claim, pointing to matrifocal families, communal child-rearing, and societies where kinship is organised differently. Feminist scholars have criticised the mid-century functionalist portrayal of the nuclear family for naturalising a strict division of labour between a breadwinning husband and a homemaking wife. Debates over "family values" in contemporary politics frequently invoke the nuclear family as a normative model.
