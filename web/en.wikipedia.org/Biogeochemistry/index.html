Biogeochemistry

Biogeochemistry is the scientific discipline that involves the study of the chemical, physical, geological, and biological processes and reactions that govern the composition of the natural environment — including the biosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere, and lithosphere — and the cycles of chemical elements such as carbon and nitrogen through them.

Scope

Biogeochemists study the reservoirs in which elements reside, the fluxes between them, and the rates of transformation, from molecular processes in microbes to global-scale cycles. The field draws on chemistry, biology, geology, oceanography, and atmospheric science.

Biogeochemical cycles

Key cycles include the carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulphur, and water cycles. Human activity has profoundly altered several of them: fossil fuel combustion and deforestation have increased atmospheric carbon dioxide, and industrial nitrogen fixation for fertilisers has roughly doubled the flux of reactive nitrogen into ecosystems.

History

The term and the discipline were shaped by the Russian scientist Vladimir Vernadsky, whose 1926 work on the biosphere framed life as a geological force, and by G. Evelyn Hutchinson in the mid-20th century.
