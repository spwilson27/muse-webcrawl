Italian resistance movement

The Italian resistance movement (Italian: Resistenza italiana) was the armed and political opposition to the German occupation of Italy and to Mussolini's Italian Social Republic during the final phase of the Second World War, from September 1943 to April 1945. It fought alongside the Allies in the Italian campaign and became the founding tradition of the Italian Republic.

Formation

When the Armistice of Cassibile was announced on 8 September 1943, Germany occupied northern and central Italy. Anti-fascist parties — Communists, Socialists, Christian Democrats, Liberals, the Action Party and Republicans — formed the Committee of National Liberation (CLN) in Rome and, in the north, the CLNAI based in Milan. Disbanded Italian soldiers, escaped prisoners and young people evading labour conscription formed the first partisan bands in the mountains and countryside.

The partisan war

The largest formations were the communist-led Garibaldi Brigades; the Action Party fielded the Justice and Liberty brigades, the Socialists the Matteotti brigades, and there were Catholic and autonomous units. Partisans — eventually numbering well over 200,000 active fighters, supported by many more civilians — sabotaged railways and communications, gathered intelligence for the Allies, sheltered escaped prisoners and Jews, and struck garrisons. Women served as couriers (staffette), nurses and fighters. German and Fascist reprisals were savage, including the massacres at the Ardeatine Caves (1944), Marzabotto and Sant'Anna di Stazzema. Large anti-fascist strikes in Turin and Milan in March 1944 paralysed war production. In some liberated zones partisans proclaimed short-lived "partisan republics", such as Ossola in 1944.

The April 1945 insurrection

As the Allies launched their final offensive, the CLNAI ordered a general insurrection. Between 24 and 27 April 1945 partisans liberated Genoa, Milan, Turin and many other northern cities, largely before Allied troops arrived, and captured Mussolini, who was executed on 28 April. German forces in Italy surrendered effective 2 May 1945.

Legacy

Tens of thousands of partisans and civilians died in the struggle. The Resistance provided the political legitimacy from which the Republic of 1946 and the Constitution of 1948 emerged, and Liberation Day (25 April) remains a national holiday. Its memory has remained politically contested in Italy ever since.
