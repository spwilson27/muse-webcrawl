Satires of Juvenal

The Satires are a collection of satirical poems by the Roman author Juvenal, written in the late 1st and early 2nd centuries AD. Sixteen satires, divided into five books, survive complete (a seventeenth is fragmentary). Written in dactylic hexameter, they attack the vices, corruption, and social absurdities of imperial Rome with indignation and biting wit.

Themes

Famous satires include the first, a manifesto explaining why the corruption of Rome compels him to write satire; the third, on the miseries of life in the city, spoken by Umbricius; the sixth, a long attack on women and marriage; and the tenth, on the vanity of human wishes, which gave rise to the phrase mens sana in corpore sano ("a healthy mind in a healthy body"). Juvenal also coined the phrase "bread and circuses" (panem et circenses) for the means by which rulers pacified the populace.

Influence

Juvenal's indignant style defined the "Juvenalian" mode of formal verse satire, in contrast to the gentler Horatian mode. He influenced later satirists including Samuel Johnson, whose London and The Vanity of Human Wishes imitate the third and tenth satires.
