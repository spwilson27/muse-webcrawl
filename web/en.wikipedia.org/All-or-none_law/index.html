All-or-none law

The all-or-none law is a principle in physiology stating that the strength of a response of a nerve cell or muscle fiber does not depend on the strength of the stimulus: if a stimulus exceeds the threshold potential, the nerve or muscle fiber will give a complete response; if it does not reach threshold, no response will occur.

Explanation

An individual nerve fiber either fires with its maximum amplitude action potential or does not fire at all. Increasing the strength of a suprathreshold stimulus does not increase the size of the individual action potential; instead, stronger stimuli are encoded by increasing the frequency of firing and by recruiting more fibers. The principle was first described in studies of cardiac muscle by Henry Pickering Bowditch in 1871 and was later applied to skeletal muscle and neurons.

Muscle contraction

In whole muscles, graded contraction strength is achieved through motor unit recruitment rather than through any change in the response of individual fibers, each of which obeys the all-or-none law.
