English subjunctive

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The subjunctive in English is a clause type used in some contexts to express irrealis moods: wishes, conjectures, demands, and conditions contrary to fact. Unlike languages such as Spanish or French, English has no rich subjunctive inflection; its subjunctive is vestigial, surviving in a few distinct constructions.

Forms

The present (mandative) subjunctive uses the plain, uninflected form of the verb — no third-person -s and be for all persons — in subordinate clauses after verbs and expressions of demand, recommendation and necessity: "I insist that he go", "It is essential that she be informed". It is especially common in American English; British English often substitutes should ("that he should go") or an indicative.

The irrealis were is the one distinct past-subjunctive form in the language: were used with singular subjects in counterfactual conditions and wishes — "If I were you", "I wish it were Friday". Otherwise, hypothetical meaning is conveyed by the ordinary past tense used with "modal remoteness": "If you came tomorrow..."

Other survivals

Formulaic subjunctives persist in fixed expressions: "God save the King", "be that as it may", "come what may", "long live the republic", "suffice it to say". Archaic uses abound in older texts, notably in the King James Bible and Shakespeare.

History

Old English had a fully inflected subjunctive paradigm, which eroded as inflectional endings were lost in Middle English, leaving the few relic constructions used today. The mandative subjunctive, however, has actually strengthened in American English during the twentieth century — a rare case of a subjunctive gaining ground.
