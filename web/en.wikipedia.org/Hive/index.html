Hive


A modern beehive of stacked boxes

A hive is an enclosed structure in which some honey bee species live and raise their young. In nature, bees build nests in hollow trees, rock cavities and other sheltered spaces; in beekeeping, artificial hives house colonies so that honey and other products can be harvested and pollination services provided. The term may also denote the colony itself, or the nests of other social insects. A place where hives are kept is an apiary.

Natural nests

Wild colonies of the western honey bee (Apis mellifera) typically occupy tree cavities of roughly 40 litres, where workers build parallel wax combs for brood and stores. Scouts advertise candidate sites, and swarms choose new nests by a collective decision process studied by biologists such as Thomas Seeley. Other social bees, wasps, ants and termites construct nests sometimes called hives, though beekeepers reserve the term chiefly for bees.

Artificial hives

Humans have kept bees for millennia — ancient Egyptian art depicts beekeeping — using hives of clay, straw (skeps), wood and hollow logs. Traditional skeps required destroying the colony to harvest honey. The key innovation was the movable-frame hive: Lorenzo Langstroth patented his design in 1852 after recognising "bee space" (about 6–9 mm), the gap bees keep open, allowing frames of comb to be lifted out for inspection and honey extraction without harming the colony. The Langstroth hive — stacked rectangular boxes (supers) with frames — became the world standard. Alternatives include the top-bar hive, the Warré hive and regional designs such as the Dadant. Modern hives may incorporate queen excluders, screened floors and plastic foundation.

Other meanings

"Hive" is also used metaphorically for a busy place ("a hive of activity"), in medicine as a common name for urticaria (hives), and in computing for structures such as the Windows Registry hives and Apache Hive, a data warehouse system built on Hadoop.
