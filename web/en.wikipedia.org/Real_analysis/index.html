Real analysis

Real analysis is the branch of mathematical analysis that studies the behaviour of real numbers, sequences and series of real numbers, and real-valued functions. It provides the rigorous foundation for calculus, replacing intuitive arguments about infinitesimals and limits with precise definitions, most famously the epsilon–delta definition of a limit.

Scope

The subject begins with the construction and properties of the real number system, including its completeness: every non-empty set of real numbers bounded above has a least upper bound. From this foundation it develops the theory of sequences and their convergence, infinite series, continuity, differentiability, and the Riemann integral, together with central theorems such as the intermediate value theorem, the extreme value theorem, the mean value theorem, and the fundamental theorem of calculus.

History

Although calculus was developed in the 17th century by Newton and Leibniz, its foundations remained intuitive until the 19th century, when mathematicians such as Cauchy, Bolzano, Weierstrass, Dedekind, and Cantor gave rigorous definitions of limit, continuity, and the real numbers themselves. Weierstrass's arithmetisation of analysis and Dedekind's construction of the reals via cuts (and Cantor's via Cauchy sequences) made real analysis a model of mathematical rigour. Later developments include measure theory and the Lebesgue integral, which extend the Riemann theory, and functional analysis, which generalises it to infinite-dimensional spaces.

Key concepts

Core notions include metric and topological structure on the real line, compactness (as in the Bolzano–Weierstrass and Heine–Borel theorems), uniform convergence, power series, and Fourier series.
