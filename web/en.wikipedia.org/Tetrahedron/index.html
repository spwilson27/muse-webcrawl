Tetrahedron

TypePlatonic solid
Faces4 triangles
Edges6
Vertices4
DualTetrahedron (self-dual)

In geometry, a tetrahedron (plural: tetrahedra or tetrahedrons), also known as a triangular pyramid, is a polyhedron composed of four triangular faces, six straight edges, and four corners. The tetrahedron is the simplest of all the ordinary convex polyhedra, and the only one with fewer than five faces.

Regular tetrahedron

A regular tetrahedron is one in which all four faces are equilateral triangles. It is one of the five regular Platonic solids, known since antiquity, and it is self-dual: joining the centres of its faces produces another regular tetrahedron. For a regular tetrahedron of edge length a, the volume is a³/(6√2) and the surface area is √3 a². The angle between two faces (the dihedral angle) is arccos(1/3) ≈ 70.53°.

Properties

Unlike other Platonic solids, a tetrahedron has no parallel faces, and all of its vertices lie on a sphere. It can be inscribed in a cube in such a way that each vertex is a vertex of the cube and each edge is a diagonal of a face of the cube. The tetrahedron is the three-dimensional case of the more general concept of a simplex.

Applications

Tetrahedral geometry is fundamental in chemistry: the carbon atom in methane and many other molecules bonds to four substituents arranged tetrahedrally, a prediction of VSEPR theory. Four-sided dice are tetrahedra, and the shape appears in structural engineering, in the geometry of water molecules' hydrogen-bond networks, and in the tetrahedral packaging pioneered by Tetra Pak.
