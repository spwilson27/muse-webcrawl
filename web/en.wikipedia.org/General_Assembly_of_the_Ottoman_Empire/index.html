The General Assembly (Ottoman Turkish: مجلس عمومي, Meclis-i Umûmî) was the parliament of the Ottoman Empire, established by the Ottoman constitution of 1876. It consisted of two chambers: an upper house, the Senate (Meclis-i Âyan), whose members were appointed for life by the Sultan, and a lower house, the Chamber of Deputies (Meclis-i Mebûsan), elected by a limited, indirect male franchise. It is considered the first attempt at representative government in the empire.

First Constitutional Era (1876–1878)

The assembly first convened on 19 March 1877 with deputies drawn from across the empire's provinces and religious communities — Muslims, Greeks, Armenians, Jews, Arabs and others. After two short sessions marked by criticism of the government during the Russo-Turkish War, Sultan Abdul Hamid II prorogued the parliament on 14 February 1878 and suspended the constitution, ruling autocratically for thirty years while the Senate ceased to function as well.

Second Constitutional Era (1908–1920)

The Young Turk Revolution of July 1908 forced the restoration of the constitution, and elections were held in 1908, 1912, 1914 and 1919. The restored parliament, dominated successively by the Committee of Union and Progress and its rivals, legislated through the Balkan Wars and the First World War, enacting major reforms in administration, education and family law. After the Ottoman defeat and the Allied occupation of Constantinople, the last Ottoman parliament adopted the National Pact (Misak-ı Millî) in early 1920; the Allies raided and dissolved it on 11 April 1920, prompting deputies to regroup in Ankara as the Grand National Assembly of Turkey.
