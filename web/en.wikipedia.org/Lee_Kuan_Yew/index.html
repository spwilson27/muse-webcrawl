Lee Kuan Yew (16 September 1923 – 23 March 2015), often referred to by his initials LKY, was a Singaporean statesman who served as the first Prime Minister of Singapore from 1959 to 1990. He is widely recognised as the founding father of modern Singapore, overseeing its transformation from a poor, resource-scarce colonial port into one of the world's wealthiest and most developed countries within a single generation.

Early life and career

Lee was born in Singapore to a wealthy Hakka-Peranakan family, the eldest of five children. Educated at Raffles Institution, his studies were interrupted by the Japanese occupation (1942–45), during which he survived the Sook Ching massacres and traded on the black market. After the war he read law at Fitzwilliam College, Cambridge, graduating with a double first-class honours and qualified as a barrister. Returning to Singapore in 1950, he practised law, advised trade unions, and in 1954 co-founded the People's Action Party (PAP) with a group of fellow professionals.

Path to power

The PAP won the 1959 general election, and Lee became Prime Minister of the self-governing State of Singapore. He campaigned for merger with the Federation of Malaya, achieved in 1963 with the formation of Malaysia, but political and ethnic tensions led to Singapore's separation and independence on 9 August 1965 — a moment at which Lee famously wept on television, calling it "a moment of anguish".

Premiership

Governing a small island with no natural resources, high unemployment and a departing British military presence, Lee's government pursued export-oriented industrialisation, attracting multinational investment through the Economic Development Board, building a vast public housing programme through the Housing and Development Board (which came to house over 80 per cent of the population), establishing compulsory national service, and making English the working language while promoting bilingualism. Anti-corruption measures, meritocratic administration, and pragmatic economic management produced sustained growth; per-capita income rose from a few hundred US dollars in 1965 to among the highest in the world by the end of his tenure.

His governments were also criticised for restrictions on press freedom and civil liberties, the use of detention without trial under the Internal Security Act, defamation suits against opposition politicians, and intrusive social policies. Lee defended this model — often described as a form of "soft authoritarianism" — as necessary for stability and development, and articulated a doctrine of "Asian values" emphasising communal order over individual rights.

Later years and death

Lee stepped down in 1990 in favour of Goh Chok Tong, remaining in cabinet as Senior Minister and then, from 2004 under his son Lee Hsien Loong, as Minister Mentor, before retiring from the cabinet in 2011 after that year's general election. He died of pneumonia on 23 March 2015, aged 91, and was given a state funeral attended by world leaders, with tens of thousands of Singaporeans queuing to pay respects.

Legacy

Lee is credited with Singapore's economic miracle and with shaping an influential model of developmental governance studied worldwide. His memoirs, The Singapore Story (1998) and From Third World to First (2000), remain key texts on the country's development. Assessment of his legacy continues to balance admiration for Singapore's prosperity, safety and lack of corruption against criticism of its constrained political pluralism.
