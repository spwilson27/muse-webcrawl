35 mm film is a film gauge used in filmmaking and still photography. In motion pictures, film 35 millimetres wide with four perforations ("sprocket holes") per frame on both edges was the dominant gauge for more than a century after its introduction in the 1890s and standardisation in 1909. In photography, the same stock loaded into light-tight cartridges is known as 135 film and became the most popular still format of the 20th century.

History

William Kennedy Dickson, working for Thomas Edison, slit 70 mm roll film to produce the 35 mm format for the Kinetoscope in 1892–93, cutting sprocket holes along both edges so the film could be pulled through the mechanism. The format was adopted internationally at a 1909 congress in Paris, making 35 mm the first global film standard. Early stock used dangerously flammable cellulose nitrate base until safety (acetate) film replaced it around 1948–51.

Technical characteristics

The standard camera aperture for sound films ("Academy" format) is 21.95 × 16.03 mm, leaving room for an optical sound track along one edge. Film runs at 24 frames per second, i.e. 27.4 metres (90 feet) per minute. Common variants include VistaVision (horizontal 8-perforation pulldown), Super 35 (using the full width), and anamorphic CinemaScope, which squeezes a 2.39:1 widescreen image onto the frame. Projection prints use slightly larger sprocket holes (KS) than camera negative (BH).

Decline and survival

Digital cinematography and digital projection displaced 35 mm from most production and exhibition in the 2000s–2010s; Kodak, the last major manufacturer, entered bankruptcy in 2012 before restructuring. Nevertheless 35 mm remains in use by some directors who prefer its look, in film archives (where prints serve as preservation masters), and in a reviving still-photography market, where 135 film sales recovered during the analogue revival of the 2010s and 2020s.
