Survival of the fittest

"Survival of the fittest" is a phrase that originated with the English philosopher Herbert Spencer in his Principles of Biology (1864), after reading Charles Darwin's On the Origin of Species. Darwin himself adopted the phrase in later editions of the Origin (from the fifth edition, 1869) as an alternative expression for natural selection, partly at the suggestion of Alfred Russel Wallace, who thought "natural selection" invited personification of nature.

Meaning

In biology, "fitness" means reproductive success: an organism is "fit" insofar as its traits help it survive and leave offspring in a given environment. The phrase therefore does not necessarily mean survival of the strongest or most aggressive; a fit organism may be small, cooperative or specialised. Because of this, biologists often avoid the phrase, which invites misunderstanding and has been called tautological — the fittest are defined as those that survive. Darwin's own use was descriptive, not a moral or social prescription.

Social misuse

The phrase was taken up by advocates of laissez-faire capitalism and by social Darwinists and eugenicists to argue that competition and inequality in human society are natural and beneficial. Historians note that Spencer's social philosophy predated Darwin's theory and rested on Lamarckian and progressivist assumptions; Darwin expressed reservations about crude applications of the phrase to society. The association of "survival of the fittest" with justifying poverty, imperialism or eugenics remains controversial in discussions of evolution's public image.
