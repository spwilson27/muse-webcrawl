One-party state

A one-party state, single-party state, or one-party system, is a form of government in which a single political party holds power and governs the country, with opposition parties either banned or permitted only in a subordinate, controlled role. Such states may be established in law (de jure) or arise in practice (de facto) when one party dominates despite a formally multi-party constitution.

Characteristics

The ruling party typically merges with the state apparatus: party membership is a route to office, the party controls appointments, and state institutions such as the military may swear loyalty to the party as well as the nation. Justifications offered for one-party rule include national unity, avoidance of factionalism, and — in Marxist-Leninist theory — the idea that a vanguard party represents the whole people's interests.

Examples

Current one-party states include China (Communist Party), Cuba, Laos, Vietnam, North Korea (where minor satellite parties exist under the ruling front), and Eritrea. Historically, the Soviet Union and most of the Eastern Bloc were one-party states, as were Nazi Germany and Fascist Italy. Several African and Middle Eastern states were formally one-party systems during the twentieth century.

Analysis

Political scientists contrast one-party states with multi-party democracies and debate whether "dominant-party systems," in which one party wins repeated free elections, should be classed with them. Critics argue one-party rule suppresses dissent and accountability; defenders point to stability and developmental achievements in some cases.
