Wax

Chemical natureLong-chain esters / hydrocarbons
Natural typesBeeswax, carnauba, candelilla
Petroleum typesParaffin wax, microcrystalline wax
Melting rangeTypically 40–100 °C

Waxes are a diverse class of organic compounds that are lipophilic, malleable solids near ambient temperatures. They include higher alkanes and lipids, typically with melting points above about 40 °C (104 °F), melting to give low-viscosity liquids. Waxes are insoluble in water but soluble in nonpolar organic solvents. All waxes are organic compounds, both synthetic and naturally occurring.

Natural waxes

Animals and plants secrete waxes for protection: beeswax is used by honey bees to build honeycomb; carnauba wax, the hardest natural wax, coats the leaves of a Brazilian palm; lanolin is obtained from sheep's wool; and the cuticle of many leaves and fruits carries a waxy coating that limits water loss. Earwax and spermaceti are animal waxes of historical importance.

Petroleum and synthetic waxes

Paraffin wax, refined from crude oil, is the most widely used wax, consisting mainly of straight-chain alkanes. Microcrystalline waxes have finer crystals and higher melting points. Polyethylene and Fischer–Tropsch waxes are produced synthetically.

Uses

Waxes are used in candles, polishes, cosmetics, crayons, chewing gum, food coatings, packaging, lubricants, and the lost-wax casting of metal sculpture. Wax figures and wax museums exploit the material's lifelike mouldability, while ski wax and surfboard wax tune friction on snow and boards.
