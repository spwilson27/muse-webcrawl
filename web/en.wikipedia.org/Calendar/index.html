Calendar

A calendar is a system of organizing days. This is done by giving names to periods of time, typically days, weeks, months and years. A date is the designation of a single and specific day within such a system.

Astronomical basis

Calendar systems are based on the cycles of the Sun and the Moon. A solar calendar, such as the Gregorian calendar, tracks the tropical year of about 365.24 days, governing the seasons. A lunar calendar, such as the Islamic calendar, tracks the synodic month of about 29.53 days and drifts against the seasons. Lunisolar calendars, including the Hebrew and Chinese calendars, insert intercalary months to reconcile lunar months with the solar year.

Major calendars in use

The Gregorian calendar, introduced by Pope Gregory XIII in 1582 as a reform of the Julian calendar, is the internationally accepted civil calendar. Other calendars in current use include the Islamic (Hijri) calendar, the Hebrew calendar, the Iranian (Solar Hijri) calendar, the Indian national calendar, the Chinese calendar, the Ethiopian calendar, and the Buddhist calendar.

Structure

Most calendars divide the year into months and the month into weeks of seven days. The seven-day week, of Babylonian and Jewish origin transmitted through Roman practice, is nearly universal. Days are counted within months, and years are numbered from an epoch — for the Gregorian calendar, the traditional year of the birth of Jesus (Anno Domini). Special days such as holidays, saints' days, and festivals are superimposed on the basic structure.

History

Calendars are among the oldest products of astronomy and agriculture: Neolithic and Bronze Age arrangements such as those at Warren Field in Scotland suggest lunisolar reckoning over 10,000 years ago. The Roman calendar was reformed by Julius Caesar in 45 BC and again under Augustus. The Gregorian reform deleted ten days in October 1582 and refined leap-year rules so that century years are leap years only when divisible by 400.
