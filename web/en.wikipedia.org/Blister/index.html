Blister

A blister is a small pocket of body fluid (lymph, serum, plasma, blood, or pus) within the upper layers of the skin, usually caused by forceful rubbing (friction), burning, freezing, chemical exposure, or disease. Most blisters are filled with a clear fluid called serum or plasma. Blisters can also be blood-filled (blood blisters) or infected with pus.

Causes

Friction blisters are common on the feet and hands of hikers, runners, and manual workers, forming when shear forces separate the epidermis from the dermis and fluid collects in the space. Thermal burns and frostbite produce second-degree blisters. Other causes include contact dermatitis, insect bites, viral infections such as herpes simplex and chickenpox, and autoimmune blistering diseases such as bullous pemphigoid and epidermolysis bullosa.

Management

An intact blister roof is a natural sterile barrier; most friction blisters heal on their own if protected from further rubbing with padding or dressings. Large or painful blisters may be drained with a sterile needle while preserving the roof. Warning signs of infection include increasing redness, warmth, and pus. Recurrent or widespread blistering warrants medical evaluation for underlying disease.
