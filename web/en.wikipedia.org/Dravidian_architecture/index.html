Dravidian architecture

Dravidian architecture, also known as the South Indian temple style, is an architectural idiom in Hindu temple architecture that emerged in the southern part of the Indian subcontinent, reaching its final form by the sixteenth century. It is distinguished from the Nagara style of northern India principally by the shape of its towers: the Dravidian vimana is a stepped pyramid rising in tiers, while the Nagara shikhara is curvilinear.

Key elements

A Dravidian temple complex typically includes a towering gateway or gopuram, often the tallest structure in the complex; a stepped pyramidal vimana over the main shrine (garbhagriha); pillared halls (mandapas); and concentric enclosure walls (prakarams). Temples frequently incorporate large water tanks and extensive corridors lined with sculpture.

History

The style developed under the Pallavas, Cholas, Pandyas, Cheras, Chalukyas, Rashtrakutas, Hoysalas, and the Vijayanagara Empire. Early rock-cut examples appear at Mahabalipuram under the Pallavas in the 7th century. The Chola period produced monumental stone temples such as the Brihadisvara Temple at Thanjavur (c. 1010 CE), a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Under Vijayanagara and the Nayaks, gopurams grew to enormous scale, as at Madurai's Meenakshi Temple.

Materials and construction

Granite and other hard stones predominate in major temples, supplemented by brick and stucco in smaller shrines and in the sculptural ornament of gopurams. Sculptors covered nearly every surface with figures from Hindu mythology, a feature that intensified in the later medieval period.
