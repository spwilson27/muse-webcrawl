Tally stick

A tally stick (or simply tally) is an ancient memory and record-keeping device in which notches are cut into a piece of wood (or bone) to record numbers, debts, or messages. Tally sticks were used from prehistoric times through the medieval period and, remarkably, in England's royal Exchequer into the 19th century.

Prehistoric tallies

The oldest known tally-like artefacts include the Lebombo bone (around 35,000 years old) and the Ishango bone from central Africa, marked with groups of notches that some researchers interpret as early numerical or calendrical records.

Split tallies

The most sophisticated form was the split tally: notches recording a sum were cut across a squared stick of hazelwood, which was then split lengthwise into two matching halves — the longer "stock" kept by the creditor and the shorter "foil" by the debtor (or vice versa). Because the grain of the wood and the notch pattern had to match exactly when the halves were rejoined, the system was highly resistant to forgery. The word "stockholder" ultimately derives from the stock of a tally.

The English Exchequer

The English Exchequer used tally sticks to record tax payments and loans to the Crown from the 12th century until the practice was abolished in 1826. The sticks then lingered in storage until 1834, when officials decided to burn the accumulated tallies in furnaces beneath the Houses of Parliament. The fire got out of control and destroyed the old Palace of Westminster — an event recorded by onlookers including Charles Dickens, who mocked the "obstinate adherence to an obsolete custom."
