Rhetoric

Rhetoric is the art of persuasion. It is one of the three ancient arts of discourse (alongside grammar and logic/dialectic) also known as the trivium. Rhetoric aims to improve the capability of writers or speakers to persuade, inform, or motivate audiences in specific situations. Aristotle defined rhetoric as "the faculty of observing in any given case the available means of persuasion".

Classical rhetoric

Rhetoric emerged in the democracies of ancient Greece, where citizens argued cases in court and debated in assemblies; itinerant teachers called Sophists taught it for fees. Aristotle's Rhetoric systematised the subject around three artistic proofs: ethos (the speaker's character), pathos (emotion) and logos (argument), and three genres — deliberative, judicial and epideictic. Roman orators Cicero and Quintilian codified the five canons: invention, arrangement, style, memory and delivery.

Medieval and Renaissance rhetoric

In medieval education rhetoric remained one of the seven liberal arts, applied largely to letter-writing and preaching. Renaissance humanists revived classical texts and made rhetoric central to schooling and civic life; Erasmus and Ramus reshaped its teaching, with Peter Ramus controversially assigning invention and arrangement to dialectic.

Modern rhetoric

After a period of decline into the study of style ("mere rhetoric" as a term of abuse), the 20th century saw a revival: Kenneth Burke's theory of identification, the argumentation theory of Perelman and Olbrechts-Tyteca, and the "rhetorical turn" across the humanities. Contemporary rhetorical studies encompass composition, political communication, science communication and visual and digital rhetoric.

Criticism

From Plato's Gorgias onward, rhetoric has been suspected of manipulating audiences independently of truth — a critique answered by Aristotle and Quintilian, who insisted the true orator must be a "good man skilled in speaking".
