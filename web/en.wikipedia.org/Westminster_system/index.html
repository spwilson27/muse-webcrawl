The Westminster system, or Westminster model, is a type of parliamentary government that incorporates a series of procedures for operating a legislature, first developed in England. Key aspects of the system include an executive branch made up of members of the legislature, and a ceremonial head of state who is separate from the head of government. The term comes from the Palace of Westminster, the seat of the Parliament of the United Kingdom.

Characteristics

A Westminster system of government may incorporate the following features:

  • A sovereign or head of state who functions as the nominal holder of executive power and holds numerous reserve powers, represented in many countries by a governor-general.
  • A head of government — usually called a prime minister — appointed by the head of state, in practice the leader of the political party or coalition with the support of a majority in the legislature.
  • An executive branch led by the head of government and cabinet ministers who are, by convention, members of the legislature and collectively responsible to it.
  • A civil service that is independent and non-partisan, serving governments of different parties impartially.
  • A parliamentary opposition, led by the Leader of the Opposition, which scrutinises the government and presents itself as an alternative government.
  • A legislature, often bicameral, at least one house of which is elected, and the ability of the lower house to dismiss the government through a vote of no confidence.

Operation

The executive government remains in office only while it commands the confidence of the lower house of parliament. Cabinet decisions are taken collectively, and ministers are individually responsible for their departments and collectively responsible for government policy — the doctrine of collective ministerial responsibility. Parliamentary procedure follows forms such as question time, and the head of state normally acts on ministerial advice except in rare exercises of reserve power.

Spread and adaptations

The model was exported throughout the British Empire and is now used, with local variations, in Canada, Australia, New Zealand, India, Pakistan, Malaysia, Singapore, South Africa, Ireland, Israel and numerous Caribbean and Pacific states. Some countries blend Westminster features with others: Australia's system combines Westminster conventions with a powerful elected Senate, sometimes called the "Washminster system".
