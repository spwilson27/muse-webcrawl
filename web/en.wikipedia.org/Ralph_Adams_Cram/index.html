Ralph Adams Cram, America's leading Gothic Revival architect.

Ralph Adams Cram (December 16, 1863 – September 22, 1942) was an American architect of collegiate and ecclesiastical buildings, principally in the Gothic Revival style. With his firms Cram, Goodhue & Ferguson and later Cram & Ferguson, he designed hundreds of churches, chapels and university buildings, and became the foremost American advocate of Gothic as a living moral and spiritual architecture.

Career

Largely self-taught after an apprenticeship in Boston, Cram converted to Anglo-Catholicism and, after travel in England and Italy, resolved to revive medieval church building. His firm's breakthrough was the plan for the Cathedral of St. John the Divine in New York (from 1911); other landmarks include the Princeton Graduate College and University Chapel, Rice University in Houston, the United States Military Academy at West Point, and numerous parish churches. Cram also served as head of the architecture department at MIT (1914–1921).

Writings and influence

Cram was a prolific author on architecture, religion and society, arguing in works such as Church Building (1901) and The Substance of Gothic (1917) for craftsmanship and tradition against industrial modernism. His Collegiate Gothic shaped the image of the American university campus, and his firm continued under successors after his death in 1942.
