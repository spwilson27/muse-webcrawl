Kidney failure, also called renal failure, is a medical condition in which the kidneys no longer adequately filter metabolic waste products from the blood. It occurs in two main forms: acute kidney injury, which develops rapidly over hours or days and may be reversible, and chronic kidney disease, in which function declines gradually over months or years. The most advanced stage of chronic disease (stage 5, with a glomerular filtration rate below 15 ml/min) is termed kidney failure or end-stage renal disease.

Causes

Worldwide the leading causes of chronic kidney failure are diabetes and high blood pressure, followed by glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease and long-standing obstruction of the urinary tract. Acute kidney injury commonly results from reduced blood flow to the kidneys (for example in shock or severe dehydration), direct kidney damage from infections, toxins or certain medicines, or sudden blockage of urine flow.

Signs and symptoms

As waste products such as urea accumulate (uraemia), patients may experience fatigue, nausea and vomiting, loss of appetite, itching, muscle cramps, swelling of the legs, breathlessness from fluid overload, anaemia, and in severe cases confusion, seizures or coma. High potassium levels and metabolic acidosis are dangerous complications.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on blood tests — particularly serum creatinine and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) — urine tests for protein or blood, and imaging. Early chronic disease is managed by treating the underlying cause, controlling blood pressure and blood sugar, and using kidney-protective drugs such as ACE inhibitors. In established kidney failure, treatment options are dialysis (haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis), kidney transplantation, or comprehensive conservative care without dialysis. Kidney failure is a major and growing global health burden, affecting an estimated several million people receiving renal replacement therapy.
