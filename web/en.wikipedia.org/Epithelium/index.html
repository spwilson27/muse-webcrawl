Epithelium

Epithelium or epithelial tissue is one of the four basic types of animal tissue, along with connective tissue, muscle tissue and nervous tissue. Epithelial tissues line the outer surfaces of organs and blood vessels throughout the body, as well as the inner surfaces of cavities in many internal organs. Examples include the epidermis (the outermost layer of skin), the lining of the gastrointestinal tract, and the endothelium of blood vessels.

Structure

Epithelial cells are packed tightly together with little intercellular material, forming continuous sheets. They sit on a basement membrane, are polarised (with distinct apical and basal surfaces), and are avascular — nourished by diffusion from underlying connective tissue. Junctions between cells (tight junctions, adherens junctions, desmosomes and gap junctions) seal and reinforce the sheet.

Classification

Epithelia are classified by the number of cell layers and by cell shape:

  • Simple (one layer) vs. stratified (multiple layers), plus pseudostratified and transitional types.
  • Squamous (flat), cuboidal (cube-shaped), or columnar (tall).

Thus the skin surface is stratified squamous epithelium, the alveoli of the lungs are lined by simple squamous epithelium suited to gas exchange, and the small intestine is lined by simple columnar epithelium specialised for absorption, often bearing microvilli or cilia.

Functions

Epithelium serves as a protective barrier, and performs absorption, secretion, excretion, filtration, diffusion and sensory reception. Glands are formed from epithelial tissue and are specialised for secretion; cancers arising from epithelium are called carcinomas and account for the great majority of human cancers.
