Liberal arts

The seven liberal arts
The seven liberal arts, from the Hortus deliciarum

The liberal arts (from Latin artes liberales, "arts worthy of a free person") are the traditional programme of education originating in classical antiquity. In the medieval curriculum they comprised seven subjects: the trivium of grammar, rhetoric and logic (or dialectic), and the quadrivium of arithmetic, geometry, music and astronomy. In modern usage, "liberal arts" describes a broad undergraduate education spanning the humanities, social sciences, natural sciences and mathematics, exemplified by the American liberal arts college.

History

Greek and Roman writers such as Plato, Aristotle, Cicero and Seneca argued that free citizens should study subjects pursued for their own sake rather than for vocational purposes. Varro listed nine disciplines; Martianus Capella's allegory On the Marriage of Philology and Mercury (5th century AD) fixed the canonical seven, which Boethius, Cassiodorus and Isidore of Seville transmitted to the medieval schools. The trivium and quadrivium formed the foundation of the medieval university curriculum, studied before the higher faculties of theology, law and medicine.

The seven arts

  • Trivium (the "three ways" of language): grammar, rhetoric, logic.
  • Quadrivium (the "four ways" of number): arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy.

Modern usage

Today a liberal arts education aims at breadth of knowledge, critical thinking and writing skills rather than narrow professional training. Debates continue over the place of the liberal arts amid vocational pressures and the rising prominence of STEM subjects, though advocates argue that their transferable skills remain valuable across careers.
