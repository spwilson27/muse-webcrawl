Daniel O'Connell

Born6 August 1775, Carhen, County Kerry, Ireland
Died15 May 1847, Genoa, Italy

Daniel O'Connell (1775–1847), hailed as "the Liberator," was the dominant Irish political leader of the first half of the 19th century. A barrister by profession, he championed the cause of Catholic emancipation — the removal of the civil disabilities imposed on Ireland's Catholic majority — through mass peaceful mobilisation.

Catholic emancipation

In 1823 O'Connell founded the Catholic Association, funded by a "Catholic rent" of a penny a month, which turned the campaign into a mass movement. His election as MP for County Clare in 1828 — though as a Catholic he could not legally take his seat — forced the British government of the Duke of Wellington to concede the Roman Catholic Relief Act 1829, opening Parliament to Catholics.

Repeal campaign

O'Connell then campaigned for repeal of the Acts of Union 1800 and restoration of an Irish parliament, organising enormous "monster meetings" in 1843, the largest at Tara. When the government banned the Clontarf meeting, O'Connell called it off rather than risk bloodshed; he was convicted of conspiracy (later overturned on appeal) and his health declined. He died in Genoa in 1847 while travelling to Rome.
