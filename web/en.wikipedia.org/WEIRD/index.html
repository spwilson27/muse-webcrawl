WEIRD

WEIRD is an acronym used in psychology and the social sciences for research subjects from Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, and Democratic societies. The term was popularised by a widely cited 2010 paper by Joseph Henrich, Steven J. Heine, and Ara Norenzayan, "The weirdest people in the world?", which argued that psychological research relies overwhelmingly on WEIRD subjects — especially American undergraduates — and that these subjects are frequently outliers on the very measures being studied.

Behavioural research samples come disproportionately from a small, atypical slice of humanity.

The argument

Henrich and colleagues documented that top psychology journals draw the great majority of their samples from Western industrialised nations, with American undergraduates alone making up a large share — yet the populations studied represent only a small fraction of humanity. Reviewing evidence across domains such as visual perception (including the Müller-Lyer illusion), fairness and cooperation in economic games, self-concept, and moral reasoning, they showed that WEIRD subjects often sit at the extreme end of global variation, so conclusions about "human nature" drawn from them may not generalise.

Impact

The WEIRD critique became a standard caution in behavioural science and is linked to the broader replication crisis and to calls for cross-cultural research, more diverse samples, and reporting of sample provenance. Henrich's later book The WEIRDest People in the World (2020) extended the argument into a historical thesis about how distinctive Western psychology emerged. Critics note that the category is coarse, but the underlying sampling problem it names remains widely acknowledged.
