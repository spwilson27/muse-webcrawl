Naturally aspirated engine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A naturally aspirated engine is an internal combustion engine in which air intake depends solely on atmospheric pressure and does not rely on forced induction through a turbocharger or a supercharger. Most petrol engines fitted to automobiles up to the mid-2010s, and many aircraft and motorcycle engines, were naturally aspirated.

Operation

During the intake stroke, the descending piston creates a partial vacuum that draws the air–fuel charge into the cylinder at roughly ambient pressure. The amount of air—and therefore fuel—that can be burned is limited by this pressure, so power per unit displacement is lower than in forced-induction engines. Volumetric efficiency in a naturally aspirated engine can approach or slightly exceed 100% at tuned speeds, aided by intake resonance and variable valve timing.

Advantages and disadvantages

Naturally aspirated engines are simpler, lighter, and often more predictable in throttle response, with linear power delivery prized in sports cars. Their drawbacks are lower specific output and reduced performance at altitude, where lower air density further reduces the charge mass. Turbocharging, by contrast, recovers waste exhaust energy and restores sea-level performance at altitude.

Applications

Most piston aircraft engines are naturally aspirated, though turbo-normalizing is common for high-altitude flight. In motorsport, Formula One used naturally aspirated engines from 1989 until 2013. Emissions and efficiency rules have driven a broad shift to downsized turbocharged engines in production cars.
