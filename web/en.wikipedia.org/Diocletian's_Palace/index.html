Diocletian's Palace

Diocletian's Palace is an ancient palace built for the Roman emperor Diocletian at the end of the third century AD, which today forms about half of the old town of Split, Croatia. Diocletian built the massive palace in preparation for his retirement on 1 May 305, after ruling from 284. It lies in a bay on the south side of a short peninsula jutting out from the Dalmatian coast.

Despite being called a "palace", the complex more closely resembled a large Roman military fortress: half of it was for Diocletian's personal use, and the rest housed the military garrison. The rectangular complex, roughly 215 by 180 metres, was enclosed by walls up to 26 metres high, with towers at the corners and four monumental gates.

Layout

Two main streets, the cardo and decumanus, divided the palace into quarters. The southern half contained the emperor's apartments, arranged along the sea front with a long gallery, together with the Vestibule, the Peristyle courtyard, the Temple of Jupiter, and Diocletian's mausoleum — later converted into Split's Cathedral of Saint Domnius, considered among the oldest cathedral buildings in the world still in use.

Later history

After Diocletian's death in 316 the palace remained imperial property; in the seventh century, refugees from the destroyed city of Salona settled within its walls, transforming it into a living city. Successive generations built houses directly into the Roman fabric. The Scottish architect Robert Adam surveyed the ruins in 1764 and published influential engravings that inspired Neoclassical architecture.

The palace was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1979. Remarkably, it remains inhabited: shops, cafés, apartments, and the cathedral occupy its ancient spaces today.
