1080p


A chart comparing screen resolutions; 1080p (1920×1080) is shown relative to 720p, 480p and 4K.

1080p (1920 × 1080), also known as Full HD, FHD, or Full High Definition, is a set of HDTV high-definition video modes characterized by 1,920 pixels displayed across the screen horizontally and 1,080 pixels down the screen vertically. The p stands for progressive scan, meaning that the whole frame is drawn in a single pass rather than in two interleaved fields, in contrast to 1080i, the interlaced alternative that dominated early HDTV broadcasting.

The term usually assumes a widescreen aspect ratio of 16:9, yielding a total pixel count of just over 2.07 megapixels. For most of the 2010s, 1080p was the de facto standard resolution for consumer television, Blu-ray Disc video, video game consoles and full-screen computer displays, before being gradually displaced at the high end by 4K UHD (3840 × 2160).

Overview

Any display device or content described as "1080p" displays or encodes the picture at 1920 pixels wide by 1080 pixels tall. Progressive scanning produces a smoother picture with less flicker and better motion rendering than interlaced scanning at the same vertical resolution, because each frame is complete. Frame rates commonly used with 1080p include 24, 25, 30, 50 and 60 frames per second, often written as 1080p24, 1080p30 or 1080p60.

The display resolution sits between 720p (1280 × 720, generally marketed simply as "HD") and 1440p (2560 × 1440, "QHD"), a common resolution for computer monitors and smartphones. Because 1080p represented a doubling of vertical resolution over mainstream standard-definition formats, it was marketed heavily during the transition to digital television.

History

The roots of 1080-line production go back to experimental high-definition systems of the 1980s and 1990s, including the Japanese Hi-Vision/MUSE system, which used 1035 active interlaced lines. Digital 1080 formats were standardized within the ATSC and DVB frameworks as broadcasting moved from analogue to digital in the 2000s. Early terrestrial HDTV services generally used 1080i because interlacing halved the required transmission bandwidth, while 1080p25/30 content could be carried inside 1080i signals.

The Blu-ray Disc format, launched in 2006, made 1080p24 the standard for film releases on home video, since most feature films are shot at 24 frames per second. Flat-panel LCD and plasma televisions with native 1920 × 1080 panels — sold as "Full HD" — became common in the late 2000s, and by the early 2010s 1080p televisions dominated retail sales. Video game consoles of the eighth generation, such as the PlayStation 4 and Xbox One, targeted 1080p rendering as their headline resolution, while streaming services like Netflix and YouTube adopted 1080p as a standard streaming tier.

Resolutions

Although 1920 × 1080 is the dominant form, the label 1080p strictly refers to the vertical resolution of 1080 lines combined with progressive scan. Production formats include 1080p24, used for film-originated material, and 1080p50/1080p60 high-frame-rate variants used in broadcasting standards such as DVB and in gaming. The SMPTE 274M standard defines a family of 1080-line formats with various frame rates for studio production.

1080p versus 1080i

In 1080i, each frame is split into two fields of 540 lines — first the odd-numbered lines, then the even-numbered lines — which reduces bandwidth but can introduce combing artefacts on fast-moving content. In 1080p, all 1080 lines are transmitted together. Modern televisions deinterlace 1080i broadcasts for display on their progressive panels, but native 1080p sources such as Blu-ray and games avoid this step entirely.

Successors

During the 2010s, manufacturers began promoting 4K UHD televisions, and 1080p moved downmarket to become the baseline resolution. Nevertheless, 1080p remains extremely common: it is the native resolution of most office monitors, laptops and broadcast streams, and its bandwidth and processing demands are far lower than 4K, making it a practical standard for video conferencing, streaming and mobile devices.
