Progressive scan

Progressive scan (alternatively referred to as noninterlaced scanning) is a format of displaying, storing, or transmitting moving images in which all the lines of each frame are drawn in sequence. This is in contrast to interlaced video used in traditional analog television systems, where only the odd lines, then the even lines of each frame (each image called a video field) are drawn alternately.

Progressive scanning draws every line of a frame in order, from top to bottom.

Technical details

In a progressive-scan system, the entire image is refreshed in a single pass. A 1080p60 signal, for example, transmits sixty complete 1920 × 1080 frames per second. Because no fields are woven together, progressive images are free of interlace flicker and the combing artefacts that appear when objects move between fields. The system's disadvantage historically was bandwidth: progressive transmission at a given spatial resolution needs roughly twice the data rate of interlaced transmission at the same frame rate.

Usage

Computer monitors have always used progressive scanning, and it became standard for digital television production and display. Broadcast formats such as 720p use progressive scanning, and LCD, plasma, and OLED televisions all display images progressively, converting interlaced inputs through deinterlacing. Film-based material shot at 24 frames per second maps naturally onto progressive formats.

Notation

Progressive formats are denoted by a "p" after the vertical resolution, as in 480p, 720p, and 1080p; the frame rate may follow, as in 1080p30 or 1080p60. A related notation, psF (progressive segmented frame), transmits a progressive frame as two fields for compatibility with interlaced equipment while retaining a progressive picture.
