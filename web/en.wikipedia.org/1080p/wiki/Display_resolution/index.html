Display resolution

The display resolution or display modes of a digital television, computer monitor, or other display device is the number of distinct pixels in each dimension that can be displayed. It is usually quoted as width × height, with the units in pixels: for example, 1024 × 768 means the width is 1024 pixels and the height is 768 pixels.

Chart of common display resolutions and their aspect ratios.

Overview

The term "display resolution" usually refers to the pixel count of the display, but it can also describe the resolution of the signal being sent to it. A display has a native resolution — the physical grid of pixels in the panel — and images supplied at other resolutions must be scaled, which can reduce sharpness. The perceived detail also depends on screen size, expressed as pixel density in pixels per inch (PPI), and on viewing distance.

Interlaced and progressive

Resolutions for television signals carry a suffix indicating the scanning method: p for progressive scan, in which all lines are drawn each frame, and i for interlaced video, in which alternate lines are drawn in successive fields. Thus 1080i60 transmits 60 fields of 1920 × 540 per second, while 1080p60 transmits 60 complete 1920 × 1080 frames.

History

Early computer displays were tied to television standards; IBM's CGA and VGA standards fixed resolutions such as 320 × 200 and 640 × 480. Dedicated monitor resolutions grew through SVGA and XGA in the 1990s, and flat-panel displays made native resolutions a fixed property of the hardware. The transition to widescreen 16:9 panels in the 2000s aligned computer displays with HDTV formats such as 720p and 1080p.
