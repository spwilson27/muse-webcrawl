High-definition television

High-definition television (HDTV) describes a television system that provides a substantially higher image resolution than the previous generation of technologies, such as standard-definition television (SDTV). HDTV is usually transmitted digitally and is associated with resolutions of 1280 × 720 (720p) and 1920 × 1080 (1080i and 1080p), with a 16:9 widescreen aspect ratio.

A flat-panel HDTV displaying a high-definition broadcast.

History

The term "high definition" dates to early television experiments of the 1930s, but modern HDTV development began in earnest with Japan's analog Hi-Vision system (1125 lines), demonstrated in the 1980s. In the United States, an all-digital approach was proposed in 1990, leading the "Grand Alliance" of companies to develop what became the ATSC standards, adopted in 1996. Europe adopted the DVB family of standards, and digital switchover replaced analogue terrestrial broadcasts in most countries during the 2000s and 2010s.

Formats

The principal HDTV formats are 720p, which uses progressive scanning and is favoured for fast motion, and 1080i, which uses interlaced scanning to deliver 1920 × 1080 fields. 1080p ("Full HD") became the dominant consumer format on Blu-ray discs, streaming services, and later broadcast standards. Subsequent generations — 4K UHD and 8K UHD — are marketed as successors to HDTV under the Ultra HD banner.

Broadcasting

HDTV broadcasts require more bandwidth than SDTV but benefit from digital compression (MPEG-2, and later H.264/MPEG-4 AVC and HEVC), allowing several HD channels to occupy spectrum that once carried a single analogue channel. Terrestrial (ATSC, DVB-T/T2, ISDB-T), satellite (DVB-S/S2), and cable systems worldwide now carry HD as their baseline service.
