Zayd ibn ʿAlī (c. 695–740 CE) was a great-grandson of the Islamic prophet Muhammad through his daughter Fatimah and Ali ibn Abi Talib, and the son of Ali ibn Husayn Zayn al-Abidin. He led an unsuccessful revolt against the Umayyad Caliphate in Kufa in 740 and was killed, becoming a martyr. The Zaydi branch of Shia Islam — historically dominant in northern Yemen — takes its name from him and follows his teachings on the imamate.

Life

Zayd was born in Medina and was renowned for his learning in the Qur'an, jurisprudence, and theology; the famous legal work Musnad al-Imam Zayd is attributed to him, and he is sometimes credited as a founder of Islamic jurisprudence and a teacher linked to Abu Hanifa. Unlike his elder brother Muhammad al-Baqir, the fifth imam of the Twelver Shia, Zayd held that an imam must actively rise against unjust rule rather than remain quietist.

Revolt and death

In 740, during the reign of the Umayyad caliph Hisham ibn Abd al-Malik, Zayd travelled to Kufa in Iraq, where supporters urged him to lead an uprising. When the revolt began, many Kufans abandoned him — tradition puts his remaining followers in the hundreds. Zayd was struck by an arrow and died; his body was exhumed by the authorities, crucified, and later burned, acts that deepened Shia grievance against the Umayyads.

Zaydism

Zaydi doctrine teaches that the imamate belongs to a descendant of Hasan or Husayn who is learned, pious, and willing to claim the office by rising (khuruj). Zaydism is theologically close to the Mu'tazila and jurisprudentially nearer to Sunni schools than Twelver Shi'ism. Zaydi imamates ruled in Tabaristan and, most enduringly, in Yemen, where a Zaydi state survived until 1962. The modern Houthi movement in Yemen arises from within this Zaydi tradition.
