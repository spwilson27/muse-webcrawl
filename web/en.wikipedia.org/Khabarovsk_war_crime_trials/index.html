Khabarovsk war crime trials

The Khabarovsk war crime trials were a series of hearings held before a Soviet military tribunal in Khabarovsk, in the Soviet Far East, from 25 to 31 December 1949. Twelve members of the Imperial Japanese Army's Kwantung Army were tried and convicted for manufacturing and using biological weapons and conducting lethal human experimentation during the Second World War.

Background

The defendants included personnel connected with Unit 731 and Unit 100, the Japanese biological warfare programme based in Manchuria, which had experimented on prisoners and spread plague and other pathogens among Chinese civilians. Unlike at the Tokyo Tribunal, where leaders of the programme were granted immunity by the United States in exchange for their research data, the Soviets prosecuted the personnel they had captured in 1945.

Trial and sentences

All twelve defendants, including the Kwantung Army's last commander, General Otozō Yamada, pleaded guilty. The tribunal heard extensive testimony about experiments on human subjects, the breeding of plague-infected fleas, and field attacks on Chinese cities. The accused were sentenced to terms of between two and twenty-five years in labour camps; several were released in the 1950s as Soviet–Japanese relations normalised.

Legacy

Long dismissed in the West as a propaganda show trial, the Khabarovsk proceedings are now recognised by many historians as substantially accurate: the trial materials, published in 1950, corroborated later research into Unit 731 and remain an important documentary source on Japanese biological warfare.
