Signalling (economics)

In contract theory and economics, signalling is the idea that one party (the agent) credibly conveys some information about itself to another party (the principal). The concept arose in the study of markets with asymmetric information, where one side of a transaction knows more than the other.

Job market signalling

Michael Spence's 1973 paper "Job Market Signaling" founded the field. Spence proposed that education can serve as a signal of a worker's ability to employers, even if education adds nothing to productivity: because obtaining credentials is less costly (in effort) for more able workers, employers can rationally pay higher wages to the more educated. The equilibrium is a "separating equilibrium" when signal costs differ enough across types that only high-ability workers find signalling worthwhile.

Applications and related concepts

Signalling models have been applied to dividend policy (firms signalling earnings quality), advertising, warranties, brand names, and counter-signalling. Signalling contrasts with screening, in which the uninformed party designs a menu of options to induce the informed party to reveal its type. Closely related ideas appear in biology as costly signalling (the handicap principle), though the economic theory was developed independently.

Spence shared the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with George Akerlof and Joseph Stiglitz for analyses of markets with asymmetric information.
