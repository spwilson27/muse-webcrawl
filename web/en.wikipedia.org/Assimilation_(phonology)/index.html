Assimilation (phonology)

In phonology, assimilation is a sound change in which a speech sound (typically a consonant) becomes more like a neighbouring sound. It is one of the most common processes in language, occurring both within words and across word boundaries.

Nasal assimilation: /n/ takes the place of articulation of a following consonant.

Types

Assimilation may be regressive (anticipatory), where a sound changes under the influence of a following sound — as when English /n/ becomes [m] in "ten bikes" — or progressive (perseverative), where the influence runs from an earlier sound to a later one. It may be total, producing two identical sounds, or partial, affecting only some features such as place of articulation, voicing, or nasality.

Examples

Latin prefixes show classic assimilations: in- becomes im- before bilabials (impossible), il- before /l/ (illegal), and ir- before /r/ (irregular). In casual speech in many languages, sequences such as "handbag" are pronounced with the /d/ elided or assimilated, and "input" may be realised with [m].

Related processes

The opposite process, dissimilation, makes neighbouring sounds less alike. Together with coarticulation, assimilation accounts for much of the difference between careful citation forms and fluent connected speech.
