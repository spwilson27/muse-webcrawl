Procopius

Constantinople in the reign of Justinian I

Procopius of Caesarea (c. 500 – after 565) was a prominent late antique Greek scholar and historian from Caesarea in Palaestina. Accompanying the imperial general Belisarius in the wars of the Emperor Justinian I, he became the principal Byzantine historian of the 6th century, writing the Wars, the Buildings, and the celebrated Secret History. He is commonly classified as the last historian of the ancient Western world and the first of the Byzantine historians.

Life

Procopius was born in Caesarea in Roman Palestine and received a classical education in rhetoric and law, probably at Berytus or Constantinople. In 527 he became the legal adviser (assessor) to Belisarius, Justinian's commander-in-chief in the East, and accompanied him on the Persian frontier, in the expedition that reconquered North Africa from the Vandals (533–534), and in the long Gothic War in Italy. He was in Constantinople during the plague of 542, which he described in harrowing detail, and by 560 had received the rank of illustris; he may be the Procopius who was urban prefect of Constantinople in 562.

Works

The History of the Wars, in eight books modeled on Herodotus and Thucydides, narrates the Persian, Vandal, and Gothic wars largely from eyewitness experience and remains the chief source for Justinian's reconquests. The Buildings is a panegyrical catalogue of Justinian's construction program across the empire, including Hagia Sophia. The Secret History (Anekdota), not published in his lifetime, is a venomous invective portraying Justinian as a demon-haunted tyrant and the Empress Theodora in scandalous terms—a work whose contradictions with the public histories have fascinated scholars ever since.

Legacy

Writing in a carefully classicizing Attic Greek, Procopius set the standard for Byzantine historiography for centuries. Modern historians mine his works critically: the Wars for their detailed, largely reliable narrative; the Secret History as evidence of elite discontent and as a window into the rhetoric of late antique invective.
