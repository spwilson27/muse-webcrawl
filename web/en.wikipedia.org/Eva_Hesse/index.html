Eva Hesse

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Eva Hesse (January 11, 1936 – May 29, 1970) was a German-born American sculptor known for her pioneering work in materials such as latex, fiberglass, and plastics. Her work helped define the postminimalist art movement of the 1960s and has been a major influence on subsequent generations of artists.

Life

Hesse was born in Hamburg to a Jewish family and escaped Nazi Germany as a small child via the Kindertransport to Amsterdam, rejoining her parents and emigrating to New York in 1939. She studied painting at Pratt Institute and the Art Students League and graduated from the Yale School of Art and Architecture in 1959, where she studied with Josef Albers. In 1964–65 she spent a formative year in Germany with her husband, sculptor Tom Doyle, where she began making relief constructions and made the transition from painting to sculpture.

Work

Hesse's mature sculptures — including Hang Up (1966), Repetition Nineteen III (1968), and Accession II (1967) — combine the serial grids of minimalism with irregular, bodily, and deliberately fragile materials. She pioneered the use of latex and fiberglass in art, materials whose discolouration and decay over time she accepted as part of the work, raising conservation questions that museums still debate. Her work was included in Lucy Lippard's landmark 1966 exhibition "Eccentric Abstraction" and the 1969 "Anti-Illusion: Procedures/Materials" show at the Whitney Museum.

Death and legacy

Hesse died of a brain tumor in 1970 at the age of 34, shortly after her first solo museum exhibition at the Fischbach Gallery. Her posthumous reputation has grown steadily; major retrospectives have been held at the Guggenheim Museum (1972), Tate Modern, and the Jewish Museum in New York, and her diaries and letters are central texts in feminist art history.
