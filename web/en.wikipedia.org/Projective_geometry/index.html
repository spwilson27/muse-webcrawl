Projective geometry

Projective geometry is a topic in mathematics. It is the study of geometric properties that are invariant with respect to projective transformations. This means that, compared to elementary Euclidean geometry, projective geometry has a different setting, projective space, and a selective set of basic geometric concepts. The basic intuitions are that projective space has more points than Euclidean space, for a given dimension, and that geometric transformations are permitted that transform the extra points (called "points at infinity") to Euclidean points, and vice-versa.

The properties that characterize projective geometry were principally developed in the 19th century, beginning with the work of Jean-Victor Poncelet and Lazare Carnot. Projective geometry was formalized as synthetic geometry and later in terms of homogeneous coordinates, which connect it to linear algebra: points of projective space correspond to one-dimensional subspaces of a vector space, and projective transformations correspond to invertible linear maps.

History

The first geometrical properties of a projective nature were discovered during the third century by Pappus of Alexandria. Filippo Brunelleschi (1404–1472), in studying perspective, started to think about projective geometry. Johannes Kepler and Gérard Desargues developed the projective plane in the 17th century; Desargues's theorem, which states that two triangles are in perspective from a point if and only if they are in perspective from a line, is one of the classical results. Blaise Pascal proved his celebrated hexagon theorem as a teenager. In the 19th century Poncelet's Traité des propriétés projectives des figures (1822) gave the subject a systematic foundation.

Overview

Projective geometry is a non-metric form of geometry, meaning that it is not based on a concept of distance. In two dimensions it begins with the study of configurations of points and lines. A key feature is duality: in the projective plane, every theorem about points and lines has a dual theorem obtained by exchanging the words "point" and "line", so that "two points determine a line" dualizes to "two lines determine a point". Unlike Euclidean geometry, any two distinct lines in the projective plane meet in exactly one point; parallel lines meet at a point at infinity.

Projective geometry can also be seen as a formalization of perspective drawing: parallel lines, like railroad tracks, appear to meet at a point on the horizon. The set of horizon points defines the line at infinity. A projective transformation maps points at infinity to ordinary points and vice versa, something impossible in classical Euclidean geometry.

Basic concepts

Projective plane

The real projective plane can be defined as the set of lines through the origin in three-dimensional Euclidean space, or equivalently as the sphere with antipodal points identified. Inhomogeneous coordinates (x, y) embed the Euclidean plane into the projective plane; the additional points form the line at infinity. Homogeneous coordinates (x : y : z) represent each point as a ratio, so that (x : y : z) and (λx : λy : λz) denote the same point for λ ≠ 0.

Cross ratio

The fundamental invariant of projective geometry is the cross ratio of four collinear points. If A, B, C, D are collinear points with coordinates a, b, c, d, the cross ratio is (c−a)(d−b)/((c−b)(d−a)). Projective transformations preserve the cross ratio, which connects projective geometry to the theory of Möbius transformations on the Riemann sphere.

Duality

Duality is a principle in projective geometry whereby statements and constructions interchange the roles of points and lines while preserving incidence. In the projective plane, the dual of a point is a line and the dual of a line is a point. Famous self-dual configurations include the complete quadrilateral and the Fano plane, the smallest projective plane, which has seven points and seven lines.

Applications

Projective geometry underlies computer vision and computer graphics, where camera models are described by projective transformations, and it appears in the theory of conic sections, since all non-degenerate conics are projectively equivalent. It also plays a role in algebraic geometry, where projective varieties are fundamental objects of study.
