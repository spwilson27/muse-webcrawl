Closed shop

A closed shop is a form of union security agreement under which an employer agrees to hire only members of a particular trade union and to require employees to remain members of the union as a condition of continued employment. It is the strongest form of union security arrangement, going beyond the union shop, which requires workers to join the union only after being hired.

Legal status

In the United States, closed shops in the private sector were outlawed by the Taft–Hartley Act of 1947, which amended the National Labor Relations Act. A notable exception historically existed in the construction industry, where pre-hire agreements were permitted. Many other countries have similarly restricted or prohibited the closed shop: in the United Kingdom, legislation in the 1980s progressively outlawed it, and the practice was made unlawful in all circumstances by 1990.

Rationale and criticism

Supporters argued that the closed shop ensured a skilled, union-trained workforce, simplified hiring through union hiring halls, and prevented employers from undermining unions by hiring non-union labor. Critics contended that it gave unions monopoly power over access to jobs, infringed on individual freedom of association, and could be used to exclude workers on arbitrary or discriminatory grounds.

Related arrangements

Related forms of union security include the union shop, the agency shop (in which non-members pay a fee for representation), and the open shop, in which union membership plays no role in employment. So-called right-to-work laws in many U.S. states prohibit even agency fees in the private sector.
