Pseudorandom generator

In theoretical computer science, a pseudorandom generator (PRG) deterministically stretches a short random seed into a much longer output that no efficient algorithm can distinguish from truly random.

In theoretical computer science and cryptography, a pseudorandom generator (PRG) is a deterministic algorithm that takes a short uniformly random seed and expands it into a longer string whose distribution cannot be distinguished from uniform by any computationally bounded test, except with negligible advantage. Pseudorandom generators are distinct from pseudorandom number generators used in simulation: in the theoretical setting, security is defined against all efficient distinguishing algorithms.

Definition

Formally, a function G mapping seeds of length n to outputs of length l(n) > n is a pseudorandom generator if, for every probabilistic polynomial-time distinguisher, the probability of distinguishing G(Un) from Ul(n) is negligibly small. The existence of strong PRGs is closely tied to the existence of one-way functions: one-way functions exist if and only if pseudorandom generators exist.

Constructions

The Blum–Micali construction builds a PRG from a one-way permutation using a hard-core predicate, iterating the permutation and outputting one pseudorandom bit at a time. The Håstad–Impagliazzo–Levin–Luby theorem showed that PRGs can be constructed from any one-way function. In derandomization, Nisan–Wigderson generators fool specific classes of algorithms, such as those using limited space, based on hardness assumptions.

Applications

Pseudorandom generators underlie stream ciphers and secure key expansion in cryptography, and in complexity theory they are central to efforts to derandomize algorithms — to show, for example, that BPP = P.
