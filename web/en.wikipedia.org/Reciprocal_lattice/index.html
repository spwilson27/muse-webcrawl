Reciprocal lattice

A direct lattice (left) and its reciprocal lattice (right) in two dimensions.

The reciprocal lattice is a fundamental concept in crystallography and solid-state physics. Given a periodic crystal lattice in real space (the direct lattice), its reciprocal lattice is the set of all wave vectors k such that a plane wave with wave vector k has the same periodicity as the lattice. Equivalently, the reciprocal lattice is the Fourier transform of the direct lattice: the diffraction pattern of a crystal — the pattern produced when X-rays, electrons or neutrons scatter from it — is, in essence, a picture of its reciprocal lattice.

The concept was introduced in the work of J. Willard Gibbs on vector analysis and was put at the centre of crystallography by Paul Peter Ewald in the early twentieth century. Reciprocal space (also called k-space or momentum space) is the natural arena for analysing wave propagation in periodic media, the electronic band structure of solids, and X-ray crystallography, because periodicity in one space becomes discreteness in the other.

Definition

Let a three-dimensional Bravais lattice be generated by primitive vectors a1, a2, a3, so that every lattice point is at a position R = n1a1 + n2a2 + n3a3 for integers ni. The reciprocal lattice is generated by primitive vectors b1, b2, b3 defined (in the convention used by physicists) by

b1 = 2π (a2 × a3) / (a1 · (a2 × a3)),

and cyclic permutations for b2 and b3. These definitions guarantee the orthogonality relations

ai · bj = 2π δij,

so each reciprocal vector is perpendicular to two of the direct primitive vectors and has units of inverse length. A general reciprocal lattice vector is G = hb1 + kb2 + lb3 with integer coefficients h, k, l. (Crystallographers often use a convention without the factor 2π, so that ai · bj = δij.)

A defining property is that for every direct lattice vector R and every reciprocal lattice vector G,

eiG·R = 1,

which is precisely the statement that a plane wave with wave vector G "sees" every lattice point in the same phase, and therefore has the periodicity of the lattice. Conversely, any wave vector satisfying this condition for all R is a reciprocal lattice vector. The reciprocal of the reciprocal lattice is the original direct lattice.

Relation to crystal planes

Reciprocal lattice vectors have a direct geometric meaning. For coprime integers (hkl) — the Miller indices of a family of lattice planes — the vector Ghkl = hb1 + kb2 + lb3 is perpendicular to those planes, and the spacing dhkl between adjacent planes is

dhkl = 2π / |Ghkl|.

Thus densely packed planes with large spacing produce short reciprocal vectors, and vice versa. This inverse relationship is the origin of the name "reciprocal".

Examples

  • Simple cubic with lattice constant a: the reciprocal lattice is also simple cubic, with lattice constant 2π/a.
  • Body-centred cubic (bcc): the reciprocal lattice is face-centred cubic (fcc), and conversely the reciprocal of an fcc lattice is bcc — a duality of great importance for metals.
  • Hexagonal: the reciprocal lattice is hexagonal, rotated by 30° about the c-axis relative to the direct lattice.
  • Two-dimensional lattices, such as the hexagonal lattice of graphene, have reciprocal lattices of the same type, scaled and rotated; graphene's reciprocal lattice underlies its famous Dirac points at the corners of its Brillouin zone.

Brillouin zone

The Brillouin zone is the Wigner–Seitz primitive cell of the reciprocal lattice: the region of k-space closer to the origin than to any other reciprocal lattice point. The first Brillouin zone contains all physically distinct wave vectors for waves in the crystal, because wave vectors differing by a reciprocal lattice vector describe modes with equivalent periodicity. Electronic band structures, phonon dispersion relations and selection rules for optical transitions are conventionally plotted along high-symmetry paths inside the first Brillouin zone.

Diffraction

The reciprocal lattice explains the geometry of diffraction. Bragg's law, nλ = 2d sin θ, can be restated in the Laue condition: a crystal diffracts radiation of wave vector k into k′ only if the scattering vector k′ − k equals a reciprocal lattice vector G. In the Ewald sphere construction, diffraction occurs when the sphere of radius 1/λ in reciprocal space passes through a reciprocal lattice point. Consequently, a measured diffraction pattern is a section through the crystal's reciprocal lattice, and crystallographers reconstruct electron density by Fourier synthesis from the measured reciprocal-space amplitudes. In electron microscopy and surface science, techniques such as selected-area diffraction and LEED likewise map reciprocal space directly.

Applications

Beyond diffraction, reciprocal space is indispensable wherever periodicity meets wave propagation:

  • Band theory: electron states in a crystal are labelled by a crystal momentum ħk defined modulo reciprocal lattice vectors, and the periodicity of the lattice potential forces band energies to be periodic in k.
  • Phonons: lattice vibrations are quantised into normal modes indexed by wave vectors within the first Brillouin zone.
  • Quasi-momentum conservation: in collisions inside a crystal, momentum is conserved only up to a reciprocal lattice vector — the origin of Umklapp processes that limit thermal and electrical conductivity.
  • Fourier analysis of periodic functions: any function with the periodicity of the lattice, such as electron density or a crystal potential, expands as a sum over reciprocal lattice vectors only.
