Tripartite Pact

Signed27 September 1940, Berlin
Original signatoriesGermany, Italy, Japan
Later adherentsHungary, Romania, Slovakia, Bulgaria, Yugoslavia (briefly), Croatia

The Tripartite Pact, also known as the Berlin Pact, was an agreement concluded on 27 September 1940 between Nazi Germany, Fascist Italy and the Empire of Japan. It formally created the Axis powers of the Second World War and pledged mutual assistance if any of the signatories were attacked by a power not then involved in the war — a provision aimed principally at deterring the United States.

Terms

The pact recognised German and Italian leadership in establishing a "new order" in Europe and Japanese leadership in Greater East Asia. Its key article provided that if one party were attacked by a power not involved in the European war or the Sino-Japanese conflict, the others would assist it with all political, economic and military means. The pact explicitly stated that it did not affect relations with the Soviet Union.

Adherents and consequences

Hungary, Romania and Slovakia joined in November 1940, Bulgaria in March 1941, and Yugoslavia briefly in March 1941 before a coup reversed its adherence — prompting the German invasion of Yugoslavia. The Independent State of Croatia joined in June 1941. In practice, military coordination between the Axis partners remained limited; Japan did not declare war on the Soviet Union, and Germany declared war on the United States in December 1941 following the attack on Pearl Harbor, although the pact did not strictly oblige it to do so.
