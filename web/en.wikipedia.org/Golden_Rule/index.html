The Golden Rule is the principle of treating others as one would want to be treated by them. It is a maxim that is found in many religions and cultures. It can be considered an ethic of reciprocity in some religions, although different religions treat it differently.

The maxim may appear as a positive or negative injunction: the positive form, expressed in the Christian Gospels as "Do unto others as you would have them do unto you" (Matthew 7:12; Luke 6:31), directs one to act benevolently; the negative form — sometimes called the Silver Rule — appears in Confucius's Analects ("Do not impose on others what you do not wish for yourself") and in the teaching of Rabbi Hillel, who told a prospective convert that "What is hateful to you, do not do to your fellow: this is the whole Torah; the rest is commentary."

History

Versions of the principle appear in ancient Egyptian and Babylonian texts, in the Indian Mahabharata, in Zoroastrianism, Buddhism ("Hurt not others in ways that you yourself would find hurtful"), Jainism, Islam (the Hadith: "None of you truly believes until he wishes for his brother what he wishes for himself"), and Sikhism. Ancient Greek philosophers including Thales, Pittacus, and Socrates in Plato's dialogues offered formulations as well.

Philosophical discussion

Immanuel Kant reformulated reciprocity as the categorical imperative, judging maxims by whether they can be universalized. Critics of the Golden Rule note limitations: people with unusual preferences might treat others in ways others do not want, and role-based duties (such as a judge sentencing a criminal) complicate a literal reading. Defenders such as Harry J. Gensler have formalized the rule to address these objections. The principle underlies modern ideas of empathy, fairness, and the ethic of reciprocity in evolutionary biology and game theory.
