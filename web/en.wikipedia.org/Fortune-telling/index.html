Fortune-telling

Tarot reading is among the most widely practised forms of fortune-telling.

Fortune-telling is the practice of predicting information about a person's life, future, or character, usually for payment, by supposedly supernatural or intuitive means. It differs from divination, its ritual counterpart in religious contexts, mainly in being a commercial and informal activity. Fortune-telling has existed in virtually every known culture since antiquity and remains a substantial global entertainment and spiritual industry.

Methods

Common methods include tarot reading, palmistry (reading the lines of the hand), astrology, crystal-ball gazing (scrying), tea-leaf reading (tasseography), numerology, runes, the I Ching, and psychic or mediumship readings. Practitioners often combine tools with observation of the client—a technique sceptics describe as "cold reading," in which general statements, body language, and client feedback are used to construct apparently specific revelations.

History

In Europe, fortune-telling was closely associated with Romani people from the late Middle Ages onward, and itinerant fortune-tellers became stock figures at fairs. In the 19th and early 20th centuries, spiritualism and occult revivals greatly expanded the trade in Britain and America. Many jurisdictions have at times regulated or prohibited fortune-telling for reward as fraud, though laws are frequently unenforced or framed as entertainment licensing.

Criticism and regulation

Scientific sceptics regard fortune-telling as pseudoscience, attributing its apparent successes to the Barnum effect (acceptance of vague, flattering generalities as personally meaningful), confirmation bias, and cold reading. Consumer-protection bodies warn that vulnerable clients may be exploited, particularly through "curse removal" and similar escalating-fee scams. Some religions, including mainstream Christianity, Judaism, and Islam, traditionally prohibit the practice as occult.
