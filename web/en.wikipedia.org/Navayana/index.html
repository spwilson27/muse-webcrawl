Navayana

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Navayana (Devanagari: Navayāna), meaning "new vehicle", is the modern reinterpretation of Buddhism propounded by the Indian jurist and social reformer B. R. Ambedkar. It is also known as Ambedkarite Buddhism or Neo-Buddhism. Navayana rejects many traditional Buddhist doctrines—including karma as cosmic justice, rebirth, and monastic renunciation—in favour of a Buddhism centred on social equality and the struggle against caste.

Origins

Ambedkar, born into a Dalit (formerly "untouchable") family, concluded after decades of social reform work that emancipation required leaving Hinduism. On 14 October 1956, at Nagpur, he converted to Buddhism together with several hundred thousand followers, administering 22 vows that renounced Hindu deities and rituals. He died weeks later, in December 1956; his book The Buddha and His Dhamma was published posthumously in 1957 as the movement's foundational text.

Doctrine

Navayana treats the Buddha as a social reformer and Buddhism as a rational, ethical path aimed at creating a just society. Ambedkar's 22 vows, the emphasis on education and political organization, and the reinterpretation of doctrines such as dukkha in social terms distinguish Navayana from Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana traditions, though adherents still observe festivals such as Buddha Purnima and visit pilgrimage sites.

Followers

Most Navayana Buddhists are in India, particularly Maharashtra, and most are former Dalits. The 2011 Indian census counted about 8.4 million Buddhists, the majority of them Ambedkarite converts. Dikshabhumi in Nagpur, the site of the 1956 conversion, is the movement's principal pilgrimage centre.
