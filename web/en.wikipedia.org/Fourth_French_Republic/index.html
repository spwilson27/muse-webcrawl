Fourth French Republic

The National Assembly at the Palais Bourbon, seat of power under the Fourth Republic

The Fourth French Republic (French: Quatrième République) was the republican government of France from 1946 to 1958, governed by the fourth republican constitution. It was established after the Second World War and was in many ways a revival of the Third Republic, with a parliamentary system dominated by the National Assembly and a relatively weak presidency and executive.

History

Following the Liberation, a Constituent Assembly drafted a new constitution, approved by referendum on 13 October 1946. The period saw rapid economic reconstruction — the Trente Glorieuses — aided by the Marshall Plan, the Monnet Plan, and early European integration: France was a founding member of the European Coal and Steel Community (1951) and signed the Treaty of Rome (1957) creating the European Economic Community.

Instability and decolonisation

The regime was marked by chronic governmental instability, cycling through more than twenty governments in twelve years. It was gravely strained by colonial wars: the First Indochina War (1946–1954), ending in defeat at Dien Bien Phu, and the Algerian War (from 1954). In May 1958, amid the Algiers crisis and fears of a military coup, Charles de Gaulle was recalled to power; a new constitution established the Fifth Republic, which took effect on 4 October 1958.

Assessment

Despite its political instability, the Fourth Republic achieved substantial economic modernisation, built much of France's welfare state, and laid foundations of European integration. Presidents of the Republic included Vincent Auriol and René Coty.
