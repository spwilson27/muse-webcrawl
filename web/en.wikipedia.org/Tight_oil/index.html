Tight oil

Tight oil (also known as shale oil in industry usage, or light tight oil, abbreviated LTO) is light crude oil contained in petroleum-bearing formations of low permeability, often shale or tight sandstone. Production requires hydraulic fracturing ("fracking") combined with horizontal drilling — the same techniques used for shale gas. Tight oil should not be confused with oil shale, which is rock containing solid kerogen that must be heated to yield oil, a much less developed resource.

Development in North America

The commercial breakthrough came in the United States in the 2000s, notably in the Bakken Formation of North Dakota and Montana — where operator EOG Resources' 2006 Parshall Field discovery demonstrated profitable horizontal wells — followed by the Eagle Ford of South Texas and the Permian Basin of West Texas and New Mexico, which became the world's most productive oil field. Rapid growth in tight oil drove total US crude production from about 5 million barrels per day in 2008 to over 13 million by 2019, making the United States the world's largest oil producer and transforming global energy markets — a development often called the "shale revolution". Outside North America, significant tight oil production has occurred in Canada (Bakken, Cardium, Duvernay, and Montney plays), Argentina's Vaca Muerta, and Russia's Bazhenov Formation, though development is slower due to geology, infrastructure, and policy.

Economics and characteristics

Tight oil wells decline steeply — often 60–70% in the first year — so producers must drill continuously to sustain output, a pattern dubbed the "shale treadmill". Development is capital-intensive but flexible: wells can be drilled in weeks, making supply more responsive to price than conventional megaprojects. Breakeven prices vary by play, commonly in the range of US$30–60 per barrel for the best acreage. Because tight oil is light and sweet (low in sulfur), it suits simple refineries but can strain infrastructure configured for heavier crudes.

Environmental issues

Tight oil development raises concerns about induced seismicity from wastewater disposal, methane leakage, flaring of associated gas, water use, land disturbance, and the carbon emissions from burning the oil itself. Its rapid rise has also been a major factor in debates over US energy independence and climate policy.
