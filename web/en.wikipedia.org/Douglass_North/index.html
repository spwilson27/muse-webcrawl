Douglass C. North, Nobel laureate economist

Douglass Cecil North (November 5, 1920 – November 23, 2015) was an American economist and economic historian, one of the founders of the new institutional economics. He shared the 1993 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences with Robert Fogel "for having renewed research in economic history by applying economic theory and quantitative methods (cliometrics) in order to explain economic and institutional change."

Life and career

North was born in Cambridge, Massachusetts, and educated at the University of California, Berkeley, receiving his BA in 1942 and PhD in 1952. During the Second World War he served in the Merchant Marine. He taught at the University of Washington from 1950 to 1983, then moved to Washington University in St. Louis, where he was the Spencer T. Olin Professor in Arts and Sciences. He served as an editor of the Journal of Economic History and president of the Economic History Association.

Contributions

North's work transformed the study of economic history. His early books, The Economic Growth of the United States, 1790–1860 (1961) and (with Robert Paul Thomas) The Rise of the Western World (1973), argued that institutions — the "rules of the game" such as property rights, contracts and enforcement — rather than purely technological change, explain long-run economic performance. In Structure and Change in Economic History (1981) and Institutions, Institutional Change and Economic Performance (1990), he developed a theory of how institutions evolve, emphasising transaction costs, path dependence and mental models. His late work, including Understanding the Process of Economic Change (2005) and Violence and Social Orders (2009, with John Wallis and Barry Weingast), explored how societies control violence and transition from "limited access" to "open access" orders.

Legacy

North's framework became central to development economics and political economy, influencing how organisations such as the World Bank think about governance and institutional reform.
