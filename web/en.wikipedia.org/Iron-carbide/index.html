Iron carbide

In chemistry, iron carbide is a group of compounds of iron and carbon. The most important is cementite, Fe3C, a metastable interstitial compound that is a key constituent of steels and cast irons. Other iron carbides include the Hägg carbides (χ-Fe5C2), the hexagonal ε-carbide (Fe2C), and the carbide found in meteoritic iron, Fe3C in its mineral form cohenite.

Cementite

Cementite contains 6.67% carbon by weight. It is hard and brittle, with an orthorhombic crystal structure in which iron atoms form a distorted lattice with carbon atoms in interstitial prismatic sites. In steels it appears as a microstructural phase whose amount and distribution — in pearlite, bainite, or as a network in white cast iron — largely determine hardness and strength. On slow cooling or prolonged exposure to high temperature, cementite can decompose into ferrite and graphite; the metastability is suppressed by alloying elements such as manganese and chromium, which stabilise the carbide.

Other carbides

Higher carbides such as χ-Fe5C2 (Hägg carbide) and ε-carbides are important intermediates in Fischer–Tropsch synthesis, where iron carbide surfaces catalyse the conversion of synthesis gas to hydrocarbons. Iron carbide nanoparticles have also been studied for magnetic applications and as catalysts.
