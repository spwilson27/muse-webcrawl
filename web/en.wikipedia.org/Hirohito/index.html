Hirohito, the 124th Emperor of Japan, whose 62-year reign was the longest of any Japanese emperor.

Hirohito (裕仁; 29 April 1901 – 7 January 1989), posthumously honoured as Emperor Shōwa, was the 124th emperor of Japan, reigning from 1926 until his death in 1989. His reign of 62 years was the longest of any Japanese emperor and encompassed Japan's militarisation, the Second Sino-Japanese War, the Pacific War, defeat and occupation, and the country's post-war economic miracle.

Early life and accession

Born at the Aoyama Palace in Tokyo, the eldest son of Crown Prince Yoshihito (later Emperor Taishō) and Crown Princess Sadako, Hirohito was the first Japanese crown prince to travel abroad, visiting Europe in 1921. Because of his father's ill health he served as regent (sesshō) from 1921 and became emperor on Taishō's death on 25 December 1926, taking the era name Shōwa ("enlightened peace"). In 1924 he married Princess Nagako, with whom he had seven children, including his successor Akihito.

War years

As emperor and, under the Meiji Constitution, supreme commander of the armed forces, Hirohito presided over Japan's expansion in Asia during the 1930s and its entry into war with the United States in December 1941. The extent of his personal responsibility for wartime decisions and atrocities remains a subject of historical debate. In August 1945, after the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki and the Soviet declaration of war, he twice intervened to break cabinet deadlock in favour of accepting the Potsdam Declaration; his surrender broadcast of 15 August 1945 — the first time most Japanese heard an emperor's voice — announced Japan's capitulation.

Post-war reign

Unlike other Axis leaders, Hirohito was not prosecuted at the Tokyo Trials, a decision shaped by General Douglas MacArthur's occupation policy. In the 1946 "Humanity Declaration" he publicly renounced the conception of the emperor as a living god. Under the 1947 constitution he became a purely ceremonial "symbol of the State and of the unity of the people". He toured Japan to encourage reconstruction, resumed overseas travel in the 1970s (including a state visit to the United States in 1975), and pursued a lifelong scientific interest in marine biology, publishing several works on hydrozoans and having species named after him.

Hirohito died of duodenal cancer on 7 January 1989 and was succeeded by his son Akihito. He is buried in the Musashi Imperial Graveyard in Hachiōji, Tokyo.
