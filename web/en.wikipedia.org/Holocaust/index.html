The Holocaust was the genocide of European Jews during the Second World War. Between 1941 and 1945, Nazi Germany and its collaborators systematically murdered some six million Jews — around two-thirds of Europe's Jewish population — across German-occupied Europe. The killings were carried out in pogroms and mass shootings, by gassing in extermination camps, and through forced labour, starvation and disease in concentration camps and ghettos. Also murdered in related Nazi persecution were Roma, ethnic Poles, Soviet prisoners of war, people with disabilities and others, bringing total victims of Nazi mass murder into the millions beyond the Jewish toll.

Origins and escalation

The genocide grew out of the antisemitic ideology of Adolf Hitler's regime, which from 1933 excluded Jews from German public life (culminating in the Nuremberg Laws of 1935 and the Kristallnacht pogrom of 1938) and after 1939 imposed ghettos and mass violence in occupied Poland and the Soviet Union. Following the invasion of the USSR in June 1941, Einsatzgruppen and other units shot over a million Jews in open-air massacres ("the Holocaust by bullets"). By early 1942 the regime had moved to total extermination: at the Wannsee Conference (January 1942) officials coordinated the "Final Solution", deporting Jews from across Europe to purpose-built killing centres in occupied Poland where most were gassed on arrival.

The killing system

Six extermination camps — Chelmno, Belzec, Sobibor, Treblinka, Auschwitz-Birkenau and Majdanek — together with the concentration camp system, formed the core of the killing apparatus. In Operation Reinhard (1942–43) alone, Belzec, Sobibor and Treblinka murdered about 1.7 million Jews. Auschwitz-Birkenau, the largest site, combined a concentration camp with gas chambers and crematoria; about 1.1 million people, mostly Jews, were killed there. Resistance, though overwhelming odds precluded success, included the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising (1943) and revolts at Treblinka and Sobibor.

Liberation, trials and memory

Allied forces liberated the surviving camps in 1944–45, confronting the world with the evidence. Leading Nazis were tried at Nuremberg (1945–46), and the genocide was codified in international law through the Genocide Convention (1948). Survivors rebuilt lives in Israel, the United States and elsewhere. The Holocaust is commemorated in memorials and museums (such as Yad Vashem and the United States Holocaust Memorial Museum), on International Holocaust Remembrance Day (27 January, the anniversary of Auschwitz's liberation), and remains the defining historical example of genocide and the dangers of state-sponsored racial hatred; its denial is rejected by historians and is illegal in several countries.
