Penny-farthing

The penny-farthing, also known as the high wheel, high bicycle, or ordinary, is an early type of bicycle with a very large front wheel and a much smaller rear wheel. Popular in the 1870s and 1880s, it took its popular name from two British coins of different sizes, the penny and the farthing. It was the first machine widely called a "bicycle".

History

The design evolved from the French boneshaker and the high-wheel machines built by companies such as Coventry Machinists in England. A large front wheel — up to about 1.5 metres (60 inches) in diameter — gave greater speed for a given pedalling rate, since the pedals drove the front wheel directly. Riders sat almost above the front axle and reached the pedals with nearly straight legs.

Characteristics and decline

Penny-farthings were expensive, fast, and fashionable, but dangerous: a sudden stop or obstacle could pitch the rider forward over the handlebars in a "header". Mounting required running alongside and vaulting into the saddle. The arrival of the chain-driven safety bicycle in the mid-1880s, with equal-sized wheels and later pneumatic tyres, made the penny-farthing obsolete within a few years, though examples are still ridden by enthusiasts and in vintage races.
