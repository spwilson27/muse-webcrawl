Appalachia

LocationEastern United States, along the Appalachian Mountains

Appalachia is a geographic and cultural region of the eastern United States defined by the Appalachian Mountains, which run from Newfoundland in Canada to central Alabama. In the United States the term usually denotes the mountain region and its foothills from southern New York to northern Mississippi, Alabama, and Georgia. The Appalachian Regional Commission (ARC), established in 1965, defines the region as 423 counties across thirteen states — all of West Virginia and parts of twelve others — covering about 206,000 square miles and roughly 26 million people.

Geography and history

The region encompasses several physiographic provinces: the Blue Ridge, the Ridge-and-Valley Appalachians, and the dissected Allegheny and Cumberland Plateaus. Before European settlement it was home to Native American peoples including the Cherokee, Shawnee, and Iroquois confederacy. Scots-Irish, German, and English settlers moved into the mountains in the eighteenth century, and relative isolation fostered distinctive dialects, music, and folkways — as well as persistent stereotypes of the "hillbilly." The name derives from the Apalachee people of Florida, transferred to the mountains on early European maps.

Economy

Timber, agriculture, and above all coal shaped the modern region. From the late nineteenth century, the central Appalachian coalfields fuelled American industrialisation, but boom-and-bust cycles, mechanisation, and the decline of coal employment since the 1980s have left many counties with high poverty, population loss, and public-health crises, including the opioid epidemic. The "War on Poverty" of the 1960s — launched symbolically by President Lyndon B. Johnson in eastern Kentucky — and the ARC's highway and development programmes targeted the region's persistent economic distress. Tourism, healthcare, and education are now the largest employers in many areas.

Culture

Appalachia is a heartland of American roots music: old-time and bluegrass string-band traditions, shape-note singing, ballads of British origin, and the 1927 Bristol Sessions, often called the "big bang" of country music. Its literature includes James Still, Harriette Arnow's The Dollmaker, and J. D. Vance's memoir Hillbilly Elegy (2016), which reignited national debate about the region. Distinctive religious life (independent Baptist and Holiness churches), foodways, quilting, and storytelling traditions remain strong regional markers.
