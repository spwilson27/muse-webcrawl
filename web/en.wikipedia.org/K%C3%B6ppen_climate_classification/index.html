Köppen climate classification

The Köppen climate classification is one of the most widely used systems for classifying the world's climates. Developed by the German-Russian climatologist Wladimir Köppen from 1884 onward and later refined with Rudolf Geiger, it divides climates into five main groups based on average monthly and annual temperature and precipitation, chosen so that the boundaries correspond roughly to vegetation zones.

Scheme

The five main groups are A (tropical), B (arid), C (temperate), D (continental), and E (polar). Additional letters denote seasonal precipitation patterns (such as f for fully humid, s for dry summer, w for dry winter, and m for monsoonal) and temperature characteristics (such as h for hot arid and a, b, c, d for summer warmth). Familiar codes include Af (tropical rainforest), Cfb (temperate oceanic), Dfc (subarctic), and ET (tundra).

Use and modifications

The system is used extensively in climatology, geography, ecology, and education. The Köppen–Geiger variant and updates by Kottek, Peel, and others have produced standard world maps from observational data, and climate-change studies project how classified zones will shift under warming scenarios.

Criticism

Critics note that the thresholds are empirical, that monthly means conceal important variability, and that the scheme performs unevenly in some mountainous and transitional regions.
