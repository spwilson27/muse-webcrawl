Cultural landscape

A cultural landscape is a landscape that has been shaped by human activity, embodying the combined works of nature and humankind. The concept was developed by geographer Carl O. Sauer in the 1920s, who defined the cultural landscape as the natural landscape transformed by a cultural group: "Culture is the agent, the natural area is the medium, the cultural landscape the result."

UNESCO definition

In 1992 UNESCO adopted cultural landscapes as a category of World Heritage, becoming the first international legal instrument to recognize and protect them. UNESCO divides them into three types: clearly defined landscapes designed intentionally, such as gardens and parklands; organically evolved landscapes, arising from social, economic, and religious imperatives — either relict (fossil) landscapes or continuing landscapes still in use, like rice terraces; and associative cultural landscapes, whose significance lies in religious, artistic, or cultural associations with the natural element rather than in material remains.

Examples

Notable World Heritage cultural landscapes include the rice terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras, the vineyard landscapes of the Wachau and the Tokaj wine region, the rock-hewn and terraced landscape of the Konso in Ethiopia, the sacred Mijikenda Kaya forests of Kenya, and the pastoral landscape of the Lake District in England. Indigenous landscapes such as Australia's Budj Bim aquaculture system also exemplify millennia of cultural shaping.

Management

Because cultural landscapes are living, changing places, their conservation involves managing change rather than freezing fabric — balancing farming, settlement, tourism, and heritage values. The concept has influenced heritage practice, landscape architecture, and rural planning worldwide.
