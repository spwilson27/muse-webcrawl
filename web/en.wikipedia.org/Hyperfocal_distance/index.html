In optics and photography, hyperfocal distance is a distance beyond which all objects can be brought into an "acceptable" focus. It is the distance from a camera lens at which everything from half that distance to infinity will be acceptably sharp, for a given focal length, aperture, and chosen circle of confusion. When a lens is focused at its hyperfocal distance, the depth of field extends from half the hyperfocal distance to infinity — the maximum possible depth of field for that aperture.

Formula

The hyperfocal distance is approximately H = f²/(N·c) + f, where f is the focal length, N is the f-number, and c is the circle of confusion limit for the format. Shorter focal lengths, smaller apertures (larger f-numbers), and larger acceptable circles of confusion all shorten the hyperfocal distance.

Use in practice

Landscape photographers focus at the hyperfocal distance to keep both foreground interest and distant scenery sharp. Older manual-focus lenses often carry depth-of-field scales from which the hyperfocal setting can be read directly; modern photographers use printed tables or smartphone apps. Focusing at exactly the hyperfocal distance puts infinity at the far limit of acceptable sharpness, so some photographers prefer to focus slightly farther to favour the background.
