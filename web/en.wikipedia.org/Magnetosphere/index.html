Magnetosphere

A magnetosphere is the region of space surrounding an astronomical object in which charged particles are affected by that object's magnetic field. Earth's magnetosphere is shaped by the interaction of its internal field — generated by the geodynamo and measurable by magnetometers at the surface — with the solar wind, a stream of charged particles from the Sun.

Structure

The solar wind compresses the magnetosphere on the day side to about ten Earth radii at the magnetopause and stretches it into a long magnetotail on the night side. Within it lie the Van Allen radiation belts, torus-shaped zones of trapped energetic particles, and the plasmasphere. The bow shock stands upstream where the solar wind slows to subsonic speeds.

Effects

The magnetosphere shields the surface from most solar and cosmic particle radiation. Disturbances — geomagnetic storms driven by coronal mass ejections — can produce auroras, disrupt radio communications and power grids, and endanger satellites and astronauts. Mercury, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune also possess magnetospheres, Jupiter's being the largest structure in the solar system. NASA's Magnetospheric Multiscale Mission studies the explosive reconnection events in Earth's magnetosphere where field lines break and rejoin.
