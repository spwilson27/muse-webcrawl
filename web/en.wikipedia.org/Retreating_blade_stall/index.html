Retreating blade stall

Retreating blade stall is a hazardous flight condition in helicopters and other rotary wing aircraft, where the retreating rotor blade has a lower relative blade speed, combined with an increased angle of attack, causing a stall and loss of lift. Retreating blade stall is a primary factor in limiting a helicopter's maximum forward speed, together with compressibility effects on the advancing blade.

Background

In forward flight, the rotor blade advancing into the relative wind experiences the sum of rotational speed and forward airspeed, while the blade retreating on the opposite side experiences their difference. This dissymmetry of lift is normally counteracted by blade flapping and cyclic pitch control, which increase the angle of attack on the retreating side and decrease it on the advancing side. As forward speed increases, the retreating blade must work at ever-higher angles of attack to produce its share of lift, while the region of reversed flow near the root of the retreating blade grows.

Development of the stall

Eventually the retreating blade's angle of attack exceeds the critical (stalling) angle, first near the blade tip. The blade stalls, losing lift and experiencing increased drag and a nose-down pitching moment. Because of gyroscopic precession, the aerodynamic effects manifest roughly 90 degrees later in the plane of rotation: the loss of lift on the retreating side causes the rotor disc to blow back, and the pitching moments cause a roll toward the retreating side. High gross weight, high density altitude, high-G maneuvering or abrupt control inputs, low rotor RPM, and turbulent air all make retreating blade stall occur at lower airspeeds.

Effects and recognition

The onset is typically announced by an abnormal two-per-revolution or low-frequency vibration, a pitching up of the nose, and a roll — usually to the left in helicopters whose rotors turn counterclockwise (viewed from above) — tending to develop as speed or load factor increases. If uncorrected, the aircraft may become uncontrollable.

Recovery

Recovery consists of reducing the demands on the rotor: reduce collective pitch (lowering the angle of attack of all blades), apply aft cyclic to slow the aircraft, reduce power and maneuvering load, and descend if necessary to increase rotor RPM and trade altitude for a lower angle of attack. Increasing forward speed during recovery aggravates the condition, so the airspeed must be reduced first.
