Earth's core

Earth's core is the innermost part of the planet, a very hot and dense region consisting mainly of an iron–nickel alloy. It is divided into a liquid outer core, beginning about 2,890 km beneath the surface and roughly 2,260 km thick, and a solid inner core with a radius of about 1,220 km. The core accounts for about 15% of Earth's volume and roughly a third of its mass.

Evidence and composition

Knowledge of the core comes principally from seismology: the Danish seismologist Inge Lehmann showed in 1936 that a solid inner core explained otherwise puzzling seismic wave arrivals. Laboratory experiments, meteorite abundances and Earth's moment of inertia indicate that the outer core is about 5–10% less dense than pure iron, implying the presence of lighter elements such as sulfur, oxygen, silicon, carbon or hydrogen alongside iron and nickel. Temperatures range from about 4,000 °C at the core–mantle boundary to around 6,000 °C at the centre — comparable to the surface of the Sun — while pressures reach some 360 GPa.

Formation and the magnetic field

The core formed by planetary differentiation early in Earth's history, as dense iron sank through a magma ocean (the "iron catastrophe") around 4.5 billion years ago; the inner core crystallised much later, probably within the last billion years or so, and is still growing as the planet cools. Convection in the liquid outer core, driven by heat loss and by the release of light elements as the inner core freezes, sustains the geodynamo that generates Earth's magnetic field.
