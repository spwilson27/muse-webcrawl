Absolute convergence

In mathematics, an infinite series of numbers is said to converge absolutely (or to be absolutely convergent) if the sum of the absolute values of the summands is finite. That is, the series Σan converges absolutely if Σ|an| converges. A series that converges but not absolutely is called conditionally convergent.

Properties

Every absolutely convergent series converges; the converse is false, as shown by the alternating harmonic series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + …, which converges to ln 2 while the harmonic series of absolute values diverges. Absolute convergence is a robust property: any rearrangement of the terms of an absolutely convergent series converges to the same sum. By contrast, the Riemann series theorem shows that a conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to converge to any value whatsoever, or to diverge. Products of absolutely convergent series also behave well: their Cauchy product converges to the product of the sums.

Generalisations

The concept extends to integrals (a function is absolutely integrable if the integral of its absolute value is finite), to series in Banach spaces — where absolute convergence means Σ‖an‖ < ∞, though in infinite-dimensional spaces this no longer guarantees convergence of every rearrangement — and to general topological settings. Tests for absolute convergence include the ratio test, root test, and comparison test, which are often applied to decide the convergence of series with mixed signs.
