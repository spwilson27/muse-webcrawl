Letter frequency is the number of times each letter of an alphabet appears on average in a written language. Letter frequencies depend on the language, the corpus, and even the subject matter, but they are remarkably stable within a language and have practical applications in cryptography, typography, data compression, and games.

English frequencies

In typical English text, e is by far the most common letter, accounting for roughly one letter in eight, followed by t, a, o, i, and n. The twelve most frequent letters spell the mnemonic ETAOIN SHRDLU — a sequence famous because it appeared on the keyboards of Linotype typesetting machines in order of frequency, and sometimes leaked into print when operators filled a bad line by running a finger down the keys. At the other extreme, z, j, and q are rare, which is why they carry the highest values in the game Scrabble.

Applications

The classic application is frequency analysis in cryptanalysis: a substitution cipher can be broken by matching the most common symbols in a ciphertext to the most common letters of the language, a technique described by the Arab scholar al-Kindi in the ninth century. Samuel Morse exploited letter frequency in designing Morse code, assigning the shortest signals (a single dot for E) to the commonest letters. Frequencies also shape keyboard design, dictionary and corpus linguistics, authorship studies, and compression algorithms.

Other languages

Other languages have different profiles: in Spanish e and a dominate; in German e reaches about 17 percent; in French e is similarly dominant. Such profiles make letter frequency a quick heuristic for identifying the language of an unknown text.
