Bornite

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Bornite, also known as peacock ore, is a sulfide mineral with chemical composition Cu5FeS4 that crystallizes in the orthorhombic system (pseudo-cubic). It is an important copper ore mineral, mined for its high copper content, and is famous for the brilliant iridescent blue, purple, and magenta tarnish that develops on freshly broken surfaces, which is the source of its common name.

Properties

Fresh bornite is copper-red to bronze-brown on a fresh surface, with a metallic luster and a gray-black streak. It has a Mohs hardness of about 3 to 3.25 and a specific gravity of roughly 5. It is named after the Austrian mineralogist Ignaz von Born (1742–1791). Bornite is brittle, commonly massive or granular, and well-formed crystals are rare.

Occurrence

Bornite occurs in many copper deposits worldwide, typically associated with other copper sulfides such as chalcopyrite, chalcocite, covellite, and digenite, together with quartz and pyrite. It forms both in hydrothermal veins and as a secondary mineral in the enriched zones of porphyry copper deposits. Famous localities include Butte, Montana; Bristol, Connecticut; Cornwall, England; and deposits in Mexico, Chile, and Kazakhstan.

Alteration

On exposure to weathering, bornite alters readily to chalcocite, covellite, cuprite, malachite, and azurite. In the supergene enrichment zones of copper deposits, bornite derived from leaching of chalcopyrite contributes to high-grade ore horizons.
