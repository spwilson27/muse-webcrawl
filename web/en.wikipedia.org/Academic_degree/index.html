Academic degree

An academic degree is a qualification awarded to a student upon successful completion of a course of study in higher education, usually at a college or university. Degrees are commonly structured in tiers: undergraduate degrees such as the associate and bachelor's degrees, and postgraduate degrees such as the master's degree and the doctorate. The awarding of degrees is one of the defining functions of a university, and degree holders typically use post-nominal letters (for example, BSc, MA, PhD) after their names.

History

The degree system grew out of medieval universities such as Bologna, Paris and Oxford, where the licentia docendi — literally a licence to teach — evolved into the bachelor's, master's and doctoral grades. The bachelor's degree originally marked admission to study for the master's, while the doctorate was the senior teaching qualification in faculties such as law, medicine and theology.

Levels

In the modern framework, an associate degree typically requires two years of study; a bachelor's degree three to five years; a master's degree one or two further years, sometimes with a thesis; and a doctorate several years of original research culminating in a dissertation. Professional doctorates such as the MD and JD prepare students for licensed practice. The Bologna Process has harmonized the bachelor–master–doctorate cycle across most of Europe.

Honorary degrees

Universities also confer honorary degrees (honoris causa) to recognize distinguished achievement, without the recipient completing the usual requirements. Holders of honorary doctorates generally do not use the title "Doctor" in formal academic contexts, by convention.
