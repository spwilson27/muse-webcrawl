Poincare conjecture

On a simply connected closed surface, every loop can be continuously shrunk to a point.

The Poincare conjecture is a theorem in geometric topology concerning the characterization of the 3-sphere. Formulated by the French mathematician Henri Poincaré in 1904, it states that every simply connected, closed 3-manifold is homeomorphic to the 3-sphere.

Statement

A 3-manifold is a space that locally looks like ordinary three-dimensional Euclidean space. The conjecture asserts that if such a space is closed (compact and without boundary) and every loop in it can be continuously tightened to a point (simply connected), then the space is topologically a 3-sphere — the set of points at unit distance from the origin in four-dimensional space.

History and proof

The conjecture resisted proof for nearly a century and became one of the most famous open problems in mathematics; it was named one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems by the Clay Mathematics Institute, each carrying a prize of one million US dollars.

Analogous statements had been proved in higher dimensions in the 1960s by Stephen Smale and others, leaving the original three-dimensional case the hardest. The proof was completed by the Russian mathematician Grigori Perelman, who posted three papers in 2002–2003 building on Richard S. Hamilton's program using the Ricci flow. Perelman's work in fact proved the more general geometrization conjecture of William Thurston, of which Poincaré's conjecture is a special case.

The proof was verified by the mathematical community by 2006. Perelman declined both the Fields Medal (2006) and the Millennium Prize (2010).
