Jeremy Bentham

Jeremy Bentham

Born: 15 February 1748, London, England

Died: 6 June 1832, London, England

Known for: Utilitarianism, panopticon

School: Utilitarianism

Jeremy Bentham (15 February 1748 – 6 June 1832) was an English philosopher, jurist and social reformer regarded as the founder of modern utilitarianism. His philosophy held that the right action is the one that produces "the greatest happiness of the greatest number".

Thought

In An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), Bentham proposed a "felicific calculus" for weighing pleasures and pains and attacked legal fictions and the accumulated irrationalities of the English common law. He advocated comprehensive codification, prison reform, the abolition of capital and corporal punishment, poor law reform and — remarkably for his time — argued in favour of animal welfare, writing that the question is not whether animals can reason but whether they can suffer.

The panopticon and legacy

Bentham's design for a panopticon prison, in which a single guard could observe all inmates, became a lasting symbol of disciplinary power, later analysed by Foucault. He was closely associated with the founding of University College London. Following his instructions, his body was preserved as an "auto-icon" — his skeleton dressed in his clothes, seated in a cabinet — which is still displayed at UCL.
