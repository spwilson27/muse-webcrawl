Booker T. Washington

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Booker Taliaferro Washington (April 5, 1856 – November 14, 1915) was an American educator, author, and orator. Between 1890 and 1915, Washington was the primary leader in the African-American community and of the contemporary Black elite. Born into slavery in Virginia, Washington gained an education after emancipation and became the first leader of the Tuskegee Normal and Industrial Institute in Alabama, now Tuskegee University.

Early life and education

Washington was born into slavery on a plantation in Franklin County, Virginia. After emancipation, his family moved to Malden, West Virginia, where he worked in salt furnaces and coal mines while learning to read. He walked much of the way to Hampton Institute in Virginia, where he studied under Samuel Chapman Armstrong, and later attended Wayland Seminary in Washington, D.C.

Tuskegee Institute

In 1881 Washington became the first principal of the newly founded Tuskegee Institute, building it from a single borrowed church building into a major institution with more than a hundred buildings and an endowment of millions of dollars. The school emphasized vocational and industrial education, teaching trades, agriculture, and teaching itself, with students literally constructing many of the campus buildings.

The Atlanta Compromise

In his 1895 Atlanta Exposition speech, Washington urged Black Americans to focus on economic self-improvement and vocational training, accepting segregation and disenfranchisement for the time being in exchange for economic opportunity — a position dubbed the "Atlanta Compromise." His stance drew sharp criticism from W. E. B. Du Bois, who demanded full civil and political rights and co-founded the NAACP. Washington's 1901 autobiography Up from Slavery became a bestseller and remains a classic of American literature.

Later life and legacy

Washington advised Presidents Theodore Roosevelt and William Howard Taft and raised large sums from philanthropists such as Andrew Carnegie and Julius Rosenwald; the Rosenwald schools built with his guidance educated hundreds of thousands of Black children across the rural South. He died in 1915 at Tuskegee. His home and the institute are now preserved as the Tuskegee Institute National Historic Site.
