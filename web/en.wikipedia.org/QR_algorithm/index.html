QR algorithm

In numerical linear algebra, the QR algorithm or QR iteration is an eigenvalue algorithm: that is, a procedure to calculate the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a matrix. The idea is based on the fact that, under suitable conditions, a sequence of matrices formed by repeatedly computing the QR decomposition of a matrix and multiplying the factors in reverse order converges to a triangular matrix, the Schur form of the original matrix, from which the eigenvalues can be read off the diagonal.

The QR algorithm was developed in the late 1950s by John G. F. Francis and by Vera N. Kublanovskaya, working independently. It is regarded as one of the most important algorithms of the twentieth century and is the method underlying the eigenvalue computations in software libraries such as LAPACK.

The basic algorithm

Starting with a matrix A0 = A, each step computes the QR decomposition Ak = QkRk, where Qk is orthogonal and Rk is upper triangular, and then forms Ak+1 = RkQk. Since Ak+1 = QkTAkQk, all iterates are orthogonally similar to A and therefore share its eigenvalues. For a symmetric matrix the iterates converge to a diagonal matrix; in general they converge to block upper triangular form.

Practical implementations

In practice, the matrix is first reduced to upper Hessenberg form (or tridiagonal form in the symmetric case) by orthogonal similarity transformations, which makes each QR step much cheaper. Convergence is accelerated by shifts, typically taken from the eigenvalues of the trailing 2×2 submatrix (the Wilkinson shift), and by implicit double-shift steps that handle complex conjugate eigenvalue pairs using only real arithmetic. Deflation is applied when subdiagonal entries become negligible, splitting the problem into smaller subproblems.
