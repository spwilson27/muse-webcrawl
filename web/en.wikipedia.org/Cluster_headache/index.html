Cluster headache

A cluster headache is a neurological disorder characterised by recurrent, excruciatingly severe headaches on one side of the head, typically around the eye. It is the most common of the trigeminal autonomic cephalalgias. Attacks last from 15 minutes to three hours and occur in clusters — periods of weeks or months during which attacks may happen several times a day, often at strikingly regular times — separated by remissions. The condition has been informally called the "suicide headache" because of the intensity of the pain.

Signs and symptoms

Attacks begin abruptly with severe burning, stabbing, or boring pain in or behind one eye, sometimes spreading to the temple, jaw, or neck. Unlike migraine, which makes people want to lie still, cluster headache makes sufferers restless and agitated, often pacing or rocking. Attacks are accompanied by autonomic signs on the same side as the pain: tearing, redness of the eye, nasal congestion or runny nose, drooping or swelling of the eyelid, sweating of the forehead, and constriction of the pupil or drooping of the lid (a partial Horner's syndrome). Many patients report that attacks wake them from sleep and follow a circadian and seasonal pattern, suggesting involvement of the hypothalamus.

Causes and pathophysiology

The exact cause is unknown. Brain imaging shows activation of the posterior hypothalamus during attacks, and the disorder involves the trigeminovascular system and parasympathetic outflow through the sphenopalatine ganglion. Unlike migraine, it has historically been more common in men, though the sex ratio has narrowed. Alcohol is a potent trigger during an active cluster period, as are strong smells and nitroglycerin.

Management

Because attacks are brief, oral painkillers are usually too slow. First-line acute treatments are inhalation of 100% oxygen through a non-rebreather mask and subcutaneous or intranasal sumatriptan; zolmitriptan nasal spray and occipital nerve stimulation are further options. Preventive treatment during a cluster period is typically verapamil, sometimes with a short tapering course of corticosteroids as a bridge; lithium, topiramate, and the monoclonal antibody galcanezumab are also used, the latter being approved for episodic cluster headache.

Epidemiology

Cluster headache affects roughly 0.1% of the population, usually beginning between the ages of 20 and 40. Most patients have the episodic form; about 10–15% have chronic cluster headache, in which remissions last less than three months or are absent for a year or more.
