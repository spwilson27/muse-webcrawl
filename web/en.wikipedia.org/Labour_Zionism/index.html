Labour Zionism

Labour Zionism (or Socialist Zionism) is the left-wing variant of Zionism. For many years it was the most significant tendency among Zionists and Zionist organisations worldwide, and was seen as the organising force for the Jewish state before Israel's founding.

The labour movement in the Yishuv, the Jewish community in Mandatory Palestine, centred on the belief that Jewish statehood could only be achieved by the efforts of the Jewish working class settling in Palestine. Central to the movement was the ideal of Jewish agricultural labour, expressed through the kibbutzim and moshavim.

Origins

Labour Zionism developed at the turn of the twentieth century from organisations such as Poale Zion, founded across Europe and North America. The socialist Zionist ideologue Ber Borochov provided its theoretical underpinning, arguing that Jewish emancipation required a Jewish working class in a Jewish homeland.

Key figures and institutions

David Ben-Gurion, Israel's first prime minister, personified the labour movement's political dominance. The Histadrut, a federation of trade unions founded in 1920, became the economic backbone of the Yishuv, at its peak employing a large share of the Jewish workforce. The Mapai party (later the Israeli Labor Party) led Israel's governments from independence in 1948 until 1977.

Second Aliyah and the kibbutz

The pioneers of the Second Aliyah (1904–1914), many influenced by A. D. Gordon's philosophy of the dignity of physical labour, founded the first kibbutzim and developed the ethos of "Hebrew labour". Chaim Arlosoroff and others later adapted these ideas into a pragmatic socialist politics.

Decline

Following the electoral upheaval of 1977, when Likud came to power, Labour Zionism's dominance waned. The movement's parties survived in reduced form, and the kibbutzim underwent waves of privatisation from the 1980s onward.
