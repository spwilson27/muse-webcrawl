Afterload

Afterload is the pressure against which the heart must work to eject blood during systole (ventricular contraction). In other words, it is the end load against which the heart contracts to eject blood. Afterload is readily broken down into components: one factor is the aortic pressure the left ventricle must overcome to eject blood.

Physiology

Afterload is proportional to the mean arterial pressure. As aortic and pulmonary pressures increase, the afterload increases on the left and right ventricles respectively. Afterload changes to adapt to the continually changing demands on an animal's cardiovascular system. Afterload is proportional to the average arterial pressure and is represented graphically by the slope of the end-systolic pressure–volume relationship.

Determinants

The main determinants of afterload are total peripheral resistance, arterial compliance, and ventricular wall stress, the latter described by the law of Laplace: wall tension rises with pressure and radius and falls with wall thickness. Conditions that increase afterload include hypertension, aortic stenosis, and vasoconstriction.

Clinical significance

Chronically elevated afterload forces the ventricle to work harder, contributing to hypertrophy and ultimately heart failure. Drugs that reduce afterload — such as ACE inhibitors, angiotensin receptor blockers, and certain vasodilators — are a mainstay of heart-failure treatment. Afterload is contrasted with preload, the stretch of the ventricle at the end of filling.
