Elegiac couplet

The elegiac couplet is a poetic form used by Greek lyric poets for a variety of themes usually of smaller scale than the epic. Later, Roman poets used it for love poetry in particular. It consists of a dactylic hexameter couplet combined with a dactylic pentameter line.

Form

The first line is a dactylic hexameter — the metre of epic — and the second a dactylic pentameter, which is not five full feet but two half-lines of two and a half feet each. The rise and fall of the two unequal lines gives the couplet its characteristic wave-like rhythm.

Greek and Roman use

Early Greek elegy covered war, politics, and mourning, as in the verses of Tyrtaeus, Solon, and Mimnermus. Roman poets — Catullus, Tibullus, Propertius, and above all Ovid — made it the metre of love elegy, epigram, and didactic play, as in Ovid's Amores and Ars Amatoria.

Later influence

The form was imitated in later European poetry; in English, "elegiac metre" inspired works written in loosely corresponding measures, and the couplet's association with lament shaped the broader meaning of the word "elegy".
