Chichen Itza was a large pre-Columbian city built by the Maya people of the Terminal Classic period, located in the northern Yucatán Peninsula of present-day Mexico. Its name means "at the mouth of the well of the Itza", referring to the sacred cenote — a natural sinkhole — that supplied the city with water.

Major structures

The stepped pyramid of El Castillo (the Temple of Kukulcán) rises 30 metres over the Great Plaza; at the equinoxes, shadows form a serpent descending its northern balustrade. Other structures include the Great Ball Court — the largest in Mesoamerica — the Temple of the Warriors, the observatory known as El Caracol, and the Sacred Cenote, into which offerings of gold, jade and human sacrifices were thrown. The architectural blend of Puuc Maya and central Mexican (Toltec) styles reflects the city's cosmopolitan trade networks.

History and decline

Founded around the 6th century AD, Chichen Itza rose to regional dominance by the late 9th century, controlling trade routes and salt beds on the north coast. It declined in the 13th century, though the site remained a pilgrimage destination into the Spanish colonial era. Restored in the 20th century, it is one of Mexico's most visited archaeological sites and was named among the New7Wonders of the World in 2007.
