Bombing of Tokyo

The bombing of Tokyo by the United States Army Air Forces during the Pacific War is the deadliest conventional bombing campaign in history. A series of firebombing raids culminated in Operation Meetinghouse on the night of 9–10 March 1945, probably the single most destructive air raid ever conducted: hundreds of B-29 Superfortress bombers dropped incendiary bombs on the Japanese capital, creating a firestorm that destroyed about 16 square miles (41 km²) of the city, killed an estimated 90,000 to 100,000 people, and left more than a million homeless.

Background

US bombing of Japan began in earnest in late 1944 from bases in the Mariana Islands. Initial high-altitude precision raids with high-explosive bombs produced poor results because of cloud cover, jet-stream winds, and dispersed Japanese cottage industry. In January 1945, Major General Curtis LeMay took command of XXI Bomber Command and shifted to low-altitude night incendiary attacks, stripping many B-29s of defensive guns to carry more bombs.

Operation Meetinghouse, 9–10 March 1945

On the night of 9–10 March, 279 B-29s attacked Tokyo, pathfinders marking the densely populated Shitamachi working-class districts with napalm-filled M-47 bombs, followed by waves dropping M-69 incendiary cluster bombs. A firestorm ensued; superheated winds drove flames through wooden neighbourhoods, canals boiled, and asphalt melted. Tokyo's fire department was overwhelmed. Estimates of the dead range from around 83,000 (a contemporary Tokyo police figure) to more than 100,000. Fourteen B-29s were lost.

Later raids and effects

Further major raids struck Tokyo in April and May 1945, and firebombing was extended to more than 60 Japanese cities before the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki and Japan's surrender in August 1945. Historians continue to debate the morality and military effectiveness of area firebombing; LeMay himself remarked that had the US lost the war, he expected to be tried as a war criminal. Japan officially commemorates the victims, and the Tokyo Memorial Hall and a museum in Koto Ward preserve the memory of the raids.
