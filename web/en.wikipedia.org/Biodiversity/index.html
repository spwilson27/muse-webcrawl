Biodiversity

Tropical rainforests cover about 6% of Earth's land surface but harbour more than half of its species.

Biodiversity (or biological diversity) is the variety and variability of life on Earth. It can be measured on various levels, including genetic variability, species diversity, and ecosystem diversity. Biodiversity is not distributed evenly on Earth; it is rich in the tropics and in marine phytoplankton in the western Pacific. Overall, terrestrial biodiversity is highest near the equator, which is thought to result from the warm climate and high primary productivity in the region.

The number of described species is about 1.6–1.9 million, while total estimates of eukaryotic species range widely (commonly 8–14 million, with some far higher). Well over 99% of all species that have ever lived are extinct; life has diversified through more than 3.5 billion years of evolution, punctuated by mass extinctions such as the end-Permian and end-Cretaceous events.

Levels and measurement

  • Genetic diversity — variation within species, the raw material of adaptation.
  • Species diversity — richness and evenness of species in an area, often summarised by indices such as Shannon or Simpson.
  • Ecosystem diversity — the variety of habitats, communities and ecological processes.

Threats and conservation

The leading drivers of biodiversity loss are habitat destruction and fragmentation, overexploitation, pollution (including air pollution), invasive species and climate change; diseases such as the historic bubonic plague show how pathogens can also restructure ecosystems. Current extinction rates are estimated at tens to hundreds of times background levels. Responses include protected areas, the Convention on Biological Diversity (1992), the Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022) with its "30 by 30" target, and the scientific field of conservation biology. Warning signals in nature, such as the aposematic colours of defended species, are among the phenomena conservation seeks to preserve.
