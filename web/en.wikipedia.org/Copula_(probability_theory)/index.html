Copula (probability theory)

In probability theory and statistics, a copula is a multivariate cumulative distribution function for which the marginal probability distribution of each variable is uniform on the interval [0, 1]. Copulas are used to describe and model the dependence (correlation) between random variables. Their name, introduced by Abe Sklar in 1959, comes from the Latin for "link" or "tie", similar in spirit in mathematics but unrelated to the grammatical term.

Sklar's theorem

Sklar's theorem states that any multivariate joint distribution can be written in terms of univariate marginal distribution functions and a copula which describes the dependence structure between the variables. Conversely, given marginals and a copula, they can be combined to form a valid joint distribution. This separation of margins from dependence makes copulas a flexible modelling tool.

Families

Common copula families include elliptical copulas (the Gaussian copula and the Student's t copula) and Archimedean copulas such as the Clayton, Gumbel, and Frank copulas, which differ in whether they capture dependence in the upper or lower tails of the distribution.

Applications

Copulas are widely used in quantitative finance for modelling correlated risks, derivatives pricing, and risk management. The Gaussian copula's use in pricing collateralized debt obligations, associated with David X. Li's model, was prominently criticized after the 2008 financial crisis for understating joint default risk. Copulas are also applied in hydrology, engineering reliability, and actuarial science.
