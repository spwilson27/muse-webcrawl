Rice riots of 1918

The rice riots of 1918 (米騒動, kome sōdō) were a series of popular disturbances that swept Japan from July to September 1918, brought about by steep inflation in the price of rice. The riots forced the collapse of the Terauchi Masatake administration and were among the largest episodes of civil unrest in modern Japanese history.

Background

During the First World War, inflation and speculative buying drove the price of rice — Japan's staple food — sharply upward, roughly doubling within a year, while wages failed to keep pace. Government requisitioning and rumours that rice was being shipped to troops intervening in Siberia worsened shortages and anger.

Course

The movement began in late July 1918 in the fishing village of Uozu, Toyama Prefecture, where fishermen's wives protested against rice being loaded onto ships for export from the district. The unrest spread through cities, towns and mining and industrial districts across the country; rioters attacked rice merchants, pawnshops and police boxes, and in some places troops were deployed to restore order. Estimates of total participants run into the millions, and more than 25,000 people were arrested.

Aftermath

The riots brought down Prime Minister Terauchi Masatake's cabinet in September 1918. His successor, Hara Takashi, became the first commoner and party politician to serve as prime minister, an important milestone in Taishō democracy.
