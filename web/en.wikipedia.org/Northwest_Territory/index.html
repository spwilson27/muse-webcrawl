The Northwest Territory, formally the Territory Northwest of the River Ohio, was a federal territory of the United States organized on July 13, 1787, by an ordinance of the Congress of the Confederation. It encompassed the lands north and west of the Ohio River, between the Appalachian colonies and the Mississippi River, and was the first organized incorporated territory of the new United States.

Northwest Ordinance

The Northwest Ordinance of 1787 established the government of the territory and the process by which new states would be carved from it and admitted to the Union on equal footing with the original thirteen. The ordinance famously prohibited slavery in the territory, guaranteed freedom of religion and trial by jury, and encouraged public education, declaring that "schools and the means of education shall forever be encouraged." Between three and five states were to be formed from the territory.

Settlement and conflict

The first permanent American settlement was founded at Marietta, Ohio, in 1788 by veterans of the Revolutionary War under Rufus Putnam. Settlement brought conflict with Native American nations of the Western Confederacy, leading to the Northwest Indian War. After defeats of American forces under Harmar and St. Clair, General Anthony Wayne's victory at the Battle of Fallen Timbers (1794) and the Treaty of Greenville (1795) opened much of Ohio to settlement.

Division and statehood

In 1800 the territory was divided, with the western portion becoming Indiana Territory. Ohio entered the Union as the 17th state on March 1, 1803, dissolving the Northwest Territory. The remainder eventually produced the states of Indiana, Illinois, Michigan, and Wisconsin, plus the part of Minnesota east of the Mississippi River.
