Gastrointestinal tract

The gastrointestinal tract (GI tract, also called the digestive tract or alimentary canal) is the organ system in humans and other animals that takes in food, digests it, absorbs nutrients and energy, and expels the remainder as faeces. In humans it runs from the mouth to the anus and is roughly nine metres long when measured at autopsy, comprising the mouth, pharynx, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, and anus, together with accessory organs such as the liver, gallbladder, and pancreas that secrete into it.

Structure and function

The tract is divided into the upper gastrointestinal tract (mouth to the duodenum) and the lower gastrointestinal tract (the rest of the small intestine and the large intestine). Food is broken down mechanically by chewing and by churning in the stomach, and chemically by enzymes and stomach acid. Most nutrient absorption occurs in the small intestine, whose folded, villi-covered surface provides a huge absorptive area. The large intestine absorbs water and salts and hosts much of the gut microbiota, trillions of microorganisms that aid digestion and immune function.

Clinical significance

The tract's activities are coordinated by the enteric nervous system, sometimes called the "second brain." Common disorders include gastroesophageal reflux disease, peptic ulcers, inflammatory bowel diseases such as Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, coeliac disease, and cancers of the colon and stomach. The medical specialty concerned with the tract is gastroenterology.
