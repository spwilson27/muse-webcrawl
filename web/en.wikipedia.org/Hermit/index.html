Hermit

A hermit, also known as an eremite or solitary, is a person who lives in seclusion. The word derives from the Latin eremita, from the Greek eremos ("desert"), originally describing early Christian desert dwellers. Hermits withdraw from society for religious discipline, prayer, and contemplation, though the term is also applied to secular recluses.

History

The eremitic tradition is prominent in Christianity, beginning with the Desert Fathers of third-century Egypt such as Paul of Thebes and Anthony the Great, and later codified in orders such as the Carthusians and Camaldolese. Hinduism honors the sannyasi who renounces worldly life, Buddhism has forest-dwelling monks, and Taoism has a long tradition of mountain recluses.

Anchorites and modern hermits

In medieval England, anchorites such as Julian of Norwich lived permanently enclosed in cells attached to churches. Modern hermits range from consecrated religious solitaries recognized by the Catholic Church to individuals seeking solitude for art, writing, or personal conviction.
