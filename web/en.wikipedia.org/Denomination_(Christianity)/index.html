Christian denomination

A Christian denomination is a distinct religious body within Christianity that comprises all church congregations of the same kind, identifiable by traits such as a name, particular history, organization, leadership, theological doctrine, worship style and sometimes a founder. It can also be described as a branch of Christianity.

Major branches

The largest divisions are between Catholicism, Protestantism, Eastern Orthodoxy, Oriental Orthodoxy, and the Church of the East, with restorationist traditions such as the Latter Day Saint movement classified separately by many scholars. Within Protestantism there are numerous denominations — Lutheran, Reformed, Anglican, Baptist, Methodist, Pentecostal, Adventist, and many others — differing in doctrine, polity (episcopal, presbyterian, or congregational), and worship practice.

Formation

Denominations have historically arisen from schisms, reform movements, revivals, and missionary expansion, including the Great Schism of 1054 between East and West and the Protestant Reformation of the sixteenth century. The term "denomination" is used most readily for the pluralistic religious landscape of the United States, where voluntary, named bodies coexist under religious liberty. Ecumenical movements, by contrast, seek cooperation or reunion among denominations.

Counting

Estimates of the number of Christian denominations vary enormously — into the tens of thousands — depending on how a "denomination" is defined and whether independent congregations and national bodies are counted separately, making such figures controversial.
