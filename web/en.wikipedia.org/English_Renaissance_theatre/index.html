English Renaissance theatre

Period: c. 1562–1642

Key figures: William Shakespeare, Christopher Marlowe, Ben Jonson

Venues: The Theatre, the Globe, the Rose, Blackfriars

English Renaissance theatre, also known as Renaissance English theatre and Elizabethan theatre, refers to the theatre of England between 1562 and 1642. It is the style of the dramas of William Shakespeare, Christopher Marlowe, Ben Jonson, Thomas Kyd, and their contemporaries, performed in the first purpose-built playhouses in London.

Playhouses

James Burbage built The Theatre in Shoreditch in 1576, followed by the Curtain, the Rose (1587), the Swan, and the Globe (1599), home of Shakespeare's company, the Lord Chamberlain's Men. Public amphitheatres held up to 3,000 spectators around a thrust stage, while private indoor halls such as Blackfriars charged higher prices. All female roles were played by boy actors, and scenery was minimal, with language establishing place and time.

Companies and playwrights

Companies operated under noble patronage and, after 1603, royal patents: the King's Men, the Admiral's Men, and others. Playwrights commonly sold plays outright to companies. Marlowe pioneered the mighty line of blank verse tragedy; Kyd's The Spanish Tragedy launched the revenge play; Jonson perfected city comedy; and later Jacobean and Caroline dramatists such as John Webster and Thomas Middleton explored darker themes.

Closure and legacy

In 1642, at the outbreak of the English Civil War, Parliament ordered the theatres closed; the ban lasted until the Restoration in 1660. The period's drama is regarded as a high point of English literature, and Shakespeare's plays have been performed continuously ever since.
