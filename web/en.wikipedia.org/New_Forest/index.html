The New Forest is one of the largest remaining tracts of unenclosed pasture land, heathland, and forest in the densely populated south-east of England. It covers south-western Hampshire and extends into south-eastern Wiltshire. Created as a royal hunting ground by William the Conqueror around 1079, it was designated a national park in 2005. Despite its name, much of the "forest" is open heath, and the landscape includes ancient woodland, bogs, and a coastline on the Solent.

History

William I evicted inhabitants and subjected the area to harsh Forest Law protecting deer and their habitat. His son William II (William Rufus) died in a hunting accident in the forest in 1100—killed, according to tradition, by an arrow fired by Walter Tirel, an event commemorated by the Rufus Stone. Over the centuries the Forest became an important source of timber for the Royal Navy, and its administration passed through the Court of Verderers, which still meets today. The inclosure acts of the nineteenth century allowed areas to be fenced for timber production, while commoners' rights survived.

Commoning and ponies

A distinctive feature of the New Forest is commoning: local residents, called commoners, hold rights to graze animals on the open forest. Several thousand semi-feral New Forest ponies, along with cattle, donkeys, pigs, and sheep, roam freely, and their grazing maintains the open landscape. Agisters employed by the Verderers oversee the animals' welfare, and annual drifts round up the ponies for health checks and sales.

Ecology

The mosaic of habitats supports rare wildlife, including Dartford warblers, smooth snakes, southern damselflies, and all six native British reptile species. Large areas are protected as Sites of Special Scientific Interest and under European conservation designations. Ancient pollarded beech and oak woods are among the finest in Britain.
