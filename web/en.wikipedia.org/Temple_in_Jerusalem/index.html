Temple in Jerusalem

The Temple in Jerusalem, or Holy Temple (Hebrew: בֵּית־הַמִּקְדָּשׁ, Beit HaMikdash), refers to the two successive temples that stood on the Temple Mount in the Old City of Jerusalem. According to the Hebrew Bible, the First Temple was built by King Solomon in the 10th century BCE and destroyed by the Neo-Babylonian Empire in 586 BCE. The Second Temple, begun after the return from exile under Zerubbabel and dedicated in 516 BCE, was greatly expanded by Herod the Great from about 19 BCE and destroyed by the Roman Empire during the siege of Jerusalem in 70 CE.

The First Temple

Known as Solomon's Temple, the First Temple is described in the Books of Kings and Chronicles as a stone-and-cedar building housing the Ark of the Covenant in its innermost chamber, the Holy of Holies. Only the High Priest could enter the Holy of Holies, and only once a year, on Yom Kippur. Direct archaeological evidence for the building is lacking because the Temple Mount cannot be excavated, though artefacts such as a small ivory pomegranate and seal impressions are sometimes associated with it. King Nebuchadnezzar II's forces destroyed the temple in 586 BCE and exiled much of Judah's population to Babylon.

The Second Temple

The returning exiles rebuilt the temple on a modest scale, completing it in the sixth year of Darius I. Five centuries later Herod rebuilt the temple on a monumental scale, enlarging the Temple Mount platform — whose Western Wall still stands — and cladding the sanctuary in white stone and gold. The Second Temple was the centre of Jewish worship, pilgrimage, and sacrifice, and the scene of events in the New Testament, including Jesus' teaching and his overturning of the money-changers' tables. Roman legions under Titus burned and demolished it in 70 CE during the First Jewish–Roman War, an event commemorated by Jews on Tisha B'Av and depicted on the Arch of Titus in Rome.

Religious significance

In Judaism the temple was the unique place of sacrificial worship administered by priests (kohanim) and Levites; its loss redirected Jewish religious life toward prayer, Torah study, and the synagogue. The Temple Mount (Haram al-Sharif) is now occupied by the Dome of the Rock and Al-Aqsa Mosque, making it one of the most sensitive religious sites in the world, holy to Jews, Muslims, and Christians alike.
