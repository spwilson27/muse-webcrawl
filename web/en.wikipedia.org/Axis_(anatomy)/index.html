In anatomy, the axis (from Latin axis, "axle") or epistropheus is the second cervical vertebra (C2) of the spine, immediately inferior to the atlas, upon which the head rests. The defining feature of the axis is the strong odontoid process known as the dens, which rises from the superior aspect of the body of the bone. The dens forms a pivot around which the atlas and the head rotate, as in shaking the head "no".

Structure

The body of the axis is deeper in front than behind and prolonged downward so as to overlap the upper and front part of the third vertebra. The dens (odontoid process) exhibits a slight constriction or neck where it joins the body, and articulates in front with the anterior arch of the atlas, being held in place by the transverse ligament of the atlas. The spinous process is large, very strong, deeply channelled on its undersurface, and bifid at its tip, giving attachment to muscles that rotate the head.

Clinical significance

Fractures of the dens are relatively common in the elderly after falls and in younger patients after high-energy trauma; they are classified by the Anderson–D'Alonzo scheme. Fracture of the pars interarticularis of the axis — the classic "hangman's fracture" — results from hyperextension injuries. Rheumatoid arthritis and Down syndrome can weaken the transverse ligament, causing atlantoaxial instability.
