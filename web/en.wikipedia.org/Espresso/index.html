Espresso

A freshly pulled shot of espresso

Espresso (Italian: [eˈsprɛsso]) is a concentrated coffee beverage and brewing method of Italian origin, in which a small amount of nearly boiling water is forced under pressure through finely ground coffee beans. Espresso is one of the most popular coffee-brewing methods worldwide and forms the basis for many other drinks, including the caffè latte, cappuccino, macchiato, and americano.

Brewing

Espresso is made with an espresso machine, which drives hot water at around 9 bars (900 kPa) of pressure through a compacted puck of finely ground coffee. A typical single shot uses about 7–9 grams of coffee and yields about 25–30 ml of beverage in 25–30 seconds. The result is a concentrated drink topped with crema, a reddish-brown foam of emulsified oils and carbon dioxide. Variables such as grind size, dose, water temperature, pressure, and extraction time strongly affect flavour; under-extracted shots taste sour and over-extracted shots bitter.

History

Angelo Moriondo of Turin patented an early steam-driven coffee machine in 1884, seeking a faster way to serve coffee. Luigi Bezzera and Desiderio Pavoni commercialised improved machines in the early 1900s, and Achille Gaggia's spring-loaded lever of 1948 first produced crema and the modern style of espresso. Pump-driven machines became standard in the 1960s, notably with the Faema E61.

Espresso-based drinks

Combining espresso with steamed milk, foam, water, or other ingredients yields a wide family of drinks: the cappuccino and latte differ in proportions of milk and foam; the macchiato is "marked" with a little foam; the americano dilutes a shot with hot water; and drinks such as the flat white and cortado are regional variations. Espresso has a higher concentration of caffeine per unit volume than most coffee drinks, though a typical serving contains less caffeine in total than a large mug of drip coffee.
