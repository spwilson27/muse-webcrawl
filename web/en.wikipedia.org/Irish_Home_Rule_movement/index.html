Irish Home Rule movement

The Irish Home Rule movement was a political movement that campaigned for self-government (or "home rule") for Ireland within the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland. Active from about 1870 until the end of the First World War, it was the dominant movement of Irish nationalism and was led successively by Isaac Butt, Charles Stewart Parnell and John Redmond.

Background

Under the Acts of Union 1800, Ireland was governed directly from Westminster. Daniel O'Connell had campaigned for Repeal of the Union in the 1830s and 1840s, but after the Great Famine attention shifted. In 1870 Isaac Butt founded the Home Government Association, replaced in 1873 by the Home Rule League. Home Rule proposed a Dublin parliament for Irish domestic affairs while Ireland remained within the United Kingdom and sent members to Westminster.

Parnell and obstruction

Charles Stewart Parnell became leader of the Irish Parliamentary Party in 1880 and transformed it into a disciplined, pledged party. Using obstruction tactics at Westminster and united behind the demand for Home Rule, the party won 86 seats in 1885, holding the balance of power. Prime Minister William Ewart Gladstone converted to the cause and introduced the first Home Rule Bill in 1886; it was defeated in the Commons, splitting the Liberal Party. A second bill passed the Commons in 1893 but was rejected by the House of Lords.

Parnell split and decline

Parnell's involvement in the O'Shea divorce case split the party in 1890, and it remained divided until reunifying under John Redmond in 1900. The party's fortunes revived when the 1910 elections again made it pivotal at Westminster. The Parliament Act 1911 removed the Lords' absolute veto, and the Government of Ireland Act 1914 (the Third Home Rule Bill) finally reached the statute book — but its implementation was suspended for the duration of the First World War.

Collapse

The Easter Rising of 1916, the executions of its leaders, and the Irish Conscription Crisis of 1918 destroyed faith in constitutional nationalism. In the 1918 general election Sinn Féin won 73 of Ireland's 105 seats, ending the Irish Parliamentary Party. The Anglo-Irish Treaty of 1921 created the Irish Free State, while the 1914 Act's machinery was adapted for Northern Ireland under the Government of Ireland Act 1920.
