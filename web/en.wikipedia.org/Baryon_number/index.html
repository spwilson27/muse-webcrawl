Baryon number

In particle physics, the baryon number is a strictly conserved additive quantum number of a system. It is defined as one third of the number of quarks minus the number of antiquarks in the system: B = (1/3)(nq − nq̄). Each quark therefore carries baryon number +1/3, each antiquark −1/3, baryons (three quarks) have B = 1, antibaryons B = −1, and mesons (quark–antiquark pairs) and leptons have B = 0.

Conservation

Baryon number is conserved in all interactions of the Standard Model that have been observed: in every reaction, the total baryon number before equals the total afterwards. This conservation law explains the stability of the proton, the lightest baryon, which cannot decay without violating the rule. No violation of baryon number conservation has ever been observed experimentally, and searches for forbidden processes such as proton decay and neutron–antineutron oscillation set extremely stringent limits.

Non-conservation in theory and cosmology

Despite its empirical exactness, baryon number is an accidental global symmetry of the Standard Model rather than a fundamental gauge symmetry, and it is not expected to be exactly conserved in nature. Non-perturbative electroweak processes called sphalerons violate baryon plus lepton number (B + L) while conserving B − L, an effect suppressed at ordinary energies but potentially important in the hot early universe. The observed cosmic excess of matter over antimatter — the baryon asymmetry — requires a period of actual baryon-number violation (one of the three Sakharov conditions), motivating grand unified theories and other extensions of the Standard Model that predict proton decay at rates beyond current experimental reach.
