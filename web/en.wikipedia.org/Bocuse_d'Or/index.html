Bocuse d'Or

The Bocuse d'Or (the Concours mondial de la cuisine, World Cuisine Contest) is a biennial world chef championship held in Lyon, France. Named for the chef Paul Bocuse, the event takes place during the Sirha, an international hospitality and food service trade show, and is frequently described as the culinary equivalent of the Olympic Games.

History

The competition was created by Paul Bocuse in 1987. It was modelled in part on the spectacle of major sporting events, with chefs competing live in open kitchens before an audience and a jury of prominent chefs, accompanied by enthusiastic crowds of supporters.

Format

Twenty-four national teams, each consisting of a chef and a commis (assistant), qualify through continental selection rounds held in Europe, Asia-Pacific, the Americas, and Africa. In the final, held over two days, each team has roughly five and a half hours to prepare two elaborate dishes — traditionally a meat platter and a fish dish — based on ingredients announced in advance, presented on grand platters in the French classical tradition. A jury evaluates taste, presentation, creativity, and technique.

Winners

France dominated the early editions of the competition, and Norway, Denmark, and the United States have also produced multiple winning teams. Winning chefs gain considerable international prestige, and the gold, silver, and bronze Bocuse statues are among the most coveted honours in professional cooking.
