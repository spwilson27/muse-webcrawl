Observational study

In fields such as epidemiology, social sciences, psychology and statistics, an observational study draws inferences from a sample to a population where the independent variable is not under the control of the researcher because of ethical concerns or logistical constraints. Unlike experiments, observational studies do not assign subjects randomly to treatment and control groups, so causal conclusions are weaker and must account for confounding.

Types

Common designs include cohort studies (following exposed and unexposed groups over time), case-control studies (comparing people with and without an outcome for prior exposures), and cross-sectional studies (measuring exposure and outcome at one point in time). Famous examples include the Framingham Heart Study and the British Doctors Study, which established the link between smoking and lung cancer.

Bias and analysis

Because treatment is not randomized, researchers use techniques such as stratification, regression adjustment, matching, and propensity scores to reduce confounding, though unmeasured confounding can never be fully excluded.
