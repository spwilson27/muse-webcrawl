Roof shingle

A roof shingle is a type of roof covering consisting of individual overlapping elements, typically flat and rectangular in shape, laid in courses from the bottom edge of the roof up, with each successive higher course overlapping the joints in the course below. Shingles are made of various materials, each with its own advantages and drawbacks.

Materials

  • Asphalt shingles — the most common type in North America, made of fiberglass or organic felt saturated with asphalt and coated with mineral granules. They are inexpensive and easy to install but have a shorter lifespan than many alternatives.
  • Wood shingles and shakes — typically cut from cedar or other rot-resistant woods; shakes are split rather than sawn and have a rougher texture.
  • Slate shingles — split stone tiles, very durable and fireproof but heavy and costly.
  • Metal shingles — steel, aluminum, or copper formed to resemble traditional shingles.
  • Clay and concrete tiles — common in Mediterranean and warm-climate architecture.
  • Solar shingles — photovoltaic shingles that double as roofing material and electricity generators.

Installation

Shingles are laid in overlapping courses over underlayment on the roof deck, typically on pitched roofs where gravity sheds water over the overlaps. Flashing is used around chimneys, vents, and valleys to prevent leaks.

History

Wood shingles were widely used in colonial America, while slate became common in the 19th century. Asphalt shingles were introduced in the early 20th century and came to dominate residential roofing in the United States.
