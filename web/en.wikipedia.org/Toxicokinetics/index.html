Toxicokinetics

Toxicokinetics is the study of how a potentially toxic substance enters, moves through and leaves the body — in effect, the pharmacokinetics of a xenobiotic at doses capable of causing harm. It describes the relationship between the administered dose of a chemical and its concentration over time in blood, tissues and organs, and is a core discipline of toxicology and chemical risk assessment.


The ADME processes described by toxicokinetics

ADME

Toxicokinetic behaviour is summarised by the acronym ADME:

  • Absorption — entry into the circulation from the gut, lungs or skin, governed by properties such as lipid solubility, ionisation and molecular size.
  • Distribution — transport to tissues, binding to plasma proteins, and accumulation in depots such as body fat for persistent compounds like DDT and PCBs.
  • Metabolism — enzymatic transformation, mainly in the liver by cytochrome P450 enzymes, in Phase I (oxidation, reduction, hydrolysis) and Phase II (conjugation) reactions, which usually detoxify but can also bioactivate a substance to a more toxic metabolite, as with parathion or aflatoxin B1.
  • Excretion — elimination in urine, bile, faeces, exhaled air or milk.

Concepts and models

Key quantities include bioavailability, volume of distribution, clearance and biological half-life. At high doses, metabolic pathways may saturate, so kinetics become non-linear and toxicity can rise disproportionately — a critical consideration when extrapolating animal studies to human exposure limits. Mathematical descriptions range from simple one-compartment models to physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) models that simulate each organ's blood flow and partition coefficients, and which regulators increasingly use for setting occupational exposure limits and assessing pesticides, industrial chemicals and pharmaceuticals.
