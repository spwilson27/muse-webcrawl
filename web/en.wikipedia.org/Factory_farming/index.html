Factory farming

Also calledIntensive animal farming; industrial livestock production
Regulatory term (US)Concentrated animal feeding operation (CAFO)
AnimalsPigs, poultry, cattle, dairy cows, farmed fish
Key issuesAnimal welfare, pollution, antibiotic resistance, greenhouse gases

Factory farming, also called intensive animal farming or industrial livestock production, is an intensive method of animal husbandry designed to maximize production while minimizing costs. Animals are kept at high stocking densities, often indoors, in large facilities resembling factories; in the United States such operations above a size threshold are regulated as concentrated animal feeding operations (CAFOs). The great majority of meat, eggs, and dairy consumed worldwide now comes from intensive systems.

Practices

Typical practices include raising broiler chickens in sheds of tens of thousands of birds on accelerated growth schedules; keeping laying hens in battery cages (banned in the European Union since 2012 in their barren form); confining breeding sows in gestation crates; and fattening cattle in feedlots on grain-based diets. Routine use of antibiotics to promote growth and prevent disease in crowded conditions has been restricted in the EU and elsewhere over concerns about antimicrobial resistance. Selective breeding has produced fast-growing animals, sometimes with associated health problems.

History

Intensive methods developed in the early 20th century with the discovery of vitamins A and D, which allowed animals to be raised indoors year-round, and expanded rapidly after the Second World War alongside cheap feed grains, antibiotics, and vertical integration of the poultry and pork industries. Ruth Harrison's 1964 book Animal Machines gave the practice its critical name and spurred the first government inquiries into farm animal welfare.

Impacts and debate

Supporters argue intensive production delivers affordable protein with less land per unit than extensive methods. Critics cite animal welfare harms, manure and nutrient runoff polluting waterways, substantial greenhouse-gas emissions, zoonotic disease risk, and antibiotic resistance. Alternatives promoted by reformers include free-range and pasture-based systems, welfare certification schemes, and plant-based or cultivated meat substitutes; several jurisdictions have legislated phase-outs of cages and crates.
