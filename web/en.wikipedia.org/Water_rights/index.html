Water rights are legal rights to extract and use water from a water source, such as a river, stream, lake or aquifer. Because water is fugitive, moving and essential to many users at once, legal systems typically treat water rights as usufructuary — a right to use, rather than outright ownership of the water itself — and allocate them under several distinct doctrines.

Riparian rights

Under the riparian doctrine, inherited from English common law and dominant in the wetter eastern United States and many Commonwealth countries, owners of land adjoining a watercourse may make reasonable use of its water, subject to the equal rights of other riparian owners. Rights attach to the land, are not lost by non-use, and in times of shortage are generally shared proportionally.

Prior appropriation

The prior appropriation doctrine — summarised as "first in time, first in right" — arose in the mining camps and irrigation districts of the arid western United States in the 19th century. Rights are acquired by diverting water and applying it to a beneficial use; in drought, senior right-holders are satisfied before junior ones, regardless of land ownership, and rights can be lost through abandonment or forfeiture for non-use. Most western states administer appropriation through permit systems overseen by a state engineer or water court.

Other doctrines

Many countries allocate water by government licence or permit under public ownership frameworks. In the United States, federal reserved water rights (the Winters doctrine, 1908) guarantee water for Native American reservations and federal lands, and the public trust doctrine limits private rights in navigable waters. Groundwater is variously governed by rule of capture, correlative rights or reasonable-use standards. Internationally, the UN General Assembly in 2010 recognised a human right to water and sanitation, and shared rivers are governed by principles of equitable and reasonable utilisation in the 1997 UN Watercourses Convention.
