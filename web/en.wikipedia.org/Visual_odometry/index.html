Visual odometry

Visual odometry
FieldComputer vision, robotics
InputCamera image sequences
OutputEstimated camera pose / trajectory

In robotics and computer vision, visual odometry is the process of determining the position and orientation of a robot by analyzing the associated camera images. It has been used in a wide variety of robotic applications, such as on the Mars Exploration Rovers, and is an alternative or complement to wheel odometry, GPS, and inertial measurement units.

Method

Most visual odometry systems follow a common pipeline: image acquisition; image correction; feature detection (using detectors such as FAST, Harris, or SIFT); feature matching or optical-flow tracking between frames; estimation of the camera motion from correspondences, typically via the essential matrix and RANSAC for outlier rejection; and local optimization, such as windowed bundle adjustment, to refine the trajectory. Systems are classed as monocular or stereo, and as feature-based ("indirect") or appearance-based ("direct"), the latter exemplified by LSD-SLAM and DSO.

Relation to SLAM

Visual odometry focuses on sequential pose estimation and is subject to drift over time; visual SLAM (simultaneous localization and mapping) builds on it with loop closure and global optimization to correct accumulated error. The term "visual odometry" was coined in 2004 by David Nister and colleagues, whose five-point algorithm paper helped popularize the approach.
