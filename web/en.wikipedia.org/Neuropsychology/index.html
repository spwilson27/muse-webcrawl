Neuropsychology is a branch of psychology and neuroscience concerned with how the structure and function of the brain relate to behaviour and cognitive processes. It studies both the effects of brain injury and disease on cognition and behaviour, and what such effects reveal about the organisation of the normal mind. Clinical neuropsychologists apply this knowledge to assess, diagnose, and rehabilitate people with neurological conditions.

History

The discipline traces its roots to nineteenth-century lesion studies. In 1861 Paul Broca described patients whose speech production was impaired following damage to the left frontal lobe, providing influential evidence for the localisation of function. Karl Wernicke subsequently described a contrasting receptive aphasia associated with posterior temporal damage. In the twentieth century, Alexander Luria systematised the clinical study of higher cortical functions, and Brenda Milner's studies of the patient H.M. demonstrated the hippocampus's central role in memory formation. The development of standardised test batteries and, later, structural and functional neuroimaging transformed the field.

Methods

Neuropsychologists use standardised tests of memory, attention, language, visuospatial ability, executive function, and processing speed to construct cognitive profiles. A central logic of the discipline is the double dissociation, in which two patients show opposite patterns of impairment, implicating separable underlying systems. Group studies and single-case studies complement one another, and modern research integrates behavioural testing with neuroimaging techniques such as fMRI and EEG.

Clinical practice

Clinical neuropsychological assessment is used in dementia diagnosis, epilepsy surgery planning, stroke and traumatic brain injury rehabilitation, and the evaluation of developmental disorders. Findings guide cognitive rehabilitation, educational accommodations, and determinations of functional capacity.
