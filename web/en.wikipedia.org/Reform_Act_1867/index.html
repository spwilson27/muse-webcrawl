Reform Act 1867

The Representation of the People Act 1867 (30 & 31 Vict. c. 102), known as the Reform Act 1867 or the Second Reform Act, was a piece of British legislation that enfranchised part of the urban male working class in England and Wales as voters, for the first time. Together with the Reform Act 1832 it was part of the gradual widening of the franchise that continued with the acts of 1884, 1918, and 1928.

Provisions

The Act gave the vote to all male householders in boroughs who paid poor rates and had lived at their address for at least a year, and to lodgers paying at least £10 a year in rent. In the counties the franchise remained more restricted, extending to occupiers paying £12 a year in rates. It also redistributed a modest number of seats from small boroughs to the growing industrial cities and created new constituencies. The electorate in England and Wales roughly doubled, from about 1.4 million to about 2.5 million men — roughly one in three adult men.

Politics and consequences

The Act was passed by a minority Conservative government under the Earl of Derby, with Benjamin Disraeli steering it through the Commons and accepting radical amendments — a manoeuvre Disraeli called "a leap in the dark". Historians debate the parties' motives: fear of popular agitation by the Reform League, which staged mass demonstrations after an earlier Liberal bill failed in 1866, and partisan calculation both played a part. The enlarged electorate contributed to the Liberal victory under Gladstone in the 1868 general election, led to the secret ballot (1872) and corrupt-practices legislation, and began modern mass party organisation.
