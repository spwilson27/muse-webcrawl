Columbian exchange

The Columbian exchange, also called the Columbian interchange, was the widespread transfer of plants, animals, precious metals, culture, human populations, technology, diseases and ideas between the New World (the Americas) and the Old World (Europe, Asia and Africa) in the late 15th and following centuries. It is named after Christopher Columbus, whose 1492 voyage began sustained contact between the hemispheres. The term was coined in 1972 by the historian Alfred W. Crosby in his book The Columbian Exchange.

Principal transfers of the Columbian exchange.

Food crops

American crops transformed diets worldwide. The potato and maize became staple foods in Europe, Africa and Asia, supporting rapid population growth; the tomato remade Mediterranean cooking; and cacao, vanilla, peanuts, cassava, sweet potatoes, chili peppers and pineapples spread globally. In return, Europeans brought wheat, rice, sugar cane, coffee, citrus fruits and bananas to the Americas.

Animals

The Americas had few domesticated animals beyond the dog, llama, alpaca, guinea pig and turkey. The introduction of the horse transformed the cultures of the Great Plains and the pampas, while cattle, pigs and sheep founded ranching economies — and caused extensive ecological change through overgrazing and the spread of weeds.

Disease

The most devastating transfer was microbial. Indigenous Americans, having no prior exposure, died in enormous numbers from smallpox, measles, influenza, typhus and other Old World diseases; many regions lost half or more of their population in the sixteenth century, a catastrophe sometimes called the Great Dying. It also facilitated European conquest and drove the demand for enslaved African labour. Syphilis may have travelled in the opposite direction, though its origins remain debated.

Long-term effects

The exchange linked the world's ecosystems and economies into a single system for the first time since the continents separated. Silver from Potosí and Zacatecas flowed through Europe to China; American crops fed industrialising populations; and the demographic collapse in the Americas contributed to reforestation that some researchers link to a dip in global atmospheric carbon dioxide.
