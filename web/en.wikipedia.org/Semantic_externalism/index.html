Semantic externalism

Semantic externalism (also called content externalism) is a position in the philosophy of language and philosophy of mind according to which the meaning of a word, or the content of a mental state, is not determined solely by what is inside a person's head, but is partly constituted by features of the person's external environment — including the physical world and the linguistic community to which the speaker belongs.

Semantic externalism holds that meaning depends partly on factors outside the mind.

Background

Traditional theories of meaning, often associated with Frege and Russell, treated the meaning of a term as a description or a mental representation grasped by the speaker. On such views, two speakers who are internally identical — molecule-for-molecule duplicates — would necessarily mean the same thing by their words and think the same thoughts. Externalism denies this.

The Twin Earth argument

The classic argument for externalism is Hilary Putnam's Twin Earth thought experiment, introduced in his 1975 paper "The Meaning of 'Meaning'". Putnam imagines a planet, Twin Earth, identical to Earth except that the liquid called "water" there is not H2O but a different compound, XYZ, which is superficially indistinguishable. In 1750, before chemistry could distinguish the two, an Earthling and his Twin Earth duplicate would have had identical internal states, yet the Earthling's word "water" referred to H2O while the Twin Earthling's referred to XYZ. Since the meanings (extensions) differ while the internal states are the same, Putnam concluded that "meanings just ain't in the head".

Social externalism

Tyler Burge extended externalism to the social environment in "Individualism and the Mental" (1979). In his "arthritis" thought experiment, a patient who believes he has arthritis in his thigh has a belief with a different content in a counterfactual community where "arthritis" is used more broadly to cover similar ailments in other joints. The content of the patient's belief thus depends on the usage of experts in his linguistic community — a phenomenon Putnam called the division of linguistic labor.

Varieties and criticism

Externalism comes in several forms: natural-kind externalism (Putnam, Kripke), social externalism (Burge), and historical/causal externalism, which traces reference to causal chains of use. Critics object that externalism threatens privileged self-knowledge (how can we know our own thoughts if their contents depend on the environment?) and sits uneasily with the idea that mental states cause behavior in virtue of their internal properties. Defenders such as Burge reply that externalism is compatible with authoritative self-knowledge, and that slow-switching cases — where a person is unknowingly moved between environments — show content can change without any internal change.
