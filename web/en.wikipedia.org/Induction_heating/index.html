Induction heating

Induction heating is the process of heating an electrically conducting material — usually a metal — by electromagnetic induction. An alternating current passed through a coil (the inductor) generates a rapidly alternating magnetic field, which induces circulating eddy currents inside the workpiece; the electrical resistance of the material converts these currents into heat (Joule heating). In ferromagnetic materials below their Curie temperature, additional heat is produced by magnetic hysteresis losses. The coil itself does not touch the workpiece and stays relatively cool, typically being made of water-cooled copper tubing.

Principles

Because of the skin effect, the induced current is concentrated near the surface of the workpiece; the "reference depth" at which current density falls to 1/e of its surface value decreases with the square root of frequency, resistivity and magnetic permeability. Choosing the frequency therefore controls the heating depth: high frequencies (100 kHz–several MHz) for surface hardening and small parts, low frequencies (50 Hz–10 kHz) for through-heating large billets before forging. An induction heater consists of a high-frequency power supply (inverter), a work head with capacitor bank forming a resonant circuit, and the coil, whose shape is tailored to the part.

Applications

  • Metalworking — forging, hardening, annealing, tempering, brazing, soldering, shrink-fitting and melting in induction furnaces (including coreless and channel furnaces used in foundries).
  • Welding and bonding — induction welding of tubes and pipes; curing adhesives; sealing containers with foil liners.
  • Induction cooking — domestic hobs heat the base of ferromagnetic cookware directly, offering fast response and efficiency.
  • Semiconductor and specialty processes — crystal growth, plasma generation, hyperthermia treatment in medicine, and heating in vacuum or inert atmospheres where flame or contact heating is unsuitable.

Advantages and limitations

Induction heating is fast, precisely controllable, repeatable, energy-efficient (heat is generated within the part rather than transferred to it) and clean at the point of use, suiting automated production lines. Limitations include the cost of power electronics, the need for coil designs matched to each geometry, and the restriction to electrically conductive materials (though non-conductors can be heated indirectly via a conductive susceptor).

History

The phenomenon rests on Faraday's law of induction (1831); eddy-current heating was noted by Foucault and others in the nineteenth century. Practical induction furnaces appeared around 1900 (the Kjellin and Ajax-Wyatt furnaces), and the technology expanded greatly with high-frequency motor-generator sets and, from the 1960s onward, solid-state inverters, which made compact, efficient induction heaters commonplace.
