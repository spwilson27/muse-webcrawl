Solid solution strengthening

Solid solution strengthening is a type of alloying that can be used to improve the strength of a pure metal. The technique involves adding atoms of one element (the alloying element) into the crystal lattice of another element (the base metal), forming a solid solution. The local non-uniformity in the lattice caused by the solute atoms makes plastic deformation more difficult by impeding the motion of dislocations.

Types of solid solutions

Substitutional solutions

In a substitutional solid solution, solute atoms replace solvent atoms on lattice sites. Favorable conditions for extensive solubility are summarized by the Hume-Rothery rules: similar atomic radii (within about 15%), the same crystal structure, similar electronegativity, and the same valency. Examples include copper–nickel, which is soluble in all proportions.

Interstitial solutions

Small atoms such as carbon, nitrogen, and hydrogen can occupy the gaps (interstices) between host atoms. Carbon in iron — the basis of steel — is the most important example; even small amounts of interstitial carbon greatly increase strength.

Mechanism

Solute atoms distort the surrounding lattice, creating stress fields that interact with the stress fields of dislocations. A dislocation must expend extra energy to move past or break free of these obstacles, which raises the yield strength. The strengthening effect generally increases with solute concentration and with the size and modulus mismatch between solute and solvent atoms. Empirical relations such as the Labusch and Fleischer models quantify the dependence on concentration.

Effects and trade-offs

Solid solution strengthening increases yield and tensile strength and hardness, but usually at some cost to ductility and electrical and thermal conductivity. It is one of the principal strengthening mechanisms available to metallurgists, alongside grain-boundary (Hall–Petch) strengthening, precipitation hardening, dispersion strengthening, and work hardening. Unlike precipitation hardening, solid solution strengthening does not require heat treatment and is stable at elevated temperatures, though it is generally less potent.

Examples

Bronze (copper–tin) and brass (copper–zinc) were history's first deliberate alloys and owe their superiority over pure copper largely to solid solution strengthening. Modern examples include austenitic stainless steels (strengthened by chromium, nickel, and nitrogen in iron), nickel-base superalloys, and aluminium alloys containing magnesium.
