Clayton Antitrust Act of 1914

The Clayton Antitrust Act of 1914 is the full formal title of the United States statute commonly shortened to the Clayton Act. Passed by the 63rd Congress and signed into law by President Woodrow Wilson on October 15, 1914, it was a centrepiece of Wilson's "New Freedom" programme and of the Progressive Era campaign against concentrated corporate power.

Legislative history

The bill was introduced by Representative Henry De Lamar Clayton Jr. of Alabama, chairman of the House Judiciary Committee. Progressives considered the Sherman Act of 1890 too vague, since its general prohibition of "restraint of trade" had been inconsistently applied by the courts — and sometimes used against striking workers. The 1914 Act therefore enumerated specific practices: price discrimination, tying and exclusive dealing, anticompetitive mergers, and interlocking directorates. It passed alongside the Federal Trade Commission Act, which created an expert agency to police "unfair methods of competition".

Contents by section

Section 2 addressed price discrimination; Section 3 covered tying and exclusive dealing in goods; Section 7 covered stock and (after 1950) asset acquisitions; Section 8 covered interlocking directorates among large corporations and banks. Section 4 authorised private treble-damage suits, Section 6 exempted labour and agricultural organisations, and Section 20 restricted injunctions against strikes and peaceful picketing.

Later development

Subsequent statutes bearing the names of their sponsors — Robinson–Patman (1936), Celler–Kefauver (1950) and Hart–Scott–Rodino (1976) — are formally amendments to the 1914 Act, so the "Clayton Act" in current law is the 1914 text as repeatedly amended.
