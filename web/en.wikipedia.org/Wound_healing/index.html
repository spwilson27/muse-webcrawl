Wound healing

Wound healing refers to the process by which living tissue repairs itself after injury. In adult mammals, injured skin and other tissues typically heal by forming scar tissue rather than by perfect regeneration. The process is classically described as a coordinated sequence of overlapping phases: haemostasis, inflammation, proliferation and remodelling.

The phases of wound healing

Phases

Haemostasis begins immediately: blood vessels constrict and platelets aggregate to form a fibrin clot that stops bleeding and provides a provisional matrix. Inflammation follows over the first days, as neutrophils and then macrophages clear debris and bacteria and release growth factors and cytokines that recruit repair cells.

During proliferation (roughly days 3–10), fibroblasts lay down new collagen and extracellular matrix, angiogenesis restores the blood supply to form granulation tissue, and keratinocytes re-epithelialize the surface while the wound contracts. Remodelling can continue for a year or more: collagen type III is replaced by stronger type I collagen and fibres reorganise, though scar tissue typically reaches only about 80% of the strength of unwounded skin.

Primary and secondary intention

Wounds with closely apposed edges (such as surgical incisions) heal by primary intention with minimal scarring. Open wounds that must fill with granulation tissue heal by secondary intention, a slower process with more contraction and scarring.

Impaired healing

Chronic wounds — venous leg ulcers, diabetic foot ulcers and pressure sores — fail to progress through the normal phases, often because of poor perfusion, infection, diabetes or repeated trauma. Factors such as smoking, malnutrition and immunosuppression delay healing, while excessive scarring can produce hypertrophic scars and keloids. Growth-factor studies, notably the work of Gail Martin and colleagues on TGF-β signalling, and clinical practices such as negative-pressure wound therapy aim to improve outcomes.
