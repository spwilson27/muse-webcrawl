Champagne-Ardenne is a former administrative region of northeastern France, abolished on 1 January 2016 when it was merged with Alsace and Lorraine to form the new region of Grand Est. It took its name from the historic province of Champagne, famed worldwide for its sparkling wine, and from the densely forested Ardennes uplands along the Belgian border.

Geography

The region covered some 25,600 square kilometres and comprised four departments: Ardennes, Aube, Marne, and Haute-Marne. Its landscapes range from the chalk plains around Châlons-en-Champagne and Troyes — historically devoted to grazing and later to intensive grain farming — to the wooded hills of the Ardennes and the vine-covered slopes of the Montagne de Reims and the Côte des Blancs. Principal rivers include the Marne, the Aube, the Seine, and the Meuse.

History

The medieval Counts of Champagne made the region prosperous through the great Champagne fairs of Troyes, Provins, and Lagny, which linked the cloth towns of Flanders with Italian merchants. Reims, where Clovis was baptized and French kings were traditionally crowned in the great Gothic cathedral, was its spiritual capital. The plains of Champagne were repeatedly a battlefield, from the Roman era through the two World Wars; the First Battle of the Marne was fought here in 1914, and the formal German surrender of 1945 was signed in Reims.

Economy

The region is dominated economically by Champagne wine, produced in delimited vineyards and exported worldwide. Agriculture, metallurgy in the Ardennes valley, and textiles around Troyes — long a centre of hosiery manufacture — were the other traditional pillars. With about 1.3 million inhabitants at its dissolution, Champagne-Ardenne was one of France's least densely populated regions.
