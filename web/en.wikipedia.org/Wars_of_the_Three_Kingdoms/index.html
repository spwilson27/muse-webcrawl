The Wars of the Three Kingdoms (sometimes called the British Civil Wars) were a series of interconnected conflicts that took place in the kingdoms of England, Scotland and Ireland between 1639 and 1653. The wars began with the Bishops' Wars in Scotland, spread to Ireland with the Irish Rebellion of 1641, and culminated in the English Civil War. They are named after the fact that the Stuart monarch Charles I ruled all three kingdoms in personal union, and that the conflicts in each kingdom influenced and fuelled those in the others.

Background

By the late 1630s, Charles I's attempts to impose religious uniformity — in particular Archbishop William Laud's reforms and the introduction of a new Book of Common Prayer in Scotland in 1637 — provoked fierce resistance. Scottish opponents signed the National Covenant in 1638, and the resulting Bishops' Wars (1639–1640) ended in humiliation for the king, who was forced to summon the English Parliament to raise funds. The Long Parliament, once assembled, refused to be dissolved and began dismantling the instruments of Charles's personal rule.

The wars

In October 1641, Irish Catholics rose in rebellion, fearing the growing power of Protestant parliaments in London and Edinburgh. Reports of massacres of Protestant settlers, exaggerated in England, deepened the crisis of trust between king and Parliament. Disputes over control of the army to be sent to Ireland pushed England into civil war in August 1642, when Charles raised his standard at Nottingham.

The First English Civil War (1642–1646) ended with Charles in Scottish custody; he was handed over to Parliament and imprisoned. After escaping to the Isle of Wight, Charles negotiated the "Engagement" with a faction of the Scots, sparking the Second Civil War in 1648, which Parliament's New Model Army under Oliver Cromwell swiftly won, culminating at the Battle of Preston. Charles was tried for high treason and executed on 30 January 1649 — an unprecedented act that shocked Europe.

Conquest of Ireland and Scotland

England was declared a republic, the Commonwealth. Cromwell led a brutal campaign in Ireland (1649–1653), notorious for the storming of Drogheda and Wexford, which subdued the Irish Confederate and Royalist alliance. When the Scots proclaimed Charles's son as Charles II and invaded England, Cromwell defeated them at Dunbar (1650) and Worcester (1651), the last major battle of the wars. Scotland and Ireland were incorporated into the Commonwealth, and the three kingdoms were governed as a single state until the Restoration of 1660.

Legacy

The wars killed a higher proportion of the population of the British Isles — particularly Ireland, where war, famine and plague may have reduced the population by 15–20 per cent or more — than the First World War would. They permanently ended the prospect of absolute monarchy in Britain, established the precedent that a king could be tried and executed by his subjects, and left deep marks on the political and religious cultures of all three nations.
