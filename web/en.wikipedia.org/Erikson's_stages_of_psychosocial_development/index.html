Erikson's stages of psychosocial development

Erikson's eight stages, each defined by a psychosocial crisis

Erikson's stages of psychosocial development are a psychoanalytic theory of personality development proposed by the German-American psychologist Erik Erikson, first set out in his book Childhood and Society (1950). Erikson proposed that a person passes through eight stages from infancy to late adulthood, each characterised by a psychosocial crisis between two opposing tendencies; a favourable resolution yields an ego strength or virtue.

The eight stages

  • Trust vs. mistrust (infancy, 0–18 months): virtue of hope.
  • Autonomy vs. shame and doubt (early childhood, 2–3 years): virtue of will.
  • Initiative vs. guilt (preschool, 3–5 years): virtue of purpose.
  • Industry vs. inferiority (school age, 6–11 years): virtue of competence.
  • Identity vs. role confusion (adolescence, 12–18 years): virtue of fidelity; the stage that produced Erikson's famous concept of the identity crisis.
  • Intimacy vs. isolation (young adulthood, 19–40 years): virtue of love.
  • Generativity vs. stagnation (middle adulthood, 40–65 years): virtue of care.
  • Ego integrity vs. despair (late adulthood, 65+ years): virtue of wisdom.

Theory

Erikson built on Freud's psychosexual stages but reframed development as a lifelong, socially embedded process governed by what he called the epigenetic principle: each stage builds on the resolution of the previous ones, and its crisis is present in some form throughout life. Unlike stage theories in which failure is fixed, Erikson held that an unfavourable outcome in one stage could be partially remedied later.

Influence and criticism

The model has been highly influential in developmental psychology, education, and counselling, and identity research by James Marcia extended the fifth stage into identity statuses. Critics note that the stages reflect mid-20th-century Western cultural assumptions, that the exact ages and sequence are debatable, and that empirical support for the full eight-stage scheme is mixed, although several of its ideas — especially identity development and generativity — remain active research topics.
