Textus Receptus

Textus Receptus (Latin: "received text") refers to the succession of printed Greek New Testament texts, first published in 1516, which constituted the translation base for many early Protestant vernacular translations — including Luther's German Bible, the Tyndale New Testament, and the King James Version (1611). The name derives from the publisher's preface to the Elzevir brothers' 1633 edition, which boasted Textum ergo habes, nunc ab omnibus receptum ("so you hold the text, now received by all").

History

The first edition was prepared in Basel by Desiderius Erasmus and printed by Froben in 1516, working hastily from a handful of late Byzantine manuscripts; for the last verses of Revelation, missing from his manuscript, Erasmus back-translated from the Latin Vulgate. Revised editions followed (notably those of Robert Estienne, whose 1550 text introduced verse divisions in 1551, and Theodore Beza). The Elzevir editions of 1624 and 1633 standardized the form from which the name stuck.

Textual character

The Textus Receptus broadly represents the Byzantine text-type but includes readings absent from the earliest Greek manuscripts — most famously the Comma Johanneum (1 John 5:7), a Trinitarian gloss that entered Erasmus's third edition under pressure. Since the nineteenth century, critical editions based on earlier manuscripts such as Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus have superseded it in academic use, though it remains the basis of the King James Version, the New King James Version, and the texts favored by the "King James Only" movement and confessional Reformed churches.
