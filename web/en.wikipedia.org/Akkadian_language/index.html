Akkadian (Akkadian: akkadû) is an extinct East Semitic language that was spoken in ancient Mesopotamia from the third millennium BC until its gradual replacement by Aramaic by around the eighth century BC, though it continued as a written language of scholarship and liturgy for centuries afterwards. It is the earliest attested Semitic language and takes its name from the city of Akkad, capital of the Akkadian Empire under Sargon.

History and dialects

Written in the cuneiform script adapted from Sumerian, Akkadian survives in hundreds of thousands of clay tablets. Its history is conventionally divided into Old Akkadian, Old Babylonian and Old Assyrian, Middle Babylonian and Middle Assyrian, Neo-Babylonian and Neo-Assyrian, and Late Babylonian stages. The two great dialect groups, Babylonian in the south and Assyrian in the north, diverged over two millennia. In the second millennium BC Akkadian served as the lingua franca of diplomacy across the Near East, as shown by the Amarna letters exchanged between Egypt and the great powers.

Grammar

Like other Semitic languages, Akkadian builds words from consonantal roots with patterned vowels. It has three cases (nominative, genitive, accusative), two genders, and a verb system organised into stems (such as the G, D, Š, and N stems) expressing basic, factitive, causative, and passive meanings. Word order is typically subject–object–verb, an unusual feature among Semitic languages attributed to Sumerian influence.

Literature

Akkadian literature includes the Epic of Gilgamesh, the creation epic Enūma Eliš, the law code of Hammurabi, royal annals, omen collections, lexical lists, and vast archives of letters and administrative records that form the foundation of Assyriology.
