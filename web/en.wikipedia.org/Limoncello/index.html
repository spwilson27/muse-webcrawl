Limoncello is an Italian lemon liqueur mainly produced in southern Italy, especially in the region around the Gulf of Naples — the Sorrentine Peninsula, the Amalfi Coast and the island of Capri — and also in Sicily and Sardinia. It is Italy's second most popular liqueur after Campari and is traditionally served chilled as an after-dinner digestivo.

Production

Limoncello is made by steeping lemon zest (peel without pith) in rectified spirit until the oils and colour are released, then mixing the resulting yellow infusion with sugar syrup. The lemons traditionally used are large, thick-skinned local varieties such as the sfusato amalfitano and the Sorrento oval lemon (limone di Sorrento), both protected by IGP designations. Commercial limoncello is usually around 25–30% alcohol by volume and has a characteristic cloudy, vibrant yellow appearance from emulsified lemon oils.

History and origin claims

The liqueur's precise origins are contested between the communities of Amalfi, Sorrento and Capri, each with local legends involving fishermen, monks or innkeepers. Industrial production began in the early 20th century; the "Limoncello" trademark was registered in 1988 by Massimo Canale, whose family business on Capri helped popularise the drink internationally. Homemade versions remain widespread in southern Italian households, and variants include crema di limoncello (made with milk or cream) and analogues from other citrus, such as arancello (orange).

Serving

Limoncello is served ice-cold, often from the freezer, in small chilled ceramic or glass cups. Beyond drinking, it is used in cocktails, desserts and patisserie, including flavouring for cakes and gelato.
