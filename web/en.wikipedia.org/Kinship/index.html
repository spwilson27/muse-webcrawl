Kinship

Kinship
FieldAnthropology, sociology
Key conceptsDescent, marriage, affinity, fictive kin
Notable scholarsLewis H. Morgan, Claude Lévi-Strauss, David Schneider

Kinship is a system of social relationships based on descent and marriage. In anthropology, kinship is one of the principal organising structures of human societies, determining inheritance, residence, political allegiance and obligations of care.

Types of kin

Relatives by birth are consanguines (blood kin), while relatives by marriage are affines. Many societies also recognise fictive kinship — relationships such as godparenthood or blood brotherhood that mimic kin obligations without birth or marriage. Kin groups include nuclear families, extended families, lineages, clans and moieties.

Descent systems

Descent may be traced patrilineally (through the father), matrilineally (through the mother), bilaterally (through both parents), or through dual or ambilineal systems. Lewis Henry Morgan's 19th-century classification of kinship terminologies — including Hawaiian, Iroquois, Crow, Omaha, Eskimo and Sudanese systems — remains a foundation of the field.

Marriage and alliance

Claude Lévi-Strauss argued in The Elementary Structures of Kinship (1949) that marriage rules, especially the incest taboo and exogamy, create alliances between groups. Marriage may be monogamous or polygamous, and residence after marriage may be patrilocal, matrilocal or neolocal.

Modern approaches

Later anthropologists such as David Schneider criticised the assumption that kinship is everywhere grounded in biology, showing how kinship is culturally constructed. Contemporary studies cover adoption, assisted reproduction, same-sex families and chosen families.
