Hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis

The HPT axis: TRH stimulates TSH release, which drives thyroid hormone production; T3 and T4 feed back negatively.

The hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis (HPT axis, also known as the thyroid homeostasis) is part of the neuroendocrine system responsible for the regulation of metabolism and also responds to stress. As its name suggests, it depends upon the hypothalamus, the pituitary gland, and the thyroid gland.

Physiology

The hypothalamus secretes thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH), which stimulates the anterior pituitary to release thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH, thyrotropin). TSH in turn acts on the thyroid gland to promote the synthesis and release of the thyroid hormones thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3). Circulating T3 and T4 exert negative feedback on both the pituitary and the hypothalamus, holding hormone levels within a narrow individual range (a "set point"). Within target tissues, deiodinase enzymes convert T4 to the more active T3 or to inactive reverse T3.

Function

Thyroid hormones control basal metabolic rate, thermogenesis, growth and development (especially of the brain), and they modulate cardiovascular, neurological, and reproductive function. The axis adapts during illness (non-thyroidal illness syndrome), pregnancy, and changes in nutrition and temperature.

Clinical relevance

Measuring TSH is the standard screening test for thyroid dysfunction: it rises in primary hypothyroidism and is suppressed in primary hyperthyroidism. Central hypothyroidism arises when the pituitary or hypothalamus fails, producing inappropriately low TSH with low T4. Resistance to thyroid hormone and TSH-secreting pituitary adenomas are rare causes of inappropriate TSH secretion.
