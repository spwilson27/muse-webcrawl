Phyllotaxis

Phyllotaxis or phyllotaxy is the arrangement of leaves on a plant stem. Phyllotactic spirals form a distinctive class of patterns in nature, visible in the arrangement of seeds in a sunflower head, the scales of a pine cone, and the florets of a cauliflower. The basic arrangements are alternate, opposite, and whorled.

Patterns

In alternate (spiral) phyllotaxis, successive leaves emerge at a fixed divergence angle, very often approaching the golden angle of about 137.5°. The numbers of visible spirals (parastichies) in seed heads are typically consecutive Fibonacci numbers, a consequence of efficient packing. Opposite phyllotaxis places two leaves per node, with successive pairs at right angles (decussate) or in a single plane (distichous); whorled phyllotaxis places three or more leaves per node.

Mechanism

The pattern is established at the shoot apical meristem. The prevailing model involves the hormone auxin: new leaf primordia form at positions of auxin maxima, and existing primordia deplete or inhibit auxin in their vicinity, so each new leaf arises as far as possible from its predecessors — a mechanism that naturally generates Fibonacci spirals. Physical models, from Hofmeister's rule to Douady and Couder's experiments with ferrofluid drops, reproduce these patterns from packing principles alone.
