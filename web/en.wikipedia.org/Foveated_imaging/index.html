Foveated imaging

Foveated imaging is an image processing technique in which the resolution of an image, or the computational effort devoted to it, varies across the field of view — sharpest at a central fixation point and decreasing toward the periphery. It is modelled on the human visual system, in which the fovea centralis of the retina provides high-acuity vision over only a few degrees of visual angle, while the periphery is sensed at much lower resolution. Foveated methods trade off uniform detail for large savings in bandwidth, storage, and computation.

Applications

The best-known application is foveated rendering in virtual and augmented reality: headsets with eye tracking render only the small region the user is looking at in full resolution, shading the periphery at reduced rates. This can cut rendering costs dramatically and is used in headsets such as PlayStation VR2 and Apple Vision Pro; simpler "fixed foveated" rendering, which assumes central gaze, was used in earlier VR hardware. Foveated techniques also appear in image and video compression, progressive image transmission (in which a sharp region of interest arrives first), remote sensing, and machine vision, where a sensor or algorithm concentrates its resolution on a target.

Methods

Foveated representations include image pyramids and multi-resolution decompositions, log-polar (retinal) mappings that convert the resolution fall-off into a uniform grid, and variable-resolution sensors. In rendering, implementations grade shading rates across tiles, reduce peripheral pixel density geometrically, or mask peripheral detail with blur and reduced contrast, relying on the visual system's insensitivity to peripheral artefacts — particularly during saccades, when vision is briefly suppressed.
