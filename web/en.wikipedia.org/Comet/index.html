Comet

A comet is an icy, small Solar System body that warms and begins to release gases when passing close to the Sun, a process called outgassing. This produces an extended, gravitationally unbound atmosphere or coma surrounding the nucleus, and sometimes a tail of gas and dust that can extend for millions of kilometres. Comets are composed of rock, dust, water ice, and frozen gases such as carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, methane, and ammonia — often described as "dirty snowballs".

Orbit and origin

Short-period comets originate in the Kuiper belt beyond Neptune's orbit and typically have orbital periods of less than 200 years; Halley's Comet, with a period of about 76 years, is the most famous. Long-period comets come from the spherical Oort cloud at the edge of the Solar System and may take millions of years to orbit the Sun. There are over 4,600 known comets, a small fraction of the total population.

Physical characteristics

The solid nucleus of a comet is usually only a few kilometres across. As a comet approaches the inner Solar System, solar radiation vaporises its ices, forming the glowing coma, which can grow larger than a planet, and two tails: a curved dust tail and a straight ion (plasma) tail, both pushed away from the Sun by radiation pressure and the solar wind. Repeated passages gradually deplete a comet's volatiles, leaving an inert rocky or rubble body resembling an asteroid.

History and exploration

Comets were long regarded as omens; Halley's prediction of his comet's 1758 return was a triumph of Newtonian mechanics. Spacecraft have visited several comets: Giotto flew by Halley in 1986, Deep Impact struck Tempel 1 in 2005, Stardust returned samples from Wild 2, and ESA's Rosetta orbited comet 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko and landed the Philae probe on it in 2014. Comets may have delivered water and organic compounds to the early Earth.
