Anechoic tile

Anechoic tiles are rubber or synthetic polymer tiles fitted to the outer hulls of submarines (and occasionally surface vessels) to reduce detection by active sonar and to damp the vessel's own radiated noise. By absorbing incoming sonar pulses rather than reflecting them, and by muffling machinery vibrations, the tiles form an early practical technology of acoustic stealth.

History

The first such coating, codenamed Alberich, was developed by the German Kriegsmarine and applied to U-boats late in the Second World War, notably U-480. The perforated rubber sheets proved difficult to keep bonded and offered limited benefit with wartime sonar frequencies. The Soviet Union took up the idea systematically in the 1960s and 1970s, fitting thick tiles to classes such as the Victor and Akula submarines, and Western navies followed with their own coatings on attack and ballistic-missile submarines.

Design

Tiles typically measure about a metre square and several centimetres thick. Their internal cavities—resonant air voids of carefully graded size&mdashconvert the pressure of an incoming sonar wave into minute motions of the rubber, dissipating acoustic energy as heat, much like the wedges of an anechoic chamber absorb sound in air. Cavity dimensions are tuned to the frequencies of expected threat sonars, and modern tiles also incorporate sound-damping layers that isolate hull-borne machinery noise.

Limitations

Tiles add weight and drag, can detach at speed or in cold water—photographs of submarines in dock often show missing tiles—and are less effective against low-frequency active sonar, whose long wavelengths pass largely unabsorbed. They remain, nonetheless, a standard feature of modern submarine design.
