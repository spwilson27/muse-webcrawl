Arithmetic geometry is a branch of mathematics that combines algebraic geometry and number theory. Roughly, it studies solutions of polynomial equations in integers and rational numbers using the geometric methods of modern algebraic geometry — that is, it studies Diophantine equations by viewing them as varieties defined over number fields, rings of integers, or finite fields.

Overview

Classical questions include which integers can be represented by a given quadratic form, the structure of rational points on curves, and the distribution of prime numbers in polynomial values. The modern era began with the theory of elliptic curves and was transformed by Alexander Grothendieck's scheme theory, which allowed number-theoretic questions to be phrased geometrically — for example, treating Spec Z as a kind of curve.

Major results

  • Mordell conjecture / Faltings's theorem (1983): a curve of genus greater than 1 over a number field has only finitely many rational points.
  • Modularity theorem and the proof of Fermat's Last Theorem by Andrew Wiles (1994–95),linking elliptic curves over Q to modular forms.
  • Weil conjectures (proved by Deligne, 1974): analogues of the Riemann hypothesis for varieties over finite fields, establishing deep connections between point counts and topology.
  • Hasse principle and its failures, the Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture, and the Langlands programme, which continues to drive the field.
