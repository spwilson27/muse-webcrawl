Evolutionary psychology

Evolutionary psychology is a theoretical approach in psychology that examines psychological structure from a modern evolutionary perspective. It seeks to identify human psychological adaptations with regard to the ancestral problems they evolved to solve. In this framework, psychological traits and mechanisms are either functional products of natural and sexual selection or non-functional byproducts of other adaptive traits.

Principles

Evolutionary psychologists hold that the brain is composed of many specialized mechanisms, sometimes called modules, each shaped by selection to solve recurrent problems in the environment of evolutionary adaptedness — the hunter-gatherer conditions under which most human psychological adaptations are thought to have evolved. Adaptationist thinking informs research on mating, parenting, kinship, status, aggression, fear, cooperation, and food preferences, among many other topics.

Key figures in the field's founding include Leda Cosmides and John Tooby, who established the Center for Evolutionary Psychology at the University of California, Santa Barbara, and popularizers such as Steven Pinker, whose book How the Mind Works (1997) brought the approach to a wide audience. David Buss's research program on mating strategies is among the most influential empirical strands.

Criticism

Critics argue that some evolutionary-psychological hypotheses are difficult to test, that claims about ancestral environments are speculative, and that the field sometimes produces "just-so stories." Others dispute the assumption of massive modularity or contend that culture and development are underweighted. Defenders reply that the approach generates testable predictions and unifies psychology with the rest of biology.
