Friedrich Albert Moritz Schlick (14 April 1882 – 22 June 1936) was a German philosopher, physicist, and the founding father of logical positivism and the Vienna Circle. He studied physics under Max Planck at the University of Berlin, writing his dissertation on the reflection of light in an inhomogeneous medium.

Career and the Vienna Circle

After posts at Rostock and Kiel, Schlick in 1922 took the chair in the philosophy of the inductive sciences at the University of Vienna, once held by Ernst Mach and Ludwig Boltzmann. Around him gathered the discussion group known as the Vienna Circle — including Rudolf Carnap, Otto Neurath, Kurt Gödel, and Herbert Feigl — which developed logical empiricism and championed the verifiability principle of meaning. Schlick's works include General Theory of Knowledge (1918), Problems of Ethics (1930), and numerous essays on relativity, causality, and meaning.

Death

On 22 June 1936, Schlick was shot and killed on the steps of the University of Vienna by Hans Nelböck, a mentally disturbed former student. The murder, and the relatively lenient treatment of the killer amid Austria's increasingly illiberal political climate, effectively ended the Vienna Circle's activity in Vienna.
