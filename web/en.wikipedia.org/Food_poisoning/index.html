Foodborne illness, commonly called food poisoning, is any illness resulting from the contamination of food by pathogenic bacteria, viruses, parasites, or by natural or chemical toxins. Symptoms most often involve the gastrointestinal tract — nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps and diarrhoea — but some agents cause neurological, hepatic or systemic disease. The World Health Organization estimates that unsafe food causes some 600 million illnesses and 420,000 deaths globally each year.

Causes

In high-income countries the single most common agent is norovirus, followed by the bacteria Campylobacter and Salmonella. Some illnesses are intoxications rather than infections: Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus cereus produce heat-stable toxins in food, causing rapid-onset vomiting, while Clostridium botulinum toxin causes botulism, a rare but life-threatening paralysis. Listeria monocytogenes is especially dangerous in pregnancy and immunosuppression; Shiga-toxin-producing E. coli can cause haemolytic uraemic syndrome in children. Natural toxins include aflatoxins in mouldy grains and tetrodotoxin in pufferfish.

Prevention

The WHO's "five keys to safer food" are: keep clean; separate raw and cooked food; cook thoroughly; keep food at safe temperatures; and use safe water and raw materials. Food-safety management systems such as HACCP, pasteurisation, refrigeration and irradiation, together with outbreak surveillance and traceback, have greatly reduced (but not eliminated) foodborne disease. Most treatment is supportive — oral rehydration — with antibiotics reserved for specific severe infections.
