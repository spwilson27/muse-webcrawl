Bézout's identity

In mathematics, Bézout's identity (also called Bézout's lemma), named after Étienne Bézout, is a theorem in elementary number theory: let a and b be integers with greatest common divisor d. Then there exist integers x and y such that ax + by = d. Moreover, the integers of the form az + bt are exactly the multiples of d.

The integers x and y are called Bézout coefficients for (a, b); they are not unique. A pair of Bézout coefficients can be computed by the extended Euclidean algorithm, and if both a and b are nonzero, there is a unique pair with |x| ≤ |b/d| and |y| ≤ |a/d|.

Example

The greatest common divisor of 12 and 42 is 6, and we have (−3) × 12 + 1 × 42 = 6, so the Bézout coefficients are −3 and 1.

History

The result for integers was stated by Claude Gaspard Bachet de Méziriac in 1624, well before Bézout. Bézout's contribution (1779) was to generalise the identity to polynomials: for polynomials in one variable over a field, there exist polynomials playing the role of the coefficients whose combination equals the polynomial greatest common divisor; compare the Bézout matrix used in his elimination theory.

Generalisation

The identity generalises to any principal ideal domain: in such a ring, the ideal generated by a and b is the principal ideal generated by their greatest common divisor. Rings in which such an identity holds are called Bézout domains.
