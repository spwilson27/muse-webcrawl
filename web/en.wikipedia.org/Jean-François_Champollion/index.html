Jean-François Champollion (23 December 1790 – 4 March 1832), also known as Champollion le jeune ("the younger"), was a French scholar, philologist, and orientalist, known primarily as the decipherer of Egyptian hieroglyphs and a founding figure in the field of Egyptology. His announcement in 1822 that he had cracked the hieroglyphic script — building on the parallel texts of the Rosetta Stone and on his deep knowledge of Coptic — opened three thousand years of ancient Egyptian history to scholarship.

Early life and education

Champollion was born in Figeac in southwestern France, the youngest child of a bookseller. A precocious linguist largely guided by his elder brother Jacques-Joseph (Champollion-Figeac), he learned Latin, Greek, Hebrew, Arabic, Syriac, and Coptic as a schoolboy in Grenoble, and presented a paper to the Grenoble Academy at sixteen. In Paris he studied under Silvestre de Sacy and others, immersing himself in Coptic, which he correctly believed preserved the language of the pharaohs.

Decipherment

The Rosetta Stone, discovered by French soldiers in Egypt in 1799 and ceded to Britain after the French surrender in Egypt, bears the same decree in hieroglyphic, Demotic, and Greek scripts. Building on the work of predecessors including Thomas Young — who had identified some phonetic values and the names in cartouches — Champollion made the decisive breakthrough in 1822: comparing the cartouches of Ptolemy and Cleopatra on the Rosetta Stone and the Philae obelisk, he showed that hieroglyphic writing was a mixed system combining phonetic signs, logograms, and determinatives. His Lettre à M. Dacier (September 1822) announced the phonetic alphabet of hieroglyphs; according to a famous anecdote, he burst into his brother's office crying "Je tiens mon affaire!" ("I've got it!") and collapsed. His fuller Précis du système hiéroglyphique followed in 1824, and he confirmed the system by reading the names of pre-Greek pharaohs such as Ramesses and Thutmose, proving the script was used for native Egyptian across millennia.

Later career

In 1826 Champollion became the first curator of the Louvre's new Egyptian collection. From 1828 to 1830 he led a Franco-Tuscan expedition to Egypt and Nubia with Ippolito Rosellini, copying inscriptions at sites along the Nile. In 1831 he was appointed to the first chair of Egyptology at the Collège de France. Exhausted by the Egyptian journey, he died of a stroke in Paris in 1832, aged only 41. His Grammaire égyptienne and Egyptian dictionary were published posthumously by his brother.

Legacy

Champollion's decipherment founded modern Egyptology, transforming the study of ancient Egypt from speculation into a text-based historical discipline. The Champollion Museum in his birthplace Figeac commemorates his life; his work also definitively settled the long-standing assumption that hieroglyphs were purely symbolic, though his rivalry with Thomas Young and questions of priority remain debated by historians.
