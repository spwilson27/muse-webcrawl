Social anxiety is the fear or discomfort experienced in social situations due to concern about being judged, embarrassed, or humiliated by others. It exists on a spectrum: at typical levels it is a common, even adaptive, emotion, but when it becomes intense, persistent, and impairing it constitutes social anxiety disorder (also called social phobia), one of the most common anxiety disorders.

Features

People with pronounced social anxiety may dread public speaking, meeting strangers, eating or writing in front of others, or using public restrooms. Anticipatory worry can begin days or weeks before an event, and afterwards sufferers often ruminate over perceived failures ("post-event processing"). Physical symptoms include blushing, sweating, trembling, nausea, and a racing heart, driven by activation of the sympathetic nervous system and heightened amygdala reactivity to social threat cues.

Causes

The condition arises from a combination of genetic temperament (notably behavioural inhibition in childhood), brain circuitry involved in fear and self-referential processing, and environmental factors such as bullying, overprotective parenting, or humiliating experiences. Cognitive models emphasise distorted self-imagery and overestimation of how negatively others evaluate one's performance.

Treatment

Cognitive behavioural therapy—particularly exposure to feared situations combined with restructuring of threat appraisals—is a first-line treatment. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are the most commonly used medications. This article concerns the psychological phenomenon and disorder; for the academic study of group behaviour and social influence, see social psychology.
