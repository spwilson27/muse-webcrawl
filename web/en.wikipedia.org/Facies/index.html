Facies

In geology, a facies (pronounced FAY-sheez; plural also facies) is a body of rock with specified characteristics, which can be any observable attribute of rocks (such as their overall appearance, composition, or condition of formation), and the changes that may occur in those attributes over a geographic area. A facies is distinct from a rock formation in that a formation is defined by lithology alone, whereas a facies may reflect depositional environment, fossil content, or metamorphic grade.

History

The term was introduced by the Swiss geologist Amanz Gressly in 1838–1841 in his studies of the Jura Mountains, where he recognised that lateral changes in rock type corresponded to different environments of deposition, analogous to the habitats of modern organisms. The concept was further developed by Johannes Walther, whose law of facies succession (1894) states that the vertical succession of facies reflects lateral environments that once lay side by side, provided there is no break in deposition.

Types of facies

Sedimentary facies

Sedimentary facies are distinguished by lithology (lithofacies) or by fossil content (biofacies). Examples include fluvial (river), deltaic, shallow-marine, reef, and deep-marine turbidite facies. Facies associations and sequences are the basis of sequence stratigraphy, widely used in petroleum exploration to predict reservoir, seal, and source rocks.

Metamorphic facies

In metamorphic petrology, a metamorphic facies is a set of mineral assemblages that form under similar pressures and temperatures. The scheme, developed by Pentti Eskola from 1915, includes the zeolite, prehnite–pumpellyite, greenschist, amphibolite, granulite, blueschist, and eclogite facies, among others, and is used to infer the conditions of metamorphism and tectonic settings.
