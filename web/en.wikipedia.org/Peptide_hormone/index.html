Peptide hormone

Peptide hormones are hormones whose molecules are peptides — short chains of amino acids — secreted by endocrine glands and transported by the blood to target tissues. Examples include insulin, glucagon, growth hormone, oxytocin, and antidiuretic hormone (vasopressin).

Mechanism of action

Because peptide hormones are water-soluble and cannot cross the cell membrane, they act by binding to receptors on the cell surface, triggering intracellular signalling cascades, often involving second messengers such as cyclic AMP or calcium ions. Their effects are usually rapid but relatively short-lived.

Synthesis

They are synthesized as larger precursor proteins (preprohormones), which are cleaved in the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus and stored in secretory vesicles until release. Unlike steroid hormones, they cannot be taken orally without being digested, which is why insulin is administered by injection or inhalation.
