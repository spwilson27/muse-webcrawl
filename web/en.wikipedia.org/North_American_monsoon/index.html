North American monsoon

The North American monsoon, also known as the Southwest monsoon or Arizona monsoon, is a pattern of pronounced seasonal change in wind direction and a corresponding increase in rainfall affecting large parts of northwestern Mexico and the southwestern United States, typically from July through September.

[Image placeholder: monsoon thunderstorm building over the Arizona desert]

Mechanism

During summer, intense solar heating of the elevated terrain of the Mexican Plateau and the US Southwest creates a thermal low, while an upper-level anticyclone (the "Four Corners high") settles over the region. The circulation draws moist air northward from the Gulf of California and the tropical Pacific, with additional moisture from the Gulf of Mexico. The result is frequent, often intense afternoon and evening thunderstorms, especially over mountain ranges.

Effects

The monsoon delivers a large fraction of the annual rainfall of northwestern Mexico and a substantial share for Arizona and New Mexico. It brings relief from early-summer heat but also hazards: flash flooding in arroyos and urban areas, dust storms (haboobs), lightning-caused wildfires early in the season, and debris flows. Monsoon moisture also supports the distinctive ecology of the Sonoran Desert, which has two rainy seasons and is the lushest of the North American deserts.

Variability

The onset date varies from year to year; in Tucson the monsoon was historically defined by dew-point criteria and is now officially reckoned from 15 June to 30 September. Strength varies with sea-surface temperatures and large-scale patterns such as ENSO, and researchers study how climate change is affecting monsoon rainfall intensity and timing.
