Qocho (also Qara-Khoja, Gaochang, and in Chinese sources Karakhoja), or the Kingdom of Qocho, was a Uyghur Buddhist kingdom in the Turpan Depression of present-day Xinjiang, China. It was founded after the collapse of the Uyghur Khaganate in 840, when Uyghur groups migrated west from Mongolia; by 843–856 they had established a state centred on the oasis city of Qocho (Chinese Gaochang), with a summer capital at Beshbalik (Beiting).

History

The kingdom flourished as a Silk Road trading state, cultivating cotton, grain and grapes in irrigated oasis agriculture. Its rulers, styled Iduq-qut, maintained relations with Tang and Song China and later with the Khitan Liao and the Tangut Western Xia. In 1209 the Iduq-qut Barchuk submitted voluntarily to Genghis Khan, and Qocho became a favoured vassal within the Mongol Empire; under the Yuan dynasty the Uyghurs of Qocho supplied many administrators and scholars. The kingdom gradually declined amid wars between the Chagatai Khanate and Yuan, and was Islamised over the 14th–15th centuries.

Culture

Qocho was a major centre of Buddhism — the nearby Bezeklik grottoes preserve celebrated wall paintings — alongside Manichaeism and Nestorian Christianity. Its scribes adapted the Sogdian script into the Old Uyghur alphabet and translated a vast literature from Sanskrit, Tocharian, Tibetan and Chinese, making Qocho one of the great translation cultures of medieval Central Asia. German expeditions to Turfan in the early 20th century recovered thousands of manuscripts and paintings from its ruins.
