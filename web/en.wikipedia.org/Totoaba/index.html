The totoaba (Totoaba macdonaldi) is a large marine fish endemic to the Gulf of California in Mexico. It is the largest member of the drum family, Sciaenidae, growing up to about 2 metres (6.6 ft) long and 100 kilograms (220 lb) in weight, and can live for more than 20 years.

Biology

Totoaba migrate to the shallow waters of the upper Gulf of California and the Colorado River Delta to spawn in spring. Juveniles spend their first years in the delta's brackish nursery habitat, making the species heavily dependent on freshwater inflow from the Colorado River.

Threats and conservation

The totoaba was heavily overfished from the early 20th century, first for its meat and later for its swim bladder, which commands extremely high prices in China as a luxury food and supposed medicinal product. Fishing was banned in Mexico in 1975 and the species is listed under CITES Appendix I, but illegal gillnet fishing for swim bladders continues and also threatens the critically endangered vaquita porpoise, which drowns as bycatch in the same nets. Captive breeding and aquaculture programs in Mexico have released juveniles into the wild.

Cultural history

Early fisheries exported totoaba swim bladders to Chinese communities in California, a trade that later expanded into a transnational market. The fishery's boomtown era around San Felipe and the Colorado Delta is a well-known episode in the environmental history of the Gulf.
