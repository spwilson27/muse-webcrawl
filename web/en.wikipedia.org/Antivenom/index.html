Antivenom

TypeAntibody-based antitoxin (serum)
First developed1890s, Albert Calmette
AdministrationIntravenous (usually)
Used forSnakebite, spider bite, scorpion sting envenomation

Antivenom, also known as antivenin or antivenene, is a medication made from antibodies created in the blood of an animal — typically a horse or sheep — that has been immunised with small, increasing doses of a specific venom. It is used to treat potentially fatal envenomations from snakebites, and in some cases from the bites or stings of spiders, scorpions, ticks, and marine animals such as box jellyfish and stonefish. Antivenoms are on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

History

The French physician Albert Calmette, working in Saigon in the early 1890s, developed the first effective snake antivenom serum after a flood forced cobras into a village near his institute, and by 1895 his serum was being produced by the Pasteur Institute. Vital Brazil in São Paulo pioneered work on South American species and showed that antivenoms needed to be species-specific. In Australia, researchers at the Commonwealth Serum Laboratories developed antivenoms for the country's highly venomous snakes, funnel-web spiders, and box jellyfish through the twentieth century.

Production

Venom is collected by "milking" venomous animals, freeze-dried, and injected in escalating doses into a host animal, whose immune system produces polyclonal antibodies. The animal's blood is drawn, and the antibody fraction — whole IgG or cleaved fragments such as F(ab')2 or Fab — is purified and formulated as a liquid or freeze-dried product. A monovalent antivenom neutralises one species; polyvalent products cover several related species.

Medical use

Antivenom is given intravenously as soon as possible after significant envenomation, though it can remain useful for some days. It neutralises circulating venom but cannot reverse damage already done to tissues or nerves. Snakebite envenoming kills an estimated 81,000 to 138,000 people a year and maims many more, overwhelmingly in rural sub-Saharan Africa, South Asia, and Latin America; the WHO classified it as a neglected tropical disease in 2017 and launched a strategy to halve deaths and disability by 2030.

Adverse effects and availability

Because antivenoms are foreign animal proteins, they can provoke allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis (managed with adrenaline) and delayed serum sickness. Shortages are chronic in parts of Africa, where production has declined for economic reasons; cost, cold-chain requirements, and species mismatch between imported products and local snakes remain major problems.
