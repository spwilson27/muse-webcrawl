Tasting menu

A tasting menu is a menu in a restaurant offering small portions of several dishes as a single meal. The French name for a tasting menu is menu dégustation. Some restaurants and chefs specialize in tasting menus, while in other cases it is a special or a chef's menu offered in addition to the regular menu. Tasting menus may be offered to present a chef's philosophy or showcase seasonal ingredients, and typically require the participation of the whole table.

Format

A tasting menu generally consists of a sequence of courses — often five to twelve or more — progressing from lighter to richer dishes. Portions are deliberately small so that diners can experience a wide range of preparations, ingredients, and techniques in one sitting. Many restaurants offer optional wine pairings selected by a sommelier to accompany each course. In Japanese cuisine, the kaiseki tradition and the chef-led omakase format are close relatives of the tasting menu.

History

The modern tasting menu rose to prominence with the nouvelle cuisine movement in France in the 1970s and was popularized internationally by restaurants such as El Bulli in Spain, The French Laundry in California, and Noma in Copenhagen. It became a hallmark of fine dining and of the Michelin Guide starred restaurant world.

Criticism

Tasting menus have been criticized for their length, expense, and rigidity, with some diners objecting to the lack of choice and the hours required to complete a meal. Some chefs have responded by offering shorter or more flexible formats, while others defend the format as the purest expression of a kitchen's work.
