Aquatic respiration

Aquatic respiration is the process whereby an aquatic organism exchanges respiratory gases with water, obtaining oxygen from oxygen dissolved in water and excreting carbon dioxide. Some species supplement or replace this with aerial respiration, breathing air at the surface. Because water contains far less dissolved oxygen per unit volume than air — and the amount decreases as temperature and salinity rise — aquatic animals have evolved diverse, efficient gas-exchange structures.

Mechanisms

Small organisms such as many invertebrate larvae exchange gases directly across the body surface by diffusion. Larger animals rely on specialized organs:

  • Gills — feathery or lamellar structures with a large surface area and a rich blood supply. In bony fish, water flows over the gills in the opposite direction to blood flow (countercurrent exchange), maximizing oxygen uptake. Fish ventilate their gills by buccal pumping or ram ventilation.
  • Spiracles and plastrons — many aquatic insects carry a film of air (a plastron) or snorkel-like tubes to the surface.
  • Lungs and air-breathing organs — lungfish, some catfish and labyrinth fish, and aquatic mammals and reptiles breathe air and must surface periodically.
  • Skin breathing — amphibians such as the hellbender and the Lake Titicaca frog rely heavily on cutaneous respiration; some sea snakes also absorb oxygen through the skin.

Challenges

Extracting oxygen from water is energetically costly: water is dense and viscous, so ventilation requires effort, and oxygen availability varies with temperature, depth, and pollution. Hypoxic (low-oxygen) conditions can cause mass die-offs of fish. Some animals, such as crucian carp and certain turtles, tolerate extended anoxia during winter.
