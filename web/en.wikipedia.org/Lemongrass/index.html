Lemongrass (Cymbopogon) is grown for its lemon-scented stalks and oils.

Lemongrass is the common name for plants in the genus Cymbopogon, a group of about 55 species of perennial grasses in the family Poaceae, native to tropical and subtropical regions of Asia, Africa and Australia. The plants are known for the strong lemon-like scent of their leaves and stalks, produced by the compound citral.

Species and uses

Cymbopogon citratus (West Indian lemongrass) is the species most widely used in cooking, especially in Thai, Vietnamese, Cambodian and Indonesian cuisines, where the crushed stalks flavour soups such as tom yum, curries, marinades and teas. Cymbopogon flexuosus (East Indian lemongrass) is grown mainly for its essential oil.

The related species C. nardus and C. winterianus are the sources of citronella oil, used in insect repellents, soaps and candles. Lemongrass oil is also used in perfumery and in the commercial production of citral, a starting material for vitamin A and ionone fragrances.

Cultivation

Lemongrass grows in dense clumps up to about 2 metres tall in warm climates. It is propagated by division of the root clumps and is widely cultivated in India, Sri Lanka, Thailand, Guatemala and Brazil. The plants are frost-sensitive and are often grown as annuals or container plants in temperate regions.

Culinary use

Only the tender lower portion of the stalk is used in cooking; the tough outer leaves are removed and the stalk is bruised to release its aroma before being added to dishes or steeped for herbal tea. Dried and powdered lemongrass is also sold, though it loses much of its flavour.

Traditional medicine

Lemongrass tea is used in folk medicine in many countries as a mild sedative and digestive aid. Some laboratory studies have examined the antimicrobial and antioxidant properties of its oil, but clinical evidence for most traditional uses remains limited.
