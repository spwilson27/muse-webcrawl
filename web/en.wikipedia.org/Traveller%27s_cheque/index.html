Traveller's cheque

A traveller's cheque is a medium of exchange used by travellers in place of hard currency. It can be exchanged for local currency at banks and agencies, or spent directly at establishments that accept them. Traveller's cheques are pre-printed, fixed-amount cheques that require a signature at purchase and a countersignature at use, allowing the cheque to be replaced if lost or stolen.

History

The modern traveller's cheque was developed by American Express in 1891 (patented by Marcellus Fleming Berry); Thomas Cook had issued a similar circular note in 1874. They were the principal way tourists carried money safely through most of the 20th century and were issued in major currencies in set denominations.

Decline

From the 1990s, international acceptance collapsed as ATM networks, credit and debit cards, and prepaid travel money cards spread; merchants increasingly refused cheques because of fraud and processing costs. American Express and other issuers still honour outstanding cheques, which do not expire.

Use

Cheques were purchased from banks for face value plus a small commission, signed immediately, and countersigned in the presence of the payee, whose verification of the two signatures provided security.
