Religious denomination

A religious denomination is a subgroup within a religion that operates under a common name, tradition and identity. The term is most often used for branches of Christianity — such as Baptists, Methodists, Lutherans and Presbyterians — but is also applied to the major branches of other religions, including the schools of Judaism, the Sunni and Shia divisions of Islam, and the Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana traditions of Buddhism.

Denominationalism

In sociology of religion, a denomination is distinguished from a sect and from a church: denominations are established, respectable bodies that accept the legitimacy of other faiths in a pluralistic society, in contrast to sects, which maintain tension with their surroundings, and state churches, which claim a monopoly in a territory. The classic church–sect typology of Max Weber and Ernst Troeltsch underpins these distinctions.

Within Christianity, denominations arise through schisms over doctrine, practice or authority — the East–West Schism of 1054 and the Reformation of the 16th century being the largest. Ecumenical movements and bodies such as the World Council of Churches seek cooperation and, in some cases, reunion among them, while "non-denominational" churches distance themselves from denominational structures altogether.
