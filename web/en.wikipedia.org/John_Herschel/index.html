John Herschel

Born: 7 March 1792, Slough, England
Died: 11 May 1871 (aged 79), Collingwood, Kent
Fields: Astronomy, mathematics, photography, chemistry
Known for: Cataloguing the southern sky; photographic terms "negative" and "positive"; inventing the cyanotype process

Sir John Frederick William Herschel, 1st Baronet (7 March 1792 – 11 May 1871) was an English polymath active as a mathematician, astronomer, chemist, inventor and experimental photographer. He was the son of astronomer William Herschel.

Astronomy

Herschel extended his father's surveys of double stars and nebulae in the northern sky, then from 1834 to 1838 worked at Feldhausen at the Cape of Good Hope, systematically cataloguing the southern heavens. His Results of Astronomical Observations made during the years 1834–8 at the Cape of Good Hope (1847) and his General Catalogue of Nebulae and Clusters (1864) laid foundations later used in the New General Catalogue (NGC).

Photography and chemistry

Herschel discovered that sodium thiosulfate ("hypo") dissolves silver halides, making it the essential photographic fixer, and invented the cyanotype blueprint process in 1842. He coined the terms "photography" (or popularised it), "negative" and "positive", and made the first photograph on glass in 1839.

Other work

He named seven moons of Saturn and four moons of Uranus, wrote the influential Preliminary Discourse on the Study of Natural Philosophy (1831), and served as Master of the Mint. He is buried in Westminster Abbey near Isaac Newton.
