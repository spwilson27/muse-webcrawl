Tripartite language
Also: ergative–absolutive language
A type of morphosyntactic alignment

A tripartite language, also called an ergative–absolutive language in some analyses, is a language that treats the subject of an intransitive verb, the subject of a transitive verb, and the object of a transitive verb each in different ways in its case system or agreement.

Alignment

Linguists denote the three core arguments as S (intransitive subject), A (transitive agent) and O (transitive patient/object). Nominative–accusative languages group S with A; ergative–absolutive languages group S with O; tripartite languages give all three distinct marking. Fully tripartite case systems are rare; examples include Nez Perce, in which nouns take distinct ergative, accusative and absolutive forms, and some Australian Aboriginal languages in restricted contexts.
