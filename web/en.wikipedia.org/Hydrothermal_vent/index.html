Hydrothermal vent

Hydrothermal vent
Location: Mid-ocean ridges and volcanic arcs, deep sea
Fluid temperature: Up to 400 °C or more
Types: Black smokers, white smokers, diffuse flow
Discovered: 1977 (Galápagos Rift, submersible Alvin)

A hydrothermal vent is a fissure on the seabed from which geothermally heated water discharges. Seawater penetrates cracks in oceanic crust, is heated by magma, dissolves minerals, and rises buoyantly to exit at temperatures that can exceed 400 °C. The surrounding ocean is near 2 °C, and the mineral-rich fluid supports unique ecosystems independent of sunlight.

Discovery

Vents were first observed in 1977 by scientists in the submersible Alvin exploring the Galápagos Rift, who found dense communities of giant tube worms, clams and crabs around warm shimmering water. Black smoker chimneys were discovered in 1979 on the East Pacific Rise.

Types and chemistry

Black smokers emit particle-laden fluids rich in iron sulfides that precipitate as chimney structures; white smokers discharge lighter-coloured minerals such as barium, calcium and silicon. Diffuse flows are lower-temperature, diluted emissions. Alkaline Lost City-type vents, driven by serpentinisation, differ chemically and are studied as possible analogues for the origin of life.

Biology

Vent ecosystems rest on chemosynthesis: bacteria and archaea oxidise hydrogen sulfide and other chemicals, and many animals host symbiotic microbes. Giant tube worms (Riftia pachyptila) grow extremely fast without a mouth or gut. More than 500 species have been described from vents, most endemic. Fields are ephemeral on geological timescales, and larvae disperse between sites.

Human interest

Vent fields host massive seafloor sulfide deposits containing copper, zinc and gold, attracting deep-sea mining interest and conservation concern.
