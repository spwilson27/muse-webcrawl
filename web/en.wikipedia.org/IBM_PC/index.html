IBM Personal Computer

The IBM Personal Computer (model 5150, commonly known as the IBM PC) is the first microcomputer released in the IBM PC model line and the basis for the IBM PC compatible de facto standard. Released on August 12, 1981, it was created by a team of engineers and designers at IBM's Entry Systems Division in Boca Raton, Florida, directed by Don Estridge.

The machine was based on the Intel 8088 microprocessor running at 4.77 MHz, came with 16 or 64 KB of memory expandable to 256 KB, and shipped with PC DOS, an operating system supplied by Microsoft. It used a five-slot expansion bus, the design of which IBM made widely available, and its modular, open architecture allowed third-party manufacturers to produce peripherals and software rapidly.

Development

IBM, the dominant mainframe computer company, had largely ignored the burgeoning personal computer market of the late 1970s. In 1980 a small team led initially by William C. Lowe proposed building a personal computer quickly, within about a year, by using off-the-shelf components and outside suppliers wherever possible — a radical departure from IBM's usual vertically integrated development. Philip "Don" Estridge took over leadership of the project, code-named "Project Chess"; its product was codenamed "Acorn".

Because IBM's own software organisation could not deliver an operating system in the available time, the team licensed 86-DOS from Seattle Computer Products, which Microsoft acquired and adapted as PC DOS (also marketed separately as MS-DOS).

Reception and impact

The IBM PC was an immediate commercial success, selling far beyond IBM's projections and establishing IBM's design as the dominant personal computer standard for business. The decision to publish detailed technical references, and the use of a clean-room-reversed BIOS by companies such as Compaq and Phoenix Technologies, meant that competitors could produce fully compatible "IBM PC compatible" machines. Within a few years, clones rather than IBM's own products made up most of the market, but the architecture the PC established — Intel x86 processors, Microsoft operating systems, and expandable hardware — dominated personal computing for decades.

The line was succeeded by the IBM Personal Computer XT (1983) and the IBM Personal Computer AT (1984), and IBM's original models were discontinued in 1987, the year the PS/2 line was introduced.

Specifications

  • Processor: Intel 8088 at 4.77 MHz
  • Memory: 16 KB to 640 KB (later models)
  • Storage: cassette interface; optional 160 KB 5.25-inch floppy drives
  • Display: 80×25 text monochrome (MDA) or colour graphics (CGA)
  • Operating system: IBM PC DOS 1.0
