Offset printing

Offset printing is a widely used printing technique in which the inked image is transferred (or "offset") from a plate to a rubber blanket and then to the printing surface. Combined with the lithographic principle — based on the repulsion of oil and water — it is the dominant method for high-volume commercial printing of newspapers, magazines, books, brochures, and packaging.

Process

In offset lithography, the image on a thin aluminium plate is treated so that image areas attract greasy ink while non-image areas, dampened by a fountain solution, repel it. The plate cylinder transfers the image to a rubber-covered blanket cylinder, which in turn prints onto paper pressed against it by an impression cylinder. Because the rubber blanket conforms to the paper surface, sharp images can be printed even on relatively rough stock. Colour work uses separate units for each of the CMYK process colours.

History

The offset principle was discovered around 1904, when American printer Ira Washington Rubel noticed that an image accidentally transferred to the rubber impression cylinder produced a sharper print on paper. Robert Barclay developed a similar press in England for printing on metal. Sheet-fed and web-fed offset presses displaced letterpress through the 20th century.

Advantages and limitations

Offset printing produces consistently high image quality, is inexpensive per copy at long runs, and supports a wide range of substrates. Its plate-making setup costs make it uneconomical for very short runs, where digital printing is preferred.
