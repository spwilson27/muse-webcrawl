TGV

The TGV (train à grande vitesse, "high-speed train") is France's intercity high-speed rail service, operated by the SNCF. It was developed during the 1970s, originally powered by gas turbines, and the first line — the LGV Sud-Est between Paris and Lyon — opened in 1981. TGV trains run at up to 320 km/h (200 mph) on purpose-built high-speed lines (lignes à grande vitesse, LGV) and continue onto the conventional network at lower speeds.

History

France began studying dedicated high-speed rail in the 1960s, spurred by the success of Japan's Shinkansen. The 1973 oil crisis pushed the programme from gas-turbine prototypes (TGV 001) to electric traction. The Paris–Lyon LGV cut journey times to about two hours and proved commercially successful, paying back its construction cost within about a decade. Successive lines reached the Atlantic coast (1989–90), northern France and the Channel Tunnel (1993), the Mediterranean (2001), the east (2007, 2016), and Brittany and Bordeaux (2017).

Technology and network

TGV trainsets are articulated, with Jacobs bogies shared between coaches and power cars at each end. Generations include the TGV Sud-Est, Atlantique, Réseau, double-deck Duplex, and the Avelia Horizon ("TGV M") entering service in the mid-2020s. The network reaches Belgium, Germany, Switzerland, Italy, Spain, Luxembourg, and (via Eurostar, a separate but related service) the United Kingdom. SNCF's low-cost brand is Ouigo; premium services are branded TGV inOui.

Records and safety

In April 2007 a specially prepared TGV set the world rail speed record of 574.8 km/h. Despite the high speeds, the TGV system on dedicated lines maintained an exceptional safety record over its first decades, with no passenger fatalities in high-speed operation on LGVs.
