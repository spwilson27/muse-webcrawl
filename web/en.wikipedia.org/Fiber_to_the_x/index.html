Fiber to the x

Fiber to the x (FTTx) is a generic term for any broadband network architecture using optical fiber to provide all or part of the local loop used for last-mile telecommunications. As fiber cables are able to carry much more data than copper cables, especially over long distances, telephone networks built in recent decades have increasingly replaced copper with fiber.

Variants

The letter "x" stands for the point where the fiber terminates:

FTTP (fiber to the premises) and FTTH (fiber to the home) run fiber all the way to the subscriber's building. FTTB (fiber to the building) terminates at the building, with the final distribution inside over other media. FTTC (fiber to the curb/cabinet) terminates at a street cabinet, typically within a few hundred metres of the premises, with the last stretch over copper such as VDSL. FTTN (fiber to the node) terminates at a more distant node, serving a neighbourhood, with copper covering the remaining distance of up to several kilometres. FTTA (fiber to the antenna) carries fiber up mobile base station towers.

Technology

Most FTTx deployments use passive optical network (PON) standards such as GPON and XGS-PON, in which a single fiber from the operator's optical line terminal is split passively to serve many subscribers — commonly 32 or 64. Active Ethernet point-to-point links are also used. Because the passive plant contains no powered electronics between the exchange and the customer, PON networks are cheap to maintain and highly reliable.

Deployment

Full-fiber (FTTH/FTTP) coverage has expanded rapidly worldwide, driven by demand for symmetric high-speed connections for streaming, remote work and cloud services, and by government broadband programmes. Where digging is expensive, operators often deploy FTTC as an intermediate step, reusing the existing copper drop.
