The Living Planet Report is a biennial publication of the World Wide Fund for Nature (WWF), produced in collaboration with the Zoological Society of London, that assesses the state of global biodiversity and the health of the planet's ecosystems. First issued in 1998, it is one of the most widely cited sources on trends in wild animal populations.

The Living Planet Index

The report's headline measure is the Living Planet Index (LPI), which tracks the average change in population sizes of monitored vertebrate species—mammals, birds, reptiles, amphibians, and fish—using time-series data from tens of thousands of populations of several thousand species. Successive editions have reported large average declines since 1970: the 2022 edition reported an average decline of about 69% between 1970 and 2018, and the 2024 edition reported about 73% through 2020, with freshwater populations showing the steepest falls. The index is a measure of average population trends, not a count of species lost.

Ecological footprint and themes

Alongside population trends, the report presents the ecological footprint of humanity—calculated with the Global Footprint Network—arguing that human demand exceeds Earth's regenerative capacity ("overshoot"). Recent editions have emphasized the interlinked crises of biodiversity loss and climate change, identified habitat loss and degradation, overexploitation, invasive species, pollution, and climate change as the main drivers of decline, and called for transformative changes in food production, energy, and finance, including protecting and restoring 30% of lands and waters.

Reception and discussion

The report receives extensive international media coverage and is frequently cited by policymakers and conservation organizations. Scientists have debated aspects of the LPI's methodology, including how a minority of severely declining populations can influence the average and how geographic sampling biases affect the figures, and ZSL publishes technical annexes and updates to address such questions.
