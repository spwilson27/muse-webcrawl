Jehovah (/dʒɪˈhoʊvə/) is a Latinization of the Hebrew יְהֹוָה, one vocalization of the Tetragrammaton יהוה (YHWH), the proper name of the God of Israel in the Hebrew Bible. The form arose when medieval Christian scholars combined the consonants of YHWH with the vowels of Adonai ("my Lord"), the word traditionally read in its place in Jewish practice, where the name itself is not pronounced.

History

The name YHWH appears nearly 7,000 times in the Hebrew Bible. Because Jewish tradition avoided pronouncing it, the Masoretic text supplied the vowel points of a substitute word. The hybrid form "Jehovah" first appeared in Latin works around 1270, in Raymond Martí's Pugio Fidei, and entered English through William Tyndale's 1530 Pentateuch. It occurs in the King James Version of 1611 in a few places (for example Exodus 6:3 and Psalm 83:18) and in compounds such as Jehovah-jireh.

Pronunciation debate

Most scholars of Hebrew hold that "Yahweh" is the more likely original pronunciation, and modern Bible translations generally render the Tetragrammaton as "the LORD" in capital letters. Nevertheless "Jehovah" remained the dominant English form for centuries and survives in hymns, place names and the King James tradition.

Use by religious groups

The name is central for Jehovah's Witnesses, who adopted their name in 1931 and regard the use of God's personal name as essential to worship; their New World Translation uses "Jehovah" throughout both testaments. Some Sacred Name movements and other groups likewise favour the form.
