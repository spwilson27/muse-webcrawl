Hellenism (modern religion)

Modern Hellenists performing a ritual at an altar dedicated to the Olympian gods.

Hellenism (Greek: Ἑλληνισμός, Ellinismós), also referred to as Hellenic reconstructionism or the Hellenic ethnic religion, is a modern pagan religious movement that seeks to revive and reconstruct the traditional religious practices, beliefs, and rituals of ancient Greece. Adherents worship the Olympian gods and other deities of the Greek pantheon, honour heroes and ancestors, and celebrate the festivals of the ancient Greek calendar, such as the Olympics-era games festivals, the Dionysia, and the Panathenaea.

History of the revival

Interest in reviving Greek religion appeared during the Renaissance and again among Romantic-era intellectuals, but organised Hellenic revivalism is largely a product of the late 20th century. In Greece, the Supreme Council of Ethnic Hellenes (YSEE), founded in 1997, became the best-known umbrella organisation. In 2017, the Greek state officially recognised the Hellenic ethnic religion as a "known religion" under Greek law, granting its bodies legal standing for places of worship.

Beliefs and practices

Modern Hellenism is polytheistic and generally reconstructionist in method: practitioners draw on ancient texts — the Homeric hymns, Hesiod's works, the writings of philosophers, and archaeological and epigraphic evidence — to recreate ancient practice in a modern context. Core virtues commonly emphasised include xenia (hospitality), eusebeia (piety), and sophrosyne (moderation). Ritual practice centres on offerings (spondai — libations of wine, water, or oil), prayers, hymns, and festivals tied to the Attic calendar.

Branches of the movement differ in emphasis: some groups focus on historical reconstruction, others on Hellenic philosophy such as Platonism or Stoicism, and still others blend Greek practice with broader modern pagan frameworks.

Organisations

Besides the YSEE in Greece, organisations such as Elaion and Hellenion in the English-speaking world, and various local kindreds and hearths, coordinate festivals, publish liturgical resources, and advocate for recognition of the religion.
