The Tokyo Round was the seventh round of multilateral trade negotiations under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), conducted from 1973 to 1979 with 102 participating countries. Named for the ministerial meeting that opened it in Tokyo in September 1973, it was the first GATT round to address non-tariff barriers systematically in addition to conventional tariff reductions.

Negotiations

The round opened amid the turmoil of the 1973 oil crisis and the collapse of the Bretton Woods fixed exchange-rate system. Negotiations covered tariffs, non-tariff measures and the "framework" issues of the trading system, including rules on safeguards, special and differential treatment for developing countries, and the treatment of agriculture and tropical products.

Results

The Tokyo Round reduced average tariffs on industrial goods in the major trading economies by roughly one-third, to an average of about 4.7%, staged over eight years. Its most distinctive achievement was a set of codes — separate agreements on subsidies and countervailing duties, technical barriers to trade (the "Standards Code"), government procurement, customs valuation, import licensing, anti-dumping, and arrangements on bovine meat and dairy products, as well as a revised agreement on trade in civil aircraft. The round also adopted the "Enabling Clause", which gave permanent legal cover to preferential treatment for developing countries under the Generalised System of Preferences.

Legacy

Because the codes were accepted only by subsets of GATT members, they were criticised as fragmenting the system into a "GATT à la carte". That weakness, together with unresolved questions about services, agriculture and intellectual property, drove the push for the next and final GATT round, the Uruguay Round, which folded most Tokyo Round codes into the single undertaking of the World Trade Organization in 1995.
