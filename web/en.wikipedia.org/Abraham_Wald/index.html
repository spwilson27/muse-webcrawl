Abraham Wald

Born: 31 October 1902, Klausenburg (Cluj), Austria-Hungary

Died: 13 December 1950, Nilgiri Mountains, India

Known for: Sequential analysis, statistical decision theory, survivorship bias

Abraham Wald (31 October 1902 – 13 December 1950) was a Hungarian Jewish mathematician who emigrated to the United States and made fundamental contributions to decision theory, geometry, sequential analysis and econometrics. He spent most of his American career at Columbia University.

Early life and career

Wald was born in Klausenburg in Transylvania and earned his doctorate at the University of Vienna under Karl Menger, working in geometry. Barred from a university post in Austria partly because he was Jewish — and prevented by his religious observance from attending Saturday classes — he worked as a tutor and at a business economics institute. After the Anschluss in 1938 he emigrated to the United States, joining Columbia's statistics faculty.

Work

During the Second World War, Wald worked with the Statistical Research Group at Columbia. His analysis of damage patterns on returning bomber aircraft became the classic illustration of survivorship bias: armour, he argued, belonged where the returning planes had no bullet holes, because planes hit there had not come back. He founded sequential analysis with his sequential probability ratio test, and his posthumous Statistical Decision Functions (1950) laid the foundations of statistical decision theory, including the Wald (maximin) criterion.

Death

Wald and his wife died in December 1950 when their Air India flight crashed in the Nilgiri Mountains during a lecture tour of India. He was 48.
