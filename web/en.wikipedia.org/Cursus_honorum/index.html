Cursus honorum

The cursus honorum (Latin for "course of honours" or "ladder of offices") was the sequential order of public offices held by aspiring politicians in the Roman Republic and early Empire. It defined minimum ages and intervals between magistracies, mixing military service with civilian administration.

Cursus honorum
Civilisation: Ancient Rome
Offices (ascending): Quaestor, (aedile/tribune), praetor, consul
Regulated by: Lex Villia Annalis (180 BC), Sulla's reforms

The ladder

After military service, a senator's son typically stood as quaestor (financial officer, entry to the Senate), then optionally as aedile or tribune of the plebs, then praetor (judge and commander), and finally consul, the Republic's highest office. Ex-praetors and ex-consuls governed provinces as propraetors and proconsuls; the censorship stood as a rare capstone.

Rules and reform

The Lex Villia Annalis of 180 BC set minimum ages — eventually about 30 for quaestor, 39 for praetor, and 42 for consul. Sulla formalised the sequence and barred iteration of offices within ten years. Ambitious men who reached the consulship in their first eligible year were said to attain it suo anno, "in his year," a mark of prestige claimed by Cicero, a novus homo without senatorial ancestors.
