Irrational number

DefinitionReal number that is not rational
Examples√2, π, e, φ
Decimal formNon-terminating, non-repeating
First proven√2 (ancient Greece, attributed to Hippasus)
CardinalityUncountably infinite

In mathematics, the irrational numbers are all the real numbers that are not rational numbers. An irrational number cannot be expressed as a fraction p/q of two integers; equivalently, its decimal expansion neither terminates nor becomes periodic.

History

The discovery that the diagonal of a unit square is incommensurable with its side — that √2 is irrational — is traditionally credited to the Pythagorean Hippasus in the 5th century BC and was said to have shaken the Pythagorean doctrine that all is number. Euclid's Elements contains a famous proof by infinite descent. Lambert proved π irrational in 1761, Hermite proved e transcendental in 1873, and Lindemann proved π transcendental in 1882, settling the impossibility of squaring the circle.

Properties

The classic proof that √2 is irrational proceeds by contradiction: if √2 = p/q in lowest terms, then p² = 2q² forces both p and q to be even, contradicting the assumption. Irrational numbers may be algebraic (roots of polynomial equations, such as the golden ratio φ) or transcendental (such as π and e). The sum of two irrationals can be rational — for example √2 + (−√2) = 0 — but the sum of a rational and an irrational is always irrational.

Cardinality

Georg Cantor showed that the rational numbers are countable while the real numbers are not; it follows that almost all real numbers are irrational, and indeed almost all are transcendental, even though proving a specific constant irrational — or even deciding open cases such as π + e — can be extremely difficult.
