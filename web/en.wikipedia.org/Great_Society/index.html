Great Society

The Great Society was a series of domestic programs, policies and legislation enacted by Democratic President Lyndon B. Johnson in the United States between 1964 and 1968. Johnson first used the term in a May 1964 commencement speech at the University of Michigan, envisioning a society that would end poverty and racial injustice and build on American abundance.

Programmes

The main goal was the total elimination of poverty and racial injustice. Landmark measures included:

  • The War on Poverty, launched by the Economic Opportunity Act of 1964, creating the Job Corps, Head Start and Community Action Programs;
  • Medicare and Medicaid (1965), providing health insurance for the elderly and the poor;
  • The Civil Rights Act of 1964 and Voting Rights Act of 1965, outlawing segregation and protecting minority voting rights;
  • Federal aid to education (Elementary and Secondary Education Act; Higher Education Act);
  • Environmental and consumer protections, public broadcasting, the National Endowments for the Arts and Humanities, food stamps, and urban renewal and housing programs;
  • The Immigration and Nationality Act of 1965, abolishing national-origin quotas.

Legacy

Official poverty rates fell sharply in the 1960s, and Medicare and Medicaid became permanent pillars of American social policy. Critics argued that some programs were wasteful or expanded federal power excessively, and the costs of the Vietnam War drained funds and political support. Nonetheless, the Great Society remains the most ambitious wave of American social legislation since the New Deal.
