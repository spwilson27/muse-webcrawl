Excise

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An excise, also known as an excise tax or excise duty, is an indirect tax charged on the sale of particular goods, or on licenses for specific activities. Unlike a customs duty, which is levied on goods crossing a border, an excise is an inland tax imposed within a country, typically on the manufacturer or producer, who passes the cost on to consumers in the price of the product.

Typical targets

Excises are most commonly applied to a narrow range of products: alcoholic beverages, tobacco, motor fuels, and other goods whose consumption governments wish to discourage or whose demand is relatively price-inelastic, making them a reliable source of revenue. Excises on alcohol and tobacco are sometimes described as "sin taxes." More recently, some jurisdictions have introduced excises on sugary drinks, plastic packaging, and carbon emissions.

An excise may be specific (a fixed amount per unit, such as per litre of spirits or per packet of cigarettes) or ad valorem (a percentage of the price). Because specific excises do not rise automatically with inflation, their real value erodes unless rates are regularly adjusted.

History

Excises were introduced in England in 1643 during the Civil War, initially as a temporary wartime measure, and became a permanent and politically contentious part of the revenue system; Robert Walpole's Excise Bill of 1733 provoked such public opposition that it was withdrawn and nearly brought down his ministry. In the young United States, a federal excise on distilled spirits imposed in 1791 triggered the Whiskey Rebellion of 1794.
