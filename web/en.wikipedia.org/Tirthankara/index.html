Tirthankara

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Row of the 24 Tirthankara statues in a Jain temple]

In Jainism, a Tirthankara (Sanskrit: tīrthaṅkara, "ford-maker") is a saviour and spiritual teacher of the dharma, the righteous path. The word refers to the founder of a tirtha — a ford across the sea of repeated birth and death — which is a pilgrimage site as well as the community of ascetics and lay followers the teacher establishes. Tirthankaras attain kevala jnana (omniscience) and then preach the path to liberation; at death they achieve moksha and become siddhas, freed souls.

The 24 Tirthankaras

Jain tradition holds that exactly 24 Tirthankaras arise in each half-cycle of time in this part of the universe. The first of the present cycle was Rishabhanatha (Adinatha), credited with teaching humanity agriculture, crafts and social organisation; the 23rd was Parshvanatha, a historical figure of roughly the 9th–8th century BCE; and the 24th and last was Mahavira (599–527 BCE by traditional dating), who reorganised the Jain community and is often mistakenly called the founder of Jainism. Each Tirthankara is associated with an emblem (cognisance) carved on the base of his statue — Rishabha's is the bull, Mahavira's the lion — by which individual images are identified.

Veneration

Tirthankaras are the supreme objects of Jain devotion, though they are not gods who grant favours: having transcended the world, they neither reward nor punish, and worship serves to inspire the devotee's own effort. Images in temples are typically shown seated in lotus posture or standing in the body-abandoning kayotsarga pose, with downcast eyes signifying detachment. Twenty of the twenty-four are believed to have attained liberation on Sammed Shikharji in present-day Jharkhand, the most sacred Jain pilgrimage site.
