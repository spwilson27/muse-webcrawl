Pulitzer Prize for Music

The Pulitzer Prize for Music is one of the American Pulitzer Prizes, awarded annually for a distinguished musical composition by an American composer that received its first performance or recording in the United States during the year. It was established in 1943 under the terms of Joseph Pulitzer's will, which had originally endowed a scholarship for music students; the administrators converted the provision into a prize for composition.

History

The first award went to William Schuman for his Secular Cantata No. 2, A Free Song, in 1943. The prize quickly became the most prestigious American honor for composers, with laureates including Aaron Copland (1945, for Appalachian Spring), Charles Ives (1947, for his Third Symphony, composed decades earlier), Elliott Carter (who won twice, in 1960 and 1973), Samuel Barber (twice), and Steve Reich (2007, for Double Sextet).

For most of its history the prize favored academic and concert-hall composers, and jazz was largely excluded. In 1997 Wynton Marsalis became the first jazz musician to win, for the oratorio Blood on the Fields. In 2018 Kendrick Lamar won for his album DAMN., the first popular-music and hip-hop artist to receive the prize, a decision widely covered as a milestone in the award's evolution. Ornette Coleman (2007, for the live album Sound Grammar) and Henry Threadgill (2016) also broadened its scope.

Selection

A jury of five musicians, critics, and composers nominates three finalists, and the Pulitzer board selects the winner. The prize carries a cash award of $15,000. Works are eligible if by American composers and given their premiere performance or recording in the United States in the award year.

Multiple winners

Composers who have won more than once include Elliott Carter, Samuel Barber, Dominick Argento, Gian Carlo Menotti, Walter Piston, and John Luther Adams's contemporaries in the modern era; the list reflects the breadth of twentieth- and twenty-first-century American art music.
