Manchuria

Manchuria is a historical region in Northeast Asia, corresponding roughly to modern Northeast China (the provinces of Heilongjiang, Jilin and Liaoning, and part of Inner Mongolia) and, in its widest "Outer Manchuria" sense, adjacent parts of the Russian Far East. It takes its name from the Manchu people, who founded China's Qing dynasty.

History

The region was successively home to peoples and states including the Sushen, Goguryeo, Balhae, the Khitan Liao and the Jurchen Jin dynasties. In the 17th century the Manchu leader Nurhaci unified the Jurchen tribes; his successors conquered China and established the Qing dynasty (1644–1912). Qing policy long restricted Han migration into the homeland, but in the late 19th century, and after Russia's advance to the Amur, large-scale Han settlement transformed the region.

20th century

Manchuria became a prize in Russo-Japanese rivalry: Japan won influence after the Russo-Japanese War (1904–05), and in 1931 the Imperial Japanese Army staged the Mukden Incident and occupied the region, creating the puppet state of Manchukuo (1932–1945) under the last Qing emperor, Puyi. After 1945 the region was returned to China; its heavy industry, developed under the Japanese and with Soviet assistance, made it an early industrial base of the People's Republic, centred on cities such as Shenyang, Harbin, Changchun and Dalian.
