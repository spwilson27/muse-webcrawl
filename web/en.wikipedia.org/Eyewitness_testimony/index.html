Eyewitness testimony

Eyewitness testimony is an account given by a person who witnessed an event, typically a crime, describing what they perceived. Such testimony is a powerful form of evidence in legal proceedings, and jurors tend to find it highly persuasive. However, decades of psychological research have shown that human memory is reconstructive and fallible, and erroneous eyewitness identification is the leading cause of wrongful convictions later overturned by DNA evidence.

Eyewitness accounts are persuasive but fallible evidence

Reliability

The accuracy of eyewitness memory can be affected by many factors, including stress, the presence of a weapon ("weapon focus"), poor lighting or brief exposure, expectations and stereotypes, and the delay between the event and the recall. The misinformation effect, demonstrated by psychologist Elizabeth Loftus, shows that information encountered after an event can alter a witness's memory of it, and leading questions can change reported details.

Identification procedures

Because of these problems, psychologists and reformers recommend improved police procedures, such as double-blind lineups (in which the administrator does not know who the suspect is), sequential rather than simultaneous presentation of lineup members, and instructions warning the witness that the perpetrator might not be present. Witness confidence is only weakly related to accuracy, although confidence expressed at a first identification, under good conditions, is more informative.
