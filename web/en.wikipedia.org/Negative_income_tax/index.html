Negative income tax

Proposed byJuliet Rhys-Williams (1942); Milton Friedman (1962)
PurposeGuaranteed minimum income via the tax system
RelatedUniversal basic income, earned income tax credit

In economics, a negative income tax (NIT) is a system in which people earning below a certain amount receive supplemental payments from the government instead of paying taxes. The concept was popularised by Milton Friedman in his 1962 book Capitalism and Freedom, in which he proposed it as a replacement for the patchwork of welfare programmes, though a version had been proposed earlier by Juliet Rhys-Williams in the 1940s.

Mechanism

Under a negative income tax, a threshold income is set. People earning above it pay tax at a normal rate on the excess; people earning below it receive a payment equal to a fixed percentage (the negative tax rate) of the difference between their earnings and the threshold. The result is a guaranteed minimum income that tapers gradually as earnings rise, avoiding the abrupt loss of benefits that can discourage work in means-tested systems.

Experiments and influence

The United States conducted large negative income tax experiments between 1968 and 1980, including in Seattle and Denver, which measured modest reductions in labour supply. President Richard Nixon's Family Assistance Plan, closely related to the idea, passed the House of Representatives in 1969 but failed in the Senate. The Earned Income Tax Credit, adopted in 1975, incorporates some of the same principles, and the NIT remains a reference point in debates over universal basic income.
