Free Silver

Free Silver was a major economic policy issue in the United States in the late 19th century. Its advocates, known as "Silverites", supported an expansionary monetary policy featuring the unrestricted coinage of silver into money on demand — "free" coinage at the legal ratio of 16 to 1 with gold — as opposed to a strict gold standard. Supporters argued that bimetallism would increase the money supply, relieve indebted farmers and workers, and counter the deflation of the Long Depression era.

History

The Coinage Act of 1873 ended the minting of silver dollars — denounced by silver advocates as the "Crime of '73". The Bland–Allison Act (1878) and Sherman Silver Purchase Act (1890) restored limited government purchases of silver, but the free-silver movement peaked with the Panic of 1893 and the repeal of the Sherman Act. In 1896 the Democratic Party nominated William Jennings Bryan, whose famous "Cross of Gold" speech championed free silver; Bryan lost to William McKinley, and the Gold Standard Act of 1900 ended the issue.
