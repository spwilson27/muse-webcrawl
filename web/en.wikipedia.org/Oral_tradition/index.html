Oral tradition

Griots in West Africa preserve history and genealogy through memorised performance.

Oral tradition, or oral lore, is a form of human communication in which knowledge, art, ideas, and cultural material are received, preserved, and transmitted orally from one generation to another. The transmission is through speech or song and may include folktales, ballads, chants, prose, or poetry.

Characteristics

Oral traditions rely on memory aids such as metre, formulaic phrases, music, and repetition. The research of Milman Parry and Albert Lord on South Slavic epic singers showed how oral poets compose in performance using stock formulas and themes — a model that reshaped understanding of the Homeric epics. Because each performance can vary, oral texts are often fluid where written texts are fixed, though some traditions, such as the Vedic recitation of India, achieved extraordinary fidelity over millennia through rigorous mnemonic techniques.

Examples

Famous oral traditions include the epics of Homer before they were written down, the West African griot (or jeli) tradition preserving works such as the Epic of Sundiata, Aboriginal Australian songlines that encode geography and law, Icelandic sagas, and the Serbian guslar epics. Religious texts including the Vedas and early Buddhist sutras were transmitted orally for centuries before being committed to writing.

Study

The academic study of oral tradition draws on folklore, anthropology, and history; historians such as Jan Vansina developed methods for using oral traditions as evidence for the past, particularly in societies without written records.
