Mustard (condiment)

Mustard is a condiment made from the seeds of a mustard plant — white/yellow mustard (Sinapis alba), brown mustard (Brassica juncea), or black mustard (Brassica nigra). The whole, ground, cracked, or bruised seeds are mixed with water, vinegar, lemon juice, or other liquids and spices to create a paste or sauce ranging in colour from pale yellow to dark brown and in flavour from sweet to spicy hot.

Preparation and chemistry

The pungency of mustard comes from isothiocyanates — in brown and black mustard, allyl isothiocyanate — produced when the enzyme myrosinase acts on glucosinolates in the crushed seed upon contact with liquid. Cold water preserves the sharp heat; hot water and acid moderate it, which is why vinegars and wine are used to stabilise different styles. Prepared mustard also commonly contains turmeric, which gives bright yellow mustards their colour.

Varieties

Styles include mild American yellow mustard; French Dijon mustard, made with verjuice or white wine and brown seeds, centred historically on the city of Dijon; whole-grain and stone-ground mustards; strong English mustard (famously Colman's of Norwich); sweet Bavarian mustard; and spicy Chinese and Japanese (karashi) mustards served with dim sum and oden. Mustard seeds themselves are essential in South Asian cooking, tempered in hot oil, and mustard oil is a major cooking medium in Bengal and Nepal.

Uses and history

Mustard has been cultivated since antiquity — the Romans mixed grape must (mustum) with ground seeds, the likely origin of the name — and was spread through medieval monasteries and guild towns such as Dijon and Meaux. Today it is a standard accompaniment to hot dogs, sandwiches, pretzels and cured meats, an emulsifier in vinaigrettes and mayonnaise, and a component of sauces, glazes and spice rubs worldwide.
