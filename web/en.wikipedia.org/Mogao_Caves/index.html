The Mogao Caves, also known as the Thousand Buddha Grottoes (Qianfodong), form a system of 492 surviving decorated caves cut into a cliff about 25 km southeast of Dunhuang in Gansu province, China. Created over a millennium from the fourth to the fourteenth century, they preserve some 45,000 square metres of Buddhist murals and more than 2,000 painted sculptures — the largest and richest repository of Buddhist art in the world.

History

According to tradition, the monk Le Zun had a vision of a thousand Buddhas at the site in 366 AD and excavated the first cave. As Dunhuang flourished as a garrison town and Silk Road entrepôt at the edge of the Taklamakan Desert, merchants, officials and pilgrims sponsored new caves under successive dynasties — Northern Wei, Sui, Tang, Song and Yuan. Tang-dynasty caves are especially magnificent, with vast paradise scenes; two colossal Buddha statues, of 35.5 m and 27 m, anchor the complex.

The Library Cave

Cave 17, the "Library Cave", was a memorial chapel later used to store manuscripts and paintings. Sealed around the early eleventh century and rediscovered by the caretaker Wang Yuanlu in 1900, it held some 50,000 documents in Chinese, Tibetan, Sanskrit, Sogdian, Khotanese, Uyghur and other languages — including the world's earliest dated printed book, the Diamond Sutra of 868 AD. Expeditions led by Aurel Stein (1907) and Paul Pelliot (1908) removed tens of thousands of items, now held mainly by the British Library, the British Museum and institutions in France, Russia, Japan and China; the International Dunhuang Project works to reunite the material digitally.

Conservation

The Dunhuang Academy, founded in 1944, manages the site, which was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1987. Conservation addresses sand abrasion, salt damage and visitor impact, with daily visitor caps and a digital exhibition centre showing replica caves and high-resolution imagery.
