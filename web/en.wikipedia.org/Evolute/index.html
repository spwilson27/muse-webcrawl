Evolute

In the differential geometry of curves, the evolute of a given curve is the locus of all its centres of curvature. Equivalently, it is the envelope of the normals of the curve: the curve to which every normal line of the original curve is tangent. The original curve is called an involute of its evolute, and the relationship is reversible — every curve is the involute of each of its evolutes, which is why involutes can be generated mechanically by unwinding a taut string from the evolute.

History

Apollonius of Perga discussed the normals of conic sections in Book V of the Conics, but the general theory is due to Christiaan Huygens, who introduced the term and developed the theory in his Horologium Oscillatorium (1673). Huygens discovered that the evolute of a cycloid is another, congruent cycloid — a result that allowed him to construct the tautochrone pendulum clock, whose bob swings along a cycloid between cycloidal "cheeks".

Properties

If a curve is given parametrically, the evolute is obtained by moving from each point along its normal by the radius of curvature at that point. The evolute of a parabola is a semicubical parabola; that of an ellipse is a scaled astroid-like curve; the evolute of a tractrix is a catenary; and a circle's evolute degenerates to its centre point. Wherever the curvature of the original curve reaches a local extremum (a vertex), the evolute has a cusp. Singular points of the evolute thus mark the vertices of the original curve, a fact used in the four-vertex theorem, which states that every simple closed convex curve has at least four vertices.

Applications

Involute curves — unwound from a circular evolute — define the standard tooth profile of involute gears, because they guarantee a constant gear ratio and smooth transmission of motion. Evolutes also appear in optics, where the envelope of normals corresponds to caustic surfaces formed by reflection or refraction of light rays.
