Longinus (Greek: Λογγῖνος) is the conventional name of the author of the treatise On the Sublime (Peri Hypsous), one of the most influential works of ancient literary criticism. The manuscript attributes the work to "Dionysius Longinus" or "Dionysius or Longinus", and scholars remain divided over whether the author was the first-century rhetorician Dionysius of Halicarnassus's circle or Cassius Longinus, the third-century philosopher and adviser to Queen Zenobia of Palmyra. The author is often called Pseudo-Longinus.

On the Sublime

The treatise, of which about two-thirds survives, defines sublimity (hypsos) as the quality that makes writing transport the reader rather than merely persuade. Longinus identifies five sources of the sublime: grandeur of thought, powerful emotion, figures of speech, noble diction and dignified composition. He quotes widely, including Sappho and even the book of Genesis.

Influence

Lost for much of the Middle Ages, the work was revived after its first printed edition in 1554 and Boileau's French translation of 1674. It shaped eighteenth-century aesthetics, influencing Edmund Burke and Immanuel Kant's theories of the sublime.
