Sogdian was an Eastern Iranian language spoken in Sogdia, the region of Central Asia centred on the cities of Samarkand and Bukhara in present-day Uzbekistan and Tajikistan. As the language of the Sogdian merchants who dominated the Silk Road trade, it became a lingua franca across Central Asia and carried Buddhist, Manichaean, and Christian texts deep into China.

History and texts

The earliest substantial evidence is the Sogdian "Ancient Letters" (c. 313 AD), correspondence of merchants found by Aurel Stein in a watchtower near Dunhuang, describing trade and the fall of Luoyang. Later texts, recovered from Turfan, Dunhuang, and Mount Mugh in Tajikistan—whose archive documents the last Sogdian ruler, Devashtich, around the Arab conquest in the early eighth century—include Buddhist sutras translated from Chinese and Sanskrit, Manichaean hymns and confessions, and Christian scriptures translated from Syriac. Sogdian gradually yielded to Persian (Tajik) and Turkic languages after the Islamic conquest and was extinct as a vernacular by roughly the eleventh century, though Sogdian scribes served the early Uyghur Empire and adapted their script for Uyghur and later Mongolian and Manchu writing.

Linguistic features

Sogdian is known for its "rhythmic law," by which stems were classified as light or heavy and endings varied accordingly. It used a script derived from Aramaic, written right to left, rich in heterograms—Aramaic words read as Sogdian. Separate book hands were used by Buddhist, Manichaean, and Christian communities, the latter employing the Syriac script.

Legacy

The sole living descendant of Sogdian is Yaghnobi, spoken by a few thousand people in Tajikistan's Yaghnob Valley. Sogdian loanwords survive in Persian, Chinese transcriptions of foreign terms, and the vocabulary of Silk Road commerce.
