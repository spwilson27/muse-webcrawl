Chapter 7, Title 11, United States Code

Chapter 7 of Title 11 of the United States Code (the Bankruptcy Code) governs the process of liquidation under United States bankruptcy law. It is commonly called "Chapter 7 bankruptcy" or "straight bankruptcy". Under Chapter 7, a trustee is appointed to collect and sell the debtor's nonexempt assets and use the proceeds to pay creditors in accordance with the Code's priority provisions. In exchange, individual debtors generally receive a discharge releasing them from personal liability for most pre-petition debts.

Overview

A Chapter 7 case begins with the filing of a petition with the bankruptcy court. Individuals, partnerships, corporations, and other business entities may be debtors. Upon filing, an automatic stay generally halts most collection activity. A trustee is appointed from a panel (or, for business cases, may be elected by creditors) to administer the estate: investigating the debtor's finances, liquidating nonexempt property, avoiding certain transfers, and distributing proceeds to creditors.

Eligibility and the means test

The Bankruptcy Abuse Prevention and Consumer Protection Act of 2005 introduced a "means test" (11 U.S.C. § 707(b)(2)). If an individual debtor's current monthly income exceeds the state median for a household of the same size, further calculations determine whether a presumption of abuse arises; the case may then be dismissed or converted to Chapter 13 unless special circumstances are shown.

Discharge and exemptions

Only individuals receive a discharge in Chapter 7; corporations and partnerships do not. Certain debts are nondischargeable, including most taxes, domestic support obligations, most student loans (absent undue hardship), and debts incurred by fraud. Debtors may claim exemptions under state or federal law to protect specified property — such as a limited amount of home equity, a vehicle, and household goods — from liquidation. Secured creditors may generally enforce their liens on collateral despite the discharge of personal liability.

Process

Typical consumer cases are "no-asset" cases in which the trustee finds no nonexempt property to distribute. Debtors must complete credit counselling before filing and a debtor-education course before discharge, attend a meeting of creditors under § 341, and may reaffirm debts, redeem property, or surrender collateral. Most cases close within a few months of filing.
