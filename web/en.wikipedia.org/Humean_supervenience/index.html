Humean supervenience

Summary

Field: Metaphysics, philosophy of science

Associated with: David Lewis

Named after: David Hume

Humean supervenience is a metaphysical thesis developed by the philosopher David Lewis. It holds that everything in the world supervenes on the spatiotemporal arrangement of local, intrinsic qualities — "a mosaic of local matters of fact." All other facts, including laws of nature, causal relations, and macroscopic properties, are determined by this fundamental pattern of perfectly natural properties distributed across spacetime.

The thesis

Lewis summarised the doctrine as the claim that all there is in the world is a vast mosaic of local matters of particular fact: "one little thing, and then another." On this view there are no necessary connections between distinct existences — a rejection of necessary causal ties that echoes David Hume's philosophy. Two worlds that are identical in the distribution of fundamental local qualities are identical in every respect.

Laws of nature

Humean supervenience underpins the Humean "best system" account of laws: the laws of nature are simply the theorems of the best systematisation of the mosaic that optimally balances simplicity and strength. Laws therefore describe the pattern of events rather than governing them.

Challenges

The thesis faces objections from quantum mechanics, where entanglement appears to involve non-local, holistic properties that do not supervene on local qualities assigned to spacetime points. Critics also question whether dispositions and chances can be accommodated within the Humean mosaic. Lewis and later defenders have proposed modifications, such as including spacetime relations themselves in the supervenience base.
