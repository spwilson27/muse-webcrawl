Mahavira (Sanskrit: Mahāvīra, "great hero"), also known as Vardhamana, was the 24th and last tirthankara ("ford-maker") of Jainism. He was the spiritual successor of the 23rd tirthankara Parshvanatha. Jain tradition holds that Mahavira was born in the early part of the 6th century BCE into a royal Kshatriya family in present-day Bihar, India, and that he renounced worldly life at the age of 30, practising severe asceticism for twelve and a half years before attaining Kevala Jnana (omniscience). He then preached for 30 years and attained moksha (liberation) at Pawapuri.

Mahavira taught that observance of the vows of ahimsa (non-violence), satya (truth), asteya (non-stealing), brahmacharya (chastity), and aparigraha (non-possession) is necessary for spiritual liberation. His teachings were compiled by his disciples, notably Indrabhuti Gautama, as the Jain Agamas. Mahavira is usually depicted in a sitting or standing meditative posture, with the lion symbol beneath him.

Life according to tradition

Mahavira was born as Vardhamana to King Siddhartha and Queen Trishala of the Ikshvaku dynasty at Kundagrama near Vaishali. According to legend, his birth was preceded by auspicious dreams, and his name ("increasing") reflected the prosperity that followed. After his parents' deaths he sought permission from his elder brother and renounced the world. He tore out his hair in five handfuls, fasted, and endured hardships and abuse in silence for over twelve years, meditating under a sal tree by the Rijubalika river when he attained omniscience. For the remaining 30 years he travelled through the Ganges valley teaching, organising his followers into the fourfold order of monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen.

Teachings

Mahavira's philosophy rests on the doctrines of anekantavada (many-sidedness of reality), syadvada (conditioned predication), and karma as a material substance that binds the soul (jiva). Liberation is achieved by stopping the influx of karma through right conduct and burning off accumulated karma through austerity. His five great vows for monks — with chastity added to the four vows of Parshvanatha — remain the ethical core of Jain practice, and his absolute commitment to ahimsa extended to all living beings, including plants and microorganisms.

Historicity and legacy

Modern scholars generally accept Mahavira as a historical figure, a contemporary of the Buddha, though his traditional dates (599–527 BCE) are debated; many scholars place him in the 5th century BCE. Jainism's profound influence on Indian culture — in ethics, vegetarianism, art, and literature — traces back to the community he organised. His liberation day is commemorated as Diwali by Jains, and his birth anniversary is celebrated as Mahavir Janma Kalyanak.
