Tests of general relativity

Tests of general relativity serve to establish observational evidence for the theory of gravity put forward by Albert Einstein in 1915. The theory has passed every test to which it has been subjected, from the classical solar-system tests proposed by Einstein himself to modern observations of gravitational waves and black hole shadows.

The classical tests

Einstein proposed three tests. First, general relativity explained the anomalous advance of the perihelion of Mercury — 43 arcseconds per century that Newtonian gravity could not account for. Second, it predicted that light passing near the Sun is deflected by 1.75 arcseconds, double the value derived from Newtonian theory; Arthur Eddington's observations of the solar eclipse of 29 May 1919 confirmed Einstein's prediction and made him world-famous. Third, it predicted the gravitational redshift of light, first measured in the laboratory by Pound and Rebka in 1959 using the Mössbauer effect.

Further solar-system tests

Radar measurements of the Shapiro time delay — the slowing of signals passing near the Sun — verified the theory to high precision, notably with the Cassini spacecraft in 2002. Lunar laser ranging and planetary ephemerides test the equivalence principle and constrain variations in the gravitational constant, while the Gravity Probe B satellite (2011 results) measured frame-dragging and the geodetic effect predicted for gyroscopes orbiting the Earth.

Strong-field tests

The binary pulsar PSR B1913+21, discovered by Hulse and Taylor in 1974, loses orbital energy at exactly the rate expected from the emission of gravitational waves, earning the 1993 Nobel Prize in Physics. In 2015 the LIGO observatories directly detected gravitational waves from merging black holes, and in 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope imaged the shadow of the supermassive black hole in M87, consistent with general relativity's predictions.
