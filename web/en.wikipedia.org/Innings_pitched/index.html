In baseball, innings pitched (IP) is the number of innings a pitcher has completed, measured by the number of batters and baserunners that are put out while the pitcher is on the mound in a game. Three outs made equals one inning pitched; partial innings are recorded in thirds, so a pitcher who records two outs before being relieved is credited with 2⁄3 of an inning.

Notation

Innings pitched is conventionally written with a decimal-like notation in which the digit after the point counts outs, not tenths: "6.1 IP" means six innings plus one out, and "6.2 IP" six innings plus two outs. This notation is a common source of confusion when innings pitched are used in calculations, such as earned run average (ERA), which multiplies earned runs by nine and divides by innings pitched; statisticians convert the figure to outs (or thirds) before computing.

Use and records

Innings pitched measures a pitcher's workload and durability and is a component of rate statistics including ERA and WHIP. A starting pitcher generally must complete five innings to qualify for a win. In early professional baseball, starters routinely threw complete games and exceeded 300 innings a season; modern workloads are far lower, and a 200-inning season is a mark of durability. Cy Young's career total of 7,356 innings pitched is considered one of baseball's unbreakable records; the single-season record is 680 innings by Will White in 1879.

Workload management

Because heavy workloads are associated with arm injuries, modern teams monitor innings pitched — particularly jumps in year-to-year totals for young pitchers — and may limit a pitcher's season innings as part of injury-prevention strategies.
