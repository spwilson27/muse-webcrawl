Era of Good Feelings

Era of Good Feelings
Periodc. 1815–1825
PresidentJames Monroe (1817–1825)
ContextAftermath of the War of 1812; collapse of the Federalist Party
Key eventsMonroe Doctrine (1823); Panic of 1819; Missouri Compromise (1820)

The Era of Good Feelings marked a period in the political history of the United States that reflected a sense of national purpose and a desire for unity among Americans in the aftermath of the War of 1812. The era saw the collapse of the Federalist Party and the end of the bitter partisan disputes of the First Party System; with the Democratic-Republicans effectively unopposed nationally, politics appeared — at least on the surface — harmonious.

The phrase was coined by the Boston newspaper Columbian Centinel on 12 July 1817, during President James Monroe's goodwill tour of New England. Monroe was re-elected in 1820 with all but one electoral vote.

Stresses and end

Beneath the surface, serious tensions persisted: the Panic of 1819, the first major peacetime financial crisis, sharpened sectional and class conflict, and the Missouri crisis of 1819–1821 exposed the explosive question of slavery's expansion, settled temporarily by the Missouri Compromise. The disputed election of 1824, decided in the House of Representatives in favour of John Quincy Adams — and denounced by Andrew Jackson's supporters as a "corrupt bargain" — is generally taken to mark the era's end and the birth of the Second Party System.
