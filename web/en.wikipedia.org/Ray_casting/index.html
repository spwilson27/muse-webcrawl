Ray casting

Ray casting is a rendering technique in computer graphics in which rays are projected from a viewpoint into a scene to determine which objects are visible. For each pixel of the image, a ray is traced from the eye through the pixel and into the scene; the first object the ray intersects determines the colour of that pixel. Shading is then computed at the intersection point, but unlike full ray tracing, ray casting generally does not recursively follow reflected or refracted rays.

History

The basic idea descends from geometric optics and was formalised for computer graphics in the late 1960s and 1970s; Arthur Appel's 1968 work on ray casting for shading is an early landmark. Ray casting became widely known in the 1990s through video games such as Wolfenstein 3D (1992), which used a simplified two-dimensional grid-based form of ray casting to render pseudo-3D environments in real time.

Uses

  • Rendering: hidden-surface removal and simple shaded scenes.
  • Video games: fast pseudo-3D environments on limited hardware, and spatial queries such as line-of-sight tests.
  • Physics and engineering: ray–object intersection tests, collision detection and volume calculations.
  • Seismology and optics: tracing rays through layered media.
