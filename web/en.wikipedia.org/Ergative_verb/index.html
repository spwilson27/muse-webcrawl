Ergative verb

"The door opened" / "She opened the door": the object of the transitive use is the subject of the intransitive

An ergative verb (also called an unaccusative or labile verb in this usage) is a verb that can be used both intransitively and transitively, such that when it is used intransitively, its subject corresponds to the direct object of the transitive use. The classic English examples are break, open, melt, and sink: in "The window broke" and "John broke the window", the window bears the same semantic role (the thing that breaks) in both sentences.

Terminology

The term draws an analogy with ergative–absolutive alignment: the intransitive subject patterns like the transitive object. In generative linguistics, a related distinction is drawn between unaccusative verbs, whose single argument is an underlying object, and unergative verbs, whose single argument is an underlying subject. Ergative verbs in the labile sense form an important subclass of unaccusatives.

Examples in English

Many English verbs of change of state or motion alternate in this way: The butter melted / She melted the butter; The boat sank / They sank the boat; Dinner is cooking / She is cooking dinner. Not all verbs allow the alternation: disappear cannot be used transitively in standard English, and destroy cannot be used intransitively in the same sense.

Cross-linguistic notes

Languages differ in how freely they permit the alternation and whether it is marked morphologically. French marks many such intransitives with the reflexive pronoun se (as in la porte s'est ouverte), while German uses sich for some verbs but allows others, like zerbrechen, to alternate without marking. The alternation is of interest in language acquisition, since children sometimes overgeneralise it, producing forms like "Don't giggle me".
