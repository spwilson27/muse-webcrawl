Organophosphate poisoning

Organophosphate poisoning
Specialty: Toxicology, emergency medicine
Causes: Exposure to organophosphate pesticides or nerve agents
Treatment: Atropine, pralidoxime, benzodiazepines, decontamination

Organophosphate poisoning is poisoning due to organophosphates (OPs), chemicals found in insecticides, herbicides and nerve agents such as sarin and VX. Organophosphates irreversibly inhibit the enzyme acetylcholinesterase, causing acetylcholine to accumulate at synapses and producing a cholinergic crisis.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms, often summarised by the mnemonic SLUDGE/DUMBELS, include salivation, lacrimation, urination, defecation, gastrointestinal cramps, miosis (pinpoint pupils), bronchospasm and excess bronchial secretions, bradycardia, muscle fasciculations and weakness, and in severe cases seizures, coma and respiratory failure, which is the usual cause of death.

Causes and mechanism

Most cases worldwide result from occupational or accidental exposure to agricultural pesticides, or from deliberate self-poisoning in rural communities of the developing world, where OP poisoning kills an estimated hundreds of thousands of people each year. After binding acetylcholinesterase, the bond undergoes "aging", after which the enzyme can no longer be reactivated.

Treatment

Management begins with removing contaminated clothing and washing the patient, while protecting staff. Atropine blocks muscarinic effects and is titrated to clear the chest; pralidoxime reactivates acetylcholinesterase if given before aging; benzodiazepines control seizures and agitation. Supportive care, including ventilation, is often required.
