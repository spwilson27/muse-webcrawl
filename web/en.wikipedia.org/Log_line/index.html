Log line

Heaving the chip log

A log line or logline is a knotted length of cord attached to a chip log, a wooden board dropped over the stern of a moving vessel. The knots in the line are counted as they pass through a sailor's hands in a fixed interval of time, measured with a sandglass, giving the ship's speed through the water. From this device comes the nautical unit of speed, the knot: one nautical mile per hour.

Use

The chip log was thrown into the water, where the weighted wooden quadrant floated roughly stationary while the ship sailed away from it, paying out the line from a freely turning reel. Knots were tied at regular intervals — for a 28-second glass, about 47 feet 3 inches (14.4 m) apart, proportional to the nautical mile. Counting the knots that ran out during the glass gave the speed directly in nautical miles per hour. The measurement was recorded in the ship's logbook, from which the word "log" for any record derives.

History

The chip log was in use from the late 16th or early 17th century; an early description appears in William Bourne's A Regiment for the Sea (1574). Later improvements introduced mechanical taffrail logs, which trailed a rotator and registered distance on dials. Although satellite navigation has made the chip log obsolete in practice, the knot and the nautical mile remain standard in maritime and aviation use.
