Peremptory challenge

In English and American law, a peremptory challenge is a right in jury selection for the attorneys to reject a certain number of potential jurors without stating a reason. A peremptory challenge results in the juror being excused without any stated reason, in contrast to a challenge for cause, in which the attorney must show the court a basis for excusing the juror.

Practice

The number of peremptory challenges available varies by jurisdiction and by the type of case. In United States federal courts, for example, each side is entitled to three peremptory challenges in civil cases and in misdemeanour criminal trials, and six for the government and ten for the defence in felony criminal cases not involving the death penalty, with additional challenges permitted in capital cases and when multiple defendants are tried together.

Limits

Although a peremptory challenge ordinarily requires no justification, the Equal Protection Clause of the US Constitution forbids striking jurors solely on account of their race or sex. In Batson v. Kentucky (1986), the Supreme Court held that a defendant could challenge the prosecution's use of peremptory strikes against members of the defendant's race; the doctrine was later extended to civil cases, to defence strikes, and, in J.E.B. v. Alabama (1994), to gender. If a prima facie case of discrimination is shown, the striking party must offer a neutral explanation, and the trial court decides whether purposeful discrimination occurred.

Other jurisdictions

Peremptory challenges existed in England and Wales from 1305 until they were abolished by the Criminal Justice Act 1988, following concern that defendants could "pack" juries. Canada abolished peremptory challenges in 2019, a reform upheld by the Supreme Court of Canada in R v Chouhan (2021). They remain in use in a number of common-law jurisdictions, including the United States, Australia and Scotland (in reduced numbers).
