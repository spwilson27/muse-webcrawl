Eudaimonia

Greekεὐδαιμονία
Literal meaning"good spirit" / "having a good guardian daemon"
Common translationsHappiness; flourishing; welfare

Eudaimonia (Ancient Greek: εὐδαιμονία) is a Greek word literally translating to the state or condition of "good spirit". In the works of Aristotle and other classical philosophers it names the highest human good: the goal of practical philosophy, including ethics and political philosophy. It is commonly translated as "happiness" or "flourishing", though both renderings are debated, since eudaimonia concerns living and doing well over a whole life rather than a passing feeling.

Aristotle's account

In the Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle argues that every activity aims at some good, and that eudaimonia is the final end chosen always for itself. Through the "function argument" he identifies it with rational activity performed excellently — activity of the soul in accordance with virtue. A eudaimon life also requires external goods such as friends, health and some fortune, since misery can impede virtuous activity.

Other schools

The Stoics held that virtue is sufficient for eudaimonia, treating external goods as "indifferents". Epicurus identified the good life with pleasure understood as the absence of pain and disturbance (ataraxia). Plato connected flourishing with the harmony of the soul's parts under the rule of reason.

Modern usage

Eudaimonia is central to virtue ethics and to positive psychology, where "eudaimonic well-being" — meaning, growth and purpose — is distinguished from hedonic pleasure. Philosophers such as Alasdair MacIntyre revived the concept in critiques of modern moral theory.
