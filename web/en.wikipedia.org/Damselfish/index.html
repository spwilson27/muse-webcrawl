Damselfish

A damselfish hovering over coral

Damselfishes are a group of marine fish comprising the subfamilies Abudefdufinae, Chrominae, Lepidozyginae, Pomacentrinae and Stegastinae within the family Pomacentridae. Most species are small, brightly coloured reef fish found in tropical and subtropical oceans worldwide. The family also includes the clownfishes, famous for their symbiotic relationship with sea anemones.

Description

Damselfishes are generally deep-bodied and laterally compressed, with a single nostril on each side of the head and an interrupted lateral line. Most species are less than 15 cm in length, though a few reach 30 cm or more. Many species are brilliantly coloured in blues, yellows and greens, and juveniles are often more vivid than adults.

Behaviour and ecology

Many damselfishes are highly territorial. Herbivorous species such as those of the genus Stegastes "farm" patches of algae on coral rubble, weeding out unpalatable species and aggressively defending their gardens against other grazers, including much larger fish and sea urchins. Others are planktivores that hover in schools above the reef, or omnivores feeding on algae and small invertebrates. Males typically guard and aerate adhesive eggs laid on a prepared surface until they hatch.

Distribution

Damselfishes occur throughout the tropical and subtropical Atlantic, Indian and Pacific oceans, with the greatest diversity in the Indo-Pacific. A few species inhabit temperate waters, and the genera are well represented on coral reefs, rocky shores and seagrass beds.

In aquaria

Hardy and colourful, damselfishes are popular in the marine aquarium trade, though their territorial aggression can make them difficult tankmates. Clownfish, among the most recognisable damselfishes, are now widely bred in captivity.
