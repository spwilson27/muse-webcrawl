1989 Tiananmen Square protests and massacre

The 1989 Tiananmen Square protests were student-led demonstrations in Beijing, China, in the spring of 1989, calling for political reform, freedom of the press, and action against corruption and inflation. The movement began in April with mourning for the reformist Communist Party leader Hu Yaobang and grew into a mass occupation of Tiananmen Square involving, at its peak, around a million people. The protests ended in a violent military crackdown on the night of 3–4 June 1989, when People's Liberation Army troops opened fire on civilians in and around the square and along the western avenues of the city.

Background

A decade of market-oriented reform under Deng Xiaoping had raised living standards but also produced inflation, corruption and inequality, while political structures remained under the control of the Chinese Communist Party. Students in Beijing, inspired by earlier reform campaigns and by liberalisation elsewhere in the communist world, demanded dialogue with the government, press freedom and democratic reforms. A hunger strike begun on 13 May, timed to precede a state visit by Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev, drew massive public sympathy and support from workers, journalists and other citizens.

The crackdown

After hardliners prevailed within the leadership, martial law was declared on 20 May. When troops initially advanced, citizens blocked the streets and the army withdrew. On the night of 3 June, armed units fought their way into central Beijing, shooting civilians along Chang'an Avenue and at Muxidi and other points; the square itself was cleared by the early hours of 4 June. Estimates of the death toll vary widely, from several hundred to several thousand. On 5 June, an unknown lone protester — the "Tank Man" — stood before a column of tanks on Chang'an Avenue, producing one of the most famous images of the twentieth century.

Aftermath

Party general secretary Zhao Ziyang, who had favoured negotiation, was purged and placed under house arrest for the rest of his life; Jiang Zemin rose to succeed him. Thousands were arrested and some were executed; protest leaders fled abroad. The crackdown drew international condemnation and sanctions. Within China, public discussion of the events remains heavily censored, while annual commemorations long held in Hong Kong were banned after 2020.
