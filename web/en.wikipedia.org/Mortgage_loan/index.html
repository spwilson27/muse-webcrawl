A mortgage loan, or simply a mortgage, is a loan used by purchasers of real property to raise funds to buy real estate, or by existing property owners to raise funds for any purpose while putting a lien on the property being mortgaged. The loan is secured by the collateral of the specified property, meaning that if the borrower defaults, the lender may seize and sell the property through foreclosure to recover the debt.

Structure

Mortgage borrowers repay principal plus interest in regular instalments over a fixed term, commonly 15 to 30 years. In a fully amortising loan, payments are calculated so that the balance reaches zero at the end of the term. Interest may be fixed for the whole term or adjustable, varying with a benchmark rate. Borrowers typically make a down payment — a fraction of the purchase price — and the ratio of the loan to the property's value (loan-to-value) strongly affects the interest rate offered.

Types

Common categories include fixed-rate and adjustable-rate mortgages, government-insured loans (such as FHA and VA loans in the United States), interest-only mortgages, and reverse mortgages for older homeowners. In many countries, long-term fixed-rate lending is facilitated by securitisation, in which mortgages are pooled and sold as mortgage-backed securities.

Risks and regulation

Default and the resulting foreclosure impose losses on both borrower and lender. Lax lending standards and the widespread issuance of subprime mortgages contributed to the global financial crisis of 2007–08, prompting tighter underwriting rules in many jurisdictions.
