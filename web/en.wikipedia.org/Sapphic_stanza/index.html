Sapphic stanza

The Sapphic stanza is a poetic form named after the ancient Greek poet Sappho of Lesbos (c. 630 – c. 570 BC). It consists of four lines: three lines of eleven syllables (Sapphic hendecasyllables) followed by a shorter line of five syllables called an Adonic.

Structure

In classical quantitative metre, the Sapphic hendecasyllable follows the pattern of long and short syllables: — ⏑ — × — ⏑ ⏑ — ⏑ — ×. The Adonic line is — ⏑ ⏑ — ×. Sappho's contemporary Alcaeus used a related form, and the stanza became one of the most celebrated lyric metres of antiquity.

Later use

The Roman poet Horace adopted the Sapphic stanza extensively in his Odes, adapting it to Latin. Catullus also used it. The form was revived by Renaissance and later poets writing in many European languages, with accentual imitations replacing quantitative metre. English examples include poems by Algernon Charles Swinburne and Ezra Pound.
