Young Turks

The Young Turks (Turkish: Jön Türkler) were a political reform movement in the early 20th century that favoured modernisation and the replacement of the Ottoman Empire's absolute monarchy with a constitutional government. Its members, organised chiefly in the Committee of Union and Progress (CUP), carried out the Young Turk Revolution of 1908 and effectively ruled the empire from 1913 until the end of the First World War.

Origins and revolution

Opposition to Sultan Abdul Hamid II's autocracy grew among students, civil servants and officers; secret societies formed from 1889, notably in the military schools of Constantinople and among exiles in Paris. In July 1908, CUP officers in Macedonia under Enver Bey revolted, forcing the Sultan to restore the 1876 constitution and parliament — the start of the Second Constitutional Era.

Rule and the First World War

After the 1913 Raid on the Sublime Porte, the "Three Pashas" — Enver, Talaat and Cemal — dominated the government, centralising power and pursuing Turkification policies. They allied the Empire with Germany and brought it into the First World War in 1914. During the war, the CUP regime ordered the deportations and massacres of Armenians — the Armenian genocide — as well as persecutions of Assyrians and Greeks. Defeat in 1918 brought the leaders' flight; Talaat and Cemal were later assassinated by Armenian avengers.

Legacy

"Young Turks" has entered English as a generic term for young reformers challenging an established order. In Turkey, the movement's legacy is debated: it pioneered modernisation and seeded the cadre that founded the Republic of Turkey in 1923, yet its wartime nationalism and violence left deep wounds.
