Ingólfr Arnarson (spelled Ingolfr Arnarson in plain text and sometimes anglicised as Ingolfur Arnarson) was a Norwegian chieftain regarded, according to Icelandic tradition, as the first permanent Norse settler of Iceland. Together with his foster brother Hjörleifr Hróðmarsson, he fled Norway after a blood feud and, around 874 AD, established a farm at the site he named Reykjavík ("smoky bay"), after the steam rising from nearby hot springs, founding what became Iceland's capital.

Settlement of Iceland

The Landnámabók (Book of Settlements) recounts that as Ingólfr's ship approached Iceland he threw his high-seat pillars overboard, vowing to settle wherever they washed ashore — a customary appeal to the gods, in his case Thor. His slaves found the pillars two years later in the bay he then named Reykjavík, on the south-west coast. Ingólfr's settlement traditionally dates the beginning of the settlement era (landnámsöld), during which thousands of Norse and Celtic settlers colonised Iceland, leading eventually to the Icelandic Commonwealth and the Althing, founded in 930.

Legacy

Ingólfr is commemorated by a statue on Arnarhóll hill in central Reykjavík, erected in 1924 by the sculptor Einar Jónsson, and his name is attached to schools, streets and a mountain (Ingólfsfjall) near his later farm. His son, Þorsteinn Ingólfsson, is said to have founded the Kjalarnes Assembly, a precursor of the Althing.
