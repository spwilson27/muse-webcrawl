Karma in Buddhism (Pali: kamma) is the doctrine that intentional action determines future experience and rebirth. The Buddha famously declared, "It is intention, monks, that I call karma" (cetanāhaṃ bhikkhave kammaṃ vadāmi): volitional acts of body, speech, and mind plant seeds that ripen as results (vipaka) when conditions are favourable, in this life or in future lives within samsara.

Wholesome and unwholesome action

Actions rooted in greed, hatred, and delusion are unwholesome (akusala) and lead to suffering; actions rooted in generosity, loving-kindness, and wisdom are wholesome (kusala) and lead to happiness and favourable rebirth among humans or gods. The tradition lists ten courses of wholesome and unwholesome action, and grades karma by weight — habitual, near-death, and weighty deeds being most potent. Not everything experienced is caused by past karma, however: the Buddha rejected fatalism, teaching that illness and fortune also arise from other conditions, and that present effort can transform one's future.

Karma and liberation

Because karma perpetuates rebirth, the Buddhist goal of nirvana requires ending the production of karma. An awakened being (arahant) still acts, but without the volitional craving that generates karmic results; when the results of past karma are exhausted at death, there is no further rebirth. In Mahayana Buddhism, bodhisattvas may dedicate the merit of their wholesome actions to the liberation of all beings.
