Protection racket

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A protection racket is a type of racket and a scheme of organized crime perpetrated by a potentially hazardous organized crime group, known as a racket, that generally guarantees protection outside the sanction of the law to another entity or individual from violence, robbery, ransacking, arson, vandalism or any form of duplicity, in exchange for payments, known as protection money, made at regular intervals and according to a specific agreement. In many cases the "protection" is chiefly protection from the racketeers themselves: businesses that refuse to pay face damage or violence from the very group offering protection.

Overview

The protection racket is commonly associated with the Sicilian Mafia (which collects the pizzo), other Italian organized crime groups, the Chinese triads, Russian organized crime, and various gangs worldwide. It thrives where state institutions are weak, distrusted, or unable to enforce contracts and property rights. Sociologists and historians such as Diego Gambetta have analysed the Mafia as, in essence, a business of private protection, selling a commodity — protection — in markets where trust is scarce.

Payments may be demanded in cash, in kind, or through coerced business arrangements such as purchasing supplies from a designated vendor at inflated prices. Refusal can lead to vandalism, arson, assault, or murder, while compliance typically guarantees that the paying business is left alone and may receive genuine protection against other criminals.

State analogy

Several scholars, including Charles Tilly in his essay "War Making and State Making as Organized Crime" (1985), have drawn parallels between early state formation and protection rackets, arguing that governments that monopolize violence while offering protection against threats they themselves help define exhibit structural similarities to racketeers. The analogy is used analytically and does not imply moral equivalence in all cases.

Legal treatment

Protection rackets are prosecuted under extortion and racketeering statutes, such as the Racketeer Influenced and Corrupt Organizations Act (RICO) in the United States. In Italy, demanding the pizzo is punishable under anti-mafia laws, and movements such as Addiopizzo encourage businesses to refuse payment collectively.
