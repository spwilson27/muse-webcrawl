Tattoo

A tattoo is a form of body modification in which a design is made by inserting indelible ink into the dermis layer of the skin to change its pigment. The word derives from the Tahitian tatau, introduced into English after Captain James Cook's voyages to the Pacific in the 18th century. Tattooing has been practised in cultures across the world since Neolithic times, for reasons ranging from status and religious devotion to punishment and decoration.

History

Evidence of ancient tattooing includes the mummified remains of Ötzi the Iceman (c. 3370–3100 BCE), whose skin bears simple line and cross tattoos, and elaborate tattooed mummies from Egypt, Siberia and South America. Polynesian peoples developed sophisticated geometric tattoo traditions; in Japan, full-body irezumi flourished during the Edo period. In the West, tattooing was long associated with sailors, soldiers and marginal groups, but it spread through all social classes after the invention of the electric tattoo machine, patented by Samuel O'Reilly in 1891.

Methods

Modern tattooing uses an electric machine that drives solid needles up and down, puncturing the skin between 50 and 3,000 times per minute and depositing ink in the dermis, where it remains permanently because the ink particles are too large for immune cells to remove. Traditional methods persist, including hand-tapped techniques in Polynesia and hand-poked (tebori) tattooing in Japan. Coloured inks use pigments based on metal salts and organic compounds; regulations on ink safety vary by country.

Society and culture

Tattoos have become mainstream in many Western countries since the late 20th century, with a substantial minority of adults tattooed. They remain stigmatised in some contexts — in Japan, tattoos' association with the yakuza leads many bathhouses to exclude tattooed patrons. Religious views differ: some traditions prohibit tattooing, while others embrace it as devotion, as in the Hindu and Coptic traditions.

Health risks and removal

Risks include allergic reactions, skin infections and blood-borne disease if equipment is not sterile, so tattooing is regulated and licensed in many jurisdictions. Removal is possible, most effectively with Q-switched lasers that break up ink particles, though it is costly, slow and rarely complete.
