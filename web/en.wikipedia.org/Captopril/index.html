Captopril

Captopril, sold under the brand name Capoten among others, is an angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE) inhibitor used for the treatment of hypertension and some types of congestive heart failure. It was the first ACE inhibitor developed and is considered a landmark in rational drug design.

History

Captopril was developed in the 1970s at Squibb by Miguel Ondetti, David Cushman, and colleagues, based on peptides found in the venom of the Brazilian pit viper Bothrops jararaca that potentiated bradykinin and inhibited ACE. It was approved for medical use in 1980. The work showed that understanding an enzyme's structure and a natural inhibitor could lead directly to an effective oral drug, and it paved the way for later ACE inhibitors such as enalapril and lisinopril.

Medical use and side effects

Captopril is taken by mouth, typically two or three times daily because of its short duration of action. Common side effects include a persistent dry cough, dizziness, rash, and taste disturbances; like other ACE inhibitors it can cause angioedema and is contraindicated in pregnancy. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.
