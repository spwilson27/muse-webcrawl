Kon-Tiki expedition

LeaderThor Heyerdahl
Crew6 (five Norwegians, one Swede)
Dates28 April – 7 August 1947 (101 days)
RouteCallao, Peru → Raroia, Tuamotu Islands
Distance~6,980 km (4,300 mi)

The Kon-Tiki expedition was a 1947 voyage by raft across the Pacific Ocean from South America to the Polynesian islands, led by Norwegian explorer and writer Thor Heyerdahl. Heyerdahl undertook the journey to demonstrate that ancient South American peoples could, in principle, have crossed the ocean on balsa rafts and settled Polynesia, supporting his theory of trans-Pacific contact.

The raft and crew

The raft Kon-Tiki — named for the Inca sun god — was built in Callao, Peru, from nine balsa logs lashed together with hemp rope, with a bamboo cabin, a mast of mangrove wood, and a square sail. No modern materials were used in its construction, though the crew carried a radio, navigation instruments, and film cameras. Besides Heyerdahl, the crew comprised Herman Watzinger, Erik Hesselberg, Knut Haugland, Torstein Raaby, and Bengt Danielsson.

Voyage

Towed clear of the coastal shipping lanes on 28 April 1947, the raft drifted and sailed westward on the Humboldt Current and the trade winds. The crew subsisted largely on fish that gathered beneath the raft, supplemented by stored provisions and desalinated water. After 101 days and some 6,980 kilometres, on 7 August 1947 the raft struck the reef at Raroia in the Tuamotu archipelago; all six men reached shore safely.

Legacy

Heyerdahl's book The Kon-Tiki Expedition became an international bestseller, and the expedition's documentary film won an Academy Award in 1951. The raft is preserved at the Kon-Tiki Museum in Oslo. Although mainstream scholarship holds that Polynesia was settled from the west and Heyerdahl's migration theory is not accepted, later genetic studies have confirmed some pre-Columbian contact between Polynesians and South Americans, and the voyage remains a landmark of experimental archaeology.
