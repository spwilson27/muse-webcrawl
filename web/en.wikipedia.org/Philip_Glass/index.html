Philip Morris Glass (born January 31, 1937) is an American composer and pianist. He is widely regarded as one of the most influential composers of the late 20th century. Glass's work has been associated with minimalism, built up from repetitive phrases and shifting layers; he has described himself as a composer of "music with repetitive structures."

Glass founded the Philip Glass Ensemble in 1968 and has composed more than twenty operas, fourteen symphonies, twelve concertos, numerous string quartets and film scores, three of which were nominated for Academy Awards. He has collaborated with artists including Robert Wilson, Ravi Shankar, David Bowie, Brian Eno and Leonard Cohen.

Early life and education

Glass was born in Baltimore, Maryland, the son of Jewish immigrants from Lithuania. His father owned a record store, and the young Glass encountered modern composers such as Shostakovich and Bartók through unsold stock. He learned the flute, entered the University of Chicago at fifteen, and graduated in 1956 with a degree in mathematics and philosophy. He then studied composition at the Juilliard School in New York, where his teachers included Vincent Persichetti and William Bergsma, and later with Darius Milhaud at Aspen.

In 1964 Glass won a Fulbright Scholarship and studied in Paris with the renowned pedagogue Nadia Boulanger, who subjected him to rigorous training in counterpoint and harmony. In Paris he was hired to transcribe the music of Indian sitarist Ravi Shankar for a film score, an encounter that redirected his thinking toward additive rhythmic processes drawn from Indian music.

Minimalism and the Ensemble

Returning to New York in 1967, Glass formed the Philip Glass Ensemble in 1968 to perform his new compositions, including Music in Fifths (1969), Music in Contrary Motion (1969) and the evening-length Music in Twelve Parts (1971–74). Alongside Steve Reich, Terry Riley and La Monte Young, he became one of the leading figures of musical minimalism — though Glass has long resisted the label, preferring to describe his later work in terms of theatre and narrative.

Opera and film

Glass's breakthrough came with Einstein on the Beach (1976), a five-hour opera created with director Robert Wilson and premiered at the Avignon Festival before performances at the Metropolitan Opera. It was followed by the "portrait trilogy" completed by Satyagraha (1980), about Gandhi, and Akhnaten (1983), about the Egyptian pharaoh. His score for Godfrey Reggio's film Koyaanisqatsi (1982) brought his music to a wide audience, and he went on to score films including Mishima (1985), Candyman (1992), Kundun (1997), The Hours (2002) and Notes on a Scandal (2006). The 1982 album Glassworks introduced his music to popular audiences.

Later work

From the 1980s onward Glass's music broadened beyond strictly repetitive structures, encompassing symphonies — including three based on David Bowie's Berlin albums (Low, "Heroes" and Lodestone) — concertos, chamber works and operas such as The Fall of the House of Usher, Kepler and The Perfect American. His memoir Words Without Music was published in 2015. Glass received the Kennedy Center Honors in 2018 and remains one of the most frequently performed living composers.
