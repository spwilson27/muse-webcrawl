Vehicular cycling

Vehicular cycling (also known as "bicycle driving") is the practice of riding bicycles on roads in concert with other vehicles, exercising the rights and following the duties of the driver of a vehicle. The term and philosophy are associated with John Forester, whose 1976 book Effective Cycling promoted the principle that "cyclists fare best when they act and are treated as drivers of vehicles."

Principles

Advocates teach riding predictably in the traffic lane, positioning at intersections like motor vehicles, using hand signals, and obeying traffic lights and signs, rather than riding on sidewalks or hugging the curb. Proponents argue lane control reduces overtaking collisions and conflicts at junctions, and that training of this kind reduces crash rates.

Criticism and debate

Critics argue that vehicular cycling can feel unsafe for less confident riders and discourages mass cycling; they favour separated cycle infrastructure, as in the Netherlands and Denmark, which correlates with high cycling rates. The debate shaped cycling advocacy in the United States and Britain from the 1970s onward.
