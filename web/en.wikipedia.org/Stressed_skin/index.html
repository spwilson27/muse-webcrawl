Stressed skin

Stressed skin is a type of rigid construction, intermediate between monocoque and a frame with a non-loaded covering. In a stressed-skin structure, the skin or covering takes a significant part of the structural load, working together with internal elements such as stringers, frames, and spars. The term is used mainly in aerospace engineering: most modern aircraft fuselages and wings are semi-monocoque stressed-skin structures made of aluminium alloy or composites.

Principles

The skin resists shear and torsional loads and part of the bending load, while longitudinal stringers carry axial loads and frames maintain shape and redistribute loads. Riveted or bonded skin panels stiffened in this way achieve high strength-to-weight ratios, but local buckling and fatigue around cutouts and fasteners must be carefully managed.

Applications

Beyond aircraft, stressed-skin panels are used in building construction (structural insulated panels), vehicle bodies, and stressed-skin bridges. The technique emerged in the 1930s with aircraft such as the Douglas DC-3. See also monocoque, fuselage, and semi-monocoque.
