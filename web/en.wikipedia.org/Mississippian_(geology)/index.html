Mississippian (geology)

The Mississippian is a subperiod in the geologic time scale, the earlier part of the Carboniferous Period, lasting from about 358.9 to 323.2 million years ago. It is named for the Mississippi River valley, where rocks of this age are extensively exposed. In North American usage the Mississippian and the succeeding Pennsylvanian are treated as separate subperiods; in the international scale they correspond roughly to the Lower and Upper Carboniferous, though the term "Mississippian" is now internationally recognized as a subperiod of the Carboniferous System.

Subdivisions

The Mississippian is divided into three epochs—Early (Tournaisian), Middle (Viséan), and Late (Serpukhovian)—comprising the stages Tournaisian, Viséan, and Serpukhovian. North American regional stages include the Kinderhookian, Osagean, Meramecian, and Chesterian.

Paleogeography and life

During the Mississippian, much of North America lay beneath shallow tropical seas, depositing the vast limestones that today host caves such as Mammoth Cave. Crinoids, blastoids, brachiopods, bryozoans, and rugose corals flourished in these seas—so abundant are crinoid remains that the interval is sometimes called the "Age of Crinoids." On land, forests of lycopods and seed ferns spread, and amphibians (early tetrapods) diversified; the first amniotes appeared near the end of the subperiod. Gondwana's approach to Laurussia built the Appalachian–Variscan mountain belts, and the close of the Mississippian saw falling sea levels and the onset of the late Paleozoic ice age.

Economic significance

Mississippian limestones and sandstones are major petroleum and natural gas reservoirs in the North American midcontinent, including the "Mississippi Lime" play of Kansas and Oklahoma, and they supply building stone—Indiana's Salem Limestone is a classic example.
