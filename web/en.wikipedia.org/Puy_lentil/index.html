Puy lentil

The Puy lentil (French: Lentille verte du Puy) is a small, dark green, slate-marbled lentil grown in the volcanic soils around Le Puy-en-Velay in the Auvergne region of south-central France. It was the first French vegetable to receive an appellation d'origine contrôlée (AOC), granted in 1996, followed by European protected designation of origin (PDO) status. Puy lentils are prized by chefs for their peppery flavour and firm texture: unlike many lentils, they hold their shape after cooking and require no pre-soaking.

Cultivation

The lentils are grown at altitudes of 600 to 1,200 metres on basalt-rich volcanic soils in the Velay plateau. The dry, sunny microclimate — influenced by the foehn effect from the surrounding mountains — stresses the plants late in the season, producing lentils with thinner skins and lower starch content, the characteristics behind their quick cooking and non-mushy texture. Harvesting takes place in late summer; the lentils are dried, cleaned, and sorted, and must be produced, processed, and packaged within the delimited area to carry the appellation.

Imitation

Green lentils of similar appearance are grown elsewhere in the world, notably in Canada, and are often marketed generically. Only lentils grown in the defined Velay zone under the specification may be sold as "Lentille verte du Puy". Similar small French-type green lentils grown elsewhere are sometimes labelled "Puy-style".

Culinary use

Puy lentils are used in salads, soups, and as an accompaniment to salt pork, sausages, and salmon. A classic regional preparation serves them warm with a shallot vinaigrette. Their cooking time of about 20–25 minutes and ability to retain a slight bite make them a staple of modern French bistro cooking.
