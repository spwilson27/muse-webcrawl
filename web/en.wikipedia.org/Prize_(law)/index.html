Prize (law)

In admiralty law, a prize is movable property captured at sea during wartime, typically an enemy vessel and its cargo, which may be condemned by a prize court and appropriated by the capturing state. Prize law also historically encompassed goods captured on land, though naval prize was its main subject.

Prize courts and proceedings

A warship that seized a vessel could not simply keep it: the capture had to be brought before a prize court — in Britain, the High Court of Admiralty sitting as a prize court — which determined whether the vessel was enemy property, whether it carried contraband, and whether the capture was lawful. If condemned, the prize was sold and the proceeds distributed as prize money among the captors.

History

Prize law flourished in the age of sail and privateering. Letters of marque authorised private vessels to seize enemy shipping. The Declaration of Paris (1856) abolished privateering among its signatories and clarified rules on blockade and contraband. Prize money for Royal Navy crews was abolished in the 20th century (finally in 1948), and the advent of submarine and aerial warfare made traditional prize rules — requiring a warship to stop, search, and ensure the safety of a merchant crew — largely impractical in total war.

Modern status

Prize law remains part of the law of armed conflict at sea, reflected in manuals such as the San Remo Manual (1994), though it has rarely been applied since the Second World War.
