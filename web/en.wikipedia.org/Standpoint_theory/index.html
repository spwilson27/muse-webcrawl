Standpoint theory

FieldEpistemology, feminist theory, sociology of knowledge
Key thinkersNancy Hartsock, Sandra Harding, Dorothy Smith, Patricia Hill Collins
InfluencesMarx, Lukács, Hegel's master–slave dialectic

Standpoint theory is an epistemological framework holding that knowledge is socially situated, and that marginalised groups' positions can provide less partial and distorted perspectives on social reality — perspectives that are "achieved" through critical reflection and struggle rather than automatically possessed.

Origins

The theory draws on Hegel's master–slave dialectic and Marx's account of the standpoint of the proletariat, developed by Georg Lukács. Feminist theorists adapted it in the 1970s and 1980s: Nancy Hartsock proposed a feminist standpoint grounded in the sexual division of labour, Dorothy Smith developed a sociology from women's everyday experience, and Sandra Harding formulated "strong objectivity", arguing that inquiry should start from marginalised lives.

Key claims

  • Social position shapes what is visible and invisible to knowers.
  • Dominant groups have an interest in not understanding relations of domination, whereas the oppressed must understand both their own world and the rulers'.
  • A standpoint is an achievement of critical consciousness, not merely a perspective given by identity.
  • Patricia Hill Collins extended the analysis with the "outsider-within" and intersectional accounts of Black feminist thought.

Criticism

Critics question whether any standpoint can claim epistemic privilege, note the diversity of views within marginalised groups, and warn against essentialism. Defenders respond that standpoint theory evaluates claims by their critical genesis, not by the identity of the speaker alone.
