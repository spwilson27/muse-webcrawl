Megalith

Megalith
EtymologyGreek megas "great" + lithos "stone"
PeriodMainly Neolithic and Bronze Age
ExamplesStonehenge, Carnac stones, dolmens, menhirs

A megalith is a large stone that has been used to construct a prehistoric structure or monument, either alone or together with other stones. Megalithic monuments are found on every inhabited continent and were built in many periods, most famously during the Neolithic and Bronze Ages of Europe, where they include stone circles, alignments, passage graves, and the great chambered tombs known as dolmens.

Types

Common forms include the menhir, a single upright standing stone; the dolmen, a chamber formed by uprights supporting a capstone; passage graves such as Newgrange in Ireland, where a long corridor leads to a burial chamber under a mound; stone circles such as Stonehenge and Avebury in England; and long alignments such as the Carnac stones in Brittany, which comprise thousands of stones in parallel rows.

Distribution and chronology

In Europe, megalith building began around 4800 BCE in Brittany and spread along the Atlantic façade to Iberia, Britain, Ireland, and Scandinavia; radiocarbon research suggests the tradition may have originated in northwestern France and spread by sea. Independent megalithic traditions arose elsewhere: the moai of Easter Island, the stone spheres and tombs of Korea (which has the world's largest concentration of dolmens), megaliths in Ethiopia, Indonesia, and Madagascar, and the living megalithic practices of peoples such as the Naga and the Toraja.

Construction and purpose

Moving stones weighing many tons required coordinated labour, sledges, rollers, ropes, and earthen ramps; experimental archaeology has shown that even very large stones can be moved by teams of a few dozen people. Most megalithic monuments had funerary or ritual functions, and many show astronomical alignments to solstices and equinoxes. Their construction in societies without writing or metal tools implies complex social organization and shared cosmology.

Interpretation

Antiquarians once attributed the monuments to Druids or giants; modern archaeology treats them as products of early farming communities. In recent years, popular pseudoarchaeological claims notwithstanding, scientific study — including ancient DNA analysis — has clarified the relationships among the communities that raised them.
