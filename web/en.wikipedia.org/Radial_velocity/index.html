Radial velocity

The radial component of a velocity vector points along the line of sight.

The radial velocity of an object with respect to a given point is the rate of change of the distance between the object and the point. Equivalently, it is the component of the object's velocity vector along the line of sight — positive if the object is receding, negative if approaching. Motion perpendicular to the line of sight is the tangential (or transverse) velocity.

Measurement in astronomy

In astronomy, radial velocity is measured from the Doppler shift of spectral lines (redshift for recession, blueshift for approach) and is sometimes called the Doppler velocity. It is one component of a star's space velocity, combined with proper motion to give full three-dimensional motion. Precise radial-velocity measurements with spectrographs such as HARPS and ESPRESSO are used to detect exoplanets via the star's reflex "wobble" — the radial velocity method — which yielded the first exoplanet found around a Sun-like star, 51 Pegasi b, in 1995.

Other uses

Doppler weather radar measures the radial velocity of precipitation to detect rotation in storms; police radar guns likewise measure only the radial component of a vehicle's speed.
