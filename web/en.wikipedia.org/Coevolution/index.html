Coevolution

Coevolution is the process by which two or more species reciprocally affect each other's evolution through natural selection. The term was popularised by ecologists Paul Ehrlich and Peter Raven in their 1964 paper on butterflies and plants.

Examples

Classic examples include predator-prey arms races, host-parasite interactions, and mutualisms such as flowering plants and their pollinators. The rough-skinned newt and garter snake illustrate an arms race in tetrodotoxin resistance. Fig trees and fig wasps show obligate mutualism refined over tens of millions of years.

Types

Pairwise coevolution occurs between two species, while diffuse coevolution involves groups. Gene-for-gene coevolution is well documented in plant-pathogen systems. The Red Queen hypothesis, proposed by Leigh Van Valen in 1973, holds that species must continually adapt to keep pace with coevolving opponents.
