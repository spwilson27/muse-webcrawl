Vaccinology

Vaccinology is the science of vaccine development and the study of how the immune system responds to vaccines. It encompasses the discovery and design of vaccines, their production, clinical evaluation, delivery and the monitoring of their safety and effectiveness in populations. The term derives from variolae vaccinae, Edward Jenner's term for cowpox, the material he used in 1796 to demonstrate protection against smallpox.

Scope

Vaccinologists draw on immunology, microbiology, molecular biology, epidemiology and public health. Their work includes identifying protective antigens, choosing vaccine platforms — such as live attenuated, inactivated, subunit, toxoid, conjugate, viral vector and mRNA vaccines — designing adjuvants that enhance immune responses, and conducting the phased clinical trials required before licensure.

History

The field grew out of Jenner's vaccination experiments and the work of Louis Pasteur, who developed vaccines against anthrax and rabies in the nineteenth century and whose laboratory coined much of its vocabulary. The twentieth century added vaccines against diphtheria, tetanus, pertussis, polio and measles, and vaccinology matured into a distinct discipline with its own journals, societies and training programmes. The rapid development of mRNA vaccines during the COVID-19 pandemic is often cited as a landmark demonstration of modern vaccinology, including "reverse vaccinology", in which candidate antigens are identified from pathogen genomes computationally.

Challenges

Open problems in vaccinology include vaccines for HIV, malaria and tuberculosis, universal influenza vaccines, thermostable formulations that reduce cold-chain requirements, and understanding why vaccine responses vary between individuals and populations. Safety surveillance systems and the study of vaccine hesitancy are also part of the field's remit.
