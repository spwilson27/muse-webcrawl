Interference fit

An interference fit, also known as a press fit or friction fit, is a fastening method in mechanical engineering in which two parts are joined by making one part slightly larger than the space it must occupy in the other: a shaft is machined slightly larger than the hole it enters, so that when the parts are forced together the elastic deformation of both creates high contact pressure and friction that hold the joint without separate fasteners. The amount of interference — the difference between the shaft diameter and the hole diameter — determines the strength of the joint and the stresses induced in the components.

Assembly methods

Small interferences can be assembled with an arbor press or hammer blows (press fitting); larger ones use thermal methods. In shrink fitting, the outer part is heated (or the inner part cooled, for example with dry ice or liquid nitrogen) so that differential thermal expansion opens the joint, the parts are assembled, and they lock together on returning to a common temperature. Railway tyres shrunk onto wheel centres and cylinder liners fitted into engine blocks are classic applications.

Design considerations

The designer must ensure that the hoop stress generated in the hub does not exceed the material's yield strength, that the shaft is not crushed, and that the joint can transmit the required torque or axial load through friction. Standardised systems of limits and fits (such as ISO 286) specify grades of interference fit — from light drive fits that can still be dismantled to heavy force fits that are effectively permanent. Excessive interference risks cracking brittle hubs, particularly cast iron; insufficient interference may allow the joint to slip or fret under load.

Applications

Interference fits are used for bearing races on shafts and in housings, gear and pulley mounting, dowel and bushing installation, valve seats and guides in cylinder heads, and permanent assembly of crankshaft webs and pins. Unlike welding or adhesives, the joint involves no metallurgical change and can sometimes be separated with presses or heating, though usually at the cost of damaging the mating surfaces.
