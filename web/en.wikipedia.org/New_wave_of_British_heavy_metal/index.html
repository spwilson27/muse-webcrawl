New wave of British heavy metal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The new wave of British heavy metal (often abbreviated as NWOBHM) was a nationwide musical movement that began in England in the mid-1970s and achieved international attention by the early 1980s. Journalist Geoff Barton coined the term in the music magazine Sounds in 1979. The movement developed as a reaction against the perceived stagnation of earlier heavy metal and the dominance of punk rock, combining the speed and aggression of punk with the twin-guitar harmonies and virtuosity of earlier metal bands.

Characteristics

NWOBHM bands favoured fast tempos, galloping rhythms, palm-muted guitar riffs, melodic twin lead guitars, and high-pitched, powerful vocals. Many bands were formed by working-class musicians and promoted themselves through independent singles, demo tapes, and specialist fanzines and radio shows, most notably the Friday Rock Show hosted by Tommy Vance on BBC Radio 1.

Major bands

The movement's most successful acts include Iron Maiden, Def Leppard, Saxon, Diamond Head, Motörhead (often associated with the movement), Judas Priest (whose late-1970s work helped shape it), Venom, Raven, Angel Witch, and Tygers of Pan Tang. Iron Maiden and Def Leppard went on to become two of the best-selling heavy metal acts in the world.

Influence

The NWOBHM profoundly influenced later metal genres. American thrash metal bands such as Metallica — whose Lars Ulrich was an avid collector of NWOBHM records — took its speed and intensity further. Venom's raw extremity seeded black and death metal, while the movement's do-it-yourself ethos influenced underground metal worldwide. A revival under the banner "NWOTHM" (new wave of traditional heavy metal) emerged in the 2010s, drawing directly on the original sound and imagery.
