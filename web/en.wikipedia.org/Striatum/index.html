Striatum

The striatum (also called the striate body, striate nucleus, or corpus striatum) is a nucleus (cluster of neurons) in the subcortical basal ganglia of the forebrain. The dorsal striatum consists of the caudate nucleus and the putamen, divided in primates by the internal capsule; the ventral striatum includes the nucleus accumbens and the olfactory tubercle. The striatum is the largest component of the basal ganglia and the main input station of the basal ganglia system.

The striatum receives glutamatergic and dopaminergic inputs from various sources, notably the cerebral cortex, thalamus, and the substantia nigra pars compacta and ventral tegmental area. Its principal neurons are GABAergic medium spiny neurons, which make up about 95% of its neurons and project along the direct and indirect pathways of the basal ganglia.

Function

The striatum is a critical component of the motor and reward systems; it coordinates multiple aspects of cognition, including motor and action planning, decision-making, motivation and reinforcement, and procedural learning and habit formation. The dorsal striatum is more involved in sensorimotor and associative functions, while the ventral striatum (nucleus accumbens) is central to reward and addiction.

Clinical significance

Dysfunction of the striatum is implicated in Parkinson's disease (loss of dopaminergic input), Huntington's disease (degeneration of striatal neurons causing chorea), Tourette syndrome, obsessive–compulsive disorder, schizophrenia, and drug addiction.
