In toxicology, the median lethal dose, LD50 (abbreviation for "lethal dose, 50%"), LC50 (lethal concentration, 50%) or LCt50 is a toxic unit that measures the lethal dose of a given substance. The value of the LD50 for a substance is the dose required to kill half the members of a tested population after a specified test duration. LD50 figures are frequently used as a general indicator of a substance's acute toxicity: a lower LD50 indicates a more toxic substance, while a higher LD50 indicates lower toxicity.

The measure was developed by J. W. Trevan in 1927. Animal tests using LD50, historically performed on rodents, have long been required by regulators for classifying chemicals and setting safety data requirements, though the classic "LD50 test" (OECD Test Guideline 401) was deleted in 2002 in favour of procedures using fewer animals, such as the fixed-dose procedure and the acute toxic class method.

Conventions

The LD50 is usually expressed as the mass of substance administered per unit mass of the test subject, typically milligrams per kilogram of body mass. Results vary with species, route of exposure (oral, dermal, inhalation), and test conditions, so values are always reported together with the test animal and route — for example, table salt has an oral LD50 of about 3,000 mg/kg in rats, whereas botulinum toxin is lethal in the nanogram-per-kilogram range.
