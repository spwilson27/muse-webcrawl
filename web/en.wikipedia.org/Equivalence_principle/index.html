Equivalence principle

In the theory of general relativity, the equivalence principle is any of several related concepts dealing with the equivalence of gravitational and inertial mass, and with Albert Einstein's observation that the gravitational "force" as experienced locally while standing on a massive body (such as the Earth) is the same as the pseudo-force experienced by an observer in a non-inertial (accelerated) frame of reference.

Forms

  • Weak equivalence principle (Galilean): the trajectory of a point mass in a gravitational field depends only on its initial position and velocity, and is independent of its composition and structure. Galileo's experiments with falling bodies and Eötvös's torsion-balance experiments established it; modern tests (including the MICROSCOPE satellite) confirm it to about one part in 1015.
  • Einstein equivalence principle: the weak principle, plus the outcomes of non-gravitational experiments are independent of the velocity of the freely-falling reference frame (local Lorentz invariance) and of where and when in the universe they are performed (local position invariance).
  • Strong equivalence principle: the Einstein principle extended to bodies with gravitational self-energy, implying gravity itself must be geometry. This underpins general relativity.

Role in general relativity

Einstein called the insight "the happiest thought of my life" (1907): that an observer in free fall would feel no gravity. From it he developed the view that gravity is not a force but curvature of spacetime, with free particles following geodesics. Tests of the principle include gravitational redshift measurements (Pound–Rebka experiment), Shapiro time delay, and frame-dragging experiments such as Gravity Probe B.
