Nembutsu

Nembutsu
Japanese念仏 (nembutsu)
Chinese念佛 (niànfó)
PhraseNamu Amida Butsu ("Homage to Amida Buddha")
TraditionPure Land Buddhism
Key figuresHōnen, Shinran

The nembutsu is the central practice of Pure Land Buddhism: the recitation of the name of Amida Buddha, in Japanese "Namu Amida Butsu" ("I take refuge in Amida Buddha"). Practitioners believe that sincere recitation, relying on Amida's Primal Vow, ensures rebirth in the Pure Land (Sukhavati) after death, from which enlightenment is readily attained.

Origins

The practice derives from the Indian Buddhist discipline of buddhanusmṛti, "recollection of the Buddha". In China, masters such as Tanluan, Daochuo, and Shandao systematised recitation of the Buddha's name (nianfo), teaching that in the latter age of the Dharma (mappō) other practices were too difficult for most people. Chinese forms include silent, spoken, and meditative recitation, sometimes coordinated with visualisation of Amida and his Pure Land as described in the Larger Sutra of Immeasurable Life.

In Japan

The monk Hōnen (1133–1212) founded the Jōdo-shū school, teaching that the nembutsu alone, repeated as often as possible, secures rebirth in the Pure Land. His disciple Shinran (1173–1263), founder of Jōdo Shinshū, radicalised the teaching: even one recitation — indeed, the very faith (shinjin) that prompts it — is the working of Amida's grace ("other-power", tariki) rather than the practitioner's own merit ("self-power", jiriki). The nembutsu thus becomes an expression of gratitude rather than a means to an end.

Practice today

Nembutsu is recited in temples and homes, often with a rosary (juzu), at funerals and memorial services, and in communal chanting sessions. It remains the most widely practised form of Buddhism in Japan.
