Vitamin

ClassesFat-soluble (A, D, E, K); water-soluble (B complex, C)

A vitamin is an organic molecule that is an essential micronutrient: an organism needs it in small quantities for the proper functioning of its metabolism, but cannot synthesise it in sufficient amounts and must obtain it through diet.

History

Diseases such as scurvy, beriberi and rickets were recognised as dietary deficiencies in the early 20th century. In 1912 Casimir Funk coined “vitamine” (vital amine) for the factor preventing beriberi. Subsequent work isolated vitamins A through K and the B-complex group.

Classification

Thirteen vitamins are universally recognised: A, C, D, E, K and the eight B vitamins (thiamine B1, riboflavin B2, niacin B3, pantothenic acid B5, B6, biotin B7, folate B9, cobalamin B12). Fat-soluble vitamins are stored in body fat and can accumulate to toxic levels; water-soluble ones are excreted more readily.

Function and deficiency

Vitamins act as coenzymes, antioxidants and regulators — vitamin D in calcium metabolism and bone health, vitamin C in collagen synthesis, vitamin A in vision, folate in preventing neural tube defects. Deficiencies cause specific diseases: scurvy (C), beriberi (B1), pellagra (B3), rickets (D) and night blindness (A). Foods such as wheat germ and fortified cereals contribute B vitamins to many diets.

Supplements

Dietary supplements are widely used, though for most people eating a varied diet evidence of benefit from multivitamins is limited, and megadoses of some vitamins can be harmful.
