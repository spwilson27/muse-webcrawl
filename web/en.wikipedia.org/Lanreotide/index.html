Lanreotide

Lanreotide, sold under the brand name Somatuline among others, is a medication used in the management of acromegaly and symptoms caused by neuroendocrine tumors, most notably carcinoid syndrome. It is a long-acting analogue of somatostatin, like octreotide. By binding to somatostatin receptors, particularly subtypes 2 and 5, lanreotide suppresses the secretion of growth hormone and a range of gastrointestinal and pancreatic hormones.

Medical uses

Lanreotide is approved for the long-term treatment of acromegaly in patients who have had an inadequate response to surgery or radiotherapy, and for the treatment of gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (GEP-NETs) in adults. It is also used to control symptoms of carcinoid syndrome, such as flushing and diarrhea, reducing the need for rescue short-acting octreotide therapy.

Pharmacology

Like the natural hormone somatostatin, lanreotide inhibits the release of numerous secondary hormones. It binds predominantly to somatostatin receptor subtypes 2 (SSTR2) and 5 (SSTR5), which are widely expressed on pituitary adenomas and neuroendocrine tumors. The result is reduced secretion of growth hormone, insulin, glucagon, gastrin and other peptides, as well as antiproliferative effects on tumor cells.

Formulation

The drug is supplied as a prolonged-release gel in a prefilled syringe for deep subcutaneous injection, typically given once every four weeks at doses of 60, 90 or 120 mg. The gel forms a depot at the injection site from which the peptide is released slowly.

Adverse effects

Common adverse effects include gastrointestinal symptoms (diarrhea, abdominal pain, nausea), cholelithiasis (gallstones) resulting from reduced gallbladder motility, injection-site reactions, and disturbances of glucose regulation, including both hyperglycemia and hypoglycemia. Bradycardia has also been reported.

History

Lanreotide was developed by the French pharmaceutical company Ipsen. It was approved in various European countries in the 1990s and 2000s, and the United States Food and Drug Administration approved it for acromegaly in 2007 and for gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumors in 2014.
