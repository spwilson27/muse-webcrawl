Council Tax

Council Tax is a local taxation system used in England, Scotland and Wales. It is a tax on domestic property that was introduced in 1993 by the government of John Major, replacing the unpopular Community Charge ("poll tax"). Households pay according to the valuation band of their dwelling, and the revenue funds local services such as rubbish collection, policing, fire services and libraries.

Bands and billing

Every dwelling is assigned to one of eight bands (A to H) based on its capital value on 1 April 1991 in England and Scotland (2003 values in Wales). Band D is the reference band; other bands are charged as fractions or multiples of it. Local authorities set the Band D rate each year. Bills are issued to the resident aged 18 or over, with a 25% discount where only one adult lives in the property and various exemptions and reductions for students, people on low incomes and some other groups.

History

Council Tax succeeded domestic rates and then the Community Charge, whose introduction in 1989–1990 provoked mass protests and riots and contributed to the fall of Margaret Thatcher. Properties in England and Scotland have not been generally revalued since the tax began, a long-standing source of controversy about its fairness.

Criticism and reform

Critics argue the tax is regressive, since the ratio between the highest and lowest bands is capped at 3:1 while property values vary far more widely. Successive governments have considered but mostly avoided fundamental reform, though Wales revalued properties in 2003 and added a ninth band.
