Ecological anthropology

Ecological anthropology is a sub-field of anthropology and is defined as the "study of cultural adaptations to environments". The sub-field is also defined as, "the study of relationships between a population of humans and their biophysical environment". The focus of its research concerns "how cultural beliefs and practices helped human populations adapt to their environments, and how people used elements of their culture to maintain their ecosystems".

History

Ecological anthropology developed in the 1960s as a response to cultural ecology, a sub-field that Julian Steward helped pioneer. Steward argued that culture was influenced by the environment through a methodology he termed "cultural ecology". Ecological anthropologists, in contrast, examined populations as units and explained culture in terms of population dynamics and energy flows, drawing on systems theory and human ecology.

Roy Rappaport's work, particularly Pigs for the Ancestors (1968), was foundational, using energetic accounting to explain ritual regulation of pig populations among the Tsembaga Maring of New Guinea. Andrew P. Vayda and others developed "progressive contextualization", focusing on human responses to environmental hazards and events.

Approaches

The field encompasses neo-evolutionism, human behavioral ecology, ethnoecology, historical ecology, and political ecology. Contemporary ecological anthropology studies topics such as climate change adaptation, conservation, resource management, and the environmental consequences of globalization, often applying its insights to practical problems.
