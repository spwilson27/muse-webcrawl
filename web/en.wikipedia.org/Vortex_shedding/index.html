Vortex shedding

Vortex shedding is an oscillating flow phenomenon that takes place when a fluid such as air or water flows past a bluff (as opposed to streamlined) body at certain Reynolds numbers. Vortices are created at the back of the body and detach periodically from either side, forming a von Kármán vortex street in the wake, named after the engineer Theodore von Kármán.

Mechanism

As flow separates from a cylinder or similar obstacle, shear layers roll up alternately on each side. The shedding frequency is characterised by the dimensionless Strouhal number, St = fD/V, which is about 0.2 for a circular cylinder over a wide range of Reynolds numbers. The alternating vortices produce fluctuating side forces (lift) on the body.

Effects and mitigation

If the shedding frequency approaches the natural frequency of a structure, resonance can cause large vibrations — vortex-induced vibration — a concern for chimneys, bridge decks, offshore risers, power lines (aeolian vibration and galloping) and heat-exchanger tubes. The 1940 collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge is often popularly attributed to this effect, though aeroelastic flutter was the primary mechanism. Mitigations include helical strakes on chimneys, fairings, and splitter plates that disrupt the regular alternation of the wake.
