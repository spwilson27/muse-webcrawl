Snowbelt

Deep lake-effect snow is the hallmark of the Snowbelt

The Snowbelt is the region near the Great Lakes of North America where heavy snowfall — much of it lake-effect snow — is a defining feature of winter weather. The principal snowbelts lie on the lee (usually southern and eastern) shores of the lakes: east and south of Lake Erie and Lake Ontario in New York, Pennsylvania and Ohio (including Buffalo, Rochester, Syracuse and Erie), south of Lake Michigan in Indiana and southwestern Michigan, and around Lake Superior in Michigan's Upper Peninsula and Ontario.

Lake-effect snow

Lake-effect snow forms when cold Arctic air passes over the relatively warm, unfrozen lake water, picking up moisture and heat. On reaching the far shore, the air rises — often enhanced by higher ground — and deposits the moisture as intense, narrow bands of snow. Within a band, snowfall rates of several centimetres per hour and totals exceeding a metre in a day are possible, while a few kilometres away the sky may be clear. Syracuse, New York averages around 290 cm (115 in) of snow a year, and parts of the Tug Hill Plateau east of Lake Ontario exceed 500 cm; the Keweenaw Peninsula of Michigan records similar totals.

Impacts

Snowbelt cities are adapted with large snow-removal fleets, snow fences and building practices for heavy roof loads, but extreme events can paralyse the region, as in Buffalo's November 2014 and December 2022 storms. The abundant, reliable snow also supports ski resorts and winter tourism, notably in western New York and Michigan.
