Rotten and pocket boroughs

Image: Old Sarum, the most notorious rotten borough

A rotten borough or pocket borough was a parliamentary borough or constituency in England, Great Britain, or the United Kingdom before the Reform Act 1832 that had a very small electorate and could be used by a patron to exercise undue and unrepresentative influence within the unreformed House of Commons.

Background

A rotten borough was one that had once been prosperous but had declined economically, its population shrinking while it retained the right to send two members of Parliament. Old Sarum in Wiltshire, an abandoned hill fort with a handful of voters, was the most famous example. A pocket borough, by contrast, had a small but not necessarily vanished electorate that was effectively "in the pocket" of a local landowner, who could nominate the MPs.

Reform

The borough system persisted because many MPs sitting for such boroughs would not vote themselves out of existence. After a long campaign, the Reform Act 1832 disenfranchised 56 boroughs entirely and reduced the representation of many others, redistributing seats to the growing industrial cities such as Manchester and Birmingham, which had previously had no MPs of their own.
