Gill raker

Gill rakers project from the gill arches and help strain food particles from the water.

Gill rakers in fish are bony or cartilaginous processes that project from the branchial (gill) arch and are involved with suspension feeding tiny prey. They are not to be confused with the gill filaments, which form the respiratory part of the gill. Rakers are usually present in two rows, front and back, along the edges of the gill arch.

Function

As water taken in through the mouth passes out through the gills, the rakers act as a sieve, trapping food particles and directing them toward the oesophagus while protecting the delicate gill filaments from damage. In filter-feeding fishes such as herring, paddlefish, and whale sharks, the rakers are long, numerous, and closely set, forming an effective filter for plankton. In predatory fishes they are short, widely spaced, and often toothed, serving mainly to keep struggling prey from escaping through the gills.

Use in taxonomy

Because the number and form of gill rakers are closely related to diet and are relatively constant within species, ichthyologists routinely count rakers on the first gill arch as a diagnostic character for identifying fish species and for distinguishing closely related forms, such as freshwater and anadromous populations of whitefish.
