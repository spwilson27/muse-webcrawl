Monomyth

The monomyth, or hero's journey, is a common template of stories that involve a hero who goes on an adventure, is victorious in a decisive crisis, and comes home changed or transformed. The concept was popularized by Joseph Campbell in his 1949 book The Hero with a Thousand Faces, drawing on the work of earlier scholars of mythology and psychology, including Otto Rank, Carl Jung, and James Joyce, from whose Finnegans Wake Campbell borrowed the term "monomyth."

Campbell summarized the pattern: "A hero ventures forth from the world of common day into a region of supernatural wonder: fabulous forces are there encountered and a decisive victory is won: the hero comes back from this mysterious adventure with the power to bestow boons on his fellow man."

Structure

Campbell divided the journey into three acts — Departure, Initiation, and Return — encompassing seventeen stages in total:

Departure

  • The Call to Adventure
  • Refusal of the Call
  • Supernatural Aid
  • The Crossing of the First Threshold
  • Belly of the Whale

Initiation

  • The Road of Trials
  • The Meeting with the Goddess
  • Woman as the Temptress
  • Atonement with the Father
  • Apotheosis
  • The Ultimate Boon

Return

  • Refusal of the Return
  • The Magic Flight
  • Rescue from Without
  • The Crossing of the Return Threshold
  • Master of the Two Worlds
  • Freedom to Live

Not all myths contain all stages, nor in this order; Campbell emphasized that the stages can be combined, omitted, or elaborated. Screenwriting teacher Christopher Vogler condensed the scheme into a twelve-stage model in The Writer's Journey (1992), which became influential in Hollywood.

Influence

The monomyth has had a major impact on modern storytelling. George Lucas credited Campbell's work as an influence on Star Wars, and the hero's journey structure is widely taught in creative writing and film. Campbell's ideas also permeate popular psychology and self-help literature.

Criticism

Folklorists and mythologists have criticized the monomyth as overly universal: selecting examples that fit while ignoring differences, imposing a single pattern (often centered on male heroes and psychoanalytic interpretation) on diverse traditions. Scholars such as Alan Dundes and Barre Toelken argued that Campbell cherry-picked sources and that many narratives do not follow the pattern. Others note alternative journey structures centered on heroines, collectives, or cyclical time.
