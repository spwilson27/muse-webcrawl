Optical coherence tomography (OCT) is a non-invasive imaging technique that uses low-coherence light interferometry to capture micrometre-resolution cross-sectional images of tissue. Analogous to ultrasound but using light instead of sound, OCT measures the echo time delay and intensity of backscattered light to build two- and three-dimensional images of subsurface structure, typically to a depth of 1–2 millimetres in scattering tissue.

Principle

In OCT, light from a broadband source is split between a reference arm and the sample. Reflections from structures within the tissue interfere with the reference beam only when their path lengths match within the source's short coherence length, so scanning the system yields a depth profile (A-scan) at each point; sweeping the beam laterally assembles cross-sections (B-scans) and volumes. Modern systems use spectral-domain or swept-source detection, acquiring hundreds of thousands of A-scans per second.

History

OCT was first demonstrated in 1991 by a group led by James Fujimoto at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, with David Huang as first author of the landmark Science paper. It was commercialised for ophthalmology in the mid-1990s and has since become one of the most widely performed imaging procedures in medicine, with tens of millions of scans each year.

Applications

OCT transformed ophthalmology: it resolves the individual layers of the retina and is indispensable for diagnosing and monitoring age-related macular degeneration, diabetic macular oedema, and glaucoma, and for assessing responses to anti-VEGF therapy. Intravascular OCT, delivered by a catheter, images coronary artery walls and stent placement. Other applications include dermatology, gastroenterology (via endoscopic probes), dentistry, and non-medical uses such as art conservation and materials inspection. OCT angiography extends the technique to map blood flow without injected dye.
