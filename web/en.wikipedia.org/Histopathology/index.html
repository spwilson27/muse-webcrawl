Histopathology

Histopathology (from Greek histos "tissue", pathos "suffering", logia "study") is the microscopic examination of tissue in order to study and diagnose disease. In clinical practice it is the work of specialist physicians — histopathologists, a branch of anatomic pathology — who examine surgical biopsies and resection specimens and issue the diagnoses on which much of medical treatment, especially cancer treatment, depends. It is often called the "gold standard" of diagnosis against which other tests are calibrated.

Procedure

Tissue removed from the patient is fixed, usually in formalin, to preserve its structure; it is then processed, embedded in paraffin wax, and cut into extremely thin sections (typically 3–5 micrometres) with a microtome. Sections are mounted on glass slides and stained — most routinely with haematoxylin and eosin (H&E), which colours nuclei blue-purple and cytoplasm and connective tissue pink — before examination under the microscope. Special stains highlight particular features (organisms, amyloid, iron), and immunohistochemistry uses antibodies to detect specific proteins, now essential for classifying tumours and selecting targeted therapies.

Rapid and specialised techniques

Where a diagnosis is needed during an operation, a frozen section is prepared within minutes by freezing fresh tissue, at some cost in quality. Histopathological reporting describes the type of lesion, its grade (degree of differentiation), its extent and margins, and staging information such as invasion and lymph-node involvement. Increasingly, histology is complemented by molecular and genetic testing of the same tissue.

History

The discipline rests on the microscope work of the 19th century and on Rudolf Virchow's cellular pathology (1858), which established that disease arises in cells and tissues. Histopathology remains the definitive method for diagnosing most cancers and many inflammatory, infectious and degenerative diseases.
