Hirschsprung disease (congenital aganglionic megacolon) is a birth defect in which nerves are missing from parts of the intestine. It occurs when neural crest cells fail to migrate completely during fetal development, leaving a segment of colon — most often the rectosigmoid region — without ganglion cells of the enteric nervous system. Without these nerves the affected segment cannot relax, producing a functional obstruction.

Signs and symptoms

Newborns typically fail to pass meconium within the first 48 hours of life and develop abdominal distension, bilious vomiting and feeding difficulty. Older infants and children present with severe chronic constipation and poor growth. A serious complication is Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis, which can be life-threatening.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is suggested by contrast enema showing a narrow aganglionic segment with a dilated proximal colon, supported by anorectal manometry, and confirmed by rectal biopsy demonstrating absent ganglion cells. Mutations in the RET proto-oncogene account for many cases, and the condition is associated with Down syndrome. Treatment is surgical: the aganglionic segment is removed and healthy bowel is pulled through to the anus ("pull-through" procedures, e.g. Swenson, Duhamel and Soave), usually in a single operation in infancy. Most children do well, though some have long-term bowel problems.

The disease is named after the Danish paediatrician Harald Hirschsprung, who described it in 1888.
