Skene's gland

In female human anatomy, Skene's glands or the Skene glands (also known as the lesser vestibular glands, paraurethral glands, or female prostate) are glands located around the lower end of the urethra. The glands are surrounded by tissue that swells with blood during sexual arousal, and they secrete a fluid from openings near the urethral orifice.

The glands were described by the French surgeon Alphonse Guérin and named after the Scottish gynaecologist Alexander Skene, who described them in 1880. They are homologous to the male prostate gland, deriving from the same embryological tissue, and they produce prostate-specific antigen (PSA) and prostatic acid phosphatase.

Function and clinical significance

The glands are believed to secrete a mucus-like fluid that lubricates the urethral opening, and they are associated in the literature with female ejaculation, a topic on which research findings remain contested. Clinically, the glands can become infected (skenitis), obstructed, or develop cysts, which may be mistaken for urethral diverticula and usually respond to antibiotics or surgical marsupialisation.
