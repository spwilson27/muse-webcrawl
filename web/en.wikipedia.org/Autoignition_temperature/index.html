Autoignition temperature

Laboratory apparatus used to determine the autoignition temperature of a substance.

The autoignition temperature or self-ignition temperature of a substance is the lowest temperature at which it will spontaneously ignite in a normal atmosphere without an external source of ignition, such as a flame or spark. This temperature is required to supply the activation energy needed for combustion. The temperature at which a chemical ignites decreases as the pressure is increased or the concentration of oxygen is increased. It is usually applied to a combustible fuel mixture.

Measurement

Autoignition temperatures of liquid chemicals are typically measured using a 500 mL flask placed in a temperature-controlled oven in accordance with the procedure described in ASTM E659. The value depends on the test method, the contact time and the conditions, so published figures for the same substance can vary; a longer exposure lowers the observed temperature because ignition is a time–temperature phenomenon.

Examples

Representative values include: white phosphorus, about 34 °C (it ignites in air near room temperature); carbon disulfide, about 90 °C; diethyl ether, about 160 °C; paper, about 218–246 °C (the origin of the famous figure of 451 °F); gasoline, about 247–280 °C; magnesium, about 473 °C; and hydrogen, about 536 °C. By contrast, the flash point of a fuel — the lowest temperature at which it gives off vapour that can ignite with an ignition source — is much lower than its autoignition temperature; confusing the two is a common error in fire safety.

Applications

Autoignition is the working principle of the diesel engine, in which air is compressed until its temperature exceeds the autoignition temperature of the injected fuel. It is also a hazard in the storage of oily rags, hay and coal, where slow oxidation can raise the temperature of a poorly ventilated mass until spontaneous ignition occurs.
