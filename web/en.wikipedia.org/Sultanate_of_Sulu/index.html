The Sultanate of Sulu (Tausug: Kasultanan sin Sūg) was a Muslim state in the Sulu Archipelago of the southwestern Philippines, founded in the early 15th century. At its height its influence extended over the islands between western Mindanao and Borneo, and it maintained that it had received overlordship of the northeastern coast of Borneo — the historical basis of the Philippine claim to Sabah.

History

Tradition dates the sultanate's founding to around 1405, when the Arab-Malay scholar Sayyid Abu Bakr Abirin, who had married into the local royal family of Buansa, became its first sultan. Islam spread from Sulu through the southern Philippines, and the sultanate grew rich on maritime trade — and raiding — across the Sulu and Celebes Seas. Sulu traded with China, and its divers and sailors were famous for pearl fishing.

From the 16th century the sultanate resisted Spanish colonization for over three centuries in the long Moro–Spanish conflict; Spain occupied Jolo only in 1876. In 1878 the sultan signed an agreement with British commercial interests over North Borneo — its interpretation (cession versus lease) remains disputed to this day. After the Spanish–American War, the United States governed the area under the Bates Treaty (1899) and then direct rule; the 1915 Carpenter Agreement extinguished the sultan's remaining temporal powers.

Legacy

The sultanate endures as a cultural and political institution: rival claimants have continued to style themselves Sultan of Sulu, and the North Borneo question resurfaced dramatically in the 2013 Lahad Datu standoff in Sabah. The sultanate also figures prominently in Tausug identity and in debates over Bangsamoro autonomy in the Philippines.
