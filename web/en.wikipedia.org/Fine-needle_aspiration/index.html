Fine-needle aspiration

Fine-needle aspiration
Other namesFine-needle aspiration biopsy (FNAB); fine-needle aspiration cytology (FNAC)
SpecialtyPathology, radiology
PurposeObtain cells from a lump or mass for microscopic examination
Common sitesThyroid, breast, lymph nodes, salivary glands, liver, pancreas

Fine-needle aspiration (FNA) is a diagnostic procedure used to investigate lumps or masses. In this technique, a thin (23–25 gauge), hollow needle is inserted into the mass to extract cells, which are then stained and examined under a microscope by a pathologist. Fine-needle aspirations are generally safe, minor surgical procedures, and are commonly used as an alternative to open surgical biopsy.

Applications

Fine needle aspiration is widely used to evaluate palpable and image-detected masses. The most common applications include the investigation of thyroid nodules, breast lumps, enlarged lymph nodes, and salivary gland tumours. When a lump cannot be felt, or its location is deep within the body, the procedure may be guided by ultrasound, CT scan, or mammography. In the thyroid, FNA results are commonly reported using the Bethesda System for Reporting Thyroid Cytopathology.

Procedure

The skin above the area to be biopsied is swabbed with an antiseptic solution and draped with sterile towels. Local anaesthetic may be used. The needle is passed into the mass, often several times in different directions, while suction is applied or capillary action draws cells into the needle. The aspirated material is expelled onto microscope slides, smeared, fixed and stained for cytological examination.

Advantages and limitations

FNA is quick, relatively painless, inexpensive, and usually avoids the need for an operation. However, because it samples only individual cells rather than tissue architecture, it may be less definitive than a core-needle or excisional biopsy, and a non-diagnostic or indeterminate result may require repeat sampling or histological biopsy.

History

Aspiration of tumours with needles was described in the nineteenth century, but the modern technique was developed in the 1950s and 1960s, notably at the Karolinska Institute in Stockholm, Sweden, where it became a routine diagnostic method before spreading internationally.
