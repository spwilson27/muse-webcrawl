Tambov Rebellion

The Tambov Rebellion (1920–1921), also called the Antonovshchina, was one of the largest peasant uprisings against the Bolshevik government during the Russian Civil War. It took place in Tambov Governorate, south of Moscow, and was led by Alexander Antonov, a former Socialist Revolutionary.

Causes

The revolt was provoked by prodrazvyorstka, the Bolshevik policy of forced grain requisitioning, which seized peasant harvests at fixed (and effectively confiscatory) quotas, often taking seed grain and leaving villages to starve. In August 1920, resistance to a requisitioning detachment in the village of Khitrovo escalated into open rebellion that spread rapidly through the region.

The uprising

Antonov organized the insurgents into a surprisingly disciplined partisan army, at its height numbering tens of thousands, supported by a political organization, the Union of Toiling Peasantry, which demanded the overthrow of the Communist regime, free elections to the Constituent Assembly, and an end to requisitioning. The rebels controlled large parts of Tambov province for months.

Suppression

The Red Army, freed by the end of major White offensives, deployed overwhelming force under Mikhail Tukhachevsky, including artillery, aircraft, and — controversially — poison gas against rebel-held villages and forests. Concentration camps were established for hostages and rebels' families. The rebellion was crushed by mid-1921, though remnants held out into 1922. Tens of thousands were killed, imprisoned, or deported. The crisis contributed to Lenin's decision to replace requisitioning with a fixed tax in kind under the New Economic Policy (NEP).
