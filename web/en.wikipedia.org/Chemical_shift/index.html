Chemical shift

In nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, the chemical shift is the resonant frequency of an atomic nucleus relative to a standard in a magnetic field. It is the position and number of chemical shifts that reveal the electronic environment of nuclei and hence the structure of a molecule.

Definition

Chemical shift δ is expressed in parts per million (ppm) as δ = (νsample − νref) / νref × 106, making values independent of the spectrometer's field strength. For proton and carbon-13 NMR the usual reference is tetramethylsilane (TMS), assigned δ = 0. Nuclei shielded by surrounding electron density resonate at lower frequency ("upfield", smaller δ), while deshielded nuclei appear "downfield" (larger δ).

Chemical shift ranges

In ¹H NMR, most organic protons appear between 0 and 12 ppm: alkyl protons near 1 ppm, protons on carbons bearing oxygen near 3–4 ppm, aromatic and alkene protons around 5–8 ppm, aldehydes near 9–10 ppm and carboxylic acids around 11–12 ppm. ¹³C shifts span roughly 0–220 ppm. Electronegative substituents, ring currents in aromatic systems, hydrogen bonding and solvent all influence the shift, and tables of characteristic ranges underpin routine structure determination.

Applications

Chemical shift information is central to NMR-based structure elucidation in chemistry and to chemical-shift imaging and spectroscopy in medicine and biochemistry. Related phenomena include spin–spin coupling (splitting of signals) and the Knight shift in metals.
