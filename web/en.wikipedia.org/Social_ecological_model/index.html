Social ecological model

Social ecological model
FieldPublic health, psychology, sociology
Key figureUrie Bronfenbrenner
LevelsIndividual, interpersonal (relationships), community, organisational, societal/policy
ApplicationsHealth promotion, violence prevention, health behaviour interventions

The social ecological model is a framework for understanding the dynamic interrelations among personal and environmental factors in human behaviour. Rooted in Urie Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory (1979), which described child development as embedded in nested systems from the immediate microsystem to the broader macrosystem, the model was adapted by public health researchers—including McLeroy and colleagues in 1988—into a model of health behaviour with multiple levels of influence.

Levels of influence

  • Individual: knowledge, attitudes, beliefs, skills, and biology.
  • Interpersonal: family, friends, peers, and social networks that shape identity and provide support or pressure.
  • Organisational: schools, workplaces, and institutions whose rules and culture affect behaviour.
  • Community: relationships among organisations, neighbourhood characteristics, and the built environment.
  • Societal / policy: laws, policies, cultural norms, and economic structures.

Principles and use

The model holds that behaviour both affects and is affected by the social environment, and that interventions are most effective when they act at several levels simultaneously. It guides major public health programmes: the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention uses a four-level social ecological model for violence prevention, and the framework informs strategies on obesity, smoking, infectious disease, and health disparities. Rather than blaming individuals for unhealthy choices, the model directs attention to environments that make healthy choices easier or harder.

Criticism

Critics note that the model can be descriptively useful but predictively weak: it lists influences without specifying how they interact or which are most powerful, and multi-level interventions are costly and difficult to evaluate.
