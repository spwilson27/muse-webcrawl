Criterion of embarrassment

The criterion of embarrassment is a tool of historical analysis used in biblical studies, particularly in research on the historical Jesus. It holds that accounts or details which would have been embarrassing or awkward for the early Christian community are more likely to be historically authentic, because authors and transmitters would have had no motive to invent material that created difficulties for their own cause.

Application

Classic examples include the baptism of Jesus by John the Baptist — which implies John's superiority or Jesus' need for a baptism of repentance — and the crucifixion itself, since a crucified messiah was, in Paul's words, a "stumbling block" to Jews and folly to Gentiles. The denial of Jesus by Peter and the discovery of the empty tomb by women, whose testimony carried little legal weight in the ancient world, are also frequently cited.

Criticism

Critics caution that what counts as "embarrassing" depends on modern assumptions about ancient audiences, and that a detail might serve a theological or literary purpose even if it appears awkward. The criterion is therefore generally used alongside other criteria of authenticity, such as multiple attestation and coherence, rather than on its own.
