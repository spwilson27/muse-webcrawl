Aspartame

TypeArtificial sweetener
Chemical nameMethyl L-α-aspartyl-L-phenylalaninate
FormulaC14H18N2O5
Sweetness~200 times sucrose
E numberE951

Aspartame is an artificial non-saccharide sweetener commonly used as a low-calorie sugar substitute in foods and beverages. It is about 200 times sweeter than sucrose, so only very small quantities are needed, and its caloric contribution is negligible in practice.

Discovery and approval

Aspartame was discovered accidentally in 1965 by chemist James Schlatter at G.D. Searle & Company while working on an anti-ulcer drug. After lengthy review, it was approved for use in dry foods in the United States in 1981 and in carbonated beverages in 1983, and later in many other countries. It is marketed under brand names such as NutraSweet and Equal.

Metabolism and safety

In the body aspartame is broken down into phenylalanine, aspartic acid, and methanol. Because of the phenylalanine content, products containing aspartame must be avoided by people with phenylketonuria (PKU). Regulatory agencies including the FDA and EFSA have repeatedly reviewed its safety and maintained an acceptable daily intake, though it remains one of the most publicly debated food additives; in 2023 the IARC classified it as "possibly carcinogenic" (Group 2B) while JECFA reaffirmed the established intake limit.

Uses

Aspartame is used in diet soft drinks, sugar-free gums, desserts, and tabletop sweeteners. It is unstable at high temperatures and prolonged heating, so it is less suitable for baking than some other sweeteners.
