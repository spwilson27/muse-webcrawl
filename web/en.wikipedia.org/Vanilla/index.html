Vanilla

Vanilla pods and a vanilla orchid flower (Vanilla planifolia).

Vanilla is a flavouring derived from orchids of the genus Vanilla, primarily obtained from pods of the flat-leaved vanilla (Vanilla planifolia). The plant is a vine native to Mexico and Central America, where it was cultivated by Mesoamerican peoples long before the Spanish conquest. The word "vanilla" derives from the Spanish vainilla, a diminutive of vaina (sheath or pod).

Vanilla is one of the most popular flavourings in the world and one of the most expensive spices by weight, after saffron. Its high price reflects the labour-intensive cultivation: the flowers must be pollinated by hand outside their native range, and the green pods undergo a lengthy curing process of sweating and drying over several months before they develop their characteristic aroma. The principal flavour compound is vanillin, one of several hundred compounds in natural vanilla extract.

History

The Totonac people of the Gulf Coast of Mexico were among the earliest cultivators of vanilla, and the Aztecs used it to flavour their chocolate drink, xocolatl. After the conquest, the Spanish brought vanilla to Europe, where it was used in chocolate and later in its own right. For centuries Mexico held a monopoly, because the flowers were pollinated by specific native bees. In 1841, Edmond Albius, a twelve-year-old enslaved boy on the island of Réunion, devised a practical hand-pollination method still used today, which allowed cultivation to spread to Madagascar, the Comoros, Indonesia, and other tropical regions. Madagascar became and remains the largest producer, especially of "Bourbon vanilla".

Production and preparation

Vanilla vines are grown on supports or trees in hot, humid climates. The flowers open for only a single morning and must be pollinated by hand within hours. The resulting pods are harvested while still unripe and green, then cured — typically by blanching, sweating in cloth or boxes, and slow sun-drying — during which enzymatic reactions produce vanillin and the pods turn dark brown or black and become supple and aromatic. The main commercial forms are whole pods, vanilla extract (an alcoholic solution), vanilla powder, and vanilla sugar. Because natural vanilla is costly, most "vanilla-flavoured" products use synthetic vanillin, made from guaiacol, lignin, or other feedstocks.

Uses

Vanilla is used above all in sweet foods: ice cream, custards, cakes, chocolate, and soft drinks such as cola and cream soda. It is also used in perfumery, in pharmaceuticals to mask unpleasant flavours, and in aromatherapy. In cooking, pods may be split and the seeds scraped out; both pod and seeds carry flavour. Beyond its culinary role, "vanilla" has acquired a figurative meaning in English for something plain or conventional, because vanilla is the default flavour of ice cream.

Chemistry

Vanillin (4-hydroxy-3-methoxybenzaldehyde) is the primary component of the aroma of cured vanilla beans, but natural vanilla contains many minor compounds — including vanillic acid, p-hydroxybenzaldehyde, and various sugars and phenolics — that give it a complexity synthetic vanillin lacks. Analytical methods can distinguish natural from synthetic vanillin by isotopic composition.
