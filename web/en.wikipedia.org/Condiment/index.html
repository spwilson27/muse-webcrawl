Condiment

An assortment of condiments.

A condiment is a preparation that is added to food, typically after cooking, to impart a specific flavour, to enhance the flavour, or to complement the dish. The term originally described pickled or preserved foods, but its meaning has broadened to include sauces, relishes, mustards, and spice mixtures served at the table. Some condiments are added during cooking, such as soy sauce, but many are applied by the diner in specific quantities.

History

Condiments have ancient roots: the Romans used garum, a fermented fish sauce, much as Asian cuisines use fish sauce today, and medieval cooks prepared elaborate spiced sauces such as cameline. Mustard, ketchup, and mayonnaise became industrial products in the 18th and 19th centuries, and the development of canning, bottling, and refrigeration made commercial condiments a global business. Heinz ketchup, Tabasco sauce, and Worcestershire sauce are among the best-known branded examples.

Types

Common condiments include table sauces such as ketchup, mayonnaise, mustard, barbecue sauce, and hot sauce; fermented preparations such as soy sauce, fish sauce, and Worcestershire sauce; relishes and chutneys; pickled vegetables; and dry seasonings used at the table, from salt and pepper to spice blends like Japanese shichimi. Cultures have characteristic condiments: salsa in Mexican cooking, harissa in North Africa, wasabi and soy sauce in Japan, and HP Sauce in Britain.

Market and health

Condiments form a major segment of the food industry, and product development increasingly targets reduced sugar, salt, and artificial additives in response to health concerns, since many sweet and fatty condiments are calorie-dense and are consumed in addition to, rather than instead of, other foods.
