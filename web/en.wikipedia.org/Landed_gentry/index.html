Landed gentry

The landed gentry, or simply the gentry, is a largely historical British social class of landowners who lived on the income from their estates without needing to work, ranking below the peerage but above the yeomanry. Gentry families provided the justices of the peace, members of Parliament, and officers who ran local government in England for centuries.

Composition

In the formal order of precedence the gentry comprised, in descending order, baronets, knights, esquires, and gentlemen. Membership rested on landownership, armorial bearings, and recognition by one's peers; new fortunes from trade or the professions could enter the class by purchasing an estate. Genealogies of the class were recorded in reference works such as Burke's Landed Gentry, first published in the 1830s.

Role and decline

Below the great aristocratic magnates, the gentry dominated county society and the House of Commons until the later 19th century. Their position was eroded by the agricultural depression of the 1870s, the introduction of death duties in 1894, the losses of the First World War, and the great wave of estate and country-house sales after 1918, which broke up many holdings. Analogous landowning classes existed elsewhere, including the Anglo-Irish gentry, Polish szlachta, German Junker, and the scholar-gentry of imperial China, who held status through office and examination rather than land alone.
