Modular arithmetic

In mathematics, modular arithmetic is a system of arithmetic for integers in which numbers "wrap around" when reaching a certain value, called the modulus. The modern approach was introduced by Carl Friedrich Gauss in his Disquisitiones Arithmeticae of 1801. The familiar example is the 12-hour clock: if it is 9 o'clock now, it will be 2 o'clock in five hours, because 9 + 5 = 14 is replaced by its remainder on division by 12, namely 2.

Congruence

Two integers a and b are congruent modulo n, written a ≡ b (mod n), if n divides their difference — equivalently, if they leave the same remainder on division by n. Congruence is an equivalence relation, and addition, subtraction and multiplication of whole equations are preserved under it. The integers modulo n form a ring, denoted Z/nZ; when n is prime this ring is a field, in which every non-zero element has a multiplicative inverse.

Key results

Foundational theorems of modular arithmetic include Fermat's little theorem (ap ≡ a mod p for prime p), Euler's theorem generalising it using the totient function, the Chinese remainder theorem for solving simultaneous congruences with coprime moduli, and Wilson's theorem characterising primes by factorials. Modular inverses, computed with the extended Euclidean algorithm, allow "division" modulo n whenever the divisor is coprime to the modulus.

Applications

Modular arithmetic is fundamental to number theory and pervasive in computing: it underlies checksums and hash functions, cyclic redundancy checks, pseudorandom number generators and the ISBN and credit-card check-digit schemes. It is indispensable in cryptography — RSA, Diffie–Hellman key exchange and elliptic-curve systems all operate in modular structures — and in computer science, where fixed-width machine arithmetic is inherently arithmetic modulo 2k.
