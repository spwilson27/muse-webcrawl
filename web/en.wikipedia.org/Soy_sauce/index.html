Soy sauce

Soy sauce in a small dish

Soy sauce (also called simply soy in American English and soya sauce in British English) is a liquid condiment of Chinese origin, traditionally made from a fermented paste of soybeans, roasted grain, brine, and Aspergillus oryzae or Aspergillus sojae moulds. It is considered to contain a strong umami taste.

History

Soy sauce in its current form was created in China about 2,200 years ago during the Western Han dynasty, evolving from fermented pastes such as jiang. From China it spread throughout East and Southeast Asia, where it became a staple seasoning. Japanese shōyu was developed in the medieval period, notably at Yuasa in Wakayama Prefecture, and was introduced to Europe by Dutch traders in the 17th century.

Production

Traditional brewed soy sauce is made by culturing soybeans and wheat with kōji mould, mixing the result with brine to form a mash called moromi, and fermenting for weeks to months before pressing, pasteurising and bottling. A cheaper method, acid hydrolysis of defatted soy meal, produces "chemical" soy sauce much more quickly but with a different flavour profile.

Varieties and use

Chinese soy sauces are divided into light (thin, salty, for flavouring) and dark (aged with molasses, for colour). Japanese varieties include koikuchi, usukuchi, tamari (made with little or no wheat), shiro and saishikomi. Korean ganjang and Indonesian kecap manis (sweetened) are further regional forms. Soy sauce is used as a cooking ingredient, marinade and table condiment throughout Asian cuisines and, increasingly, worldwide.
