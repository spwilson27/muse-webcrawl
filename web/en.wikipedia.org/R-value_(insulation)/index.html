R-value (insulation)

In building and construction, the R-value is a measure of how well a two-dimensional barrier, such as a layer of insulation, a window, or a complete wall, resists the conductive flow of heat. The higher the R-value, the greater the insulating effectiveness. Formally, R-value is the ratio of the temperature difference across an insulator to the heat flux through it.

Units

In the United States, R-values are given in imperial units of ft²·°F·h/Btu. The metric equivalent is the RSI value in m²·K/W; an R-value of 1 (imperial) equals about 0.176 RSI. Building codes specify minimum R-values for walls, ceilings, and floors by climate zone.

Factors affecting performance

The R-value of insulation depends on the material and its thickness — fiberglass batts give roughly R-3 to R-4 per inch, while rigid foam boards can exceed R-6 per inch. Installed performance is reduced by air leakage, moisture, compression, and thermal bridging through studs, so the effective R-value of a whole assembly is lower than that of the insulation alone.

Relation to U-value

The R-value is the reciprocal of the U-value (thermal transmittance): U = 1/R. Windows are commonly rated by U-factor in North America, with lower values indicating better performance.
