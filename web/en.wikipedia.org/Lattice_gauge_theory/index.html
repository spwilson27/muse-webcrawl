Lattice gauge theory

In physics, lattice gauge theory is the study of gauge theories on a spacetime that has been discretised into a lattice. It provides a non-perturbative formulation of quantum field theory, most importantly quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the theory of the strong interaction.

Formulation

The framework was introduced by Kenneth Wilson in 1974. Gauge fields are represented by group elements ("links") on the edges between lattice sites, while matter fields live on sites. The action is built from plaquettes — products of links around elementary squares — ensuring exact gauge invariance at finite lattice spacing. The continuum theory is recovered as the lattice spacing tends to zero.

Lattice QCD

Lattice QCD calculations are performed by Monte Carlo sampling of field configurations on supercomputers. They yield first-principles predictions of hadron masses, decay constants, and the quark–gluon plasma equation of state, and provide evidence for quark confinement: the Wilson loop area law shows the potential between static quarks rising linearly with distance.

Challenges

Simulating fermions on a lattice is subtle because of the fermion-doubling problem; solutions include Wilson, staggered, domain-wall, and overlap fermions. Finite volume, finite spacing, and the sign problem at nonzero baryon density remain active areas of research.
