Venona project

The Venona project was a United States counterintelligence program initiated during World War II by the United States Army's Signal Intelligence Service and later absorbed into the National Security Agency (NSA). The project ran from 1 February 1943 until 1 October 1980, and involved the cryptanalysis of messages sent by several Soviet intelligence agencies, including the KGB and GRU, to their stations in the United States.

Background

During the early 1940s, the Soviet Union used ostensibly secure one-time pad systems for its diplomatic and intelligence communications. Errors in the manufacture of the pads in the Soviet Union during the chaos of the German invasion in 1941–42 led to some pages being duplicated, which made portions of the traffic vulnerable to cryptanalysis. American codebreakers at Arlington Hall in Virginia, including Meredith Gardner, gradually succeeded in reconstructing parts of the codebooks and reading thousands of messages.

Findings

The decrypts revealed extensive Soviet espionage in the United States during the 1940s, including penetration of the Manhattan Project. Codenames appearing in the traffic were linked by historians and investigators to figures such as Julius Rosenberg, Klaus Fuchs, and Alger Hiss, and the decrypts corroborated information from defectors. The project identified hundreds of individuals who had cooperated with Soviet intelligence, many of whom were never prosecuted because the evidence could not be revealed in court without compromising the source.

Disclosure

Venona remained highly classified for decades. Its existence was publicly acknowledged and the first decrypts released in 1995, after the end of the Cold War. The releases substantially influenced historical debate about Soviet espionage and the early Cold War, including continuing controversy over the guilt or innocence of some individuals named in the messages.
