Double negation

In classical logic, ¬¬P has the same truth value as P.

Double negation is the repetition of negation in language or logic. In classical logic, the double negation of a proposition P (written ¬¬P, "not not-P") is logically equivalent to P itself; the rule permitting their interchange is called double negation elimination (or introduction, in the reverse direction). In natural language, however, double negatives may either cancel ("I didn't see nothing" meaning "I saw nothing" in some dialects) or intensify the negation, depending on the language and dialect.

In logic

Double negation elimination is valid in classical logic but not in intuitionistic logic, where ¬¬P is weaker than P: from "it is impossible that P is false" an intuitionist cannot conclude that a proof of P exists. The failure of this rule is one of the defining differences between the systems. Gödel and Gentzen showed that classical logic can nonetheless be embedded into intuitionistic logic by a "double-negation translation".

In language

Two negatives make an affirmative in standard English ("I am not unconvinced"), but many languages and English dialects use negative concord, in which multiple negative forms reinforce a single negation — as in Spanish No vi nada ("I didn't see anything") or African American Vernacular English. Chaucer and Shakespeare used stacked negatives for emphasis, a usage later stigmatised by 18th-century prescriptive grammarians.
