Kinase

In biochemistry, a kinase is an enzyme that catalyses the transfer of a phosphate group from a high-energy donor molecule, usually ATP, to a specific substrate molecule. This process, called phosphorylation, is one of the most common mechanisms for regulating protein activity, switching enzymes and receptors on or off and transmitting signals within and between cells.

Types

Kinases are classified by the substrate they phosphorylate. Protein kinases — the largest group — are further divided by the amino acid residue they modify: serine/threonine kinases, tyrosine kinases, and dual-specificity kinases. Other classes include lipid kinases (such as phosphoinositide 3-kinase), carbohydrate kinases (such as hexokinase, the first enzyme of glycolysis) and nucleoside kinases.

Role in signalling and disease

The human genome encodes more than 500 protein kinases, together called the kinome. Cascades such as the MAPK/ERK pathway relay signals from the cell surface to the nucleus through successive phosphorylation steps. Because mutations that leave kinases permanently active drive many cancers, kinase inhibitors have become a major class of drugs, beginning with imatinib, which blocks the BCR-ABL tyrosine kinase in chronic myeloid leukaemia.
