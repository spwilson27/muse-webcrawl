Indexicality

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the philosophy of language, linguistics, and semiotics, indexicality is the phenomenon of a sign or expression whose meaning or reference depends directly on the context in which it is used. Indexical expressions include personal pronouns (I, you), demonstratives (this, that), and adverbs such as here, now, today, and tomorrow.

The notion derives from the semiotic theory of Charles Sanders Peirce, who classified the index as a sign connected to its object by a real, causal, or spatiotemporal relation — as smoke is an index of fire, or a pointing finger is an index of its target — in contrast to icons (which signify by resemblance) and symbols (which signify by convention).

Character and content

David Kaplan's influential theory distinguishes an expression's character — the rule determining its content in a context — from its content in a particular utterance. The character of "I" is constant: it refers to the speaker. But its content shifts: when two different people say "I am tired", they express different propositions. Kaplan argued that indexicals are directly referential: their content is the referent itself, not a description.

In social science

Ethnomethodologists such as Harold Garfinkel treated all practical action as irreducibly indexical: instructions, rules, and accounts always rely on unstated background understandings to be applied in concrete situations. Linguistic anthropologists extend the idea to social indexicality — the way pronunciation, word choice, and register point to ("index") a speaker's identity, stance, or social relationships.
