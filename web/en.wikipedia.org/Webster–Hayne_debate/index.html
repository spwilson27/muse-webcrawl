Webster–Hayne debate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Webster–Hayne debate was a famous exchange in the United States Senate in January 1830 between Senator Daniel Webster of Massachusetts and Senator Robert Y. Hayne of South Carolina. Ostensibly about a resolution by Senator Samuel Foot of Connecticut proposing to restrict the sale of public lands in the West, the debate became a celebrated confrontation over the nature of the Union, states' rights, and the doctrine of nullification.

The debate

Hayne, a leading spokesman for the South and an ally of Vice President John C. Calhoun, defended the theory that the Constitution was a compact among sovereign states, which retained the right to nullify unconstitutional federal laws. Webster, in his "Second Reply to Hayne" delivered on January 26–27, 1830, argued that the Constitution was made by "the people" as a whole and that the Union was perpetual and indivisible. His peroration — "Liberty and Union, now and for ever, one and inseparable!" — became one of the most quoted passages in American oratory.

Significance

The debate was widely reported and reprinted, and Webster's speech was long memorised by American schoolchildren. It crystallised the opposing constitutional theories that would eventually collide in the Nullification Crisis of 1832–33 and, a generation later, in the Civil War. Abraham Lincoln's arguments for the Union echoed Webster's.
