Newton's method, also known as the Newton–Raphson method, is a root-finding algorithm in numerical analysis that produces successively better approximations to the roots (zeroes) of a real-valued function. Starting from an initial guess, the method replaces the function with its tangent line and takes the tangent's x-intercept as the next, usually improved, approximation.

The iteration

For a function f and its derivative f′, the iteration is xn+1 = xn − f(xn)/f′(xn). Under favorable conditions—starting close enough to a simple root—the number of correct digits roughly doubles with each step, a property called quadratic convergence. A classic example is computing square roots: iterating xn+1 = (xn + a/xn)/2 converges rapidly to √a.

Limitations and generalizations

The method can fail when the derivative is near zero, when the starting point is far from any root, or when the iterates enter cycles; fractals drawn from Newton's method applied to complex polynomials reveal basins of attraction with intricate boundaries. The same idea generalizes to systems of equations (using the Jacobian matrix) and to optimization, where it is applied to the derivative to find maxima and minima.
