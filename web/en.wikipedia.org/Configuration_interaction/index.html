Configuration interaction

Mixing of electronic configurations improves the Hartree–Fock wavefunction.

Configuration interaction (CI) is a post-Hartree–Fock linear variational method for solving the nonrelativistic Schrödinger equation within the Born–Oppenheimer approximation for a quantum chemical multi-electron system. The wavefunction is constructed as a linear combination of configuration state functions, or equivalently Slater determinants, built from a set of spin orbitals: the single Hartree–Fock determinant plus determinants in which one, two, or more electrons are promoted to virtual orbitals.

Method

The expansion coefficients are found by diagonalising the Hamiltonian in the basis of determinants, applying the variational principle. Including all possible excitations gives full CI, which yields the exact energy within the given basis set but is feasible only for small systems. Practical truncations include CIS (singles, used mainly for excited states), CISD (singles and doubles), and CISDT (singles, doubles, and triples). Because most of the correlation energy recovered comes from double excitations, CISD was for many years a standard benchmark method in quantum chemistry.

Properties and limitations

CI is variational and systematically improvable, but truncated CI is not size-extensive: the energy of two separated molecules computed with CISD is not the sum of their individual CISD energies. This defect motivated coupled cluster theory, which dominates high-accuracy work, and corrections such as the Davidson correction estimate the missing higher-excitation contribution to CISD. Multireference CI (MRCI), built from several reference determinants, handles systems with strong static correlation such as bond breaking.

Applications

Configuration interaction remains important for computing accurate potential energy surfaces, spectroscopic constants, and excited-state properties of small molecules, and full CI serves as an exact benchmark for testing cheaper electronic structure methods.
