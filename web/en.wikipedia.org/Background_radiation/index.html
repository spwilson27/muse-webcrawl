Background radiation

Background radiation is the ionizing radiation present in the environment from natural and artificial sources other than the source being measured or discussed. Everyone is continuously exposed to a low level of background radiation; the worldwide average natural exposure is about 2.4 millisieverts (mSv) per year, though it varies greatly by location.

Background radiation
Type: Ionizing radiation
Average natural dose: ~2.4 mSv/year worldwide
Main source: Radon gas (inhalation)
Unit: Sievert (Sv)

Natural sources

Natural background radiation comes from cosmic rays and from radioactive elements in the Earth's crust, principally the decay chains of uranium and thorium and potassium-40 in rocks, soil, building materials, and even the human body. Inhaled radon gas, which seeps from the ground into buildings, accounts for roughly half of the average natural dose. Some regions — such as Ramsar in Iran and parts of Kerala, India, and Guarapari, Brazil — have unusually high natural background levels because of radioactive mineral deposits.

Artificial sources

Man-made contributions include fallout from historical nuclear weapons testing, emissions from nuclear facilities and accidents, and — now the largest artificial component in developed countries — medical uses of radiation such as CT scans. These are generally small compared with natural exposure.
