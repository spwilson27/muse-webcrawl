Staple (fastener)

A strip of standard office staples

A staple is a type of two-pronged fastener, usually metal, used for joining or binding together materials such as paper, wood, plastic, and masonry. Staples are driven through the material by a stapler or staple gun, and their legs are either bent flat against the back of the material (as in a desktop stapler, which folds them against an anvil plate) or remain straight, gripping the substrate (as with heavy-duty staple guns).

Types

Office staples are thin wire forms supplied in glued strips; the most common standard, the No. 10 and 26/6 sizes, bind up to about 20–30 sheets of paper. Heavier staples driven by manual, electric, or pneumatic staple guns are used in upholstery, construction, packaging, and cable installation. Surgical staples, made of stainless steel or titanium, close wounds and join tissue in place of sutures and are removed with a specialised tool. Staple shape is described by crown width, leg length, and wire gauge.

History

Fasteners called staples have existed for centuries in masonry and carpentry, but the modern paper staple developed alongside the stapler in the 19th century, with key patents in the 1860s–1870s and the introduction of pre-formed staple strips in the early 20th century. The word is unrelated to "staple" meaning a principal commodity, which has a separate origin in medieval markets.

Removal

Office staples are removed with a staple remover, a claw-like device that lifts the crown and unbends the legs; heavier staples require pry tools, and incorrect removal can damage the fastened material.
