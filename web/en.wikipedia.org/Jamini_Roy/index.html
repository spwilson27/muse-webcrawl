Jamini Roy (11 April 1887 – 24 April 1972) was an Indian painter, one of the most significant modernists of twentieth-century Indian art. Trained in the academic Western style, he famously abandoned it in favour of a deliberately indigenous idiom drawn from Bengal's folk traditions, especially the Kalighat temple paintings and the patua scroll painters of his native village.

Early life and training

Roy was born in Beliatore village in Bankura district, Bengal, into a moderately prosperous family. He studied at the Government School of Art in Calcutta under Abanindranath Tagore, graduating in 1908 in the European academic manner, and began his career as a commissioned portraitist.

The folk turn

From about 1921 Roy broke with academic naturalism, adopting the sweeping black lines, flat areas of earthy colour, and frontal, almond-eyed figures of Bengali folk art. His mature style reduced forms to bold, rhythmic contours; his subjects came from the Ramayana and the life of Krishna, from the Santal tribal people, and from village life — mothers and children, dancers, animals, and rural labour. He also painted some works on Christian themes. Working with inexpensive natural pigments on paper and cloth, he produced a vast output — he estimated over a thousand paintings a year at his peak — and signed works from his workshop, intending art to be affordable to ordinary people rather than a luxury.

Recognition

Roy's folk-based modernism was exhibited internationally from the 1940s, with shows in London and New York, and he became one of India's most honoured artists. He received the Padma Bhushan in 1954 and was elected a fellow of the Lalit Kala Akademi. His house and studio in Ballygunge, Kolkata, where he died in 1972, preserve his working environment; the National Gallery of Modern Art in New Delhi holds a major collection of his work.
