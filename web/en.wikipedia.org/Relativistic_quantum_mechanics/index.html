Relativistic quantum mechanics (RQM) is any Poincaré-covariant formulation of quantum mechanics. It applies to massive particles propagating at speeds comparable to the speed of light, and its most successful application has been in describing elementary particles and their interactions before the development of full quantum field theory.

Motivation

The Schrödinger equation treats time and space asymmetrically and rests on the non-relativistic kinetic energy p²/2m. Replacing this with the relativistic energy–momentum relation E² = p²c² + m²c⁴ yields the Klein–Gordon equation:

(□ + m²c²/ħ²)ψ = 0

Developed in 1926 by Oskar Klein, Walter Gordon, and others, it correctly describes spin-0 particles but produced negative probability densities, because its solutions include negative-energy states associated with a conserved current that is not positive definite.

The Dirac equation

In 1928 Paul Dirac sought an equation first-order in both space and time. His equation introduced four-component spinors and the Dirac matrices; spin emerged naturally rather than being added ad hoc, and the electron's magnetic moment (g-factor ≈ 2) was predicted. The negative-energy solutions were reinterpreted by Dirac as a "sea" of filled states whose holes are positrons — the first prediction of antimatter, confirmed when Carl Anderson discovered the positron in 1932.

Other equations and developments

The Proca equation describes massive spin-1 particles; the Rarita–Schwinger equation describes spin-3/2 particles; and the Bargmann–Wigner equations generalise the approach to arbitrary spin. Relativistic wave equations underlie the prediction of antiparticles, spin–statistics connections, and much of particle physics phenomenology.

Limitations

A single-particle interpretation of relativistic wave equations is inconsistent: localising a particle within its Compton wavelength requires energies sufficient to create particle–antiparticle pairs, so particle number is not conserved. The fully consistent framework is quantum field theory, in which fields rather than particles are fundamental and the relativistic wave equations reappear as field equations. Nevertheless, relativistic quantum mechanics remains a useful approximate framework for problems where pair creation is negligible.
