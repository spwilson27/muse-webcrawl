The Eurasian wren (Troglodytes troglodytes), also simply called the wren, is a very small insectivorous bird, and the only member of the wren family Troglodytidae found in Eurasia and Africa (Maghreb). It is famous for its very loud, complex song in relation to its small size. The English name "wren" derives from Old English wrenna; the scientific name derives from the Greek troglodytes ("cave-dweller"), from the bird's habit of disappearing into crevices whilst hunting or roosting.

Description

The wren is a plump, rusty-brown bird, 9–10 cm long, with fine barring, a pale supercilium, and a short, stiffly cocked tail. It has a pointed bill for picking insects and spiders from bark and leaf litter. The sexes are alike.

Distribution and behaviour

It breeds across Europe, a belt of Asia from northern Iran and Afghanistan to Japan, in a wide variety of habitats including woodland, reedbeds, moorland, offshore islands, and gardens. Northern populations migrate south in severe winters. In cold weather, many wrens may roost communally in a single nest box or cavity for warmth — over 60 have been recorded in one box. The male builds several domed "cock nests"; the female lines one for the clutch of five to eight eggs.

In culture

The wren appears in folklore across Europe as the "king of the birds" (from an Aesop fable of the wren outwitting the eagle) and in the St Stephen's Day "hunting the wren" tradition in Ireland and Wales. It featured on the British farthing coin (1937–1956).
