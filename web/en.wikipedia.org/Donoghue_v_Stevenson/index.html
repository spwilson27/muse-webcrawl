Donoghue v Stevenson

An opaque ginger beer bottle, like the one said to contain a decomposed snail.

Donoghue v Stevenson [1932] AC 562 is a landmark case in Scots and English law decided by the House of Lords. It founded the modern tort of negligence by establishing a general duty of care and the "neighbour principle". The case arose from an allegation that a decomposed snail had been found in a bottle of ginger beer manufactured by the defender, David Stevenson.

Facts

On 26 August 1928 May Donoghue went to a café in Paisley, Scotland, where her friend bought her a Scotsman ice cream float — a glass of ginger beer poured from an opaque bottle over ice cream. After drinking some, Donoghue claimed the remains of a decomposed snail fell out of the bottle, causing her shock and severe gastroenteritis. Because she had not bought the drink herself, she had no contract with either the café or the manufacturer, and sued the manufacturer in delict.

Judgment

By a majority of 3–2, the House of Lords held that Stevenson owed Donoghue a duty of care. Lord Atkin famously formulated the neighbour principle: "You must take reasonable care to avoid acts or omissions which you can reasonably foresee would be likely to injure your neighbour" — neighbours being persons so closely and directly affected that one ought reasonably to have them in contemplation.

Significance

The decision created a general duty of care owed by manufacturers to ultimate consumers and, more broadly, transformed negligence from a collection of specific duties into a general principle of liability. It is routinely cited across the Commonwealth and remains a cornerstone of tort law teaching. The case eventually settled out of court in 1934.
