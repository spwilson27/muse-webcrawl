Communitarianism

Communitarianism is a philosophy that emphasizes the connection between the individual and the community. Its overriding philosophy is based upon the belief that a person's social identity and personality are largely molded by community relationships, together with a tendency to value social cohesion and the common good alongside individual rights.

Origins and development

Although the term originated in the 19th century, modern communitarianism took shape in the 1980s as a critique of the liberal political philosophy associated with John Rawls's A Theory of Justice (1971). Philosophers such as Michael Sandel, Alasdair MacIntyre, Charles Taylor, and Michael Walzer argued that liberalism presented an implausibly atomistic picture of the self — an "unencumbered self" abstracted from the constitutive attachments, traditions, and shared understandings that give moral life its content.

Key ideas

Communitarians stress that communities are not merely aggregations of individuals but sources of meaning and value. Many advocate a balance between rights and responsibilities, criticizing both state paternalism and market individualism. In the 1990s the sociologist Amitai Etzioni and others developed a "responsive communitarianism" expressed in a platform calling for a moratorium on the minting of new rights and for renewed attention to civic virtues and social duties.

Criticism

Critics argue that communitarianism can slide into majoritarianism or an oppressive traditionalism, subordinating individual liberty and minority rights to communal norms. Others note that "community" is itself contested and plural, and that the liberal–communitarian debate has largely been superseded by theories that incorporate insights from both sides.
