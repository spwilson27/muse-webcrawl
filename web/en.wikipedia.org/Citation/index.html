Citation

A citation is a reference to a source of information — a book, article, website, legal decision, or other work — that supports statements in a text and allows readers to locate the original. Citations are fundamental to scholarship, journalism, and law, and failing to cite borrowed words or ideas constitutes plagiarism.

Styles

Citation conventions vary by discipline and are codified in style guides, including the Chicago Manual of Style (notes–bibliography and author–date systems), MLA style (humanities), APA style (social sciences), Vancouver style (medicine), and the citation manuals of law, such as The Bluebook in the United States. A typical citation gives author, title, publisher or journal, date, and pinpoint pages; digital works add a DOI or URL. Parenthetical in-text references, footnotes, and endnotes are the main placements, usually feeding a bibliography or works-cited list.

Functions

Beyond crediting sources, citations document evidence, enable verification and further reading, and map the structure of scholarly fields. Citation analysis — counting and network-mapping citations, pioneered by Eugene Garfield and the Science Citation Index — underlies bibliometrics and measures such as the impact factor and h-index. Legal citation similarly identifies precedents with precision, recording court, year, reporter volume, and page.
