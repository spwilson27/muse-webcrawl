Berkeley Open Infrastructure for Network Computing

The Berkeley Open Infrastructure for Network Computing (BOINC) is an open-source software platform for volunteer computing — the use of idle home and institutional computers, donated by the public, to perform scientific computation. Developed at the University of California, Berkeley under the leadership of David P. Anderson and launched in 2002, BOINC grew out of the success of SETI@home. Project servers distribute small units of work to volunteers' computers, which process them with a client application and return results for validation; the system handles redundancy, credit accounting, and cross-platform deployment automatically.

How it works

A BOINC project consists of servers that generate "workunits," split into tasks sent to volunteer's hosts. The BOINC client runs project science applications at low priority when the machine is idle. Results are typically validated by replication — sending the same task to several hosts and comparing outputs — and volunteers earn "credit" (measured in cobblestones) recorded on public leaderboards, which has spawned a global community of teams and competitions. GPU computing support (CUDA and OpenCL) greatly increased throughput from the late 2000s.

Notable projects

Well-known BOINC projects include SETI@home (searching radio data for extraterrestrial signals; hibernated in 2020), Einstein@Home (gravitational-wave and pulsar searches), Rosetta@home and Folding@home-adjacent protein research, ClimatePrediction.net, MilkyWay@home, World Community Grid (hosted by IBM and later Krembil Research Institute), and PrimeGrid. Together, BOINC volunteers have at times sustained computing power comparable to major supercomputers.
