Iconoscope

The iconoscope, an early television camera tube developed at RCA.

The iconoscope was the first practical video camera tube to be used in early television cameras. It was invented by Vladimir K. Zworykin and his team at RCA, with a patent filed in 1923 and a working system demonstrated in the early 1930s. The iconoscope employed a high-velocity electron beam scanning a photoemissive mosaic of silver globules deposited on a mica plate, an arrangement that stored charge between scans and thereby greatly improved sensitivity compared with earlier image dissector tubes.

History

Zworykin filed his first patent application for an all-electronic television system in 1923 while working at Westinghouse, but the iconoscope only became practical after his move to RCA in 1929. A rival device, the image dissector developed by Philo Farnsworth, competed with it throughout the 1930s. The iconoscope formed the basis of the cameras used in the first regular electronic television broadcasts, including the BBC's high-definition service from Alexandra Palace beginning in 1936, which used a closely related tube, the Emitron.

Operation

Light focused onto the mosaic produced a charge image proportional to the brightness of each point of the scene. A scanning electron beam then discharged each globule in sequence, generating a video signal. The iconoscope required very bright studio lighting and suffered from spurious signals, tilts, and black-level instability. It was superseded after the Second World War by the image orthicon and later the vidicon, which offered far better sensitivity and picture quality.
