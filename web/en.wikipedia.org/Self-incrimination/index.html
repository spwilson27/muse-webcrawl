Self-incrimination

Self-incrimination is the act of exposing oneself, or another person, to criminal prosecution by making a statement or providing evidence that links the speaker to a criminal act. The privilege against self-incrimination is a legal protection, recognised in many jurisdictions, that allows a person to refuse to answer questions or produce evidence that would incriminate them.

United States

In the United States, the privilege derives from the Fifth Amendment to the Constitution, which provides that no person "shall be compelled in any criminal case to be a witness against himself." The right applies in criminal trials, and also in civil, legislative, and administrative proceedings where testimony could expose the witness to prosecution. In Miranda v. Arizona (1966), the Supreme Court held that detained suspects must be informed of their right to remain silent before custodial interrogation. Colloquially, invoking the privilege is known as "pleading the Fifth" or "taking the Fifth."

The privilege protects testimonial communications; it generally does not prevent the state from compelling physical evidence such as blood samples, handwriting exemplars, or participation in a line-up. Prosecutors may overcome the privilege by granting immunity, after which the witness can be compelled to testify.

Other jurisdictions

The right of silence and protection against compelled self-incrimination appear in the law of most common-law countries and in international instruments such as the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (Article 14(3)(g)). The scope varies: in England and Wales, courts may in some cases draw adverse inferences from a defendant's silence, a qualification not permitted in United States criminal trials.
