Electromagnetic mass

Electromagnetic mass
FieldClassical electrodynamics, history of physics
Key figuresJ. J. Thomson, Lorentz, Abraham, Kaufmann

Electromagnetic mass was a concept in late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century physics according to which some or all of the mass of a charged particle arises from its own electromagnetic field. Because a moving charge's field carries energy and momentum, the charge behaves as though it has additional inertia.

Development

J. J. Thomson showed in 1881 that a charged sphere moving through the ether experiences an electromagnetic resistance to acceleration. Hendrik Lorentz and Max Abraham developed models of the electron as a charged sphere whose mass increases with velocity, and Walter Kaufmann's experiments on cathode rays appeared to confirm a velocity-dependent mass, stimulating debate about the structure of the electron.

Relation to relativity

The attempt to derive all of the electron's mass electromagnetically encountered difficulties, including the famous "4/3 problem" and the need for non-electromagnetic Poincaré stresses to hold the electron together. Einstein's special relativity (1905) recast mass–energy relations generally through E = mc², making a purely electromagnetic origin of mass unnecessary, and the concept survives mainly as an episode in the history of physics.
