Henrietta Swan Leavitt

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Henrietta Swan Leavitt (July 4, 1868 – December 12, 1921) was an American astronomer. Working at Harvard College Observatory as one of the "Harvard Computers", she discovered the period–luminosity relation for Cepheid variable stars — the relationship between how fast such a star pulsates and how bright it intrinsically is. This discovery provided astronomers with the first standard candle for measuring distances to galaxies and underpinned Edwin Hubble's demonstration that the universe extends far beyond the Milky Way.

Life and career

Leavitt was born in Lancaster, Massachusetts, the daughter of a Congregational minister. She graduated from Radcliffe College (then the Society for the Collegiate Instruction of Women) in 1892, having taken up astronomy late in her studies. An illness left her profoundly deaf. She joined the staff of Harvard College Observatory under Edward Charles Pickering in 1893, initially as a volunteer and later as a permanent member paid — like the other women computers — about 25 to 30 cents an hour. She eventually became head of the photographic photometry department.

The period–luminosity relation

Leavitt was assigned to study variable stars in the Small and Large Magellanic Clouds using photographic plates taken at Harvard's southern station in Arequipa, Peru. By 1908 she had catalogued 1,777 variable stars, and she noticed that the Cepheids with longer periods appeared brighter. In a 1912 paper (published under Pickering's name as a Harvard Circular, with her contribution noted), she confirmed the relation with 25 Cepheids in the Small Magellanic Cloud: the period of pulsation is tightly related to luminosity. Because all these stars lie at roughly the same distance, their apparent brightness reflected intrinsic brightness; once the relation was calibrated, measuring a Cepheid's period anywhere gave its true brightness and hence its distance.

Impact and recognition

Ejnar Hertzsprung and Harlow Shapley calibrated Leavitt's law; Shapley used it to measure the size of the Milky Way, and Hubble used Cepheids in the Andromeda Nebula in 1924–25 to show it was a separate galaxy, transforming cosmology. Leavitt died of cancer in 1921, aged 53, little known outside astronomy. In 1925 the Swedish mathematician Gösta Mittag-Leffler, unaware of her death, wrote to propose her for the Nobel Prize in Physics. Asteroid 5383 Leavitt and the lunar crater Leavitt are named in her honour.
