Enrico Fermi (29 September 1901 – 28 November 1954) was an Italian and naturalised American physicist and the creator of the world's first artificial nuclear reactor, Chicago Pile-1. He has been called the "architect of the nuclear age" and is regarded as one of the few physicists to excel in both theoretical and experimental physics.

Life and work

Born in Rome, Fermi studied at the Scuola Normale Superiore in Pisa and became professor of theoretical physics at the University of Rome in 1926. There he developed Fermi–Dirac statistics (1926) and the theory of beta decay (1933–34), introducing the weak interaction, and led experiments bombarding elements with neutrons, discovering that slow neutrons were especially effective at inducing radioactivity. He received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1938 for this work. That year, fleeing Italy's racial laws (his wife Laura was Jewish), he emigrated to the United States.

At Columbia University and then the University of Chicago, Fermi led the team that on 2 December 1942 achieved the first self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction in Chicago Pile-1, built under the stands of Stagg Field. He became a key figure in the Manhattan Project, working at Los Alamos, where he was present at the Trinity test. After the war he returned to Chicago, co-founded its Institute for Nuclear Studies, and mentored a generation of physicists including several future Nobel laureates. He died of stomach cancer in 1954, aged 53.

Legacy

The element fermium (Fm, 100) is named after him, as are Fermilab, the Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope, Fermi–Dirac statistics, the Fermi paradox about extraterrestrial life, and Fermi problems — order-of-magnitude estimation questions exemplified by his legendary back-of-the-envelope calculations.
