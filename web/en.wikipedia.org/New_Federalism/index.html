CountryUnited States

New Federalism is a political philosophy of devolution in the United States, centred on returning powers and responsibilities previously concentrated in the federal government to the states and the people. The term was first prominently used in the administration of President Richard Nixon, and the approach was revived and expanded under President Ronald Reagan. It stands in contrast to the centralising tendencies of the New Deal and Great Society eras.

Nixon's New Federalism

Nixon proposed giving state and local governments greater discretion over federal funds, most notably through general revenue sharing, enacted in 1972, which distributed federal money to states and localities with few strings attached. His administration also consolidated categorical grants into block grants in areas such as manpower training and community development.

Reagan's New Federalism

Reagan's 1981–82 initiative sought a sweeping sorting-out of responsibilities: the federal government would assume full responsibility for Medicaid, while states would take over the food stamp programme (SNAP) and Aid to Families with Dependent Children, along with dozens of other programmes, supported temporarily by a federal trust fund. The full "swap" was never enacted, but Reagan succeeded in consolidating numerous categorical grants into block grants and in reducing federal aid to states in real terms. The philosophy influenced later reforms, including the 1996 welfare reform law, which converted AFDC into the state-administered block grant Temporary Assistance for Needy Families.

Judicial New Federalism

A parallel development occurred in the courts. Under Chief Justice William Rehnquist, the Supreme Court revived limits on federal power under the Commerce Clause in cases such as United States v. Lopez (1995) and restricted Congress's ability to compel state action in Printz v. United States (1997). Separately, "judicial" new federalism also refers to state courts interpreting their own constitutions to provide greater rights protections than the federal floor.

Criticism

Critics argue that devolution produces unequal services between wealthy and poor states, weakens national standards, and can shift costs onto state budgets. Supporters contend that state governments are closer to citizens, more innovative, and more accountable.
