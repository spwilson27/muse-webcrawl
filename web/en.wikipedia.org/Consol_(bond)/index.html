Consol (bond)

Consols (originally short for consolidated annuities) were a form of British government bond — perpetual bonds with no maturity date, paying a fixed coupon forever unless redeemed by the government. First issued in 1751, they became a cornerstone of British public finance and of the incomes of generations of investors.

History

The original 3% consols were created by Chancellor of the Exchequer Henry Pelham by consolidating several government annuities into a single stock. Successive issues financed Britain's wars, including the Napoleonic Wars, and the coupon was reduced in the 19th century, famously to 2.5% under Chancellor George Goschen in 1888. Consols were quoted daily in London and served as the benchmark long-term interest rate for the British economy.

Redemption

Because the bonds were redeemable at par at the government's option, they could be retired once refinancing became attractive. The last remaining consols and related perpetual gilts were finally redeemed by the UK Treasury in 2014–2015, ending more than 260 years of continuous existence.

In finance theory

The consol is the textbook example of a perpetuity: its price equals the annual coupon divided by the yield, a formula that underlies the Gordon growth model and much of asset pricing pedagogy.
