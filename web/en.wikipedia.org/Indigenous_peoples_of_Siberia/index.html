Indigenous peoples of Siberia

The indigenous peoples of Siberia are the ethnic groups native to the vast region of North Asia stretching from the Ural Mountains to the Pacific Ocean. They include Turkic peoples such as the Sakha (Yakuts), Tuvans, Khakas and Altaians; Mongolic Buryats; Uralic peoples such as the Khanty, Mansi, Nenets, Selkup and Sami-related groups; Tungusic peoples such as the Evenki and Evens; and the Paleo-Siberian peoples — Chukchi, Koryaks, Itelmens and Yukaghirs — whose languages have no confirmed wider affiliations. Russian law recognises a category of "Indigenous small-numbered peoples of the North, Siberia and the Far East", numbering some 40 groups protected as indigenous minorities, while the larger peoples such as the Sakha and Buryats have their own republics.

History

Russian expansion into Siberia began in the late 16th century with the campaigns of Yermak, bringing the fur tax (yasak), epidemic disease and settlement that sharply reduced indigenous populations. Shamanism, clan organisation and reindeer nomadism persisted in many regions despite missionisation. Under the Soviet Union, collectivisation, boarding schools and industrial development transformed indigenous life; the post-1991 period saw an ethnic revival and the founding of the Russian Association of Indigenous Peoples of the North (RAIPON) in 1990.

Cultures

Traditional livelihoods include reindeer herding, hunting, fishing and gathering across tundra, taiga and steppe. Several Siberian peoples, notably the Sakha, Turkic and Buryat groups, form the historical bridge between the indigenous peoples of Asia and, via ancient migrations across Beringia, the indigenous peoples of the Americas, whose languages and genetics preserve distant Siberian connections.
