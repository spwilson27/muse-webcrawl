Erythropoietin (/ᵻˌrᵻθroʊˈpɔɪ.ᵻtᵻn/), commonly abbreviated as EPO, is a peptide hormone produced by the kidneys (85%–90%) and to a lesser extent by the liver. It binds to erythropoietin receptors (EPOR) on red-cell progenitor cells in the bone marrow and triggers red blood cell production (erythropoiesis), increasing the blood's oxygen-carrying capacity.

Physiology

The kidney is exquisitely sensitive to arterial oxygen content through the hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) pathway; when oxygen falls (e.g. at altitude or in anaemia), EPO secretion rises, and red-cell mass increases over days to weeks. EPO also has additional effects on the nervous system and tumour biology, although these are less established than its haematopoietic role.

Medical use

Recombinant human EPO (epoetin alfa, marketed as Epogen and Procrit/Eprex) is used to treat anaemia due to chronic kidney disease, chemotherapy, antiretroviral therapy, and to reduce transfusion in surgery. The drug lasts about 24 hours and is administered intravenously or subcutaneously. Longer-acting variants (darbepoetin alfa) followed. Risks include hypertension, increased blood viscosity, and thromboembolic events; rare pure red-cell aplasia was linked to certain formulations.

Doping in sport

Because it increases aerobic capacity, recombinant EPO was widely abused as a performance-enhancing drug in endurance sports, particularly professional cycling during the 1990s and 2000s. It is banned by the World Anti-Doping Agency; detection tests distinguish recombinant from natural EPO by molecular characteristics.
