Aphrodite

Aphrodite is the ancient Greek goddess of love, beauty, pleasure, and procreation. Her Roman equivalent is Venus. She is identified with the planet Venus, and her major symbols include myrtles, roses, doves, sparrows, and swans. The cult of Aphrodite was largely derived from that of the Phoenician goddess Astarte, a cognate of the East Semitic goddess Ishtar, whose cult was based on the Sumerian cult of Inanna.

Etymology

Hesiod derives Aphrodite from aphros ("sea-foam"), interpreting the name as "risen from the foam", but most modern scholars regard this as a spurious folk etymology. The name more likely derives from a Semitic theonym related to Astarte.

Origins

According to Hesiod's Theogony, Aphrodite was born when Cronus severed Uranus's genitals and threw them into the sea; from the foam (aphros) that gathered around them, the goddess arose and was carried to Cyprus. In the Iliad, by contrast, she is the daughter of Zeus and Dione.

Worship

Aphrodite's main cult centres were Cythera, Cyprus, Corinth, and Athens. Her festivals included the Aphrodisia, celebrated in various cities across Greece. She was honoured as Aphrodite Ourania ("heavenly") and Aphrodite Pandemos ("of all the people"), aspects that Plato in the Symposium distinguished as two separate goddesses.

Mythology

Though married to Hephaestus, Aphrodite's most famous lover was Ares, god of war; their affair was exposed by Helios and the two were caught in a net by the humiliated Hephaestus. She loved the mortal Adonis, whose death at the tusks of a boar is one of Greek mythology's most poignant tales, and she was the mother of Aeneas by the Trojan Anchises. In the judgement of Paris she bribed Paris with the love of Helen, precipitating the Trojan War.

In art

Aphrodite was a favourite subject of ancient sculpture, from the Venus de Milo to the Aphrodite of Knidos by Praxiteles, the first life-size female nude in Greek art. She was widely depicted in Renaissance and later painting, notably in Botticelli's Birth of Venus.
