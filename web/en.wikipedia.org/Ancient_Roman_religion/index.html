Ancient Roman religion

Ancient Roman religion comprised the various religions practised in ancient Rome and Italy and the cults carried by its people into the provinces. Rome's religious system was fundamentally polytheistic and ritualistic, placing great emphasis on the correct performance of rites (orthopraxy) to maintain the pax deorum, the "peace of the gods", on which the state's welfare was thought to depend.

Gods and cults

The supreme deity was Jupiter, worshipped on the Capitoline Hill together with Juno and Minerva as the Capitoline Triad. Other major gods included Mars, Neptune, Venus, Apollo, Diana, Ceres, Vulcan, Vesta, and Mercury — many identified with Greek counterparts through interpretatio graeca. Household religion centred on the lares and penates, guardian spirits of the home, and on ancestor veneration. Rome was famously absorptive, adopting foreign cults such as those of Cybele, Isis, and Mithras.

Priesthoods and ritual

Religion was a public and political affair: the major priestly colleges — the pontifices (headed by the Pontifex Maximus), augurs, and the keepers of the Sibylline Books — were staffed by leading citizens. Augury and haruspicy (examining entrails) were used to discern divine will before public undertakings. Festivals crowded the Roman calendar, and the Vestal Virgins tended the sacred fire of Vesta.

Imperial cult

From Augustus onward, the worship of the emperor's genius and, after death, of deified emperors (divi) became a unifying loyalty cult across the Empire, though its forms differed between the Greek East and the Latin West.

Toward Christianity

Roman religion coexisted, sometimes uneasily, with Judaism and the growing Christian movement. In the 4th century CE, Christianity became the favoured and then the state religion under Constantine and Theodosius I, and traditional public cults were progressively suppressed by the end of the century.
