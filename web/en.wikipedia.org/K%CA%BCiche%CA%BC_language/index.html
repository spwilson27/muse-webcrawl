Kʼicheʼ language

Kʼicheʼ (also spelled Quiché) is a Mayan language spoken by the Kʼicheʼ people of the central highlands of Guatemala. With more than a million speakers, it is the most widely spoken Mayan language and the second most spoken language in Guatemala after Spanish. It belongs to the Quichean branch of the eastern Mayan languages.

History

Kʼicheʼ was the language of the powerful Kʼicheʼ kingdom of Qʼumarkaj, conquered by Pedro de Alvarado in 1524. In the colonial period it was written in a Latin-based orthography; the most famous work in Classical Kʼicheʼ is the Popol Vuh, the mythico-historical narrative of the Kʼicheʼ people, transcribed in the 1550s and preserved through the copy made by the friar Francisco Ximénez around 1701–1703. Other important texts include the Título de Totonicapán and the dance-drama Rabinal Achí.

Linguistic features

Like other Mayan languages, Kʼicheʼ is ergative–absolutive in its verbal agreement, has a large set of positional roots, and contrasts plain and glottalised (ejective) consonants. It has five short and five long vowels in most descriptions, though many modern dialects have developed a six-vowel system. Word order is typically verb-initial (VOS or VSO). The standard orthography is that of the Academia de Lenguas Mayas de Guatemala (ALMG), established in 1990.

Status

Kʼicheʼ is recognised as a national language of Guatemala. It is used in bilingual intercultural education and in local media, although Spanish dominates in government and commerce.
