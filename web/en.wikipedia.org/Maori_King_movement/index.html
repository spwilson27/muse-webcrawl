Maori King movement

Tūrangawaewae Marae, Ngāruawāhia
Founded:1858
First monarch:Pōtatau Te Wherowhero
Seat:Tūrangawaewae, Ngāruawāhia
Region:Waikato, New Zealand

The Māori King movement, called the Kīngitanga in the Māori language, is a movement that arose among some of the Māori iwi (tribes) of New Zealand in the central North Island in the 1850s, to establish a role similar in status to that of the monarch of the British colonists, as a way of halting the alienation of Māori land and uniting Māori in the face of settler encroachment.

Origins

In June 1858, after years of discussion among tribal leaders, the Waikato chief Pōtatau Te Wherowhero was installed as the first Māori King at Ngāruawāhia. The movement sought a single figurehead who could negotiate with the Crown as an equal and impose a rāhui (prohibition) on further land sales to settlers. The Kīngitanga drew on both Māori tradition and Old Testament models of kingship.

War and confiscation

The colonial government regarded the movement as a challenge to British sovereignty. In 1863 Governor George Grey ordered the invasion of the Waikato, sparking the Waikato War of the New Zealand Wars. King Tāwhiao and his followers were driven into exile in the rugged King Country, while some 1.2 million acres of Waikato land were confiscated (raupatu). Tāwhiao did not lay down arms formally until 1881.

Modern role

The monarchy is hereditary in practice, centred on Tūrangawaewae Marae at Ngāruawāhia. The monarch has no constitutional or legal authority within the New Zealand state, but the office carries great mana and acts as a focus of Māori unity and a voice on treaty and social issues. Successive monarchs include Mahuta, Te Rata, Korokī, and Dame Te Atairangikaahu, who reigned from 1966 until her death in 2006 — the longest-serving Māori monarch — and was succeeded by her son Tūheitia. Following Tūheitia's death in 2024, his daughter Ngā Wai Hono i te Pō was chosen as the eighth Māori monarch.
