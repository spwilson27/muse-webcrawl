Homelessness

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Homelessness, also known as homelessness crisis or houselessness, is the condition of lacking stable, safe, and functional housing. It includes situations where a person or a family lacks a fixed, regular, and adequate nighttime residence, such as living on the streets, in shelters, in transitional housing, or in places not meant for human habitation. Definitions vary between jurisdictions: some count only "literal homelessness" (sleeping rough or in emergency accommodation), while others, such as the European ETHOS typology, also include people in insecure or inadequate housing.

Globally, the United Nations estimated in 2005 that around 100 million people were homeless worldwide, and as many as 1.6 billion lacked adequate housing; more recent estimates suggest roughly 150 million people experience homelessness and over a billion live in informal settlements or slums. In 2024 the United States recorded more than 770,000 people experiencing homelessness on a single night, the highest figure since national counts began.

Causes

Research identifies structural causes — shortages of affordable housing, rising rents, stagnant wages, unemployment, poverty, and the loss of social housing — alongside individual vulnerabilities such as mental illness, substance use disorders, domestic violence, family breakdown, and discharge from institutions (prisons, hospitals, foster care) without accommodation. Natural disasters, war, and displacement create acute homelessness on large scales. Scholars generally emphasise that homelessness results when personal vulnerabilities coincide with structural pressures and the absence of support.

Effects

People experiencing homelessness face greatly elevated risks of illness, violence, and premature death; studies in several countries find average life expectancy decades below the housed population. Homelessness disrupts children's education and is associated with worse physical and mental health outcomes. Sleeping rough is the most visible form, but most homeless people are sheltered in emergency accommodation, staying temporarily with others ("sofa surfing"), or living in vehicles or encampments.

Responses

Policy responses include emergency shelters, transitional housing, permanent supportive housing, and prevention programmes such as eviction mediation and rent assistance. The Housing First model, developed in New York in the 1990s and adopted nationally in Finland, provides permanent housing without preconditions of treatment or sobriety, coupled with voluntary support services; it has been credited with large reductions in chronic homelessness where implemented at scale. Debates continue over encampment clearances, the criminalisation of rough sleeping, and the balance between enforcement and services.
