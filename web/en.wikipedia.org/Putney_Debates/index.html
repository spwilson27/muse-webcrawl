Putney Debates

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Putney Debates were a series of discussions between representatives of the New Model Army — including its senior officers (Grandees) and elected Agitators from the ranks, many associated with the Levellers — held in the Church of St Mary the Virgin, Putney, in the autumn of 1647. The debates concerned the constitutional settlement of England after the First English Civil War, and are regarded as a foundational moment in the history of modern democracy.

Background

After Parliament's victory over Charles I, the Army — radicalised by arrears of pay and indemnity grievances — became a political force. Its elected Agitators promoted the Agreement of the People, a proposed written constitution asserting popular sovereignty. The General Council of the Army met at Putney from 28 October 1647 to consider the Agreement and an officers' alternative, the Heads of the Proposals. The discussions were recorded in shorthand by the secretary William Clarke and rediscovered in the nineteenth century in a library at Worcester College, Oxford.

The debates

The central question was the franchise: should the vote extend to all adult men, or only to property holders? Oliver Cromwell and Henry Ireton, representing the Grandees, argued that only those with a "permanent fixed interest" in the kingdom — property — should vote, warning that universal suffrage threatened property itself. The Leveller spokesman Colonel Thomas Rainsborough replied in the debates' most famous passage:

"For really I think that the poorest he that is in England hath a life to live, as the greatest he; and therefore truly, sir, I think it's clear, that every man that is to live under a government ought first by his own consent to put himself under that government."

The meetings ended without agreement in early November amid news of the king's escape from Hampton Court, which reunited the Army. The Leveller programme was later suppressed at the Burford mutiny of 1649.

Legacy

The Putney Debates are celebrated as an early, remarkably direct articulation of democratic principles — government by consent, equality before the law and (for some speakers) near-universal male suffrage — and are commemorated at St Mary's Church, Putney, where the debates took place.
