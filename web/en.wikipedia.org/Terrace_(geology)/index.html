Terrace (geology)

In geology, a terrace is a step-like landform: a relatively flat, horizontal or gently inclined surface bounded on one side by a steeper ascending slope and on the other by a steeper descending slope. Terraces are commonly produced when a river, lake, or sea that once flowed or stood at a higher level cuts down or recedes, leaving its former bed or shoreline stranded as a bench above the present water level. Flights of terraces record the history of uplift, climate change, and base-level fall in a landscape.

Fluvial terraces

River terraces are the most familiar type. When a river incises — because of tectonic uplift, a fall in base level, or a change in discharge and sediment load — it abandons its former floodplain, which remains as a flat tread above the new channel, separated by a steep riser. Paired terraces occur at matching heights on both sides of the valley when incision is mainly vertical; unpaired terraces form staircases when the river also migrates sideways. Bedrock (strath) terraces have thin sediment caps, while fill terraces consist largely of old alluvium. Because their deposits can be dated, terrace sequences are key archives of Quaternary climate and tectonics; the Thames and the Rhine have celebrated terrace staircases.

Other types

Marine terraces are former wave-cut platforms and beaches raised above the sea by uplift, as along the coasts of California and New Zealand. Lacustrine terraces mark shorelines of shrunken lakes such as pluvial Lake Bonneville. Kame terraces form against glacier margins, and structural or fault terraces arise from differential erosion or faulting. Artificial terracing for agriculture imitates the form.
