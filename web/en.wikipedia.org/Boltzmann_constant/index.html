The Boltzmann constant (kB or k) is the physical constant that relates the average relative thermal energy of particles in a gas with the thermodynamic temperature of the gas. It occurs in the definitions of the kelvin and the gas constant, and in Planck's law of black-body radiation and Boltzmann's entropy formula.

S = k log W

Introduced by Max Planck in 1900 and named for Ludwig Boltzmann, the constant links the microscopic and macroscopic descriptions of matter: the average kinetic energy of a particle in an ideal gas is (3/2)kBT at absolute temperature T.

Value and the SI redefinition

Since the 2019 revision of the SI, the Boltzmann constant has the exact defined value 1.380649 × 10−23 joules per kelvin. The kelvin is now defined by fixing this value, rather than by reference to the triple point of water. In electronvolts, kB ≈ 8.617 × 10−5 eV/K.

Roles in physics

  • Ideal gas law: pV = NkBT, the particle-level form of the gas law.
  • Entropy: Boltzmann's formula S = k log W connects entropy to the number of microstates W.
  • Equipartition: each quadratic degree of freedom carries an average energy of (1/2)kBT.
  • Semiconductors: the thermal voltage VT = kBT/q governs diode behaviour.
  • Johnson–Nyquist noise: thermal noise power in a resistor is proportional to kBT.
