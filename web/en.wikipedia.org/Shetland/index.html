Shetland

Shetland, also called the Shetland Islands, is a subarctic archipelago of Scotland, lying around 170 km (105 mi) north of the Scottish mainland and about 230 km (140 mi) west of Bergen, Norway. It is Scotland's northernmost region and one of its 32 council areas. The islands comprise about 100 islands, 16 of them inhabited, with a population of roughly 23,000, about a third living in the only town, Lerwick. Shetland is closer to the Arctic Circle than to London, and its culture retains a strong Norse character from centuries of Scandinavian rule.

Geography and climate

The largest island, Mainland, is joined by the inhabited islands of Yell, Unst, Whalsay, Bressay, Fetlar, Papa Stour and others; remote outliers include Fair Isle, midway to Orkney, and Foula. The coast is deeply indented by voes (sea inlets), and nowhere is more than about 5 km from the sea. Despite its latitude (60°N), Shetland has a mild oceanic climate tempered by the North Atlantic Current, with cool summers, mild but stormy winters, and long summer daylight — the "simmer dim", when the sun barely sets — balanced by near-darkness in midwinter. Treeless, windswept landscapes of peat moorland and crofting townships predominate.

History

Shetland has been inhabited since the Mesolithic; Neolithic and Bronze Age monuments and Iron Age brochs — most famously Jarlshof and Mousa Broch, the best-preserved broch anywhere — attest to early settlement. Norse settlers arrived in the 8th–9th centuries, and the islands became part of the Norse earldom of Orkney and then the Norwegian crown. In 1469 Shetland was pawned to Scotland by Christian I of Denmark–Norway as security for the unpaid dowry of his daughter Margaret, betrothed to James III of Scotland; the pledge was never redeemed, and the islands were annexed to Scotland in 1472. Norse law, language (Norn, extinct by the 18th–19th century) and customs persisted long after.

Economy and culture

Fishing (whitefish, pelagic fish from Lerwick and Scalloway, and salmon and mussel farming) and the oil industry — centred on the Sullom Voe terminal, one of Europe's largest, receiving oil from North Sea fields since 1978 — dominate the economy, alongside crofting, knitwear (Fair Isle patterns and fine Shetland lace), and tourism. Cultural life features the fire festival Up Helly Aa in Lerwick each January, with a torchlit procession and the burning of a Viking galley; Shetland ponies, Shetland sheepdogs and the native sheep are internationally known. Seabird colonies (puffins, gannets at Hermaness and Noss) are of world importance.
