Non-Hodgkin lymphoma

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Non-Hodgkin lymphoma (NHL) is a group of blood cancers arising from lymphocytes — white blood cells of the immune system — that includes all lymphomas other than Hodgkin lymphoma. There are more than 60 distinct subtypes, broadly divided into B-cell lymphomas (about 85% of cases) and T-cell and NK-cell lymphomas. Common types include diffuse large B-cell lymphoma, the most frequent and an aggressive form, and follicular lymphoma, a typically indolent (slow-growing) form.

Signs, causes and diagnosis

The most common presentation is painless swelling of lymph nodes, often accompanied in advanced disease by "B symptoms": fever, drenching night sweats and unexplained weight loss. Risk factors include immune suppression (such as HIV infection or post-transplant immunosuppression), certain infections — Epstein–Barr virus, Helicobacter pylori (associated with gastric MALT lymphoma), hepatitis C, and HTLV-1 — autoimmune diseases, and some environmental exposures. Diagnosis rests on excisional lymph node biopsy with immunophenotyping and cytogenetic studies; staging uses imaging and bone marrow examination under the Ann Arbor/Lugano system.

Treatment and prognosis

Treatment depends on subtype and stage and may include watchful waiting for indolent disease, chemotherapy regimens such as CHOP combined with the anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody rituximab (R-CHOP), radiation therapy, targeted agents, stem-cell transplantation and CAR T-cell therapy. Prognosis varies widely by subtype, from highly curable aggressive lymphomas to chronic, manageable but usually incurable indolent ones.
