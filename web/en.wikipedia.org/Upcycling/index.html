Upcycling

Upcycling, also known as creative reuse, is the process of transforming by-products, waste materials, useless, or unwanted products into new materials or products perceived to be of greater quality, such as artistic value or environmental value.

Concept

The term was coined in 1994 by Reiner Pilz and was popularized by William McDonough and Michael Braungart in their 2002 book Cradle to Cradle: Remaking the Way We Make Things. Upcycling is the opposite of downcycling, in which recycling processes convert materials into lesser-quality products. Whereas recycling generally involves breaking materials down to their base state, upcycling keeps the material largely intact and reimagines its use — a glass jar becomes a lamp, a shipping pallet becomes furniture, discarded textiles become new garments.

Applications

Upcycling appears in art, fashion, interior design, and industry. Artists have long used found objects in assemblage and readymades; contemporary makers and online marketplaces have commercialized upcycled furniture, clothing made from reclaimed fabrics, and buildings incorporating salvaged components. In the developing world, resource scarcity has long made creative reuse a necessity, from repurposed oil drums to footwear made from tires.

Environmental significance

Supporters argue upcycling reduces consumption of new raw materials, lowers energy use and greenhouse-gas emissions compared with manufacturing from virgin inputs, and diverts waste from landfills. Critics note that upcycled products are often low-volume and artisanal, and that reuse can complicate eventual end-of-life processing. The concept is a component of circular economy thinking.
