Four-wheel drive, also called 4×4 ("four by four") or 4WD, is a two-axle vehicle drivetrain capable of providing torque to all four wheels simultaneously. It may be full-time or on-demand, and is typically linked via a transfer case providing an additional output drive shaft and, in many instances, additional gear ranges.

Terminology

A four-wheeled vehicle with torque supplied to both axles is described as "four-wheel drive" (4WD). However, "all-wheel drive" (AWD) is often used to describe systems that are permanently engaged or electronically controlled, typically in passenger cars, whereas 4WD traditionally denotes selectable systems with low-range gearing used in off-road vehicles and trucks.

History

The first four-wheel-drive car was arguably the Lohner–Porsche of 1900, designed by Ferdinand Porsche, which used electric hub motors. The Dutch Spyker 60 HP of 1903 is often cited as the first petrol-engined four-wheel-drive car with a mechanical drivetrain and a centre differential. The American Four Wheel Drive Auto Company, founded in 1909 in Clintonville, Wisconsin, popularised the technology in trucks. During the Second World War, the Willys MB "Jeep" made 4WD famous worldwide.

Systems

Part-time systems require the driver to engage four-wheel drive manually, usually via a lever or switch; they lack a centre differential and should not be used on dry paved surfaces because driveline wind-up can damage components. Full-time systems use a centre differential (sometimes lockable) that allows use on any surface. Modern vehicles increasingly use electronically controlled multi-plate clutches and torque vectoring.

Advantages and disadvantages

Four-wheel drive improves traction on slippery or loose surfaces, in mud, snow and sand, and when towing. Disadvantages include greater weight, higher cost, increased mechanical complexity and usually higher fuel consumption compared with two-wheel drive.
