Agriculture in the United Kingdom

Arable farmland in southern England.

Agriculture in the United Kingdom uses about 70 percent of the country's land area, employs roughly 1.5 percent of the workforce, and contributes around 0.5–0.6 percent of gross value added. It produces about 60 percent of the food consumed domestically by value. British agriculture is intensive and highly mechanized by world standards, with large average farm sizes, high yields of cereals, and a significant livestock sector.

Structure and production

The United Kingdom has on the order of 200,000 farm holdings across about 17 million hectares of agricultural land. The wetter west and north are dominated by grassland and livestock — sheep and cattle in upland areas such as Wales, Scotland and the Lake District — while the drier east of England is dominated by arable farming, growing wheat, barley, oilseed rape, sugar beet, potatoes and field vegetables. Dairy farming is concentrated in the west and southwest. Important horticultural regions include Kent, the Fens and the Vale of Evesham. The UK is among the world's largest producers of sheep meat and a major producer of milk, poultry and eggs.

History

British agriculture was transformed by the Agricultural Revolution of the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, involving enclosure, new rotations such as the Norfolk four-course system, selective livestock breeding and mechanization, which fed a rapidly industrializing population. The repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846 opened the country to cheap imported grain, and agriculture declined in the late Victorian era. Two world wars forced renewed emphasis on home production. From 1973 to 2020, UK farming operated under the European Union's Common Agricultural Policy, with subsidies and production incentives; following Brexit, the UK nations have been replacing it with new schemes, notably Environmental Land Management in England, which pays farmers for public goods such as biodiversity, soil health and flood mitigation.

Issues

Contemporary challenges include farm profitability and supermarket pricing, labour shortages after Brexit, animal disease (such as bovine tuberculosis and foot-and-mouth disease), climate change, environmental concerns about fertilizer runoff and greenhouse gas emissions, and debates over land use between food production, rewilding, housing and renewable energy.
