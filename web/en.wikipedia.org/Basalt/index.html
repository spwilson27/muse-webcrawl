Basalt is a dark, fine-grained volcanic rock formed from the rapid cooling of low-viscosity lava rich in magnesium and iron (mafic lava) exposed at or near the surface of a rocky planet or moon. It is the most common rock type of the Earth's ocean floor, and vast flood-basalt provinces — such as the Deccan Traps of India, the Siberian Traps and the Columbia River Basalts — record some of the largest eruptions in the planet's history. Basalt also forms the lunar maria and much of the surfaces of Mars and Venus.

Characteristics

Basalt typically consists of plagioclase feldspar and clinopyroxene, with smaller amounts of olivine, magnetite and ilmenite. Because it cools quickly, its crystals are too small to see without a microscope. Common textures include vesicular basalt, full of gas bubbles; scoria, highly vesicular and frothy; and porphyritic basalt, with larger early-formed crystals in a fine matrix. When thick lava flows cool and contract slowly, they may crack into striking hexagonal columnar jointing, as at the Giant's Causeway and Fingal's Cave.

Types and settings

Geochemists distinguish tholeiitic basalts (typical of mid-ocean ridges and flood basalts), alkali basalts (common on ocean islands and continental rifts) and high-alumina basalts of island arcs. Mid-ocean ridge basalt (MORB), erupted at spreading centres, continually renews the oceanic crust; hotspot volcanoes such as Hawaiʻi and Iceland build shields of basaltic lava.

Uses

Basalt is widely used as crushed stone for road base, concrete aggregate and railway ballast, and as dimension stone and paving. Melted and drawn into fibres, basalt fibre is used as a corrosion-resistant reinforcement in composites. Historically, basalt was carved for statues and grinding stones, and its dark, durable surfaces paved Roman roads.
