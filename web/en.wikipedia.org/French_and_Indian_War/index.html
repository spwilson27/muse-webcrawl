The French and Indian War (1754–1763) was a theater of the global Seven Years' War, fought in North America between the colonies of Great Britain and France, each supported by military units from the parent country and by Native American allies. At the start of the war, the French colonies had a population of roughly 60,000 settlers, compared with 2 million in the British colonies. The outnumbered French particularly depended on their Indigenous allies.

The war began over competing claims to the country of the upper Ohio River valley, where both French and British traders and settlers sought control. In May 1754, a young Virginia militia officer, George Washington, ambushed a French patrol at the Battle of Jumonville Glen, an event often treated as the opening shot of the war. Washington then built Fort Necessity, where he was forced to surrender to a larger French force that July.

Background

Through the early 1750s, French expansion into the Ohio Country drove repeated conflict with British claims, especially those of Virginia speculators organized in the Ohio Company. The French built a chain of forts — Presque Isle, Le Boeuf, Machault and Duquesne — to link Canada with Louisiana and to exclude British traders. Native nations of the region, including the Lenape, Shawnee and Mingo, attempted to preserve their own autonomy between the two empires.

Course of the war

In 1755 the British launched several expeditions. General Edward Braddock's army was destroyed at the Battle of the Monongahela near Fort Duquesne, where Braddock himself was killed. The years 1756–1757 brought French successes under the Marquis de Montcalm, including the capture of Fort Oswego and Fort William Henry, the latter followed by a notorious attack by France's Native allies on the surrendered British garrison and camp followers.

The tide turned in 1758 as British Secretary of State William Pitt the Elder poured resources into the American war. British forces captured Louisbourg, Fort Frontenac and, after the French burned it, Fort Duquesne — rebuilt as Fort Pitt, the seed of Pittsburgh. In 1759, General James Wolfe defeated Montcalm at the Battle of the Plains of Abraham outside Quebec City; both commanders died of their wounds. Montreal capitulated in 1760, effectively ending the war in North America, though fighting continued elsewhere until the global peace.

Consequences

By the Treaty of Paris (1763), France ceded Canada and all its claims east of the Mississippi River (except New Orleans) to Britain, and Louisiana west of the Mississippi to Spain. The removal of the French threat changed the relationship between Britain and its American colonies: London sought revenue through new taxes to pay war debts, while colonists resisted, and the Royal Proclamation of 1763 — reserving lands west of the Appalachians to Native nations — deepened resentment. The war thus set the stage for the American Revolution a decade later. Pontiac's War (1763–1766) followed as Native nations resisted British occupation of the interior.
