Banzai charge

A banzai charge (Japanese: バンザイ突撃 or banzai totsugeki) is the term that was used by the Allied forces of World War II to refer to Japanese human wave attacks and swarming staged by infantry units. The term came from the Japanese battle cry tennōheika banzai ("long live His Majesty the Emperor"), shortened to banzai, and it specifically refers to a tactic called gyokusai (玉砕, "shattered jewel") — a form of honourable suicide attack.

Origins

The tactic was rooted in the Japanese military doctrine influenced by bushidō, regarding surrender as dishonourable. Mass charges were used earlier, for instance at Port Arthur during the Russo-Japanese War, where they proved costly against prepared defences.

World War II

Large-scale charges were mounted at Guadalcanal (the Ichiki detachment and the Battle of Edson's Ridge, 1942), at Attu (1943), and most famously on Saipan on 7 July 1944, one of the largest banzai charges of the Pacific War. By late war, such attacks were frequently launched as a final act when a position was untenable, and were usually annihilated by Allied firepower with relatively few defender casualties.
