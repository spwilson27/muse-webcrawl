Guyenne

Guyenne (French pronunciation: [ɡɥijɛn]; Occitan: Guiana) is a historic province of southwestern France. The name is a corruption of "Aquitaine", and under the Ancien Régime it designated a large government centred on Bordeaux, covering at various times much of the territory between the Loire and the Pyrenees. Its boundaries shifted considerably over the centuries, at times embracing Agenais, Périgord, Quercy, Rouergue and parts of Gascony.

History

From 1154, Guyenne formed part of the Angevin Empire through the marriage of Eleanor of Aquitaine to Henry II of England, and it remained an English possession — as the Duchy of Aquitaine — for three centuries. It was a principal theatre of the Hundred Years' War, and French control was only finally established after the Battle of Castillon in 1453. Thereafter Guyenne was governed as a province of the French crown, with a parlement at Bordeaux established in 1462.

The province was a stronghold of Protestantism in the 16th century and suffered heavily during the Wars of Religion. The revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685 drove many Huguenots into exile. During the French Revolution the province was divided into departments, principally Gironde, Dordogne, Lot-et-Garonne, Lot, Aveyron and Tarn-et-Garonne.

Legacy

The name Guyenne survives in local usage, in wine appellations of the Bordeaux region, and in institutions such as the régiment de Guyenne of the old royal army. The modern administrative region of Nouvelle-Aquitaine covers most of the historic province.
