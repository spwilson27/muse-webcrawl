In particle physics, a pion (or pi meson) is any of three subatomic particles: π⁺, π⁰ and π⁻. Pions are the lightest mesons and play an important role in explaining the low-energy properties of the strong nuclear force. Each pion consists of a quark and an antiquark: the charged pions combine up and down quarks with their antiparticles, while the neutral pion is a quantum-mechanical mixture of up–anti-up and down–anti-down pairs.

History

The pion was predicted in 1935 by Hideki Yukawa as the carrier particle of the strong force binding protons and neutrons in the atomic nucleus, in analogy with the photon's role in electromagnetism. Charged pions were discovered in cosmic-ray exposures of photographic emulsions at high altitude in 1947 by C. F. Powell, César Lattes, Giuseppe Occhialini and collaborators; the neutral pion followed in 1950. Yukawa received the Nobel Prize in 1949 and Powell in 1950.

Properties and decay

Charged pions have a mass of about 139.6 MeV/c² and a mean lifetime of 26 nanoseconds, decaying almost always into a muon and a muon neutrino. The neutral pion, at about 135 MeV/c², is far shorter-lived and decays almost immediately into two gamma-ray photons. Because pions are produced copiously when cosmic rays strike the upper atmosphere and in particle accelerators, they were central to early particle physics and remain important in nuclear and hadronic physics; in quantum chromodynamics they are understood as the (pseudo) Nambu–Goldstone bosons of spontaneously broken chiral symmetry.
