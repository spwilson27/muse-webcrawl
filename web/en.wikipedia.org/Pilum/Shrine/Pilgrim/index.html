Pilgrim

A pilgrim (from the Latin peregrinus, "foreigner") is a traveller who journeys to a sacred place for religious reasons. Pilgrims undertake their journeys — pilgrimages — out of devotion, penance, thanksgiving, or the hope of spiritual or physical healing.

Pilgrims in world religions

In Islam, the pilgrim (hajji) travels to Mecca to perform the Hajj, one of the Five Pillars of Islam. In Christianity, pilgrims have journeyed since late antiquity to Jerusalem, Rome, and the shrine of St James at Santiago de Compostela, often carrying staffs and badges as signs of their undertaking. Hindu pilgrims (yatris) travel to sites such as Varanasi, Prayagraj and the Char Dham; Buddhist pilgrims visit Lumbini, Bodh Gaya, Sarnath and Kushinagar, the sites of the Buddha's life.

Historical practice

Medieval pilgrims often travelled in groups for safety, stayed in hospices maintained by religious orders, and sought indulgences or contact with relics at shrines. The figure of the pilgrim became a powerful literary symbol — in Chaucer's Canterbury Tales and Bunyan's The Pilgrim's Progress — for the spiritual journey of life itself.
