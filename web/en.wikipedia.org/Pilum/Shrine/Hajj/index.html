Hajj

The Hajj is an annual Islamic pilgrimage to Mecca, Saudi Arabia, the holiest city for Muslims. It is a mandatory religious duty for Muslims that must be carried out at least once in a lifetime by all adult Muslims who are physically and financially capable of undertaking the journey. The Hajj is one of the Five Pillars of Islam.

Hajj
Type: Islamic pilgrimage
Location: Mecca, Saudi Arabia
Timing: 8–13 Dhu al-Hijjah
Participants: ~2–3 million annually

Rites

During the Hajj, pilgrims perform a series of rituals over five to six days: entering the state of ihram, circumambulating the Kaaba (tawaf), walking between the hills of Safa and Marwah (sa'i), the Day of Arafah spent in prayer on the plain of Arafat, the symbolic stoning of the devil at Mina, and the sacrifice of an animal marking Eid al-Adha.

Significance

The pilgrimage retraces the footsteps of Muhammad, Abraham (Ibrahim), Hagar (Hajar) and Ishmael (Ismail) in Islamic tradition. The gathering — drawing two to three million pilgrims in recent years — is among the largest annual assemblies of people in the world, and its management is a vast logistical undertaking for Saudi Arabia.

History

The Hajj in its current form was established by Muhammad in the 7th century CE, building on much older traditions of pilgrimage to the Kaaba.
