In particle physics, a kaon, also called a K meson and denoted K, is any of a group of four mesons distinguished by a quantum number called strangeness. In the quark model they consist of one strange quark (or antiquark) bound with an up or down antiquark (or quark).

Discovery and properties

Kaons were discovered in 1947 in cosmic-ray cloud-chamber photographs as "V-particles" with unexpectedly long lifetimes, which led to the introduction of the strangeness quantum number. The four kaons are the charged K⁺ and K⁻ and the neutral K⁰ and its antiparticle; the neutral kaons mix to form the short- and long-lived states KS and KL.

CP violation

In 1964, James Cronin and Val Fitch discovered CP violation in the decays of neutral kaons — the first evidence that the weak interaction does not perfectly conserve the combined symmetry of charge conjugation and parity. The discovery earned them the 1980 Nobel Prize in Physics and remains central to explaining the matter–antimatter asymmetry of the universe.
