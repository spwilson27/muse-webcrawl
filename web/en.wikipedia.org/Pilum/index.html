Pilum

The pilum (plural pila) was a javelin commonly used by the Roman army in ancient times. It was generally about 2 metres (6 ft 7 in) long overall, consisting of an iron shank about 7 millimetres (0.28 in) in diameter and 60 centimetres (24 in) long with a pyramidal head, mounted on a wooden shaft. Total weight was between 2 and 5 kilograms (4.4 and 11.0 lb), depending on the era.

Design

The pilum's design allowed it to penetrate shields and armour. The long iron shank was deliberately made of softer iron so that it would bend upon impact, weighing down an enemy's shield and preventing the javelin from being thrown back or easily removed. Even if the pilum did not kill or wound the enemy soldier, a shield with a bent pilum stuck in it became unwieldy and was often discarded, leaving its owner exposed to the legionaries' sword attack.

Use

Legionaries of the late Republic and early Empire were often equipped with two pila, one lighter and one heavier. They were thrown at a range of about 15 to 30 metres just before engaging the enemy with swords, to disrupt the enemy formation at the moment of impact. Vegetius records that heavy infantry carried pila and lighter javelins called lanceae.

Origins and development

The pilum may have been adopted from the Samnites or Iberian peoples. Changes in its design over time included the use of a tang and socketed variants, and its weight decreased during the Principate. By late antiquity the pilum was replaced by other throwing weapons such as the spiculum and the plumbata, a weighted throwing dart.
