Pilgrimage

A pilgrimage is a journey made for religious or spiritual purposes, typically to a place regarded as holy—a shrine, tomb, temple, sacred river or mountain. The person making the journey is a pilgrim. Across cultures, pilgrimage is distinguished from ordinary travel by the intention of the traveller and by the expectation that the journey, with its separation from everyday life, trials along the way and eventual arrival, will bring spiritual benefit, merit, healing or transformation.

Major pilgrimage traditions

Nearly every world religion has pilgrimage sites. Muslims undertake the Hajj to Mecca and visit Medina; Hindus travel to Varanasi, the Char Dham circuit and the great Kumbh Mela gathering; Buddhists revere Lumbini, Bodh Gaya, Sarnath and Kushinagar; Christians journey to Jerusalem, Rome, Santiago de Compostela, Lourdes, Guadalupe and Canterbury; Sikhs visit the Golden Temple at Amritsar; Jews traditionally went up to Jerusalem for the festival pilgrimages and still pray at the Western Wall; and Shinto shrines such as Ise and Baháʼí shrines in Haifa and Akko draw pilgrims from around the world.

Character and study

Anthropologists, following Victor Turner, have described pilgrimage in terms of liminality: pilgrims leave their normal social position, share a temporary sense of equality and community (communitas) on the road, and return changed. In the modern world pilgrimage has expanded rather than contracted, aided by cheap transport, and shades into religious tourism and secular journeys of remembrance or personal quest, from battlefield tours to visits to Graceland. Some routes, such as the Camino de Santiago and Japan's Shikoku pilgrimage, are themselves protected cultural heritage.
