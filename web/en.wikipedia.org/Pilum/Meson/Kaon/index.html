Kaon

A kaon (also called a K meson) is any of a group of four mesons distinguished by the quantum number strangeness. Kaons are composed of a strange quark (or antiquark) bound with an up or down antiquark (or quark), and they played a central role in the history of particle physics.

Kaon
Composition: Strange quark + up/down antiquark
Types: K⁺, K⁰, K⁻, anti-K⁰
Mass: ~494–498 MeV/c²
Discovered: 1947

Discovery and strangeness

Kaons were discovered in cosmic rays in 1947 by George Rochester and Clifford Butler, appearing as "V particles" that were produced strongly but decayed slowly — a puzzle resolved by assigning them a new quantum number, strangeness, conserved in strong interactions but violated in weak decays. Hideki Yukawa's meson theory had anticipated particles mediating the strong interaction, and the expanding meson family of the 1940s–50s grew out of that framework.

Neutral kaons and CP violation

Neutral kaons mix with their antiparticles, producing the short-lived KS and long-lived KL states. In 1964, experiments by James Cronin and Val Fitch on KL decays revealed CP violation — a landmark discovery that showed matter and antimatter do not behave as perfect mirror images.

Properties

The charged kaons have a mass of about 494 MeV/c² and a mean lifetime of about 1.24 × 10⁻⁸ s, decaying most often into a muon and neutrino or into pions.
