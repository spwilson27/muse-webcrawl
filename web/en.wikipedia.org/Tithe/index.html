Tithe

A tithe (from Old English teogoþa, "tenth") is a one-tenth part of something, paid as a contribution to a religious organisation or, historically, as a compulsory tax to support the clergy and church. Tithing has roots in the Hebrew Bible, where a tenth of produce and livestock was owed to the Levites and the Temple, and Abraham is depicted giving a tenth to Melchizedek.

History

In medieval Europe the tithe became a legal obligation enforced by church and secular courts, usually levied on agricultural produce and stored in tithe barns. It was bitterly resented at times — tithe disputes contributed to unrest such as the German Peasants' War — and was abolished in many countries during the 18th and 19th centuries, often replaced by state taxes or commuted to rent charges (in England, by the Tithe Act 1836). In some countries, notably Germany, a church tax (Kirchensteuer) performs a similar function today.

Modern practice

Many churches teach tithing as a voluntary discipline: members of the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, for example, are expected to tithe 10 percent of their income as a requirement for temple attendance, and many evangelical and Pentecostal churches encourage the practice. Judaism and Islam have related obligations, such as ma'aser (tithes in Judaism) and zakat (almsgiving in Islam, though not a strict tenth).
