Photon Bunching

Photon bunching is the tendency of photons from a thermal (chaotic) light source to arrive at a detector in clusters rather than at random intervals. It is measured by the second-order correlation function g(2)(τ), which for thermal light equals 2 at zero delay and decays to 1 for delays longer than the coherence time.

History and explanation

The effect was demonstrated by Robert Hanbury Brown and Richard Twiss in the 1950s in their intensity interferometer, initially to measure the angular diameters of stars. Classically, bunching reflects the fluctuating intensity of thermal light: photons are more likely to be detected during intensity peaks. In quantum optics, it is understood through the bosonic statistics of photons and two-photon interference.

Contrasts

An ideal laser (coherent state) shows no bunching, with g(2)(0) = 1. By contrast, a source of single photons shows photon antibunching, g(2)(0) < 1, a distinctly nonclassical effect used to verify single-photon emitters.
