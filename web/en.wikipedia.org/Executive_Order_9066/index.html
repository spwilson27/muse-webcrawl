Executive Order 9066

Executive Order 9066 was a United States presidential executive order signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt on February 19, 1942, during World War II. It authorized the Secretary of War to prescribe military areas from which any or all persons might be excluded. Although the order did not name any ethnic group, it was applied to authorize the forced removal and incarceration of roughly 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry — about two-thirds of them US citizens — from the West Coast to inland camps.

Implementation

General John L. DeWitt, head of the Western Defense Command, issued a series of public proclamations and civilian exclusion orders in spring 1942. Families were given days to dispose of homes, farms, and businesses, and were first sent to temporary "assembly centers" — often fairgrounds and racetracks — before transfer to ten "relocation centers" administered by the War Relocation Authority in remote areas of California, Arizona, Utah, Wyoming, Colorado, Arkansas, and Idaho. Camps such as Manzanar and Tule Lake held internees behind barbed wire under armed guard until releases began in 1944–45.

Legal challenges and redress

The Supreme Court upheld the exclusion and curfew orders in Hirabayashi v. United States (1943) and Korematsu v. United States (1944), decisions widely condemned by later generations; in 2018 the Court described Korematsu as "gravely wrong the day it was decided." In 1983 the federal Commission on Wartime Relocation and Internment of Civilians concluded the incarceration was the product of "race prejudice, war hysteria and a failure of political leadership." The Civil Liberties Act of 1988 issued a formal apology and paid $20,000 in reparations to each surviving internee. In the 1980s, coram nobis petitions vacated the wartime convictions of Fred Korematsu, Gordon Hirabayashi, and Minoru Yasui after evidence emerged that the government had suppressed and altered intelligence reports.
