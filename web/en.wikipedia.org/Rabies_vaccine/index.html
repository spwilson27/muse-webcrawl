Rabies vaccine

Rabies vaccine
Type: Inactivated virus vaccine
Uses: Pre- and post-exposure prophylaxis against rabies
First developed: 1885, by Louis Pasteur and Émile Roux
Status: WHO List of Essential Medicines

The rabies vaccine is a vaccine used to prevent rabies. It can be given before exposure to people at high risk — veterinarians, animal handlers, laboratory staff and travellers to remote endemic areas — or, crucially, after exposure, where it is virtually always effective if administered before symptoms begin. Rabies vaccines are also used to immunise domestic dogs and wildlife.

History

Louis Pasteur and Émile Roux developed the first rabies vaccine from desiccated spinal cords of infected rabbits, using it in 1885 to save nine-year-old Joseph Meister, who had been mauled by a rabid dog. Early nerve-tissue vaccines caused serious reactions in some recipients; they were progressively replaced from the 1950s by safer vaccines grown in cell culture, such as the human diploid cell vaccine, and later by purified chick embryo cell and Vero cell vaccines.

Use

Modern post-exposure prophylaxis combines immediate wound washing, infiltration of rabies immunoglobulin around the wound for severe exposures, and a series of vaccine doses over two to four weeks (given intramuscularly or, in some programmes, intradermally to save cost). Pre-exposure vaccination simplifies later treatment. Oral vaccine baits distributed from aircraft have eliminated fox rabies from much of western Europe.
