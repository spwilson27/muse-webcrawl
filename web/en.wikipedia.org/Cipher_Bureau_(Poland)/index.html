Cipher Bureau (Poland)

The Cipher Bureau (Polish: Biuro Szyfrów) was the interwar Polish General Staff's organization charged with signal intelligence, cryptology, and cryptanalysis. Formed in 1919 during the Polish–Soviet War, it achieved one of the most consequential feats in cryptologic history: the first breaking of the German Enigma machine cipher, in December 1932.

Origins

The Bureau grew out of the cipher section created by Lieutenant Józef Serafin Stanslicki in May 1919. Its analysts, including Jan Kowalewski, read Soviet ciphers during the Polish–Soviet War of 1919–1921, contributing to Poland's victory at the Battle of Warsaw. In 1931 the Bureau was consolidated under Captain (later Lieutenant Colonel) Gwido Langer, with Maksymilian Ciężki heading the German section (BS-4).

Breaking Enigma

In 1929 the Bureau took the unusual step of recruiting young mathematicians from Poznań University — Marian Rejewski, Jerzy Różycki, and Henryk Zygalski — and in late 1932 Rejewski, using permutation theory and documents supplied by French intelligence from the spy Hans-Thilo Schmidt, reconstructed the Enigma's wiring. The Bureau developed the cyclometer, the cryptologic bomb (bomba kryptologiczna), and Zygalski sheets to recover daily keys, and read German Enigma traffic for nearly seven years.

Transfer to the Allies and wartime fate

On 25 July 1939, five weeks before the Second World War began, the Bureau presented its Enigma breakthroughs and replica machines to French and British intelligence at Pyry near Warsaw — the foundation on which Bletchley Park's Ultra effort was built. After the German invasion, Bureau personnel evacuated through Romania to France, continuing work at PC Bruno and later Cadix in Vichy France. Rejewski and Zygalski reached Britain in 1943; Różycki died when his ship was torpedoed in 1942.
