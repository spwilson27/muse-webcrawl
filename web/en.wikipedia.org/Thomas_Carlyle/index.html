Thomas Carlyle (4 December 1795 – 5 February 1881) was a Scottish essayist, historian, philosopher, and translator. A leading writer of the Victorian era, he is remembered for the idiosyncratic and electrifying style of his prose — sometimes called "Carlylese" — and for works including the novel Sartor Resartus (1833–34), the three-volume history The French Revolution (1837), On Heroes, Hero-Worship, and the Heroic in History (1841), Past and Present (1843), and the multi-volume biography History of Friedrich II of Prussia (1858–65).

Life

Born in Ecclefechan in Dumfriesshire to a stonemason's family of strict Calvinist faith, Carlyle studied at the University of Edinburgh, intending first for the ministry and then abandoning it. After years of tutoring, translation, and periodical writing — during which he immersed himself in German literature and translated Goethe — he married Jane Welsh in 1826. The couple moved to London in 1834, settling at 5 (now 24) Cheyne Row, Chelsea, where their home became a centre of literary society. A famous anecdote of his career is the accidental burning of the manuscript of the first volume of The French Revolution by John Stuart Mill's household, which forced Carlyle to rewrite it from scratch.

Ideas and influence

Carlyle attacked the materialism and utilitarianism of the industrial age, denounced the "cash nexus" as the sole bond of modern society, and championed the role of great individuals — heroes — in history. His notion of "The Everlasting No" and "The Everlasting Yea" in Sartor Resartus articulated a spiritual gospel of work and renunciation. He coined the phrase "the dismal science" for economics in his controversial 1849 essay "Occasional Discourse on the Negro Question". His influence on Victorian literature and social thought — on Dickens, Ruskin, and many others — was immense, though his reputation later suffered for his authoritarian views and racial attitudes.
