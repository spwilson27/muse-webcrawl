Free-rider problem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The free-rider problem is a type of market failure that occurs when those who benefit from resources, public goods, and common pool resources do not pay for them or under-pay, which ultimately leads to the good being under-produced, overused, or degraded. Because it is not possible, or not practical, to exclude non-payers from using a public good, individuals have an incentive to let others bear the cost while enjoying the benefits themselves.

Examples

Classic examples include national defense, street lighting, and public broadcasting: once provided, they serve payers and non-payers alike. If enough people free-ride, voluntary contributions fall short and the good may not be provided at all, even though everyone would be better off if it were. The problem also arises in collective bargaining, where workers may benefit from a union contract without joining the union, and in environmental protection, where countries may benefit from others' emission reductions without reducing their own.

Solutions

Governments commonly address the free-rider problem by financing public goods through compulsory taxation, converting individual incentives into collective provision. Other approaches include making goods excludable through technology or property rights (turning them into club goods), social pressure and norms, assurance contracts that refund contributions if a threshold is not met, and selective incentives such as those described by Mancur Olson in The Logic of Collective Action (1965).
