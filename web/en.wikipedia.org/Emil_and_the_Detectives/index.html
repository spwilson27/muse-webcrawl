Emil and the Detectives (German: Emil und die Detektive) is a 1929 children's novel by the German writer Erich Kästner, illustrated by Walter Trier. It was Kästner's first major success and remains his best-known work, remarkable at the time for its realistic contemporary setting, colloquial dialogue, and portrayal of children acting cooperatively rather than through magic or fantasy.

Plot

Emil Tischbein, a schoolboy from the provincial town of Neustadt, is sent by train to Berlin carrying 140 marks — money his widowed mother, a hairdresser, has saved for his grandmother. On the train, a mysterious fellow passenger, Herr Grundeis, offers Emil a sweet containing a sedative; Emil dreams strangely and wakes to find the money stolen. Arriving in Berlin, Emil spots the thief and follows him, determined not to involve the police because he fears trouble over a prank at home. He is befriended by Gustav, a local boy with a car horn, who assembles a gang of child "detectives". Together they shadow Grundeis across the city, gather evidence, and finally trap him in a bank as he tries to exchange the stolen notes — which Emil had prudently pinned inside his pocket, leaving identifying pinpricks. Grundeis turns out to be a wanted bank robber, and Emil receives a reward of 1,000 marks.

Reception and legacy

The novel's urban realism broke with the fairy-tale tradition of German children's literature and it was translated into dozens of languages. Notably, it was one of the few works by Kästner not banned by the Nazi regime, though his other books were burned in 1933. The story has been adapted for film many times, including a 1931 German screen version with a screenplay involving Billy Wilder, a 1935 British remake, and a 1964 Walt Disney film. A sequel, Emil and the Three Twins, followed in 1934.
