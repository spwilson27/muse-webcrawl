Hero of Alexandria

Lived: fl. 1st century AD (c. 10 – c. 70 AD)
Residence: Alexandria, Egypt
Known for: Aeolipile, Heron's formula, automata

Hero of Alexandria (Greek: Ἥρων ὁ Ἀλεξανδρεύς), also known as Heron of Alexandria, was a Greek mathematician and engineer active in his native city of Alexandria, Roman Egypt, in the 1st century AD. He is regarded as one of the greatest experimenters of antiquity, and his work is representative of the Hellenistic scientific tradition.

Inventions

Hero described the aeolipile, a rocket-like steam turbine regarded as the first recorded steam engine; it spun when water in a sealed vessel was heated and steam escaped through bent nozzles. Among his many devices were a windwheel driving an organ (an early wind-powered machine), automatic temple doors, a self-filling wine bowl, programmable wheeled automata driven by falling weights and knotted ropes — sometimes called the first robot — and a syringe-like device for controlling air or liquids. His Pneumatica catalogues dozens of siphon-, water-, and air-driven machines.

Mathematics

Hero is credited with Heron's formula for the area of a triangle from the lengths of its three sides (A = √(s(s−a)(s−b)(s−c)), where s is the semiperimeter), and with an iterative method for approximating square roots — Heron's method, known earlier to the Babylonians. His Metrica treats the measurement of areas and volumes, and he proved the law of reflection (angle of incidence equals angle of reflection) in his work on optics, deriving it from a shortest-path principle.

Legacy

Many of Hero's manuscripts were lost, and his works survive partly through Arabic translation. His blend of practical engineering and mathematical rigour influenced Islamic and Renaissance engineers; the aeolipile is still invoked as evidence that steam technology was conceivable, though unexploited, in the ancient world.
