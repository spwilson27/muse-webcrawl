The greenhouse effect: gases absorb and re-radiate infrared energy

Greenhouse gases (GHGs) are the gases in the atmosphere that raise the surface temperature of planets such as Earth. They do this by absorbing infrared radiation emitted by the surface and re-radiating part of it back down—the greenhouse effect. Without greenhouse gases Earth's average surface temperature would be about −18 °C instead of roughly 15 °C.

Properties and sources

The primary greenhouse gases in Earth's atmosphere are water vapour, carbon dioxide (CO₂), methane (CH₄), nitrous oxide (N₂O), and ozone (O₃). Molecules absorb infrared when they have vibrational modes that change their dipole moment; the main constituents of air, N₂ and O₂, are essentially transparent to infrared. Human activities since the Industrial Revolution have raised atmospheric CO₂ by about 50% (from ~280 ppm to over 420 ppm), mostly through burning fossil fuels and deforestation; methane has more than doubled, from agriculture, waste, and fossil-fuel extraction.

Global warming potential

Gases differ in potency and lifetime. Methane traps far more heat per molecule than CO₂ but survives only about a decade in the air, while a fraction of emitted CO₂ persists for centuries to millennia. Scientists compare emissions using global warming potential (GWP) over 20- or 100-year horizons; fluorinated gases such as SF₆ are thousands of times more potent still, though emitted in smaller quantities.

Effects and policy

Rising GHG concentrations are the dominant driver of modern climate change, causing global warming, sea-level rise, and ocean acidification. International responses include the Kyoto Protocol and the Paris Agreement, which aim to limit warming to well below 2 °C above pre-industrial levels. Greenhouse gas inventories track emissions by country and sector under the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change.
