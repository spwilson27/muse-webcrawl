Roasting (metallurgy)

Roasting is a process in metallurgy in which a sulfide ore is heated in air. The process may convert a metal sulfide to a metal oxide (oxidising roast) or to a sulfate (sulfating roast), and is commonly the first of several steps in extracting a metal from its ore.

Chemistry

In an oxidising roast, metal sulfides are converted to oxides while sulfur is driven off as sulfur dioxide: 2 ZnS + 3 O2 → 2 ZnO + 2 SO2. Conditions of temperature and air supply determine whether oxides, sulfates or a mixture form. Roasting differs from calcination, which is carried out with limited air or in an inert atmosphere, typically to decompose carbonates or hydroxides.

Practice

Roasting is carried out in multiple-hearth furnaces, rotary kilns or, in modern plants, fluidised-bed roasters, which give good temperature control and gas–solid contact. The sulfur dioxide off-gas is commonly captured to make sulfuric acid, reducing emissions. Roasting is applied to ores of zinc, copper, lead, molybdenum and other metals; gold ores may be roasted to oxidise refractory sulfides before cyanidation.
