Gaia is a space observatory of the European Space Agency (ESA), launched in 2013 and operated until 2025, whose mission was to chart a three-dimensional map of the Milky Way with unprecedented precision. Gaia measured the positions, parallaxes, proper motions, brightness, colour and — for brighter stars — radial velocities of roughly two billion stars, about one percent of the Galaxy's stellar population, succeeding ESA's earlier Hipparcos mission.

Spacecraft and instruments

Gaia spins slowly, sweeping two telescopes separated by a fixed basic angle of 106.5° across the sky. Light from both fields is projected onto the largest focal plane flown in space, a mosaic of 106 CCDs with nearly a billion pixels. Alongside the astrometric field sit blue and red photometers, which provide low-resolution spectra for estimating temperature and composition, and a radial velocity spectrometer. A 10-metre deployable sunshield kept the payload in shadow at the Sun–Earth L2 Lagrange point, some 1.5 million km from Earth.

Data releases

Because the catalogue derives from a global iterative solution of billions of observations, ESA released the data in stages: DR1 (2016) gave positions and magnitudes for 1.1 billion stars; DR2 (2018) added parallaxes and motions for 1.3 billion; EDR3 (2020) and DR3 (2022) provided astrometry for about 1.8 billion sources plus extensive astrophysical parameters, radial velocities for 33 million stars and catalogues of variable stars, binaries, Solar System objects and quasars. The data have been used to reconstruct the Milky Way's merger history — including the Gaia-Enceladus dwarf galaxy — refine the cosmic distance ladder, and find stellar-mass black holes, among them Gaia BH1 and the ~33-solar-mass Gaia BH3.

End of mission

Having long exceeded its five-year design life and exhausted its cold-gas micro-propulsion propellant, Gaia ended science observations on 15 January 2025; the spacecraft was passivated and moved into a heliocentric graveyard orbit in March 2025. Final full data releases are planned to follow from the accumulated observations.
