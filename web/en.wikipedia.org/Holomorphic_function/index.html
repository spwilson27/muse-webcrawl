In mathematics, a holomorphic function is a complex-valued function of one or more complex variables that is complex differentiable in a neighbourhood of each point in its domain. Differentiability in the complex sense is a far stronger condition than real differentiability: a function differentiable once in an open set is automatically infinitely differentiable there and equal to its own Taylor series — that is, analytic.

Definition

A function f : U → ℂ, where U is an open subset of the complex plane, is holomorphic at a point z0 if the limit of (f(z) − f(z0))/(z − z0) exists as z → z0. It is holomorphic on U if it is holomorphic at every point of U. A function holomorphic on the whole plane is called entire.

Writing f(z) = u(x, y) + i v(x, y), holomorphicity is equivalent (for continuously differentiable u, v) to the Cauchy–Riemann equations: ∂u/∂x = ∂v/∂y and ∂u/∂y = −∂v/∂x. The real and imaginary parts are then harmonic functions.

Key theorems

  • Cauchy's integral theorem: the integral of a holomorphic function around a closed contour in a simply connected domain is zero.
  • Cauchy's integral formula: values inside a contour are determined by values on it; hence derivatives of all orders exist and are bounded by the Cauchy estimates.
  • Identity theorem: two holomorphic functions agreeing on a set with a limit point agree everywhere on a connected domain.
  • Liouville's theorem: a bounded entire function is constant.
  • Maximum modulus principle and the residue theorem, foundational for evaluating integrals and counting zeros (argument principle).

Examples

All polynomials in z, the exponential function, and the trigonometric functions are entire. Rational functions are holomorphic away from their poles; the principal branch of the logarithm is holomorphic on the plane minus the negative real axis. By contrast, conjugation z → z̄ and the real part are nowhere holomorphic, since they fail the Cauchy–Riemann equations. The word "holomorphic" (from Greek holos, whole, and morphē, form) was introduced by students of Cauchy in the 19th century; the older term "analytic" is equivalent in one variable but differs in several complex variables from the stronger requirement of holomorphicity, which by Osgood's lemma and Hartogs' theory remains closely tied to power-series expansion.
