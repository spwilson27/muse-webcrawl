Dry-tooling

Dry-tooling is a form of rock and ice climbing in which the climber ascends a rock face using ice axes and crampons (or specialized fruit boots with bolted front points). Because the techniques damage rock and leave scratch marks, dry-tooling is usually practised on dedicated crags of low aesthetic or climbing value, or on steep rock sections of mixed climbs where no ice is present.

Techniques and equipment

Climbers hook axe picks into cracks and pockets, torque them in constrictions, and use figures such as the figure-four and figure-nine to extend reach. Leashless tools with ergonomic handles, monopoint crampons, and stiff boots are standard. Routes are graded with the mixed (M) scale used in mixed climbing; competition dry-tooling on artificial structures has pushed standards to around M13–M14 and beyond.

History and ethics

Dry-tooling developed from mixed climbing in the 1980s and 1990s as climbers crossed bare rock to reach ice. As a stand-alone sport it generated controversy because picks and crampon points scar rock; many areas restrict it to designated venues, quarries, and cliffs not valued for conventional rock climbing. Ice climbing competitions under the UIAA include dry-tooling-style routes on artificial towers.
