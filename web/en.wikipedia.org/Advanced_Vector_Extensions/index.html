Advanced Vector Extensions

Advanced Vector Extensions (AVX, also known as Sandy Bridge New Extensions) are SIMD extensions to the x86 instruction set architecture for microprocessors from Intel and Advanced Micro Devices (AMD). They were proposed by Intel in March 2008 and first supported by Intel with the Sandy Bridge microarchitecture shipping in Q1 2011 and later by AMD with the Bulldozer microarchitecture shipping in Q3 2011. AVX provides new features, new instructions, and a new coding scheme.

Features

AVX widened the SIMD registers from 128 to 256 bits (the YMM registers), introduced a three-operand, non-destructive instruction format encoded with the VEX prefix, and converted most legacy SSE instructions to three-operand VEX forms. It also added new instructions such as broadcasts, permutes and masked loads and stores in later iterations.

Versions

  • AVX2 (first in Haswell, 2013) expanded most integer instructions to 256 bits and added gather instructions, FMA3 (jointly introduced as a separate feature), and variable shifts.
  • AVX-512 (first in Xeon Phi x200 and Skylake-X server chips) extends registers to 512 bits (ZMM), with 32 registers, opmask registers and many specialised instruction subsets; its fragmented branding by subset caused significant market confusion.
  • AVX10 (announced 2023) unifies the vector-length model across product lines, and AVX-VNNI and related AI-oriented instructions accelerate low-precision neural-network inference.

Use

AVX instructions accelerate floating-point-heavy workloads including scientific computing, signal and image processing, 3D modelling, multimedia encode/decode and cryptography libraries; operating systems must enable the extended register state (via XSAVE) before applications can use it.
