Optical clock

An optical clock is an atomic clock that uses an optical (visible or ultraviolet) transition in an atom or ion as its frequency standard, rather than the microwave transition used in caesium clocks. Because optical frequencies are far higher, optical clocks can achieve much smaller fractional uncertainties — below 10−18 — making them the most precise timekeeping devices ever built.

TypeAtomic clock
Reference speciesStrontium, ytterbium, aluminium ion, mercury
Typical uncertainty~10−18 fractional
Key componentFemtosecond frequency comb

How it works

Atoms are trapped and cooled — in a lattice of laser light for neutral atoms such as strontium or ytterbium, or in an ion trap for single ions. A highly stable laser is locked to a narrow atomic transition, and a femtosecond frequency comb counts the optical cycles, dividing the frequency down to countable microwave or electronic signals.

Performance

Laboratory optical clocks would neither gain nor lose a second over billions of years, and they are sensitive enough to register the gravitational redshift over height differences of millimetres, opening applications in relativistic geodesy. Experiments comparing different optical clocks also test fundamental physics, such as possible variation of fundamental constants.

Redefinition of the second

Optical clocks now outperform the caesium primary standards that define the SI second, and metrologists are preparing a future redefinition of the second in terms of one or more optical transitions.
