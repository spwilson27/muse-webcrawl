Escape from Alcatraz (1962)

This article is about the historical escape. For the 1979 film, see Escape from Alcatraz (film).

The June 1962 Alcatraz escape was an escape from the maximum-security federal prison on Alcatraz Island in San Francisco Bay by inmates Frank Morris and brothers John and Clarence Anglin, who fled the island on the night of 11 June 1962 using an improvised raft. It remains one of the most famous prison escapes in American history and is officially unresolved: the FBI closed its investigation in 1979 concluding the men most likely drowned, while the U.S. Marshals Service case remains open and the men's fates are disputed to this day.

The escape

Morris, the Anglins, and a fourth conspirator, Allen West, spent months widening the ventilation ducts at the backs of their cells using spoons and an improvised drill, masked by accordion practice and the prison's music hour. They climbed through a utility corridor to the cellhouse roof and down to the shore. To delay discovery, they left in their beds dummy heads fashioned from soap, toilet paper, cement powder and real human hair collected from the barbershop, painted to resemble sleeping men. The raft and life vests were stitched from more than fifty stolen raincoats, and a small concertina was used to inflate the raft. West failed to get out of his cell in time and was left behind.

Investigation and aftermath

Pieces of the raft, a paddle, and Clarence Anglin's wallet wrapped in plastic were later recovered around the bay, but no bodies were ever found, despite the bay's cold water (around 12 °C) and strong currents. A Norwegian freighter reported sighting a body in a prison-like denim outfit floating near the Golden Gate weeks later, never recovered. MythBusters and later researchers showed the crossing was feasible, and the Anglin family has produced circumstantial claims — a Christmas card, an alleged 1975 photograph of the brothers in Brazil — suggesting survival. The escape hastened the closure of Alcatraz the following year. David Letterman's list aside, the incident inspired the 1963 book by J. Campbell Bruce and the 1979 Clint Eastwood film Escape from Alcatraz.
