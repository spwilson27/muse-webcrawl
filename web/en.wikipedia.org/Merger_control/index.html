Merger control

Merger control refers to the procedure of reviewing mergers and acquisitions under antitrust or competition law. Merger control regimes are adopted by over 100 countries. More than 90 of these make notification mandatory with penalties for failure to notify. The purpose is to prevent concentrations that would significantly impede effective competition or create or strengthen a dominant position in a market.

Procedure

In most systems, companies planning a transaction above certain turnover or market-share thresholds must notify the competition authority before closing (a suspensory regime), as in the European Union under the EU Merger Regulation, administered by the European Commission. In the United States, the Hart–Scott–Rodino Act requires pre-merger notification to the Federal Trade Commission and the Department of Justice, with a waiting period that can be extended by a "second request" for information.

Substantive test and remedies

Authorities assess whether the merger would substantially lessen competition (the US test) or significantly impede effective competition (the EU test), examining market definition, shares, unilateral and coordinated effects, entry barriers and efficiencies. Transactions may be cleared unconditionally, cleared with remedies — structural (divestiture) or behavioural commitments — or prohibited. Decisions are generally subject to judicial review, and multi-jurisdictional deals require filings in many countries, coordinated informally through networks such as the International Competition Network.
