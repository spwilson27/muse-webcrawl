Vers libre

Vers libre is a form of free verse poetry that originated in France in the late 19th century, freeing verse from the strict rules of syllable count, caesura, and rhyme that governed classical French prosody such as the alexandrine. The term is French for "free verse", and the movement's ideas strongly influenced English-language modernist poetry, where "vers libre" was used interchangeably with free verse.

Origins

Precursors of vers libre appear in the prose poems and late work of Arthur Rimbaud, whose Illuminations (written in the 1870s) broke with regular metre. The form was consciously developed in the 1880s by poets including Gustave Kahn, Jules Laforgue, Édouard Dujardin, and Francis Vielé-Griffin, and promoted through little magazines such as Kahn's La Vogue, which published Laforgue's free verse and translations of Walt Whitman. Kahn's preface to his Premiers poèmes (1897) is often treated as the movement's manifesto, arguing that rhythm should follow the poet's thought and emotion rather than inherited rules.

Influence

Vers libre reached English-language poetry through French-reading modernists. T. S. Eliot, who considered Laforgue a formative influence, wrote on the subject and practised a flexible verse that critics described as vers libre; Ezra Pound and the Imagists likewise championed it. In English the term gradually lost its specifically French sense and became a synonym for free verse in general — poetry organised by cadence, image, and line break rather than by a fixed metrical scheme.
