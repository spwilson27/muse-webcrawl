Abraham Darby III (24 April 1750 – 1789) was an English ironmaster, the third of the Darby family of Coalbrookdale in Shropshire to bear the name. He is best known as the builder of the Iron Bridge across the River Severn at Ironbridge Gorge — the world's first major bridge made of cast iron, and an enduring symbol of the Industrial Revolution.

Life

Darby was the son of Abraham Darby II, who had greatly developed the Coalbrookdale works pioneered by his own father, Abraham Darby I, the first to smelt iron successfully with coke (1709). Orphaned of his father at the age of thirteen, Abraham III took control of the family ironworks while still young, and expanded the business significantly: he enlarged furnaces at Coalbrookdale and Horsehay, built new works, and developed markets for cast-iron rails, cylinders and structural castings.

The Iron Bridge

In the 1770s a bridge was needed across the Severn gorge to link the industrial parishes of Broseley and Madeley. Darby championed an all-iron structure designed with architect Thomas Farnolls Pritchard. The bridge's ironwork — nearly 380 tons of it — was cast at Coalbrookdale in 1779, with the arch ribs erected in the summer of that year using carpentry-like joints adapted to iron. The bridge opened to traffic on 1 January 1781. Darby bore much of the construction cost personally, which contributed to his indebtedness, and he received a gold medal from the Royal Society of Arts for the project in 1788.

Death and legacy

Like his father and grandfather, Darby was a Quaker. He died in 1789, aged only 38, worn out by overwork, leaving heavy debts that the family eventually cleared. The Iron Bridge — now a UNESCO World Heritage Site as part of the Ironbridge Gorge — stands as his monument.
