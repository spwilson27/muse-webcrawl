Warfarin

Warfarin, sold under the brand name Coumadin among others, is an anticoagulant medication used to treat and prevent blood clots. It is commonly used to prevent stroke in people with atrial fibrillation, and to treat deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. Warfarin decreases blood clotting by blocking an enzyme called vitamin K epoxide reductase, which reactivates vitamin K, needed for the synthesis of several clotting factors.

History

The compound’s origins lie in a haemorrhagic disease of cattle in the 1920s traced to spoiled sweet clover hay, from which Karl Paul Link’s laboratory at the University of Wisconsin isolated dicoumarol. Link’s team synthesised warfarin in 1948 — its name derives from the Wisconsin Alumni Research Foundation (WARF) plus the “-arin” of coumarin. It was first marketed as a rodenticide, and approved for medical use in 1954; it famously was used to treat President Dwight D. Eisenhower after his heart attack in 1955.

Medical use and monitoring

Because the therapeutic dose varies widely between individuals and interacts with diet and many other drugs, warfarin therapy is monitored with the international normalised ratio (INR), a standardised measure of prothrombin time. Genetic variants in the genes CYP2C9 and VKORC1 affect dosing requirements, making warfarin a prominent example in pharmacogenomics.

Adverse effects

The main adverse effect is bleeding, ranging from bruising to life-threatening haemorrhage; effects can be reversed with vitamin K or plasma products. Warfarin is teratogenic and generally avoided in pregnancy. A rare but serious complication is warfarin-induced skin necrosis. Since the 2010s, direct oral anticoagulants such as apixaban and rivaroxaban have displaced warfarin for many indications, but warfarin remains essential for patients with mechanical heart valves and certain other conditions.

Use as rodenticide

Warfarin and related “superwarfarin” compounds such as brodifacoum have been widely used as rat poisons, though resistance in rodent populations is increasingly common.
