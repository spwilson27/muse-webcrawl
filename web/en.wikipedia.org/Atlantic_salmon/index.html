Atlantic salmon

The Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar) is a species of ray-finned fish in the family Salmonidae. It is the third largest of the Salmonidae, behind Siberian taimen and Pacific Chinook salmon, growing up to a metre or more in length. Atlantic salmon are native to the North Atlantic Ocean and the rivers that flow into it, from New England and eastern Canada in the west to northern Europe and western Russia in the east.

Life cycle

Atlantic salmon are anadromous: they hatch in freshwater gravel nests (redds) in autumn, spend one to four years in rivers as fry and parr, then transform into silvery smolts and migrate to sea in spring. After one or more winters feeding in the ocean — growing rapidly on fish and crustaceans — adults return, usually to their natal river, to spawn. Unlike Pacific salmon, many Atlantic salmon survive spawning and may repeat the cycle. Landlocked populations, such as the ouananiche of Quebec, complete their lives in lakes.

Fisheries and aquaculture

Wild Atlantic salmon were once enormously abundant and supported major commercial and recreational fisheries, but dams, pollution and overfishing caused severe declines; commercial wild catch is now small. Aquaculture, by contrast, produces several million tonnes annually — Norway, Chile, Scotland and Canada are leading producers — and farmed fish dominate the market. Farming raises concerns over sea lice, disease transfer, and escapes interbreeding with wild stocks.

Conservation

Restoration efforts include dam removal (such as on Maine's Penobscot River), fish ladders, catch-and-release angling, and hatchery supplementation. In the United States, Atlantic salmon in the Gulf of Maine are listed as endangered under the Endangered Species Act.
