Synaptic cleft

The synaptic cleft, also known as the synaptic gap, is the small space between the presynaptic terminal of one neuron and the postsynaptic membrane of another neuron or effector cell at a chemical synapse. In most synapses of the central nervous system the cleft is about 20–30 nanometres wide; at the neuromuscular junction it is roughly 50 nanometres.

Function

When an action potential arrives at the presynaptic bouton, voltage-gated calcium channels open and the influx of Ca2+ triggers synaptic vesicles to fuse with the presynaptic membrane, releasing neurotransmitter molecules into the cleft by exocytosis. The molecules diffuse across the cleft in a fraction of a millisecond and bind to receptors on the postsynaptic membrane, opening ion channels or activating signalling cascades that excite or inhibit the target cell.

Contents and clearance

The cleft contains extracellular matrix and adhesion molecules—such as neurexins and neuroligins—that align release sites with postsynaptic receptors, as well as enzymes that terminate the signal. At cholinergic synapses, acetylcholinesterase in the cleft rapidly hydrolyses acetylcholine. Elsewhere, transmitter is cleared by reuptake transporters on the presynaptic membrane or surrounding glial cells, or simply diffuses away.

Clinical significance

The synaptic cleft is the site of action of many drugs and toxins. Organophosphate nerve agents and insecticides inhibit acetylcholinesterase, flooding the cleft with acetylcholine; botulinum toxin prevents transmitter release into it; and numerous antidepressants block reuptake transporters, prolonging transmitter presence in the cleft. Autoimmune attack on cleft-side structures underlies diseases such as myasthenia gravis, in which antibodies target postsynaptic acetylcholine receptors.
