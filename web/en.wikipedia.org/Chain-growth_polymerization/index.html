Chain-growth polymerization

Chain-growth polymerization
TypePolymerization mechanism
StagesInitiation, propagation, termination
ExamplesFree-radical, ionic, coordination

Chain-growth polymerization (IUPAC recommends chain polymerization) is a polymerization technique where unsaturated monomer molecules add onto the active site on a growing polymer chain one at a time. The growth of the polymer occurs at the ends of growing chains, and each step of growth consumes one reactive particle and produces another similar particle. The active centre may be a free radical, an ion, or a metal-organic complex.

Mechanism

The process proceeds in three stages: initiation, in which an active centre is created; propagation, in which the chain grows by sequential addition of monomer units; and termination, in which the active centre is destroyed. Chain transfer, though not a stage of propagation itself, can end one chain's growth while starting another.

Comparison with step-growth

Unlike step-growth polymerization, in which monomers react with each other directly and molecular weight builds slowly, chain-growth polymerization produces high-molecular-weight polymer rapidly: the chain grows to its full length almost immediately, and monomer concentration decreases steadily throughout the reaction.

Types

Common varieties include free-radical polymerization, anionic and cationic ionic polymerization, ring-opening polymerization, and coordination polymerization such as Ziegler–Natta catalysis, used industrially for polyethylene and polypropylene.
