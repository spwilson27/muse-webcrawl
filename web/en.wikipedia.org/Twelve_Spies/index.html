Twelve Spies

The Twelve Spies, as recorded in the Book of Numbers (chapters 13–14), were a group of Israelite chieftains, one from each of the Twelve Tribes of Israel, whom Moses sent from the wilderness of Paran to scout the land of Canaan — the Promised Land — ahead of the Israelites' entry.

The report

After forty days the spies returned carrying a cluster of grapes so large it had to be borne on a pole between two men, together with pomegranates and figs. Ten of the spies gave a discouraging report: the land was fruitful, but its inhabitants were powerful and its cities fortified, and they described giants — the descendants of Anak — beside whom they seemed like grasshoppers. Only two, Joshua son of Nun and Caleb son of Jephunneh, urged the people to trust God and take the land.

Consequences

The Israelites believed the ten, wept, and rebelled against Moses, refusing to enter Canaan. As a consequence, God decreed that that generation would wander in the wilderness for forty years and die there; only Joshua and Caleb, of all the adults who had left Egypt, would live to enter the Promised Land. The ten spies who spread the bad report died by plague. The episode is commemorated in Jewish tradition as a cause of the calamities mourned on Tisha B'Av.
