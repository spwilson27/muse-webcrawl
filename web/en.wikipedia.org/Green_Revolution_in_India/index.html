Green Revolution in India

The Green Revolution in India was a period beginning in the mid-1960s in which Indian agriculture was transformed by the adoption of high-yielding varieties of wheat and rice, chemical fertilisers, pesticides and expanded irrigation. Led in India by agricultural scientist M. S. Swaminathan and drawing on semi-dwarf wheat varieties developed by Norman Borlaug in Mexico, the programme turned India from a food-deficit country dependent on imports into one of the world's leading agricultural producers.

Wheat cultivation in Punjab, the heartland of India's Green Revolution

Effects

Wheat production roughly tripled between the mid-1960s and the early 1990s, and famine — a recurring threat in earlier decades — was averted despite rapid population growth. The revolution was concentrated in irrigated regions such as Punjab, Haryana and western Uttar Pradesh. Critics note its costs: depletion of groundwater, soil salinisation and nutrient loss, reduced crop diversity, and widening regional and social inequalities. Later calls for an "evergreen revolution" seek productivity gains with greater ecological sustainability.
