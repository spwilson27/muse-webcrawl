British African-Caribbean people are an ethnic group in the United Kingdom. They are British citizens or residents of African-Caribbean ethnic and cultural heritage, whose ancestors largely emigrated from Caribbean countries in the British West Indies in the mid-20th century. They are also known as British Afro-Caribbean people, or as the Windrush generation and their descendants.

History

Small communities of African and Caribbean people existed in British port cities from the era of slavery and empire. Large-scale migration began after the Second World War: in 1948 the ship HMT Empire Windrush brought 492 Caribbean migrants to Tilbury, and through the 1950s and early 1960s tens of thousands came to work in transport, the new National Health Service, and industry. The Commonwealth Immigrants Acts of 1962 and 1968 and the Immigration Act 1971 progressively restricted this migration.

Culture

The community has had a profound influence on British culture. Caribbean music — calypso, ska, reggae, dub, jungle, UK garage and grime — shaped British popular music; Caribbean food, churches (notably Black-majority Pentecostal congregations), and literature flourished. The Notting Hill Carnival, Europe's largest street festival, was founded in the 1960s in response to racial tensions. Community members have excelled in sport, politics, media and the arts.

Discrimination and the Windrush scandal

The community faced significant racism, from the 1958 Notting Hill riots to discriminatory policing that helped spark the 1981 Brixton and other uprisings. In 2018 the "Windrush scandal" revealed that many long-settled members of the generation had been wrongly detained, denied services, and in some cases deported under "hostile environment" immigration policies, prompting a government apology and a compensation scheme.
