Supercluster

A supercluster is a large group of smaller galaxy clusters or galaxy groups; they are among the largest known structures in the universe. The Milky Way is part of the Local Group galaxy group, which in turn is part of the Virgo Cluster, which is part of the Laniakea Supercluster. The large size and low density of superclusters means that they, unlike clusters, expand with the Hubble expansion.

Existence

The existence of superclusters was first postulated by George Abell in his 1958 catalogue of galaxy clusters. Superclusters can contain dozens of individual clusters and span hundreds of millions of light-years. The number of superclusters in the observable universe is estimated to be around 10 million.

Notable superclusters

  • Laniakea Supercluster — the supercluster containing the Milky Way, defined in 2014, spanning about 520 million light-years and containing roughly 100,000 galaxies.
  • Virgo Supercluster — the older, smaller definition centered on the Virgo Cluster.
  • Shapley Supercluster — the largest concentration of galaxies in the nearby universe, in the constellation Centaurus.
  • Perseus–Pisces Supercluster — one of the largest known structures nearby, about 300 million light-years long.
  • Coma Supercluster — containing the Coma and Leo clusters.

Structure

Superclusters are not gravitationally bound as a whole; their component clusters drift apart with cosmic expansion. They are organized along galaxy filaments, separated by enormous voids nearly empty of galaxies.
