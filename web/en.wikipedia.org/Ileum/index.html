Ileum

The ileum (/ˈɪliəm/) is the final section of the small intestine in most higher vertebrates, including mammals, reptiles, and birds. In fish, the divisions of the small intestine are not as clear, and the terms posterior intestine or distal intestine may be used instead of ileum. Its main function is to absorb vitamin B12, bile salts, and whatever products of digestion that were not absorbed by the jejunum.

Structure

The ileum follows the duodenum and jejunum and is separated from the cecum by the ileocecal valve (ICV). In humans, the ileum is about 2–4 m long, and its pH is usually between 7 and 8 (neutral to slightly alkaline). There is no anatomical line separating the jejunum from the ileum; however, the ileum has thinner walls, fewer circular folds, and abundant aggregated lymphoid nodules known as Peyer's patches.

Function

The ileum absorbs vitamin B12–intrinsic factor complexes, bile acids (which are recycled to the liver via enterohepatic circulation), and remaining nutrients. Its wall contains many microscopic folds and villi that greatly increase the surface area for absorption. The Peyer's patches of the ileum are an important part of the gut-associated lymphoid tissue and play a role in immune surveillance.

Clinical significance

The ileum is commonly affected by Crohn's disease, which frequently involves the terminal ileum. Surgical removal or disease of the terminal ileum can cause vitamin B12 deficiency and bile acid malabsorption. Meckel's diverticulum, a congenital outpouching, occurs in the ileum in about 2% of the population.
