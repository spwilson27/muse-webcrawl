Endothermic process

Endothermic process
TypeThermodynamic process
Enthalpy changeΔH > 0 (heat absorbed)
OppositeExothermic process
ExamplesMelting, evaporation, photosynthesis

An endothermic process is any thermodynamic process in which the system's enthalpy increases — that is, the system absorbs heat from its surroundings. The term derives from the Greek endo- ("within") and thermē ("heat") and was coined by the French chemist Marcellin Berthelot. In chemical terms, an endothermic reaction has a positive change in enthalpy (ΔH > 0): the products hold more chemical potential energy than the reactants, the difference being supplied from the environment, so the surroundings are cooled.

Examples

Familiar endothermic processes include the melting of ice, evaporation of water, and sublimation of dry ice; the cooling effect of sweating depends on endothermic evaporation. Many chemical decompositions are endothermic: the calcination of limestone (CaCO₃ → CaO + CO₂) in cement making, the thermal cracking of hydrocarbons, and the reaction of barium hydroxide with ammonium chloride used in classroom demonstrations, which can freeze a wet board to the reaction flask. Photosynthesis is endothermic, storing light energy in glucose, as is the charging of a battery.

Thermodynamics

Whether an endothermic process proceeds spontaneously depends on the Gibbs free energy, ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. A positive ΔH works against spontaneity, but a sufficiently large positive entropy change ΔS can overcome it at high temperature — which is why endothermic processes such as melting and evaporation occur spontaneously above characteristic temperatures. Endothermic steps are also central to refrigeration and heat-pump cycles, in which a working fluid absorbs heat while evaporating at low pressure.

Distinctions

Endothermic should not be confused with endergonic, which refers to a positive Gibbs energy (a non-spontaneous process). Nor is the term related to endothermy in biology — the maintenance of body temperature by internal metabolic heat, as in mammals and birds — despite the shared prefix, since the biological usage concerns the source of an organism's heat rather than the sign of an enthalpy change.
