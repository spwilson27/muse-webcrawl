Hanbali

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Hanbali school (Arabic: ٱلْحَنْبَلِيَّة, romanized: al-Ḥanbaliyya) is one of the four major schools of Islamic jurisprudence (fiqh) within Sunni Islam. It is named after Ahmad ibn Hanbal (780–855), a scholar of Baghdad, and is noted for its strict reliance on the Quran and hadith (the practices and sayings of the Prophet Muhammad) and its relative suspicion of speculative reasoning in deriving law.

Principles

Hanbali legal theory ranks its sources as: the Quran; the Sunnah; the consensus (ijma) of the Prophet's companions; individual companion opinions; and only then, weakly, analogy (qiyas) — applied more narrowly than in the Hanafi or Maliki schools. Ibn Hanbal himself suffered imprisonment during the Mihna inquisition for refusing the doctrine that the Quran was created, cementing his reputation for scriptural literalism.

Distribution

Though historically the smallest of the four schools, Hanbalism is influential far beyond its numbers: it is the official school in Saudi Arabia and Qatar, and, through the medieval scholar Ibn Taymiyyah and the 18th-century reformer Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab, it shaped the Salafi and Wahhabi movements.
