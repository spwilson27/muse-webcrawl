Penal labour


Penal labour ranges from prison industries to forced labour camps.

Penal labour is a form of punishment in which prisoners are required to work, typically in physical labour. It spans a spectrum from ordinary prison employment and community service to hard labour imposed as an explicit part of a sentence, and to forced labour under brutal conditions in systems such as the Soviet Gulag and Nazi concentration camps.

History

Britain's Penal Servitude Acts of the 19th century replaced transportation with imprisonment with hard labour — famously including pointless tasks such as the treadmill, the crank, and oakum picking, though much labour was on public works such as dockyards and breakwaters. Convict leasing in the post–Civil War American South hired prisoners to private employers under notoriously lethal conditions. Hard labour as a formal sentence was abolished in the United Kingdom in 1948.

Modern practice and law

Work remains a normal part of imprisonment in most countries, defended as rehabilitative and challenged where wages and protections are minimal. International law (ILO Convention No. 29 and the ICCPR) prohibits forced labour but generally exempts labour exacted under a court sentence and public supervision; abuses arise where that exemption is stretched, as in mass internment-camp systems.
