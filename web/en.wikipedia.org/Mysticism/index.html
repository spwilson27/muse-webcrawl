Mysticism is the practice of religious ecstasies and the pursuit of direct, experiential knowledge of, or union with, ultimate reality, the divine, or spiritual truth. Mystical traditions exist within nearly all world religions, as well as in non-religious contexts. The term derives from the Greek myein, "to close (the eyes or mouth)", and mystikos, an initiate into secret rites.

Definitions and study

Scholars such as William James, in The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902), described mystical states as ineffable, noetic, transient, and passive. A long-running debate opposes perennialists, who hold that mystical experiences across cultures share a common core, with constructivists such as Steven Katz, who argue that experiences are shaped by the concepts and expectations of each tradition.

Mysticism in the world religions

In Christianity, mystics such as Meister Eckhart, Teresa of Ávila, and John of the Cross described stages of purification, illumination, and union with God. In Islam, Sufism developed practices of remembrance (dhikr) and produced poets such as Rumi. Hindu traditions pursue liberation (moksha) through yoga and meditation; Buddhism seeks awakening through practices leading to enlightenment; Judaism developed Kabbalah; and Taoism emphasises harmony with the Tao through contemplative stillness.

Practices

Common mystical practices include contemplative prayer, meditation, fasting, chanting, breath control, and retreat. Mystics often describe the dissolution of the ego and a sense of unity, timelessness, and profound peace. Institutional religions have alternately embraced and policed mystics, some of whom were canonised and others condemned as heretics.
