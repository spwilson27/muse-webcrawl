Video game localization

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Video game localization is the process of preparing video game software for sale in a new region or country. It goes beyond straight translation of text and dialogue to adapt language, artwork, audio, user interfaces, legal requirements and cultural references so that a game feels natural to players in the target market. In the industry, "internationalization" prepares a game to be adaptable, while "localization" performs the adaptation for a specific locale; the two together are known as globalization.

Process

Localizers translate in-game text, manuals and packaging; record dubbed voice acting to match lip movements and timing; adjust graphics containing text or culturally sensitive imagery; resize interface elements for languages whose words run longer; and convert date, number and currency formats. Games may also be edited to comply with regional law or ratings systems — historically, for example, removing blood or certain symbols for the German market — or to avoid offending local sensibilities.

The scope varies by budget and expected sales. Levels of localization are often described as: no localization; "box and docs" (packaging only); partial localization (text but no voice-over); and full localization (text, voice and art assets). Simultaneous worldwide release ("sim-ship") requires localization to proceed in parallel with development, often using incomplete builds and string databases.

History and issues

Early localized games of the 1980s and 1990s were notorious for poor translations produced quickly and without context, yielding infamous lines such as "All your base are belong to us" from Zero Wing. Larger budgets and dedicated localization departments — for example at Nintendo, Square Enix and Blizzard — have since raised quality, and fan translations have made otherwise unreleased games, such as Mother 3, playable for international audiences. Quality issues that persist include translating strings without context, censorship disputes, and regional changes to names, jokes and religious or political content.
