Inharmonicity

In music, inharmonicity is the degree to which the frequencies of overtones (known as partials or partial tones) depart from whole multiples of the fundamental frequency (harmonic series). Ideally, a vibrating string or column of air produces partials at exact integer multiples of the fundamental; in real instruments, the stiffness of the vibrating medium shifts the higher partials progressively sharp.

Causes

In piano strings, stiffness means the string resists bending, so partials are stretched: the nth partial is slightly higher than n times the fundamental. The effect is stronger for short, thick strings — hence in the bass of small pianos — and is quantified by an inharmonicity coefficient depending on the string's diameter, length, tension, and material.

Effect on piano tuning

Because of inharmonicity, piano tuners do not tune octaves to exact 2:1 frequency ratios. Instead they "stretch" octaves so that the fundamental of an upper note matches the (sharp) second partial of the note below, producing the characteristic Railsback curve, in which treble notes are tuned progressively sharper and bass notes flatter than theoretical equal temperament. Professional aural tuners and electronic tuning devices both account for this stretch.

Other instruments

Inharmonicity also colours the timbre of bells, whose prominent partials are deliberately shaped by the founder's profile, and of many percussion instruments. In digital sound synthesis, modelling inharmonicity is important for realistic piano and plucked-string sounds.
