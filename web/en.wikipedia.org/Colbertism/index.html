Colbertism

Colbertism (French: Colbertisme) is an economic doctrine and set of policies associated with Jean-Baptiste Colbert (1619–1683), who served as minister of finances and controller-general of finances under King Louis XIV of France from 1665 until his death. It is generally regarded as the French variant of mercantilism, the dominant economic doctrine of early modern Europe.

Principles

Colbert held that the wealth and power of a state depended on accumulating precious metals and maintaining a favourable balance of trade. France should export more than it imported, manufacture at home what it had previously bought abroad, and keep money circulating within the kingdom. To this end he promoted domestic manufactures through royal factories (manufactures royales), subsidies, monopolies and strict quality regulations intended to make French goods competitive with Dutch and English products.

Policies

Colbert raised tariffs on foreign goods, notably in the tariffs of 1664 and 1667, and worked to reduce internal customs barriers within France. He founded or supported prestigious manufactures such as the Gobelins tapestry works and the mirror manufactory that became Saint-Gobain, recruited skilled foreign artisans, and encouraged the import of technology. He also promoted colonial trade through chartered companies, including the French East India Company and the French West India Company, and invested in the navy, merchant shipping, ports and canals, including the Canal du Midi.

Legacy

Critics, both contemporary and later, argued that Colbert's regulation stifled enterprise and that his system favoured luxury production and the towns over agriculture. Nevertheless, "Colbertism" has remained a recurring reference point in French economic debate, often invoked to describe state-led industrial policy and strategic intervention in the economy, from the post-war dirigisme of the twentieth century to modern discussions of industrial sovereignty.
