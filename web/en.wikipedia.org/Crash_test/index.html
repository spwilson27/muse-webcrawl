Crash test

TypeDestructive safety testing
SubjectsMotor vehicles (also aircraft, structures)
Key bodiesEuro NCAP, NHTSA, IIHS, ANCAP

A crash test is a form of destructive testing used to determine the safety performance of a vehicle or other product in collisions. In automobile testing, instrumented vehicles are driven into barriers or other vehicles under controlled conditions while high-speed cameras, sensors, and crash test dummies record the forces involved, allowing engineers and regulators to evaluate occupant protection and structural crashworthiness.

Test types

Standard tests include full-width and offset frontal impacts, side impacts (including pole tests), rear impacts and whiplash evaluation, rollover tests, and pedestrian-protection tests. In the United States, the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration (NHTSA) runs the New Car Assessment Program, while the Insurance Institute for Highway Safety (IIHS) conducts its own tests, including the demanding small-overlap frontal test introduced in 2012. In Europe, Euro NCAP awards star ratings based on a battery of crash and safety-assist tests.

Crash test dummies

Anthropomorphic test devices such as the Hybrid III family are equipped with accelerometers and load cells measuring head, neck, chest, and leg loads, from which injury criteria are computed. Modern dummies include models representing women, children, and the elderly.

History

Organised crash testing began in the 1930s; General Motors conducted barrier tests from 1934, and Béla Barényi's work at Mercedes-Benz in the 1950s formalised the crumple-zone concept. Consumer test programmes from the late 1970s onward pushed substantial improvements in vehicle safety.
