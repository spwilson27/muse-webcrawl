Thermodynamic temperature

Thermodynamic temperature is the absolute measure of temperature, defined by the laws of thermodynamics such that absolute zero — the state in which a system has minimum possible internal energy and its particles approach minimum motion — is zero. Its SI unit is the kelvin (K), one of the seven SI base units.

Definition

The thermodynamic scale is defined through the efficiency of a reversible Carnot engine: the ratio of the temperatures of two reservoirs equals the ratio of heat exchanged with them in a reversible cycle. Since the 2019 redefinition of the SI, the kelvin is fixed by assigning an exact value to the Boltzmann constant, k = 1.380649 × 10−23 J/K, tying temperature directly to energy.

Kinetic interpretation

In statistical mechanics, thermodynamic temperature is a measure related to the average kinetic energy of particles: for an ideal gas, the mean translational kinetic energy per particle is (3/2)kT. Temperature also determines the direction of spontaneous heat flow between bodies in contact, per the zeroth law of thermodynamics.

Scales

Absolute scales using different degree sizes include the kelvin (Celsius-sized degrees) and the Rankine scale (Fahrenheit-sized degrees).
