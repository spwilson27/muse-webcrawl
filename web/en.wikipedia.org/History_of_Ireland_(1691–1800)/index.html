History of Ireland (1691–1800)

The history of Ireland from 1691 to 1800 covers the period from the end of the Williamite War to the Acts of Union. It is characterised by the dominance of the Protestant Ascendancy, the Penal Laws against Catholics and dissenters, the development of an Irish patriot politics in the Dublin parliament, and the revolutionary crisis of the 1790s culminating in the 1798 Rebellion and legislative union with Great Britain.

The Penal era

The Treaty of Limerick (1691) ended the Williamite War, but its terms protecting Catholics were soon undermined by the Irish Parliament, which passed a series of Penal Laws restricting Catholic land ownership, education, worship and political participation. By the mid-eighteenth century Catholics, though the large majority of the population, owned only a small fraction of the land. Presbyterians in Ulster also faced disabilities under the Test Act.

Eighteenth-century Ireland

Dublin grew into the second city of the British Empire, with great Georgian building under the Wide Streets Commission. The Irish economy prospered in linen and provisions, though trade was restricted in British interests. From the 1760s agrarian unrest by groups such as the Whiteboys, Oakboys and Steelboys protested rents, tithes and enclosure. Population rose rapidly, reaching over five million by 1800, with increasing dependence on the potato among the rural poor.

Patriot politics and 1782

The example of the American Revolution emboldened Irish Protestants: the Irish Volunteers, raised for defence against invasion, became a vehicle for demanding free trade and legislative independence. In 1782 the British government conceded the repeal of the Declaratory Act and of Poynings' Law constraints, giving the Irish Parliament — dominated by figures such as Henry Grattan — substantial legislative autonomy, though the executive in Dublin Castle remained appointed from London and Catholics remained excluded from Parliament.

Revolution and union

The French Revolution inspired the Society of United Irishmen, founded in Belfast in 1791 by Theobald Wolfe Tone and others, which sought parliamentary reform and Catholic emancipation and turned toward republican separatism. A French expedition to Bantry Bay in 1796 failed, and the rising of 1798 was crushed with great loss of life, perhaps 30,000 deaths. Convinced that the Irish Parliament was a security risk, the British government under William Pitt the Younger carried the Acts of Union 1800, which abolished the Irish Parliament and created the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland from 1 January 1801.
