Petrology (from Ancient Greek pétros 'rock' and lógos 'study') is the branch of geology that studies rocks and the conditions under which they form. Petrology combines field observation, microscopic examination (petrography), chemical and isotopic analysis, and high-pressure/high-temperature experiments to reconstruct the origin and evolution of the Earth's crust and mantle. It is closely allied with mineralogy and geochemistry.

Branches

Igneous petrology

Igneous petrology studies rocks formed by the cooling and solidification of magma or lava — intrusive (plutonic) rocks such as granite and gabbro, and volcanic rocks such as basalt and rhyolite. Central questions include how magmas are generated by partial melting of the mantle, how they evolve by fractional crystallisation, assimilation, and mixing, and how their compositions map onto tectonic settings such as mid-ocean ridges, subduction zones, and hotspots. Bowen's reaction series (1928) remains a foundational framework.

Sedimentary petrology

Sedimentary petrology examines clastic rocks (sandstone, shale), carbonates (limestone, dolomite), and chemical precipitates (evaporites, chert). It investigates provenance, transport, deposition, and diagenesis — the physical and chemical changes after burial. The field underpins petroleum geology, since sedimentary rocks form the reservoirs and source rocks for most oil and gas.

Metamorphic petrology

Metamorphic petrology studies how pre-existing rocks are transformed by heat, pressure, and chemically active fluids, producing schist, gneiss, marble, slate, and eclogite, among others. The concept of metamorphic facies and index minerals allows petrologists to infer the pressures and temperatures a rock experienced, tracing mountain-building (orogenic) events and subduction histories.

Methods

  • Petrography — description of rocks in hand specimen and in thin section under a polarising microscope.
  • Geochemistry — major- and trace-element analysis and radiogenic isotope ratios to date rocks and trace their sources.
  • Experimental petrology — reproducing crustal and mantle conditions in piston-cylinder and multi-anvil apparatus.
  • Thermodynamic modelling — computing phase equilibria (e.g., pseudosections) to reconstruct pressure–temperature–time paths.

History

Modern petrology emerged in the 19th century with the polarising microscope work of Henry Clifton Sorby and the systematic classifications of Ferdinand Zirkel and Harry Rosenbusch. In the 20th century, N. L. Bowen's experimental studies of silicate melts and Pentti Eskola's metamorphic facies concept transformed the discipline; plate tectonics in the 1960s supplied the global framework that organises petrological observations today.
