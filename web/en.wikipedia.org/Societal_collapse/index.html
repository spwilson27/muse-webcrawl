Societal collapse

Societal collapse (also known as civilizational collapse) is the fall of a complex society characterized by the loss of cultural identity and of social complexity as an adaptive system, the downfall of government, and the rise of violence. Possible causes include environmental change, resource depletion, unsustainable complexity, invasion, disease, and the breakdown of social cohesion.

Definitions and theory

Archaeologist Joseph Tainter, in The Collapse of Complex Societies (1988), defined collapse as a rapid loss of an established level of sociopolitical complexity, arguing that societies invest in complexity to solve problems but face diminishing returns that eventually make collapse an economical response. Jared Diamond's Collapse (2005) popularized a five-factor framework emphasizing environmental damage, climate change, hostile neighbours, loss of trading partners, and a society's responses.

Historical examples

Frequently cited cases include the Late Bronze Age collapse, the fall of the Western Roman Empire, the Classic Maya collapse, and the abandonment of settlements on Easter Island (Rapa Nui) — though recent scholarship disputes simpler ecocide narratives for several of these. Collapse is rarely total extinction: populations usually persist in simpler, reorganized societies.

Modern concerns

Contemporary discussion applies the concept to risks from climate change, pandemics, and financial crises, and to debates about resilience and "degrowth". Historians caution that collapse is often a transformation rather than an ending, and can even benefit ordinary people freed from extractive states.
