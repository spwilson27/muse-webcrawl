Experiment

MethodControlled manipulation of variables
PurposeTest hypotheses, establish causal relationships
Key featuresControl group, randomisation, replication, measurement
Used inNatural sciences, medicine, psychology, economics

An experiment is a procedure carried out to support or refute a hypothesis, or to determine the efficacy or likelihood of something previously untried. Unlike passive observation, an experiment deliberately manipulates one or more variables and measures the effect on others, ideally while holding everything else constant, so that a causal relationship can be inferred. Experiments provide insight into cause-and-effect by demonstrating what outcome occurs when a particular factor is varied, and their results are expected to be reproducible by other investigators following the same protocol.

Design

The classical design compares a treatment group against a control group that differs only in the variable under study. Random assignment of subjects to groups distributes confounding influences evenly; blinding — up to double-blind designs in clinical trials, where neither subjects nor experimenters know who received the treatment — guards against bias. Statisticians such as Ronald Fisher formalised the design of experiments in the early twentieth century, introducing randomisation, blocking, and factorial designs that vary several factors at once. Sample size, statistical power, and pre-registration of analysis plans have become central concerns, especially following the replication crisis in psychology and medicine.

Types and settings

Laboratory experiments maximise control but may sacrifice realism; field experiments — such as randomised controlled trials of development policies, recognised by the 2019 Nobel Prize in Economics — trade some control for real-world validity. Natural experiments exploit events outside the researcher's control (a policy change, a lottery) as if they were assignments. Not all science can experiment: astronomy, geology, and evolutionary biology rely largely on observation and prediction-testing, while in the social sciences experiments range from laboratory games to large-scale A/B tests run by technology companies.

Ethics

Experiments on humans are governed by ethical frameworks descending from the Nuremberg Code and the Declaration of Helsinki, requiring informed consent, favourable risk–benefit balance, and independent review; animal research is similarly regulated. The history of abuses, from the Tuskegee syphilis study onwards, underpins modern institutional review boards and remains a standing caution in experimental design.
