Climate change in the Arctic

Climate change in the Arctic is particularly rapid: the region is warming roughly three to four times faster than the global average, a phenomenon known as Arctic amplification. Major impacts include shrinking sea ice and the Greenland ice sheet, thawing permafrost, changing ecosystems, and new pressures and opportunities for the peoples, wildlife and shipping routes of the far north.

Amplification and sea ice

Warming is amplified by feedbacks: as bright sea ice melts, darker ocean water absorbs more solar energy; changes in clouds, water vapour and heat transport add further warming. September Arctic sea ice extent has declined by roughly 13 percent per decade since satellite records began in 1979, and the ice that remains is younger and thinner. Projections indicate the Arctic Ocean could be practically ice-free in late summer at least once before 2050.

Land and ecosystems

Permafrost thaw damages buildings, roads and pipelines, and releases carbon dioxide and methane stored in frozen soils. The Greenland ice sheet is losing mass and contributing to global sea level rise. Tundra is being colonised by shrubs and boreal forest species, while species dependent on ice, such as polar bears and ringed seals, face declining habitat. Indigenous communities confront eroding coasts, unsafe travel ice and changing subsistence hunting.

Geopolitics

Melting ice is opening the Northern Sea Route and Northwest Passage to shipping and increasing interest in Arctic oil, gas and mineral resources, raising questions of sovereignty, safety and environmental protection coordinated partly through the Arctic Council.
