Civilians as combatants-by-effort: war production, rationing and civil defence defined the home front.

Home front is an English-language term for the civilian population of a country at war, regarded as an active support system — and increasingly a target — behind the fighting fronts. The concept crystallised during the era of total war, above all the First and Second World Wars, when industrial production, food supply, morale and civilian endurance became as decisive as battlefield outcomes, and when blockade and aerial bombing deliberately attacked them.

First World War

Between 1914 and 1918 the belligerent states mobilised their whole societies: mass conscription, women entering industry and agriculture in unprecedented numbers, government control of factories, railways and food (rationing in Germany and Britain), and propaganda ministries managing morale. Britain's "home front" experienced Zeppelin and Gotha bomber raids; Germany's civilian population suffered severe deprivation under the Allied blockade (the "Turnip Winter" of 1916–17). Civilian collapse on the home fronts of Russia, Austria-Hungary and Germany in 1917–18 contributed directly to revolution and defeat.

Second World War

The home front expanded further in 1939–45. In Britain it meant evacuation of children, the Blitz, the Home Guard, rationing ("Dig for Victory") and the Beveridge Report's promise of a welfare state. The United States converted its industry into the "Arsenal of Democracy", with war bonds, scrap drives, rationing and millions of women in factory work ("Rosie the Riveter"), while Japanese Americans were interned. The Soviet home front endured evacuation of industry beyond the Urals and catastrophic civilian losses; Germany's was subjected to strategic bombing and total mobilisation under Goebbels's "Total War" speech (1943).

Legacy

The term remains in use for the domestic dimension of any conflict (for example, debates over support for war, civil liberties and media coverage in later wars), and historians of total war treat the blurring of the line between soldier and civilian — and the moral and legal questions it raised, addressed partly in post-1949 Geneva Conventions protections for civilians — as one of the defining transformations of 20th-century warfare.
