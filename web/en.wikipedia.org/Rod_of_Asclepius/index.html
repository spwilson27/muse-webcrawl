Rod of Asclepius

The Rod of Asclepius, symbol of medicine

The Rod of Asclepius, also known as the Staff of Aesculapius, is a serpent-entwined rod wielded by the Greek god Asclepius, a deity associated with healing and medicine. In modern times it is the predominant symbol for medicine and health care around the world, appearing in the emblem of the World Health Organization and in the Star of Life used by emergency medical services (there combined with the rod).

Greek mythology

Asclepius, son of Apollo, was taught healing by the centaur Chiron. In one account, Asclepius learned the art after watching a serpent bring another snake healing herbs. Zeus killed Asclepius with a thunderbolt for raising the dead, but later placed him among the stars as the constellation Ophiuchus, the Serpent-Bearer, at Apollo's request.

Interpretations

Scholars have proposed that the serpent, which sheds its skin, symbolises rebirth and rejuvenation. A popular alternative theory connects the symbol to the ancient treatment of Guinea worm disease, in which the parasitic worm was slowly wound around a stick over several days — a procedure that healers may have advertised with the image of a worm on a rod.

Confusion with the caduceus

The Rod of Asclepius is frequently confused with the caduceus of Hermes — a staff entwined by two snakes and topped with wings. The caduceus, associated with commerce and heralds, came into widespread use as a medical symbol in the United States in the 19th and 20th centuries, a usage many medical historians regard as a mistake.
