Effects of high altitude on humans

The effects of high altitude on humans are considerable. The oxygen saturation of haemoglobin determines the content of oxygen in the blood; at high altitude, where the partial pressure of oxygen in the air is reduced, less oxygen is available for respiration, producing the condition known as hypoxia. Humans can adapt to moderately high altitude through immediate and long-term acclimatization, but above roughly 8,000 metres (26,000 ft) — the "death zone" — no human body can acclimatize.

Physiological responses

The immediate response to hypoxia is an increase in breathing rate (hyperventilation) and heart rate, driven by chemoreceptors detecting low blood oxygen. Hyperventilation lowers carbon dioxide levels and causes respiratory alkalosis, which the kidneys compensate by excreting bicarbonate. Over days to weeks, the body produces more erythropoietin, increasing red blood cell production and haemoglobin concentration; changes in 2,3-bisphosphoglycerate help tissues unload oxygen.

Altitude sickness

Rapid ascent can cause acute mountain sickness (AMS), with headache, nausea, fatigue and disturbed sleep. In severe cases fluid accumulates in the lungs (HAPE) or the brain (HACE), both potentially fatal; the definitive treatment is descent. The drug acetazolamide is used for prevention and treatment of AMS.

Long-term adaptation

Populations native to high plateaus — Tibetans on the Tibetan Plateau, Andeans in the Altiplano, and Ethiopians in the Ethiopian Highlands — show distinct genetic and physiological adaptations. Tibetans, for example, carry variants in the EPAS1 gene (partly inherited from Denisovans) that moderate haemoglobin levels, while Andeans typically have higher haemoglobin concentrations and larger lung volumes. Chronic mountain sickness can affect long-term residents who overproduce red blood cells.

Performance and aviation

Aerobic performance declines with altitude, though anaerobic events can benefit from reduced air resistance; altitude training is widely used by endurance athletes. In aviation, unpressurised flight above about 3,000 m requires supplemental oxygen, and rapid decompression at cruising altitude leaves only seconds of useful consciousness.
