Nineveh was an ancient Assyrian city of Upper Mesopotamia, located on the eastern bank of the Tigris River opposite the modern city of Mosul, Iraq. It was the capital and largest city of the Neo-Assyrian Empire, and for a time the largest city in the world, with massive walls some 12 kilometres in circumference enclosing palaces and temples.

Nineveh is mentioned in the Bible as the great city to which the prophet Jonah was sent, and as the capital from which Sennacherib launched his campaigns. Under Sennacherib (r. 705–681 BC) the city was transformed with a new palace, aqueducts and waterworks; his grandson Ashurbanipal built the famous library whose cuneiform tablets—including the Epic of Gilgamesh—were rediscovered in the nineteenth century.

Fall and excavation

In 612 BC Nineveh was besieged and destroyed by a coalition of Babylonians, Medes and Scythians, ending the Assyrian Empire. The site was excavated from the 1840s by Austen Henry Layard and others, yielding colossal winged-bull statues (lamassu) and palace reliefs now in the British Museum and other collections. Parts of the ruins suffered damage during the occupation of Mosul by the Islamic State group in 2014–2017, and reconstruction efforts have followed.
