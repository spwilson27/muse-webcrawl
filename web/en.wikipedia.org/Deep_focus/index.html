Deep focus

Deep focus is a photographic and cinematographic technique using a large depth of field. With deep focus, a scene is rendered so that elements in the foreground, middle ground and background all appear acceptably sharp, allowing significant action to occur in several planes of the image at once.

Technique

Deep focus is achieved with small apertures, wide-angle lenses, bright lighting and, historically, fast film stocks; in the studio era it also depended on advances in lens coating and lighting equipment. It is the opposite of shallow focus, where only one plane is sharp and the rest falls off into blur.

Notable uses

The technique is strongly associated with cinematographer Gregg Toland and director Orson Welles on Citizen Kane (1941), and with William Wyler's films shot by Toland, where deep staging let viewers choose where to look within the frame. Film theorist André Bazin championed deep focus and the long take as a more democratic, realistic style of filmmaking than montage editing, because it preserved spatial unity and ambiguity.

Modern practice

Small-sensor digital cameras naturally produce deep focus, while contemporary filmmakers often emulate it with split-field diopters or compositing when using large-format cameras with inherently shallow depth of field.
