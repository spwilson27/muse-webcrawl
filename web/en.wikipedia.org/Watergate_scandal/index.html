The Watergate scandal was a major political scandal in the United States that began with the break-in at the Democratic National Committee headquarters in the Watergate office complex in Washington, D.C. on 17 June 1972, and the subsequent cover-up by the administration of President Richard Nixon. It culminated in Nixon's resignation on 9 August 1974 — the only resignation of a U.S. president.

The break-in and cover-up

Five men connected to Nixon's re-election committee (CREEP) were arrested inside the DNC offices, where they had been wiretapping phones and photographing documents. The administration denied involvement and paid hush money to the burglars while obstructing the FBI investigation.

Investigation

Reporting by Washington Post journalists Bob Woodward and Carl Bernstein, aided by an informant later revealed as FBI associate director Mark Felt ("Deep Throat"), kept the story alive. Senate hearings in 1973 revealed the existence of Nixon's secret Oval Office taping system. The "Saturday Night Massacre", in which Nixon ordered the firing of the special prosecutor, provoked public outrage.

Resignation and aftermath

In July 1974 the Supreme Court ordered the tapes released; the "Smoking Gun" tape proved Nixon had approved the cover-up days after the break-in. Facing certain impeachment, Nixon resigned, and his successor Gerald Ford pardoned him. The scandal produced lasting reforms on campaign finance and government ethics, and popularised the suffix "-gate" for scandals.
