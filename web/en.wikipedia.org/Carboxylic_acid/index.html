Carboxylic acid

In organic chemistry, a carboxylic acid is an organic acid that contains a carboxyl group (–C(=O)–OH) attached to an R-group. The general formula of a carboxylic acid is R–COOH or R–CO2H, with R referring to an organyl group. Carboxylic acids occur widely in nature and industry: familiar examples include acetic acid (in vinegar), citric acid (in citrus fruit), formic acid (in ant stings) and the fatty acids that form fats and oils.

Nomenclature and structure

Systematic names replace the ending of the parent hydrocarbon with -oic acid (methanoic, ethanoic, propanoic acid), but many trivial names persist. The carboxyl group is planar and strongly hydrogen-bonded: simple carboxylic acids exist largely as cyclic dimers in the liquid and solid phases, giving them unusually high boiling points for their size. Salts and esters of carboxylic acids are called carboxylates.

Acidity

Carboxylic acids are weak acids, typically with pKa values of 4–5, donating a proton to form a resonance-stabilised carboxylate anion in which the negative charge is shared equally between the two oxygen atoms. Electron-withdrawing substituents increase acidity (trichloroacetic acid is far stronger than acetic acid), while electron-donating alkyl groups slightly reduce it. They react with bases to give salts — with sodium bicarbonate producing visible effervescence of CO2, a classic test distinguishing them from phenols — and with alcohols under acid catalysis to form esters in the Fischer esterification.

Preparation and reactions

Industrially, carboxylic acids are prepared by oxidation of primary alcohols and aldehydes, by carbonylation of alkenes and methanol (acetic acid is made mainly by the Monsanto/Cativa carbonylation of methanol), and by hydrolysis of nitriles, esters and amides. Important reactions include conversion to acyl chlorides with thionyl chloride, reduction to primary alcohols with lithium aluminium hydride, decarboxylation of β-keto acids and salts on heating, and the Hell–Volhard–Zelinsky α-bromination. Carboxylic acids are feedstocks for soaps (by saponification of fats), polymers, pharmaceuticals such as aspirin and ibuprofen, and synthetic fibres.

Occurrence

Amino acids, the building blocks of proteins, contain both carboxyl and amino groups. Dicarboxylic acids such as oxalic and succinic acid occur in metabolism, tricarboxylic citric acid gives the citric acid cycle its name, and the long-chain fatty acids are central to biochemistry and nutrition.
