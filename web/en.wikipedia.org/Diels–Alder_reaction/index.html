Diels–Alder reaction

The Diels–Alder reaction is a chemical reaction between a conjugated diene and a substituted alkene, termed the dienophile, forming a substituted cyclohexene system. It is the prototypical [4+2] cycloaddition: four π-electrons of the diene and two of the dienophile combine in a single concerted, pericyclic step. Otto Diels and Kurt Alder reported the reaction in 1928 and received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1950 for its discovery and development.

Butadiene and an alkene combine to give a cyclohexene ring.

Mechanism and stereochemistry

The reaction proceeds through a single cyclic transition state, so the stereochemistry of both partners is retained in the product (the cis principle): substituents that are cis on the dienophile remain cis in the ring. Products commonly favour the endo configuration (the Alder endo rule), attributed to secondary orbital interactions. Because two carbon–carbon bonds and up to four stereocentres form at once, the reaction is exceptionally powerful for building complex ring systems.

Variants

Electron-rich dienes react fastest with electron-poor dienophiles ("normal demand"), while electron-poor dienes pair with electron-rich dienophiles ("inverse demand"). Heteroatoms can participate in hetero-Diels–Alder reactions, and the retro-Diels–Alder reaction cleaves cyclohexenes on heating. Lewis acids often catalyse and sharpen the selectivity of the reaction, and intramolecular variants assemble polycyclic natural products efficiently.

Applications

The Diels–Alder reaction is a workhorse of organic synthesis, used in routes to steroids, alkaloids and many pharmaceuticals, and features in "click" chemistry and in materials science for self-healing polymers.
