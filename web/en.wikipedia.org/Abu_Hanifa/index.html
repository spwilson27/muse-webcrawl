Abu Hanifa

Abu Hanifa al-Nu'man ibn Thabit (699 – 767 CE), often known simply as Abu Hanifa and reverently as al-Imam al-A'zam ("the Greatest Imam"), was a Sunni Muslim theologian and jurist who became the eponymous founder of the Hanafi school of Islamic jurisprudence, the largest and oldest of the four major schools (madhhabs) of Sunni law. Born in Kufa, in present-day Iraq, he spent most of his life there and died in Baghdad.

Life

Abu Hanifa was of Persian ancestry; his grandfather Zuta is said to have been brought from Kabul to Kufa as a slave and later freed. Abu Hanifa worked as a silk merchant, a trade that funded his studies and kept him independent of government patronage. He studied law under Hammad ibn Abi Sulayman for many years. Living through the fall of the Umayyad Caliphate and the rise of the Abbasids, he reportedly refused appointments as a judge, and was imprisoned by the caliph al-Mansur, dying in prison or shortly after his release in 767.

Jurisprudence

Abu Hanifa's method gave a large role to systematic reasoning: besides the Quran and Sunnah, he employed qiyas (analogical reasoning), istihsan (juristic preference), and 'urf (local custom). His approach suited the cosmopolitan commercial environment of Iraq, where novel cases required principled answers beyond the transmitted reports available in his day. His two most famous disciples, Abu Yusuf and Muhammad al-Shaybani, recorded and systematized his opinions, giving the school its classical form.

Legacy

The Hanafi school became the official madhhab of the Abbasid, Mughal and Ottoman empires and remains predominant among Sunni Muslims in Turkey, the Balkans, Central and South Asia, and parts of the Middle East — roughly a third of the world's Muslims. His tomb in Baghdad's Adhamiyah district, marked by the Abu Hanifa Mosque, remains a site of visitation.
