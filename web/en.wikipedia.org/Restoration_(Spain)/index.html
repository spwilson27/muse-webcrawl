The Restoration (Spanish: Restauración) is the name given to the period in Spanish history that began in 1874 with the restoration of the Bourbon monarchy under Alfonso XII, ending the First Spanish Republic, and ended in 1931 with the proclamation of the Second Spanish Republic.

The political system

The Restoration system was designed principally by Antonio Cánovas del Castillo and embodied in the Constitution of 1876. It rested on the turno pacífico, a managed alternation in power between the two dynastic parties — Cánovas's Conservative Party and Práxedes Mateo Sagasta's Liberal Party — with elections arranged in advance through local political bosses known as caciques. The system brought decades of relative stability after the turmoil of the Sexenio Democrático, but at the cost of widespread electoral corruption and the exclusion of republicans, regionalists, and the labour movement.

Decline

The regime was shaken by the disaster of 1898, when Spain lost Cuba, Puerto Rico, and the Philippines in the Spanish–American War, and by growing social conflict, Catalan and Basque nationalism, and war in Morocco. The 1921 disaster of Annual intensified the crisis. In 1923 General Miguel Primo de Rivera seized power with the king's acquiescence, suspending the constitution; his dictatorship collapsed in 1930, and municipal elections in April 1931, read as a plebiscite on the monarchy, led Alfonso XIII to leave Spain and the republic to be proclaimed.
