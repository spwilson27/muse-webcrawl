Perennialism

Perennialism, also known as the perennial philosophy (Latin: philosophia perennis), is a perspective in philosophy and spirituality that views the world's religious traditions as sharing a single, metaphysical truth or origin from which all esoteric and exoteric knowledge and doctrine has grown. It holds that beneath the differing rituals, doctrines and scriptures of the great religions lies a common core of universal truth about the nature of reality, the self and the divine.

History

The term philosophia perennis was first used by the Italian humanist Agostino Steuco in the 16th century and was popularised by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. In the 20th century the idea was developed by the Traditionalist School, whose members included René Guénon, Ananda Coomaraswamy and Frithjof Schuon, and was brought to a wide readership by Aldous Huxley's book The Perennial Philosophy (1945). Huston Smith later became one of its best-known expositors in comparative religion.

Core ideas

Perennialists typically affirm a distinction between the exoteric (outer, literal) and esoteric (inner, spiritual) dimensions of religion, arguing that the great traditions agree at the esoteric level. Common themes include the existence of a divine ground of being, the possibility of direct experiential knowledge of that ground, the immortality of the soul, and the unity of all reality.

Criticism

Critics argue that perennialism flattens real doctrinal differences between religions, decontextualises practices from their traditions, and reflects the preoccupations of modern Western interpreters more than those of the traditions themselves. Constructivist scholars of mysticism, such as Steven T. Katz, contend that mystical experiences are always mediated by the concepts of the experiencer's own tradition, challenging the idea of a single shared experience.
