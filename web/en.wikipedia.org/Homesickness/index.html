Homesickness

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Homesickness is the distress caused by being away from home. Its cognitive hallmark is preoccupying thoughts of home and attachment objects. Sufferers typically report anxiety and depressive symptoms, withdrawal and difficulty concentrating on topics unrelated to home. Homesickness usually disappears as people adjust to the new environment, though it varies in intensity and duration.

The word derives from the German Heimweh and was long treated as a medical condition under the name nostalgia, coined in 1688 by Swiss physician Johannes Hofer to describe the severe, sometimes fatal, longing for home observed in Swiss mercenary soldiers. Through the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries nostalgia was listed as a cause of death among soldiers; by the twentieth century the term shifted to its modern meaning of a sentimental longing for the past, while homesickness came to be studied by psychology as a normal, if sometimes intense, adjustment reaction.

Risk factors and prevalence

Homesickness is most commonly reported by children at boarding school or summer camp, students beginning university, military recruits, and migrants. Contributing factors include the distance from home, perceived control over the move, prior experience of separation, attachment style, and attitudes toward the new environment. Studies suggest that nearly all people away from home miss something about it; severe homesickness affects a smaller minority, with estimates of intense distress among first-year students and young campers commonly in the range of 5–20 percent.

Symptoms and coping

Symptoms range from tearfulness, insomnia, and loss of appetite to social withdrawal and declining performance. Research-based coping strategies emphasise keeping in contact with home while actively engaging with the new setting: building routines, making local social connections, keeping familiar objects, and reframing the separation as time-limited. Institutions increasingly provide orientation and mentoring programmes to ease transitions. Where distress persists or intensifies, counselling may be recommended, as chronic homesickness can shade into adjustment disorder or depression.
