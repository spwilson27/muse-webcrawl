Propositional logic

Propositional logic (also called propositional calculus or sentential logic) is the branch of logic that studies ways of joining and modifying entire propositions with logical connectives such as and, or, not, and if...then. Unlike predicate logic, it has no variables, quantifiers, or relations within propositions; each proposition is treated as an indivisible unit that is either true or false.

Connectives and truth tables

The standard connectives are negation (¬), conjunction (∧), disjunction (∨), implication (→), and the biconditional (↔). The meaning of a compound formula is fixed by truth tables: a conjunction is true only when both conjuncts are true, a disjunction is false only when both disjuncts are false, and a material implication is false only when its antecedent is true and its consequent false. A formula true under every assignment is a tautology; one false under every assignment is a contradiction.

Proof systems

Propositional logic can be presented through Hilbert-style axiomatic systems, natural deduction (introduced by Gentzen in 1934), or sequent calculi. The system is sound and complete: every tautology is provable. Deciding satisfiability (the SAT problem) is NP-complete, as shown by the Cook–Levin theorem, making propositional logic central to theoretical computer science.

History

Propositional logic was first systematically developed by the Stoics, especially Chrysippus, and was formalized in the modern era by Gottlob Frege, Bertrand Russell, and George Boole, whose algebra of logic underlies digital circuit design.
