Licence Raj

The Licence Raj or Permit Raj was the system of licences, regulations, and accompanying red tape that governed the setting up and running of businesses in India between 1947 and 1991. The term plays on "British Raj", the period of British rule.

System

Under laws such as the Industries (Development and Regulation) Act of 1951, private companies required government licences to establish new factories, expand capacity, change product lines, or relocate. Imports, foreign exchange, and investment were also heavily controlled, and large firms were constrained by the Monopolies and Restrictive Trade Practices Act. Supporters intended the system to direct resources to national priorities and prevent concentration of wealth.

Criticism

Critics, including the politician C. Rajagopalachari, who coined the term, argued that the Licence Raj fostered corruption, inefficiency, and rent-seeking, and restrained entrepreneurship. India's GDP growth averaged roughly 3.5 percent a year during much of this period, a rate economist Raj Krishna dubbed the "Hindu rate of growth".

Abolition

Following a balance-of-payments crisis in 1991, the government of P. V. Narasimha Rao and finance minister Manmohan Singh introduced sweeping economic liberalization, abolishing industrial licensing for all but a few sectors and opening the economy to trade and foreign investment.
