Hubris

Hubris (from Ancient Greek ὕβρις, hýbris) describes a personality quality of extreme or excessive pride or dangerous overconfidence, often in combination with arrogance. In classical Greek religion and mythology, hubris was the offence of treating others with contempt, violence, or insolence, particularly by someone in power toward those beneath them, and it was believed to attract the wrath of the gods — notably the goddess Nemesis, who delivered retribution.

In ancient Athenian law, hubris was a serious criminal charge, covering acts of deliberate dishonor, assault, and sexual violence. Aristotle defined hubris in the Rhetoric as doing and saying things that cause shame to the victim, not for any advantage to oneself but simply for the pleasure of it, emphasizing that the essence of the offence lay in dishonoring another.

In literature

Hubris is a central theme of Greek tragedy. Figures such as Oedipus, whose confidence in his own intellect leads him to defy prophecy, and Icarus, who flies too close to the sun, exemplify pride punished by fate. In later literature, the concept informs characters from Doctor Faustus to Captain Ahab, whose monomaniacal overreach brings destruction.

Modern usage

In modern usage, hubris refers to overweening pride, self-confidence, or arrogance, often coupled with a lack of humility. Historians Barbara Tuchman and David Owen have studied its role in political and corporate failure; Owen's "hubris syndrome" describes an acquired personality disorder among powerful leaders marked by contempt for advice and an exaggerated belief in their own judgment.
