Kepler problem

FieldClassical mechanics, celestial mechanics
Force lawInverse-square central force
OrbitsConic sections: ellipse, parabola, hyperbola

In classical mechanics, the Kepler problem is the special case of the two-body problem in which two bodies interact through a central force that varies in strength as the inverse square of the distance between them. It describes, to first approximation, a planet orbiting the Sun under gravity, or an electron in the Coulomb field of a nucleus.

Formulation

For two point masses m1 and m2 separated by distance r, the gravitational force is F = −Gm1m2/r². Reducing the system to a single particle of reduced mass μ moving in the central potential U(r) = −k/r (with k = Gm1m2 for gravity) converts the problem into motion in a plane, because angular momentum is conserved.

Solution

The orbits are conic sections with the centre of force at one focus: ellipses for negative total energy (bound orbits), parabolas for zero energy, and hyperbolas for positive energy. This reproduces Kepler's laws of planetary motion: planets move on ellipses with the Sun at a focus, sweep out equal areas in equal times, and have orbital periods whose squares are proportional to the cubes of their semi-major axes. Solving for the position as a function of time requires Kepler's equation, M = E − e·sin E, which must be solved numerically.

Symmetry and the Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector

Bound Kepler orbits close on themselves — the ellipse does not precess — because of an extra conserved quantity, the Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector, which points along the major axis. This hidden symmetry explains the degeneracy of the hydrogen atom's energy levels and makes the Kepler problem, with the harmonic oscillator, one of only two central-force problems (by Bertrand's theorem) in which all bound orbits are closed.

Applications

The Kepler problem underlies orbital mechanics and spacecraft trajectory design, Rutherford scattering (the hyperbolic case), and the semiclassical Bohr model of the atom.
