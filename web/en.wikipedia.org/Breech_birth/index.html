A breech birth is when a baby is born bottom first instead of head first. Most babies settle into a head-down (cephalic) position before birth; around 3–5% remain in a breech presentation at term. Breech births occur also in many other mammals.

Types

In a frank breech, the baby's buttocks come first and the legs are extended up alongside the body, feet near the head. In a complete breech, the knees are flexed so the baby sits cross-legged. In a footling breech, one or both feet present first. A kneeling presentation is rare.

Causes

Factors associated with breech presentation include prematurity, multiple pregnancy, placenta praevia, abnormalities of the uterus or fetus, and too much or too little amniotic fluid. In most cases no cause is identified.

Management

External cephalic version — manually turning the baby through the abdominal wall — is commonly attempted from about 36 weeks. If the baby remains breech, the choice is between planned caesarean section and planned vaginal breech delivery. The large Term Breech Trial (2000) found lower perinatal mortality and serious neonatal morbidity with planned caesarean section, and its publication greatly increased caesarean rates for breech worldwide; later studies suggested that, with careful selection and experienced attendants, vaginal breech birth can be a reasonably safe option for some women.

Risks

Complications of vaginal breech delivery include cord prolapse, birth injury, and entrapment of the aftercoming head. Caesarean section carries its own surgical risks and implications for future pregnancies.
