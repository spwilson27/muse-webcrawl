Missouri is a state in the Midwestern region of the United States, bordered by eight other states—tied with Tennessee for the most neighbours. It takes its name from the Missouri River, named for the Siouan-speaking Missouria people. Its capital is Jefferson City, its largest city Kansas City, and its largest metropolitan area St. Louis. Missouri is nicknamed the "Show-Me State" and the "Cave State," reflecting its roughly 6,000 known caves.

History

Missouri was acquired by the United States in the Louisiana Purchase of 1803. Its admission as a slave state was paired with Maine's admission as a free state in the Missouri Compromise of 1820, which drew the 36°30′ line across the Louisiana territory. St. Louis became the "Gateway to the West": the Lewis and Clark Expedition set out from near the city in 1804, and the great wagon-train routes began at Independence and other Missouri towns. The state remained in the Union during the Civil War despite a rival pro-Confederate government and fierce guerrilla warfare. The Pony Express began at St. Joseph in 1860, and St. Louis hosted the 1904 World's Fair and Olympics.

Geography and culture

The Missouri and Mississippi Rivers define much of the state's landscape; south of the Missouri River lie the Ozark highlands, with rolling forested hills, springs, and Lake of the Ozarks, while the north is prairie farmland. Missouri is the birthplace of Mark Twain (Hannibal), Harry S. Truman (Independence, the only Missourian president), ragtime and Kansas City jazz, and toasted ravioli and Kansas City barbecue. The 630-foot Gateway Arch in St. Louis, completed in 1965, commemorates westward expansion and is the state's most famous landmark.

For much of the 20th century Missouri was a famous presidential bellwether—the "Missouri bellwether"—voting for the winner in nearly every election from 1904 to 2004, before trending Republican in the 21st century.
