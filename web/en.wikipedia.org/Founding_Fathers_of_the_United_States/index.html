Founding Fathers of the United States

Key figuresGeorge Washington, John Adams, Thomas Jefferson, James Madison, Alexander Hamilton, Benjamin Franklin, John Jay
Key documentsDeclaration of Independence (1776), Constitution (1787), Bill of Rights (1791)

The Founding Fathers of the United States, commonly referred to as the Founding Fathers or the Founders, were a group of late-18th-century American revolutionary leaders who united the Thirteen Colonies, oversaw the War of Independence from Great Britain, established the United States, and crafted a framework of government in a single generation.

Who counts as a Founder

There is no fixed roster. The term can cover the 56 signers of the Declaration of Independence, the 55 delegates to the Constitutional Convention of 1787 (39 of whom signed the Constitution), and other political and military leaders of the Revolutionary era. Historian Richard B. Morris identified seven key figures: Benjamin Franklin, George Washington, John Adams, Thomas Jefferson, John Jay, James Madison and Alexander Hamilton.

Achievements

The Founders led the rebellion declared in 1776, won independence (recognised by Britain in the Treaty of Paris, 1783), replaced the weak Articles of Confederation with the Constitution of 1787, and secured its ratification with the Federalist Papers, written by Hamilton, Madison and Jay. The first ten amendments — the Bill of Rights — followed in 1791. Washington's two-term presidency set durable precedents, including the peaceful transfer of power in 1800.

Ideas and legacy

Drawing on Enlightenment thought and English constitutional tradition, the Founders institutionalised republicanism, separation of powers, federalism and individual rights. Their legacy is both celebrated and contested: many held enslaved people, and the Constitution's compromises with slavery helped precipitate the Civil War a lifetime later — a conflict that began at Fort Sumter. The phrase "Founding Fathers" was popularised by Warren G. Harding in the early 20th century.
