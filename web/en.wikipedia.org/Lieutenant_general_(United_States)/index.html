The three stars worn by a lieutenant general in the United States armed forces.

In the United States, a lieutenant general is a three-star general officer rank in the United States Army, Marine Corps, Air Force and Space Force. A lieutenant general ranks above a major general and below a general. The equivalent rank in the naval services is vice admiral. The pay grade is O-9.

Role and appointment

Lieutenant generals typically command corps-sized units, serve as deputy commanders of combatant commands, or hold senior staff posts in the Pentagon and the services. Because the rank is tied to specific positions of importance and responsibility, officers are nominated by the President and confirmed by the Senate for each three-star assignment; the grade is temporary and reverts when the officer leaves the position, unless the officer retires in grade under statutory conditions.

Statute limits the number of officers who may serve in three- and four-star grades at any one time, and three-star officers generally must retire after 38 years of service or at age 64, subject to exceptions and presidential deferrals.

History

The grade was first used in the United States Army during the Civil War era: Ulysses S. Grant was promoted to lieutenant general in 1864 to command all Union armies. The rank became a regular feature of the Army in the 20th century as corps commands proliferated in the two world wars, and it was adopted in parallel by the Marine Corps and the independent Air Force.

Insignia

Lieutenant generals wear three silver stars on shoulder boards, epaulettes and collars, arranged according to service custom. The Army abbreviation is LTG, the Marine Corps LtGen, and the Air Force and Space Force Lt Gen.
