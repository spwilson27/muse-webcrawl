Canadian French

Canadian French (French: français canadien) is the French language as spoken in Canada. It encompasses several regional varieties, principally Quebec French, Acadian French in the Maritimes, and smaller varieties in Ontario, the Prairies, and elsewhere. About 7.3 million Canadians — roughly one in five — report French as a mother tongue, concentrated in Quebec, where French is the sole official language, and New Brunswick, Canada's only officially bilingual province.

History

French arrived in Canada with colonists of New France from 1608 onward, drawn mainly from northwestern France. After the British conquest of 1763, the colonial variety developed in relative isolation, retaining some features of 17th-century French while evolving its own lexicon for North American realities — flora, fauna, weather, and Indigenous borrowings such as caribou (from Mi'kmaq) and atoca (cranberry).

Varieties

Quebec French is the prestige and media standard in Canada, with distinctive pronunciation (affrication of /t/ and /d/, vowel diphthongization), vocabulary (char for car, dépanneur for corner store), and idioms. Acadian French preserves older phonological features and has related creole-influenced offshoots such as Chiac in southeastern New Brunswick. Franco-Ontarian and Western Canadian varieties have receded under English pressure but remain institutionally supported.

Status and policy

French is co-official at the federal level under the Official Languages Act (1969), and the Charter of the French Language (1977) made French the language of government, work, and commerce in Quebec. The Office québécois de la langue française regulates usage and proposes terminology, such as courriel for e-mail.
