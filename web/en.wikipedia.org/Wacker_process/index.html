Wacker process


Type: Industrial oxidation process
Developed: Late 1950s, Wacker Chemie
Reactant: Ethylene
Product: Acetaldehyde
Catalysts: PdCl2 / CuCl

The Wacker process is the oxidation of ethylene to acetaldehyde in the presence of a palladium chloride catalyst. Developed at the German company Wacker Chemie in the late 1950s, it was the first industrial process based on a palladium catalyst and one of the first homogeneous catalysis processes operated on a large scale.

Chemistry

The overall reaction converts ethylene, oxygen and water into acetaldehyde:

CH2=CH2 + ½ O2 → CH3CHO

In the catalytic cycle, palladium(II) chloride oxidises the coordinated alkene and is itself reduced to palladium(0); copper(I) chloride reoxidises the palladium, and oxygen from air reoxidises the copper, so only oxygen is consumed overall. Mechanistically the process proceeds through coordination of ethylene to PdCl2, nucleophilic attack of water (hydroxypalladation), β-hydride elimination and further steps that release acetaldehyde.

Variants

Two industrial designs were used: the one-step process, in which ethylene and oxygen react together in a single reactor, and the two-step process, which regenerates the catalyst with air in a separate vessel. The chemistry generalises to other terminal alkenes — propylene gives acetone — and the underlying method remains a textbook example in organometallic chemistry, spawning a large family of laboratory Wacker-type oxidations.

History and decline

At its peak the process produced well over a million tonnes of acetaldehyde per year. Demand declined from the 1970s as acetic acid production shifted to methanol carbonylation (the Monsanto and Cativa processes) and acetaldehyde's other uses shrank, but the Wacker process remains historically important as a landmark of industrial catalysis.
