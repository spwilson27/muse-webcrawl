SETI

SETI is the acronym for the search for extraterrestrial intelligence: a collection of scientific efforts to detect evidence of intelligent life beyond Earth, principally by listening for electromagnetic signals — especially narrow-band radio transmissions — that could not arise from known natural sources. The field emerged from a 1959 paper by Giuseppe Cocconi and Philip Morrison and Frank Drake's Project Ozma in 1960.

Radio telescopes scan the sky for artificial narrow-band signals.

Methods

Most SETI searches have concentrated on the microwave "water hole" between the hydrogen (1420 MHz) and hydroxyl (1662 MHz) spectral lines. Major projects have included NASA's Microwave Observing Project (cancelled by Congress in 1993), Project Phoenix, and SERENDIP, as well as the distributed-computing project SETI@home (1999–2020), which analysed data from the Arecibo Observatory. Optical SETI searches for brief laser pulses. In 2015 the privately funded Breakthrough Listen initiative began a ten-year, $100 million programme using the Green Bank and Parkes telescopes. To date, no confirmed extraterrestrial signal has been found; the famous 1977 "Wow! signal" was never repeated.

The Drake equation and the Fermi paradox

Frank Drake's 1961 equation estimates the number of communicative civilisations in the Milky Way by multiplying factors such as the rate of star formation, the fraction of stars with planets, and the lifetime of technological civilisations. The apparent absence of any such civilisations, despite the galaxy's size and age, is the Fermi paradox.

Organisations

The nonprofit SETI Institute, founded in 1984 in Mountain View, California, conducts much SETI research and related astrobiology. Related endeavours include METI (messaging extraterrestrial intelligence) — deliberately transmitting signals, such as the 1974 Arecibo message — which remains controversial.
