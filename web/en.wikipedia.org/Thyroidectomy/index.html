Thyroidectomy

SpecialtyEndocrine surgery, general surgery
IndicationsThyroid cancer, goitre, hyperthyroidism, suspicious nodules
ApproachOpen cervical incision; endoscopic or robotic variants

A thyroidectomy is an operation that involves the surgical removal of all or part of the thyroid gland. In medicine, it is performed by endocrine surgeons or general surgeons for patients with thyroid disorders, including thyroid cancer, symptomatic goitre, and overactive thyroid (thyrotoxicosis or hyperthyroidism) that has not responded to other treatment.

Types

A total thyroidectomy removes the entire gland; a hemithyroidectomy (thyroid lobectomy) removes one lobe; a subtotal thyroidectomy leaves a small remnant; and an isthmusectomy removes the bridge of tissue between the lobes. The choice depends on the disease: total removal is usual for most thyroid cancers and bilateral disease, while lobectomy may suffice for small, low-risk, unifocal cancers and indeterminate nodules.

Procedure

The operation is performed under general anesthesia through a horizontal incision in the lower neck. The surgeon dissects to the gland, taking care to identify and preserve the recurrent laryngeal nerves and the parathyroid glands, and removes the thyroid tissue. Central or lateral neck dissection may be added when lymph node metastases are present. Minimally invasive, endoscopic, and robotic approaches, including transoral and transaxillary routes, have been developed to reduce visible scarring.

Complications

Possible complications include bleeding and hematoma (which can compromise the airway), injury to the recurrent laryngeal nerve causing hoarseness, and inadvertent removal or devascularization of the parathyroid glands, causing hypocalcemia and hypoparathyroidism. After total thyroidectomy, lifelong thyroid hormone replacement with levothyroxine is required.

History

Thyroid surgery was long considered dangerous because of hemorrhage and the gland's vascularity. Theodor Kocher, working in Bern in the late nineteenth century, refined the operation to a precise capsular dissection and greatly reduced mortality, work recognized by his Nobel Prize in 1909.
