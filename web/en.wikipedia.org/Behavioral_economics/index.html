Behavioral economics

Behavioral economics is the study of the psychological, cognitive, emotional, cultural and social factors involved in the decisions of individuals or institutions, and how these decisions deviate from those implied by classical economic theory. Behavioral economics is mainly concerned with the bounds of rationality of economic agents. Behavioral models typically integrate insights from psychology, neuroscience and microeconomic theory.

History

The field grew out of dissatisfaction with the assumption of perfect rationality in neoclassical economics. Herbert Simon proposed "bounded rationality" in the 1950s. In the 1970s psychologists Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman documented systematic heuristics and biases in judgment under uncertainty, and in 1979 published prospect theory, a descriptive model of choice under risk in which losses loom larger than gains. Richard Thaler brought these ideas into economics, cataloguing "anomalies" such as the endowment effect and mental accounting. Kahneman (2002), Robert Shiller (2013) and Thaler (2017) received Nobel Memorial Prizes in Economic Sciences for their contributions.

Main topics

Key topics include heuristics and biases (availability, representativeness, anchoring), framing effects, loss aversion, hyperbolic discounting and present bias, social preferences such as fairness and reciprocity, and choice architecture — the design of the context in which decisions are made. Thaler and Cass Sunstein popularised the "nudge" concept: small changes in choice architecture that predictably alter behaviour without forbidding options.

Applications

Behavioral economics informs behavioural finance, explaining market anomalies such as bubbles and excess volatility; public policy, where behavioural insights teams design interventions in health, savings, and tax compliance; and marketing, pricing, and consumer research. Auto-enrolment in pension schemes is a widely cited successful nudge.

Criticism

Critics argue that some findings suffer from publication bias and replication problems, that the field lacks a unified theory, and that catalogues of biases can explain behaviour only post hoc. Defenders respond that behavioral models increasingly integrate into mainstream theory and have strong out-of-sample predictive successes.
