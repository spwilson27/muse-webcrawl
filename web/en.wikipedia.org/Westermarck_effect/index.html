Westermarck effect

The Westermarck effect, also known as reverse sexual imprinting, is a psychological hypothesis proposing that people who live in close domestic proximity during the first few years of their lives become desensitized to sexual attraction to one another later in life. The effect is named after Finnish anthropologist Edvard Westermarck, who described it in his 1891 work The History of Human Marriage.

Hypothesis

Westermarck argued that close association in early childhood generates an aversion to incest, proposing an evolved mechanism to avoid inbreeding and its associated fitness costs. The proposed sensitive period is generally placed in the first years of life, roughly up to about age six, during which co-residence or close familiarity inhibits later sexual attraction between the individuals.

Evidence

Evidence commonly cited includes studies of Israeli kibbutzim, where children raised communally in peer groups rarely married or formed sexual relationships with one another despite no social prohibition; and Arthur Wolf's studies of Taiwanese "sim-pua" marriage, in which a girl was adopted into the family of her future husband and raised alongside him — such marriages showed lower fertility, higher divorce rates, and more extramarital affairs than major marriages, with the effect strongest when adoption occurred before the girl was about 30 months old.

Criticism and debate

Critics note the difficulty of separating the effect from cultural incest taboos, and some studies of post-pubertal association and of cousins raised together have produced mixed results. Sigmund Freud disputed Westermarck's account, arguing that the incest taboo exists precisely because of an innate attraction that must be repressed. The Westermarck effect remains an influential, though debated, explanation within evolutionary psychology and anthropology.
