Dynasties of China

For most of its history, China was ruled by successive dynasties — hereditary lines of emperors who claimed the Mandate of Heaven. The dynastic cycle describes the rise of a new dynasty, a flourishing reign, and eventual decline through corruption, natural disaster and rebellion, ending in replacement by a new dynasty. The first dynasty in traditional Chinese historiography is the Xia (c. 2070–1600 BC), and the last was the Qing, which fell in 1912 with the Xinhai Revolution.

Major dynasties

  • Xia (c. 2070–1600 BC) — semi-legendary first dynasty
  • Shang (c. 1600–1046 BC) — earliest dynasty with contemporary written records (oracle bones)
  • Zhou (1046–256 BC) — longest-lasting dynasty; Confucius and Laozi lived in its later centuries
  • Qin (221–206 BC) — first imperial dynasty, founded by Qin Shi Huang, who unified China
  • Han (206 BC–AD 220) — golden age; gives its name to the Han Chinese ethnic group
  • Sui (581–618) — reunified China after centuries of division
  • Tang (618–907) — cosmopolitan high point of imperial culture
  • Song (960–1279) — advances in technology, commerce and philosophy
  • Yuan (1271–1368) — founded by Kublai Khan; Mongol rule over China
  • Ming (1368–1644) — restored native Han rule; built much of the Great Wall seen today
  • Qing (1644–1912) — Manchu dynasty; the last imperial dynasty

Periods of division

Between the great unified dynasties lay periods of fragmentation, such as the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods under the late Zhou, the Three Kingdoms (220–280), the Sixteen Kingdoms, and the Northern and Southern dynasties (386–589), as well as the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms (907–979).
