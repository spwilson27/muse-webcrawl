Thunder

Thunder is the sound caused by lightning. Depending upon the distance from and nature of the lightning, it can range from a long, low rumble to a sudden, loud crack. The sudden increase in temperature and pressure caused by the lightning produces rapid expansion of the air in the path of a lightning bolt; this expansion creates a sonic shock wave, similar to a sonic boom, known as a thunderclap or peal of thunder.

Cause

A lightning discharge heats the air along its channel to temperatures approaching 30,000 kelvin — several times hotter than the surface of the Sun — in a fraction of a second. The superheated air expands explosively, producing a shock wave that decays into the acoustic wave heard as thunder. Because light travels far faster than sound, an observer sees the lightning flash before hearing the thunder; counting the seconds between flash and sound and dividing by roughly three gives the distance to the strike in kilometres (or by five for miles).

Types

The character of thunder varies with the geometry of the lightning channel and the atmosphere. A nearby strike produces a sharp crack or bang as the shock wave from the closest part of the channel arrives. Rumbling results from sound arriving over an extended period from different segments of a long, branching channel, and from reflections off clouds, terrain and temperature layers in the atmosphere, which can refract and prolong the sound. Under temperature inversions, thunder can be ducted and heard at unusually great distances, sometimes tens of kilometres away.

In culture

Thunder has been attributed to gods and supernatural beings in many cultures: Zeus and Jupiter wielded the thunderbolt in Greek and Roman mythology, Thor produced thunder with his chariot and hammer in Norse mythology, and the thunderbird appears in the traditions of many Indigenous peoples of North America. Fear of thunder is known as astraphobia (together with fear of lightning).
