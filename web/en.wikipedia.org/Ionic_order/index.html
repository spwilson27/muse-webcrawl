Ionic order

Ionic order
OriginIonia, Greek Asia Minor
EmergenceMid-6th century BC
Distinguishing featuresVolutes on the capital; slender fluted columns; continuous frieze
Famous examplesErechtheion and Temple of Athena Nike, Athens

The Ionic order is one of the three canonic orders of classical architecture, the other two being the Doric and the Corinthian. There are two lesser orders: the Tuscan (a plainer Doric) and the rich variant of Corinthian, the Composite. The Ionic order is readily identifiable by the volutes — scroll-like ornaments — of its capital. Ionic columns are normally slimmer and more elegant than Doric ones, standing on a moulded base and carrying an entablature with a continuous, often sculptured, frieze.

Origins and development

The order originated in the mid-6th century BC in Ionia, the Greek-settled coast of southwestern Turkey and the Aegean islands. Early monumental examples include the great temples of Hera at Samos and of Artemis at Ephesus, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. Ionic reached mainland Greece by the 5th century BC, where it was used for the Temple of Athena Nike and the Erechtheion on the Athenian Acropolis, and for the interior of the Propylaea.

Elements

Column

The Ionic column typically stands on a base of stacked mouldings (often a torus above a scotia and trochilus) — a key difference from the Doric, which usually sits directly on the stylobate. The shaft has 24 flutes separated by flat fillets, rather than the sharp arrises of Doric fluting, and is more slender, commonly eight or nine diameters high.

Capital

The capital consists of an echinus decorated with egg-and-dart ornament, above which a bolster-like band rolls into spiral volutes at each side. Because the volutes make the capital directional, corner columns in Ionic temples posed a design problem, solved with angled corner volutes.

Entablature

The architrave is usually divided into three horizontal bands (fasciae). The frieze, lacking the triglyphs of the Doric, may run continuously and carry relief sculpture. The cornice is comparatively restrained, often with dentils.

Later use

The Roman architect Vitruvius described the order, comparing its proportions to a woman's body, in contrast to the masculine Doric. Ionic was revived in Renaissance and Neoclassical architecture and remains common on banks, museums, libraries, and courthouses, where it conveys learned elegance.
