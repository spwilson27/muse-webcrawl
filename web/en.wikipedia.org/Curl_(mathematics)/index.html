Curl (mathematics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In vector calculus, the curl is a vector operator that describes the infinitesimal circulation of a vector field in three-dimensional Euclidean space. The curl at a point in the field is represented by a vector whose length and direction denote the magnitude and axis of the maximum circulation. A field's curl at a point can be visualised as the rotation that an imaginary paddle wheel placed at that point would experience.

Formally, for a vector field F = (Fx, Fy, Fz), the curl is written ∇ × F and has components (∂Fz/∂y − ∂Fy/∂z, ∂Fx/∂z − ∂Fz/∂x, ∂Fy/∂x − ∂Fx/∂y). The notation exploits the formal analogy with the cross product of the del operator ∇ = (∂/∂x, ∂/∂y, ∂/∂z) and the field.

Interpretation and properties

The curl measures the tendency of the field to rotate about a point. A vector field whose curl is zero everywhere is called irrotational; in a simply connected region such a field is the gradient of a scalar potential. Two fundamental identities are that the curl of a gradient is always zero (∇ × ∇φ = 0) and the divergence of a curl is always zero (∇ · (∇ × F) = 0), facts that underlie the Helmholtz decomposition of vector fields.

By Stokes' theorem, the integral of the curl of a field over a surface equals the line integral of the field around the surface's boundary, which expresses the total circulation. Curl appears throughout physics: in Maxwell's equations, the curl of the electric field is related to the changing magnetic field, and the curl of the magnetic field to currents and changing electric fields; in fluid mechanics, the curl of the velocity field is the vorticity.

Generalizations

In two dimensions, curl reduces to a scalar quantity, used in the study of planar flows. In differential geometry, curl is related to the exterior derivative of a 1-form; connections and curvature in gauge theory involve analogous non-commutative versions of the operation.
