Rodinia (from the Russian rodina, meaning "homeland" or "birthplace") was a Mesoproterozoic-to-Neoproterozoic supercontinent that assembled about 1.26–0.9 billion years ago and broke up in the Neoproterozoic, roughly 750–633 million years ago. It incorporated most or all of Earth's continental crust and is one of the earliest supercontinents reconstructed by geologists. Its existence frames much of the debate about the supercontinent cycle and the severe "Snowball Earth" glaciations that followed its breakup.

Assembly

Rodinia formed through collisions associated with the Grenville orogeny, a mountain-building episode recorded in rocks from eastern North America to Scandinavia and Australia. At its heart lay Laurentia, the cratonic core of North America and Greenland. Other cratons — including Baltica (Scandinavia and eastern Europe), Amazonia, West Africa, Siberia, India, Australia, and blocks now in China — were accreted around Laurentia along Grenville-age belts. Reconstruction relies on matching these orogenic belts, palaeomagnetic poles, and distinctive sedimentary sequences such as the Belt Supergroup of Montana.

Geography

Because almost all of Rodinia's crust lay in the southern hemisphere or around the equator, much of the supercontinent is thought to have experienced an arid, barren landscape: complex land life had not yet evolved, so continents lacked vegetation and were dominated by rocky deserts and shallow epicontinental seas rich in stromatolites and microbial life. The vast surrounding ocean is called Mirovia. Little is agreed about the exact arrangement of blocks; competing models (the SWEAT hypothesis and AUSWUS variants) place Australia and East Antarctica against different margins of Laurentia.

Breakup

Rodinia began to rift apart around 750 million years ago, possibly aided by a mantle superplume beneath the continent that uplifted and thinned the crust. Rift basins filled with thick sedimentary successions — the "Grand Canyon Supergroup", the Windermere Supergroup of Canada, and equivalents in Australia and Scotland — and by about 633–600 million years ago the fragments had largely separated, opening the ancestral Pacific and other oceans.

Climate and life consequences

The breakup of Rodinia increased continental weathering and flooded continental shelves, drawing down atmospheric carbon dioxide and plausibly triggering the Sturtian and Marinoan "Snowball Earth" glaciations (c. 717–635 Ma), the most extreme ice ages in Earth history. The nutrient runoff and new shallow-water habitats created by fragmentation may also have helped set the stage for the subsequent Ediacaran radiation of large multicellular organisms and, ultimately, the Cambrian explosion.
