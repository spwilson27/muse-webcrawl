Tenth Amendment to the United States Constitution

The Tenth Amendment (Amendment X) to the United States Constitution, part of the Bill of Rights, was ratified on December 15, 1791. It expresses the principle of federalism and the states' rights tradition: powers neither granted to the federal government by the Constitution nor prohibited to the states are reserved to the states or to the people.

Text

The powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people.

Background

Several states, when ratifying the Constitution, demanded a provision expressly reserving undelegated powers, following Article II of the Articles of Confederation, which had reserved to each state every power "not by this Confederation expressly delegated" to the national government. Anti-Federalists feared that a federal government of enumerated powers might nonetheless expand unless the reservation was stated explicitly. The First Congress included the amendment among the twelve articles of amendment proposed in 1789; ten were ratified as the Bill of Rights. The Senate rejected a proposal to insert the word "expressly" before "delegated," a point later discussed by James Madison and by the Supreme Court in McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), which confirmed that the federal government also holds implied powers under the Necessary and Proper Clause.

Interpretation and jurisprudence

In United States v. Darby (1941), the Supreme Court described the amendment as stating "but a truism that all is retained which has not been surrendered." For much of the twentieth century the amendment imposed few limits on Congress, and in Garcia v. San Antonio Metropolitan Transit Authority (1985) the Court held that states' protection lay mainly in the political process.

Beginning in the 1990s, the Rehnquist Court revived the amendment as a substantive limit. In New York v. United States (1992) and Printz v. United States (1997), the Court developed the anti-commandeering doctrine, holding that Congress may not compel state legislatures to enact laws or conscript state officers to administer federal programmes. The amendment also figures in cases about conditional federal spending, such as NFIB v. Sebelius (2012), where the Court held that threatening to withdraw all Medicaid funding was unconstitutionally coercive. It is frequently invoked in debates over the scope of the Commerce Clause and the balance of power between the federal government and the states.
