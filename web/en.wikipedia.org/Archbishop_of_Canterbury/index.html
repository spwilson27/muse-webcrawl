The Archbishop of Canterbury is the senior bishop and principal leader of the Church of England, the symbolic head of the worldwide Anglican Communion, and the diocesan bishop of the Diocese of Canterbury. The archbishop is regarded as primus inter pares ("first among equals") among the bishops of the Anglican Communion, and is styled "The Most Reverend", being addressed as "Your Grace". The current office is traditionally traced to Augustine of Canterbury, who arrived in Kent in 597 on a mission sent by Pope Gregory the Great to convert the Anglo-Saxons.

The Archbishop of Canterbury exercises metropolitan authority over the Province of Canterbury, which covers roughly two-thirds of England, and shares oversight of the Church of England with the Archbishop of York, who leads the northern province. The archbishop also has a significant role in national life: crowning the monarch at the coronation, sitting in the House of Lords as one of the Lords Spiritual, and speaking on matters of public concern.

History

The see of Canterbury was founded in 597 when Augustine established his cathedral in Canterbury, the capital of the Kingdom of Kent under King Æthelberht. After the Norman Conquest, archbishops such as Lanfranc and Anselm of Canterbury strengthened the primacy of Canterbury over York, a rivalry that persisted for centuries. Thomas Becket, archbishop from 1162, was murdered in Canterbury Cathedral in 1170 after a bitter dispute with King Henry II, and was swiftly canonised; his shrine became one of the great pilgrimage centres of medieval Europe until it was destroyed during the English Reformation.

During the Reformation, Thomas Cranmer, appointed archbishop in 1533, presided over the break with Rome and produced the Book of Common Prayer. In later centuries the office evolved alongside the global expansion of Anglicanism; the first Lambeth Conference of bishops from around the world was convened by Archbishop Charles Longley in 1867.

Roles and functions

The archbishop's responsibilities include:

  • Diocesan bishop of Canterbury, with day-to-day care largely delegated to the Bishop of Dover.
  • Metropolitan of the Province of Canterbury, with supervisory and appellate functions.
  • Primate of All England, a title reflecting seniority within the English church.
  • Head of the Anglican Communion in a symbolic and convening capacity: the archbishop calls the decennial Lambeth Conference, chairs meetings of the primates, and is president of the Anglican Consultative Council.
  • Coronation: the archbishop traditionally anoints and crowns the sovereign at Westminster Abbey.

Residences and insignia

The archbishop's official London residence is Lambeth Palace, on the south bank of the River Thames opposite Westminster, occupied by archbishops since the 13th century. In Canterbury, the Old Palace adjacent to the cathedral serves as a further residence. The archbishop's arms combine those of the see of Canterbury with personal arms, and the primatial cross of Canterbury, a distinctive processional cross, has been associated with the office since the medieval period.

Appointment

Since the 1970s the archbishop has been chosen through a process led by the Crown Nominations Commission, which submits a preferred name to the Prime Minister, who advises the monarch on the appointment. The process was reformed in the 21st century to include wider representation from the global Anglican Communion, reflecting the archbishop's international role.

Notable holders

Distinguished archbishops include Augustine (597–604), Theodore of Tarsus, Dunstan, Anselm of Canterbury, Thomas Becket, Stephen Langton, Thomas Cranmer, William Laud, William Temple, Michael Ramsey, Rowan Williams, and Justin Welby, who served from 2013 until his resignation took effect in January 2025.
