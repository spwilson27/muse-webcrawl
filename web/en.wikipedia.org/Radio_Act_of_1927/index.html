The Radio Act of 1927 was a United States federal law that established the Federal Radio Commission (FRC) and gave it the authority to license radio broadcasters and assign frequencies. It replaced the Radio Act of 1912 and was the first comprehensive federal regulation of broadcasting in the United States.

Broadcasting equipment of the 1920s, when rapid growth in stations created interference chaos on the airwaves.

Background

During the early 1920s the number of radio stations grew rapidly under the limited licensing regime of the Department of Commerce, headed by Secretary Herbert Hoover. A 1926 court decision, United States v. Zenith Radio Corporation, held that the Secretary lacked authority to assign frequencies or restrict hours of operation, producing a period of interference often called the "breakdown of the law". Stations changed frequencies and power levels at will, and Congress moved to restore order.

Provisions

The Act declared that the airwaves belonged to the public and that broadcasters could use them only under license, without acquiring ownership rights. Licenses were to be granted according to "public interest, convenience, or necessity" — a standard carried forward into later communications law. The Act also sought an equitable distribution of licenses among regions (the Davis Amendment of 1928 strengthened this requirement) and restricted license terms to three years for broadcasting.

Legacy

The Federal Radio Commission's General Order 40 (1928) reorganized the broadcast band and cleared channels for high-powered stations, shaping the structure of American commercial radio. In 1934 the Communications Act replaced the FRC with the Federal Communications Commission (FCC), but retained the 1927 Act's public-interest framework.
