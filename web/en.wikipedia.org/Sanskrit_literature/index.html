Sanskrit literature is the body of written and orally transmitted works in the Sanskrit language of ancient and medieval India, spanning more than three thousand years. It encompasses the sacred scriptures of Hinduism, two of the world's longest epic poems, court poetry and drama of extraordinary sophistication, and vast technical literatures on grammar, mathematics, medicine, law and philosophy.

Vedic literature

The oldest stratum is the Vedic corpus, composed in Vedic Sanskrit and transmitted orally with astonishing fidelity. At its core are the four Vedas — the Rigveda, Samaveda, Yajurveda and Atharvaveda — collections of hymns, ritual formulas and incantations dating from roughly 1500–900 BC. Around them grew the Brahmanas (prose ritual commentaries), the Aranyakas ("forest books") and the Upanishads, philosophical dialogues on the nature of the self (atman) and ultimate reality (brahman) that became the foundation of later Indian philosophy. Supplementary Vedanga disciplines preserved the science of language, culminating in Panini's Ashtadhyayi (c. 4th century BC), a generative grammar of Sanskrit of unmatched precision.

The epics

The two Sanskrit epics, the Mahabharata attributed to Vyasa and the Ramayana attributed to Valmiki, were composed over centuries (roughly 400 BC – AD 400). The Mahabharata, at some 100,000 verses, is the longest epic poem ever written; it narrates the Kurukshetra War between the Pandavas and Kauravas and contains the Bhagavad Gita. The Ramayana tells of Prince Rama's exile and his rescue of Sita from the demon-king Ravana. Together they supplied stories, characters and moral frameworks for art and literature across South and Southeast Asia.

Classical literature

In the early centuries AD Sanskrit became the cosmopolitan language of courtly culture from Afghanistan to Java. Its supreme poet and dramatist is Kalidasa (c. 4th–5th century AD), author of the play Abhijnanashakuntalam (The Recognition of Shakuntala) and the lyric Meghaduta (The Cloud Messenger). The court epic (mahakavya) was practised by Bharavi, Magha and Sriharsha, and ornate prose romance by Subandhu, Dandin and Bana, whose Harshacharita is also a royal biography. Drama flourished in works by Bhasa, Shudraka (Mrichchhakatika, The Little Clay Cart) and Vishakhadatta. Short lyric and didactic verse include the fables of the Panchatantra, which travelled westward into Persian, Arabic and European literatures, and Bhartrihari's epigrams. Aestheticians such as Bharata (the Natya Shastra), Anandavardhana and Abhinavagupta developed sophisticated theories of rasa (aesthetic flavour) and dhvani (suggestion).

Technical and scientific literature

Sanskrit also carried an enormous technical corpus: Aryabhata, Brahmagupta and Bhaskara II in mathematics and astronomy; the Charaka Samhita and Sushruta Samhita in Ayurvedic medicine; the Arthashastra on statecraft; the Kama Sutra on erotics and cultivated living; and the six orthodox schools of philosophy, from Nyaya logic to Vedanta metaphysics, besides Buddhist and Jain works written in Sanskrit or Sanskritised Prakrit.

Decline and revival

From around the twelfth century, regional vernaculars and Persian displaced Sanskrit at most courts, but pandits continued composing and commenting in the language into the modern period. European scholars such as William Jones, whose 1786 observation of Sanskrit's affinity with Greek and Latin launched comparative linguistics, reintroduced Sanskrit literature to the wider world. Composition in Sanskrit continues today, recognised by India's Sahitya Akademi awards.
