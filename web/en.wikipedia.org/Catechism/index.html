Catechism

A catechism is a summary or exposition of religious doctrine, traditionally presented in question-and-answer form, used for teaching the faith to children and adult converts (catechumens). The word derives from the Greek katēkhizein, "to teach orally." Catechisms typically cover the creed, the sacraments, the commandments and prayer.

History

Instruction of converts by question and answer dates to the early Church, when catechumens prepared for baptism during Lent. The genre flourished after the invention of printing. The Protestant Reformation produced famous examples such as Luther's Small Catechism (1529) and the Heidelberg Catechism (1563); the Westminster Shorter Catechism (1647) became a standard of Presbyterian teaching. In response, the Catholic Church issued the Roman Catechism (1566) following the Council of Trent. The modern Catechism of the Catholic Church, promulgated in 1992, is a comprehensive compendium of Catholic doctrine organised around the Creed, the sacraments, the Christian life and prayer, including the Lord's Prayer.

Form and use

The classic catechism is structured as a dialogue: a short question ("What is the chief end of man?") followed by a memorisable answer. This format made catechisms tools for mass religious literacy and confessional identity, and the word has been extended by analogy to secular manuals of instruction in question-and-answer form.
