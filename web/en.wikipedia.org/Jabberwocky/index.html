Jabberwocky

"Jabberwocky" is a nonsense poem written by Lewis Carroll and included in his 1871 novel Through the Looking-Glass, and What Alice Found There, the sequel to Alice's Adventures in Wonderland (1865). The poem follows a young hero who slays the fearsome Jabberwock. It is widely considered the greatest nonsense poem in English, remarkable because its invented vocabulary — much of it explained in the novel by Humpty Dumpty — still conveys grammar, mood and narrative with perfect clarity.

The poem

The poem is written in quatrains, mostly in iambic tetrameter, and framed by a repeated opening stanza. Its first verse is:

'Twas brillig, and the slithy toves
Did gyre and gimble in the wabe:
All mimsy were the borogoves,
And the mome raths outgrabe.

In the story, Alice finds the poem printed in mirror writing in a book in the Looking-glass world. She remarks that it "seems very pretty" but "rather hard to understand", adding that it fills her head with ideas without her knowing exactly what they are — a celebrated description of how nonsense verse works.

Vocabulary

Humpty Dumpty explains several words to Alice: "brillig" means four o'clock in the afternoon (the time for broiling dinner), "slithy" is a portmanteau of "lithe" and "slimy", and "toves" are something like badgers, lizards and corkscrews. Other coinages have entered English, including "chortle" (chuckle + snort), "galumphing", "frabjous" and "vorpal". Carroll had invented some of the stanza as early as 1855, when he printed it as a "Stanza of Anglo-Saxon Poetry" in a family magazine.

Legacy

The poem has been translated into dozens of languages — a notable challenge for translators, who must invent equivalent nonsense — and has inspired music, film (including Terry Gilliam's 1977 film Jabberwocky), games and countless parodies. The Jabberwock itself was memorably illustrated by John Tenniel as a long-necked, waistcoated monster.
