Hypogonadism means diminished functional activity of the gonads — the testes in males or the ovaries in females — that may result in diminished production of sex hormones. Low androgen levels (e.g. testosterone) are commonly referred to as hypoandrogenism, and low estrogen as hypoestrogenism. Hypogonadism may present with infertility, reduced secondary sex characteristics, delayed or absent puberty, low libido, fatigue, loss of muscle mass, and decreased bone density.

Classification

Primary (hypergonadotropic) hypogonadism results from failure of the gonads themselves; because negative feedback is lost, pituitary gonadotropins (LH and FSH) are elevated. Causes include Klinefelter syndrome (47,XXY) in males, Turner syndrome (45,X) in females, chemotherapy or radiotherapy, mumps orchitis, gonadal injury, and autoimmune damage.

Secondary (hypogonadotropic) hypogonadism results from dysfunction of the hypothalamus or pituitary gland, so gonadotropin levels are low or inappropriately normal. Causes include Kallmann syndrome (with anosmia), pituitary tumours, hyperprolactinaemia, haemochromatosis, opioid or glucocorticoid use, severe systemic illness, and excessive exercise or weight loss. Combined forms also occur.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis rests on symptoms together with repeatedly low morning serum testosterone in men (or low estradiol with clinical features in women), measured on at least two occasions. LH and FSH levels distinguish primary from secondary disease. Further testing may include prolactin, iron studies, karyotyping, and MRI of the pituitary.

Treatment

Treatment addresses the underlying cause where possible and replaces deficient sex hormones. In males, testosterone replacement therapy is available as injections, gels, and patches; it improves libido, muscle mass, and bone density but suppresses spermatogenesis, so men seeking fertility may instead receive gonadotropin therapy (hCG with or without FSH) or clomiphene. In females, estrogen replacement (with a progestogen if the uterus is present) is used, particularly to protect bone health in premature ovarian insufficiency.

Late-onset hypogonadism

Testosterone levels decline gradually with age in men, roughly 1% per year after middle age. The significance and treatment thresholds of age-related decline ("late-onset hypogonadism") remain debated, and guidelines recommend treatment only when symptoms coincide with consistently low testosterone.
