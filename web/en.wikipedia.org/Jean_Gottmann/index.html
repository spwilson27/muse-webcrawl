Jean Gottmann

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jean Gottmann (10 October 1915 – 28 February 1994) was a French geographer of Russian-Jewish origin who grew up in Ukraine and France. He is best known for popularising the term Megalopolis for the heavily urbanised northeastern seaboard of the United States.

Life and career

Gottmann was born in Kharkiv, in the Russian Empire (now Ukraine). After the Russian Revolution his family moved to Paris, where he studied geography at the Sorbonne under Albert Demangeon. During the Second World War he moved to the United States, working at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton and teaching at Johns Hopkins University. After the war he divided his career between France — helping to build research institutions for the French government and UNESCO — and the United States, before becoming professor of geography at the University of Oxford in 1968, a post he held until his retirement in 1983.

Megalopolis

Gottmann's landmark study Megalopolis: The Urbanized Northeastern Seaboard of the United States (1961), funded by the Twentieth Century Fund, documented the continuous urban region stretching from Boston to Washington, D.C. — BosWash — home at the time to some 37 million people. He argued that the region formed a single, integrated urban system and a new kind of urban landscape, and he took the name "megalopolis" from an ancient Greek city in Arcadia. The book became a classic of urban geography and regional planning.

Other work

Gottmann also wrote influential works on the geography of Europe, Virginia, political geography, and the significance of territory — including The Significance of Territory (1973), in which he analysed political territory through the paired forces of movement (circulation) and iconography. He died at Oxford in 1994.
