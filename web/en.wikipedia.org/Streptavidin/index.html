Streptavidin

Streptavidin is a 52 kDa protein purified from the bacterium Streptomyces avidinii. Streptavidin homo-tetramers have an extraordinarily high affinity for the vitamin biotin (also known as vitamin B7 or vitamin H) — with a dissociation constant (Kd) on the order of 10−14 mol/L, the biotin–streptavidin binding is one of the strongest known non-covalent interactions in nature. Streptavidin is used extensively in molecular biology and bionanotechnology due to the streptavidin–biotin complex's resistance to organic solvents, denaturants (e.g. guanidinium chloride), detergents (e.g. SDS, Triton), proteolytic enzymes, and extremes of temperature and pH.

Structure

Each subunit of the tetramer binds one biotin molecule and consists mainly of an eight-stranded antiparallel beta-barrel. Streptavidin is similar in size, structure and binding affinity to the egg-white protein avidin, but unlike avidin it is not glycosylated and has a near-neutral isoelectric point, which gives it much lower non-specific binding in most applications.

Applications

The biotin–streptavidin system is used to link biomolecules in assays such as ELISA, Western blotting, immunohistochemistry and DNA hybridisation detection, in affinity purification, and in the pretargeting of radioisotopes and drugs in cancer therapy. Engineered variants include monomeric streptavidin, traptavidin (with slower biotin release), and cleavable or mutated forms used in proteomics; peptide tags such as Strep-tag bind engineered streptavidin (Strep-Tactin) for gentle protein purification.
