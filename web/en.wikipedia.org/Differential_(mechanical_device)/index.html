Differential (mechanical device)

A differential is a gear train with three drive shafts that has the property that the rotational speed of one shaft is the average of the speeds of the others. In automobiles, the differential allows the driven wheels to rotate at different speeds while receiving equal torque, which is essential when turning corners, since the outer wheel must travel farther than the inner wheel.

Operation

Power enters through the pinion gear, which turns the ring gear attached to a carrier. Planet pinions mounted on the carrier engage two side (sun) gears connected to the axle shafts. When both wheels meet equal resistance, the planet gears do not spin on their own axis and the wheels turn together; when one wheel meets less resistance, the planet gears rotate, allowing speed to differ while average speed is maintained.

History

Differential-like mechanisms are ancient, appearing in the Chinese south-pointing chariot and arguably the Antikythera mechanism. The modern automotive differential was developed in the nineteenth century, with Onésiphore Pecqueur's 1827 design an important milestone.

Traction limitations

An open differential can send most power to a wheel that has lost traction; limited-slip differentials and locking differentials were developed to overcome this.
