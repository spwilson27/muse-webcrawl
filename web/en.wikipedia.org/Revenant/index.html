Revenant

A revenant is a figure in folklore who returns from death, often as a visible ghost or animated corpse, to haunt the living. The word comes from the French present participle revenant, “returning.” Medieval European revenants were commonly corporeal and dangerous rather than merely intangible apparitions.

Medieval accounts

English chroniclers such as William of Newburgh and Walter Map recorded stories of corpses leaving graves, spreading disease, attacking relatives, or seeking redress for wrongs. Remedies in the narratives and related traditions included prayer, absolution, exhumation, decapitation, removal or burning of the heart, and burning the body. Such accounts served moral, religious, and communal purposes and cannot be read simply as literal reports.

Characteristics

A revenant may return because of violent death, improper burial, excommunication, sin, unfinished business, or a curse. Unlike many modern ghosts, it may possess weight, strength, and a decaying body; unlike a vampire, it need not drink blood, although the categories overlap in later folklore scholarship. Norse draugar, Slavic undead traditions, and other returning-dead figures display comparable combinations of physical menace and social grievance.

Interpretations

Historians and folklorists connect revenant stories to epidemic disease, premature burial, decomposition, grief, disputes over inheritance, and the need to enforce burial and moral norms. Archaeological burials with prone bodies, stones, or decapitation are sometimes interpreted as anti-revenant measures, though such explanations are not certain in every case.

In popular culture

Modern fiction uses “revenant” for ghosts, reanimated corpses, and people who return implacably after apparent death. The 2015 film The Revenant applies the term metaphorically to frontiersman Hugh Glass, who survives a bear attack and abandonment; he is not an undead being.
