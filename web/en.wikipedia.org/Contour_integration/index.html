Contour integration

In the mathematical field of complex analysis, contour integration is a method of evaluating certain integrals along paths in the complex plane. Contour integration is closely related to the calculus of residues, a method of integration attributed to Augustin-Louis Cauchy, and is a powerful tool for evaluating difficult definite integrals over the real line that would be intractable by other means.

Basic notions

A contour is a directed piecewise-smooth curve in the complex plane. The contour integral of a complex function f(z) along such a path is defined by parametrizing the path and integrating f(z(t)) z′(t) over the parameter interval. Cauchy's integral theorem states that the integral of a holomorphic function around a closed contour is zero when the function is holomorphic on and inside the contour. Cauchy's integral formula then expresses the value of a holomorphic function at a point in terms of a contour integral around it, implying that such functions are infinitely differentiable and analytic.

Residue theorem

The residue theorem generalizes Cauchy's results: the integral of a function around a closed contour equals 2πi times the sum of the residues of the function at the poles enclosed. By choosing contours cleverly — semicircles closed in the upper or lower half-plane, keyhole contours, rectangular contours — analysts convert real integrals involving trigonometric, rational, and logarithmic functions into residue computations, with Jordan's lemma controlling contributions from large arcs.

Applications

Contour integration is used throughout mathematics and physics: in evaluating Fourier and Laplace transforms, in potential theory and fluid dynamics, in number theory (for instance in proofs of the prime number theorem), and in quantum field theory, where Wick rotation turns integrals into contour manipulations.
