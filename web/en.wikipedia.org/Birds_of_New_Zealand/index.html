Birds of New Zealand

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The birds of New Zealand evolved in isolation for millions of years after the archipelago separated from Gondwana. In the absence of terrestrial mammalian predators, many species became flightless, gigantic, or ground-dwelling. The avifauna includes iconic endemics such as the kiwi, kākāpō, weka, takahe, and kea, as well as the extinct moa and Haast's eagle.

Evolution and characteristics

New Zealand's bird fauna is dominated by lineages that arrived by flight over water, plus relict species such as the rifleman and other New Zealand wrens. Flightlessness evolved repeatedly — in moa, kiwi, kākāpō, weka, and several ducks and rails — because, before human arrival, the only predators were other birds, notably the enormous Haast's eagle, which preyed on moa.

Human impact

Polynesian settlement from about the 13th century brought the Pacific rat and hunting pressure; the moa were extinct within roughly two centuries. European settlement added stoats, possums, cats, and more rat species. Dozens of species are extinct and many more are threatened; New Zealand is a world leader in island eradication of predators and offshore-island sanctuaries, under the Predator Free 2050 programme.

Notable species

The five kiwi species are national symbols. The kākāpō, a flightless nocturnal parrot, survives only under intensive management on predator-free islands. The kea is famed for intelligence, the tūī and bellbird for song, and the little penguin and yellow-eyed penguin breed along the coasts.
