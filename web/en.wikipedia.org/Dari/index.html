Dari (دری, Darī, [dæˈɾiː]) is the variety of the Persian language spoken in Afghanistan. It is one of the two official languages of Afghanistan, alongside Pashto, and serves as the country's lingua franca, understood by the great majority of the population. Dari is mutually intelligible with Iranian Persian (Farsi) and with Tajik, the variety spoken in Tajikistan, which is written in the Cyrillic alphabet.

The name Dari derives from dar or darbār, meaning "court", reflecting its historical status as the language of the royal court. The Afghan government officially renamed the local Persian dialect "Dari" in the constitution of 1964, partly to distinguish Afghan Persian from that of Iran and to promote a national identity distinct from both Iran and Tajikistan.

History

Persian has been spoken in the region of present-day Afghanistan for over a millennium. Following the Arab conquest of Persia in the 7th century, New Persian emerged in the eastern Iranian world, in Khorasan and Transoxiana, where cities such as Herat, Balkh and Samarkand became centres of Persian literature. The Samanid court in the 9th and 10th centuries patronised poets such as Rudaki, regarded as the father of Persian poetry, and the region remained a heartland of Persian letters under the Ghaznavids, Ghurids and Timurids. Rumi, one of the most celebrated Persian poets, was born in Balkh.

Dari retains a number of archaic features lost in the Persian of Tehran, including aspects of pronunciation and vocabulary, and scholars sometimes describe it as a more conservative form of the language.

Phonology and differences from Iranian Persian

Dari preserves the classical distinction between the vowels /eː/ and /iː/ and between /oː/ and /uː/, which have merged in Iranian Persian. Consonantally, Dari retains the sound /w/ where Iranian Persian has /v/, and the letter ق is pronounced closer to its classical value. Dari vocabulary includes loanwords from Pashto, Turkic languages, Mongolian and English, while Iranian Persian has borrowed more heavily from French and Arabic. The two varieties remain close enough that speakers communicate with little difficulty.

Status and use

Dari is the first language of roughly a third to a half of Afghanistan's population, including most Tajiks, Hazaras and Aimaqs, and is a second language for many others. It dominates government administration, education, media and commerce in Kabul and the north and west of the country. Radio and television broadcasts, newspapers and school textbooks are produced in Dari throughout Afghanistan.
