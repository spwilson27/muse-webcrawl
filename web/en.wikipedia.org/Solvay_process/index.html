Solvay process

A Solvay plant with its carbonation towers

The Solvay process, or ammonia–soda process, is the major industrial process for the production of sodium carbonate (soda ash). Developed by the Belgian chemist Ernest Solvay in the 1860s, it displaced the earlier Leblanc process because it was cheaper, cleaner, and could be operated continuously. Soda ash is an essential raw material for glass, detergents, and chemicals.

Chemistry

Brine is saturated with ammonia and then carbonated with carbon dioxide in tall Solvay towers. Sodium bicarbonate, being sparingly soluble in the ammoniacal brine, precipitates out:

NaCl + NH3 + CO2 + H2O → NaHCO3 + NH4Cl

The bicarbonate is filtered and calcined to sodium carbonate, releasing carbon dioxide for reuse. Ammonia is recovered from the ammonium chloride by treatment with lime, which is itself produced by calcining limestone; the limestone calcination also supplies the carbon dioxide. The only major waste product is calcium chloride.

History and industry

Solvay patented the process in 1861 and founded the Solvay company in 1863, building a plant at Couillet, Belgium. The process spread rapidly; in Britain it was developed by Ludwig Mond at Winnington, the works that became part of Brunner Mond and later ICI. Although some soda ash is now mined from natural trona deposits, the Solvay process still accounts for a large share of world production.
