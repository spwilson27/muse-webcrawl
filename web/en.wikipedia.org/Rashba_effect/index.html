The Rashba effect, also called the Bychkov–Rashba effect, is the momentum-dependent splitting of spin bands in two-dimensional electron systems and at surfaces and interfaces, caused by spin–orbit coupling in the presence of structural inversion asymmetry — an electric field perpendicular to the plane. It is a central phenomenon of spintronics because the splitting can be tuned with a gate voltage, offering a route to control electron spin without magnetic fields.

Theory

In the simplest (Bychkov–Rashba) model, the effect adds to the kinetic energy a term proportional to αR(σ × k)·ẑ, where σ is the vector of Pauli matrices and k the electron wavevector. The energy bands split into two parabolas offset in momentum, with spins locked perpendicular to the momentum — a helical spin texture. The effect is contrasted with the Dresselhaus effect, which arises from bulk inversion asymmetry of the crystal itself.

History and applications

Emmanuel Rashba predicted spin splitting in non-centrosymmetric crystals in 1960 (with V. I. Sheka, for wurtzite semiconductors); Yuri Bychkov and Rashba applied the idea to two-dimensional systems in 1984. The effect underlies the 1990 Datta–Das proposal for a spin field-effect transistor. Large Rashba splittings in materials such as BiTeI and at bismuth surfaces, and the related Rashba–Edelstein effect (conversion of charge current into spin accumulation), are pursued for spin-based logic and memory. In topological and superconducting systems, Rashba coupling combined with Zeeman fields is a leading route to engineered Majorana bound states.
