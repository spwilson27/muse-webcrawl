Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky (7 May 1840 – 6 November 1893) was a Russian composer of the Romantic period. He was the first Russian composer whose music would make a lasting impression internationally. His works include seven symphonies, eleven operas, three ballets, five suites, three piano concertos, a violin concerto, and numerous songs, chamber works and piano pieces. Many of these are among the most popular works in the classical repertoire.

Life

Tchaikovsky was born in Votkinsk, the son of a mining engineer. Educated for a career as a civil servant, he worked at the Ministry of Justice before enrolling in the newly founded Saint Petersburg Conservatory, graduating in 1865; he then taught harmony at the Moscow Conservatory. A disastrous marriage in 1877 contributed to a nervous crisis, but the financial support of the wealthy widow Nadezhda von Meck — his patroness of thirteen years, whom he never met — enabled him to compose full-time. His fame grew through tours of Europe and the United States, where in 1891 he conducted at the opening of Carnegie Hall. He died in Saint Petersburg nine days after conducting the premiere of his Sixth Symphony, the Pathétique; cholera is the traditionally accepted cause, though other theories persist.

Music

Tchaikovsky fused Russian musical traditions with Western European forms, producing music of conspicuous melodic gift, rich orchestration and intense emotional expression — from the festive 1812 Overture and the fantasy-overture Romeo and Juliet to the ballets Swan Lake (1877), The Sleeping Beauty (1890) and The Nutcracker (1892), which transformed ballet music into a serious symphonic art. The last three symphonies, especially the Pathétique, are noted for their autobiographical intensity and formal originality.
