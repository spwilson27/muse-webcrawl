Diolkos

The Diolkos (Greek: Δίολκος, "across the haul") is a paved trackway near Corinth in ancient Greece that enabled boats to be moved overland across the Isthmus of Corinth. The shortcut allowed ancient vessels to avoid the long and dangerous circumnavigation of the Peloponnese peninsula.

The roadway was built around 600 BC, traditionally attributed to Periander, tyrant of Corinth. It ran for roughly 6 to 8.5 kilometres across the isthmus, paved with hard limestone slabs into which a double groove was cut about 1.5 metres apart, guiding wheeled cradles that carried ships and their cargoes, hauled by teams of men and animals.

Use and significance

The Diolkos operated with reasonable regularity for at least 650 years, until around the first century AD. It was of particular strategic value for warships: triremes were hauled across during campaigns, and ancient sources describe fleets being rushed over the isthmus to surprise enemies. Merchant cargoes were more often unloaded and carried across separately.

The Diolkos is frequently cited as one of the earliest known railways: the parallel grooves functioned like rails, constraining vehicles to a fixed path — a principle rediscovered in early modern mining tramways and modern rail transport. The statesman-poet Antipater praised its construction, and its remains, excavated in the twentieth century, lie near the modern Corinth Canal, whose builders finally realised the ancient ambition of cutting directly through the isthmus in 1893.
