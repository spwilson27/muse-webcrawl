State fair

A state fair is a state-sponsored annual competitive and recreational gathering of a U.S. state's population, usually held in late summer or early fall. It is a larger version of a county fair, often including only exhibits or competitors that have won in their categories at the more-local county fairs.

History

The first state fair in the United States was that of New York, held in Syracuse in 1841. State fairs began in the mid-19th century as exhibitions of agricultural products and livestock, reflecting the largely rural character of the young nation, and were promoted in part by agricultural societies and later by state governments.

Contents and attractions

Exhibits at state fairs have traditionally included livestock competitions, agricultural and horticultural produce, baking and canning contests, and displays of farm machinery. Modern fairs add carnival midway rides, concerts, demolition derbies, rodeos, and a wide variety of fair foods, including deep-fried specialties and items served on a stick. Many are among the largest annual events in their states; the State Fair of Texas and the Minnesota State Fair regularly draw crowds approaching or exceeding two million visitors.

Notable state fairs

Well-known state fairs include those of Texas, Minnesota, Iowa, Wisconsin, Ohio, and New York. The largest by attendance are typically the State Fair of Texas (held in Dallas for 24 days) and the Minnesota State Fair (held in Falcon Heights for 12 days ending on Labor Day).
