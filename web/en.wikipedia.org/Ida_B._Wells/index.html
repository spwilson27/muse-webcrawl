Ida Bell Wells-Barnett (July 16, 1862 – March 25, 1931) was an American investigative journalist, sociologist, educator, and early leader in the civil rights movement. She was one of the founders of the National Association for the Advancement of Colored People (NAACP) and devoted her career to combating prejudice and violence, above all through her uncompromising documentation of lynching in the United States.

Early life

Wells was born into slavery in Holly Springs, Mississippi, months before the Emancipation Proclamation. Orphaned at sixteen when her parents and a sibling died in a yellow fever epidemic, she supported her younger siblings by teaching. She attended Rust College and later moved to Memphis, Tennessee, where she taught and began writing for Black newspapers under the pen name "Iola."

Anti-lynching journalism

In 1884 Wells sued a railroad company after being forcibly removed from a first-class ladies' car for which she held a ticket; she won in local court before the Tennessee Supreme Court reversed the judgment. In 1892, after three of her friends—owners of the People's Grocery in Memphis—were lynched, Wells investigated and exposed the economic motives behind the killings and showed that lynching was routinely used to terrorise Black people who competed with whites, rather than to punish crime. Her editorials provoked a mob that destroyed her newspaper office in Memphis; warned not to return, she settled in the North and published the landmark pamphlet Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases (1892) and The Red Record (1895), a statistical study of lynching. She lectured in Britain in 1893 and 1894, building international pressure on American race practices.

Later activism

After settling in Chicago, Wells co-founded the NAACP in 1909 (though marginalised within it), founded the Negro Fellowship League and the Alpha Suffrage Club, and campaigned for women's suffrage, marching in the 1913 suffrage parade. She continued organising and writing until her death in 1931.

Legacy

In 2020 Wells was awarded a posthumous Pulitzer Prize special citation for her "outstanding and courageous reporting on the horrific and vicious violence against African Americans during the era of lynching." A major monument to her stands in Chicago's Bronzeville neighbourhood, and the Ida B. Wells Homes public housing project bore her name.
