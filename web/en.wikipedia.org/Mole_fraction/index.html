Mole fraction

In chemistry, the mole fraction (also called the molar fraction or amount fraction) is a way of expressing the concentration of a constituent in a mixture. It is defined as the amount of a constituent (expressed in moles), ni, divided by the total amount of all constituents in the mixture, ntot:

xi = ni / ntot

The mole fraction is dimensionless, and the mole fractions of all components in a mixture sum to exactly 1. Mole fractions multiplied by 100 are sometimes called mole percents.

Properties and uses

Because mole fraction depends only on the numbers of particles and not on mass or volume, it is especially useful in physical chemistry. In mixtures of ideal gases, the partial pressure of each gas equals its mole fraction multiplied by the total pressure (Dalton's law). Mole fraction also appears in Raoult's law, which relates the vapour pressure of a solution to the mole fraction of the solvent, and in the construction of phase diagrams, where compositions are typically plotted as mole (or mass) fractions. Unlike molar concentration (molarity), mole fraction is independent of temperature, since neither moles nor the ratio of moles changes with thermal expansion.

Related quantities

Mole fraction can be converted to and from other measures of composition, including mass fraction (using the molar masses of the components), molality, and molarity (using the density of the solution). In stoichiometric calculations for reacting mixtures, mole balances are often written directly in terms of mole fractions.
