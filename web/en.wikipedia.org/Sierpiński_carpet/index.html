The Sierpiński carpet is a plane fractal first described by the Polish mathematician Wacław Sierpiński in 1916. It is one of the generalizations of the Cantor set to two dimensions; the other is the Sierpiński triangle. The carpet is a universal curve: every one-dimensional planar curve can be embedded in it, a property that made it notable in topology. Its three-dimensional analogue is the Menger sponge.

Construction

The construction begins with a square. The square is cut into 9 congruent subsquares in a 3-by-3 grid, and the central subsquare is removed. The same procedure is then applied recursively to the remaining 8 subsquares, ad infinitum. At each step, the total area is multiplied by 8/9, so after infinitely many steps the remaining set has Lebesgue measure zero, while its boundary structure — the carpet itself — retains a rich, self-similar geometry.

Properties

The Sierpiński carpet is a compact, connected, locally connected set with empty interior. Its Hausdorff dimension is log 8 / log 3 ≈ 1.8928, strictly between that of a curve and a surface. Sierpiński proved that the carpet is a universal plane curve: any curve (compact, connected, locally connected metric space of topological dimension 1) can be homeomorphically embedded into it. The carpet contains homeomorphic copies of the Sierpiński triangle and of every planar graph-like continuum.

Applications and variations

Like other fractals, the carpet appears in the design of fractal antennas, whose multi-band behaviour benefits from self-similarity, and it serves as a standard example in courses on measure theory and dimension. Variations include carpets based on other grids and removal patterns, random (stochastic) carpets, and the three-dimensional Menger sponge, used to model porous materials.
