Aldous Leonard Huxley (26 July 1894 – 22 November 1963) was an English writer, novelist and philosopher, best known for his dystopian novel Brave New World (1932), one of the most influential books of the 20th century. He wrote nearly fifty books — novels, essay collections, poetry, travel writing and screenplays — ranging from the satirical social comedies of his early career to works of mysticism and psychedelic exploration.

Early life

Huxley was born into the prominent Huxley family: his grandfather was the biologist T. H. Huxley, "Darwin's bulldog", and his father Leonard Huxley was a writer and editor. An eye disease (keratitis punctata) contracted at Eton left him nearly blind for a period and permanently impaired his sight, ending his ambition to practise medicine. He read English at Balliol College, Oxford, and spent the First World War years in literary circles at Garsington Manor.

Career

His early novels — Crome Yellow (1921), Antic Hay (1923), Those Barren Leaves (1925) and Point Counter Point (1928) — satirised the fashionable society of the era. Brave New World imagined a future society engineered for stability through pleasure, conditioning and the drug soma, and is routinely compared with Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four as a prophecy of modern mass culture. Moving to California in 1937, Huxley wrote screenplays, the utopian counterweight Island (1962) and The Perennial Philosophy (1945), a comparative study of mysticism. His account of mescaline experiments, The Doors of Perception (1954), gave the rock band The Doors their name and became a foundational text of psychedelic culture.

Death and legacy

Huxley died of laryngeal cancer on 22 November 1963 — the same day as C. S. Lewis and the assassination of President John F. Kennedy, a coincidence that overshadowed his obituaries. His ashes are interred in the family vault at Watts Cemetery, Compton, Surrey.
