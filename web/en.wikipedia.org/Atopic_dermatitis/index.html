Atopic dermatitis

Atopic dermatitis, often called atopic eczema, is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory skin disease characterised by intensely itchy, red, dry and cracked skin. It usually begins in infancy or childhood and is associated with asthma and allergic rhinitis in the "atopic march". It affects roughly 15–20% of children and 1–3% of adults in developed countries.

Signs and symptoms

The distribution changes with age: the face and extensor surfaces in infants, and the flexures — inside the elbows and behind the knees — in older children and adults. Scratching produces excoriations, thickening of the skin (lichenification), and vulnerability to bacterial and viral skin infections. Itch and sleep loss are major burdens.

Causes and pathophysiology

Loss-of-function mutations in the filaggrin gene (FLG) impair the epidermal barrier, allowing irritants and allergens to penetrate. Immune skewing toward type 2 responses (IL-4, IL-13) drives inflammation; antigen-presenting cells and IgE play roles in many patients. Food allergy, soaps, detergents, wool, and Staphylococcus aureus colonisation can aggravate the disease.

Management

Treatment centres on regular emollients, trigger avoidance, topical anti-inflammatory therapy, wet-wrap treatment in severe flares, and phototherapy. Moderate-to-severe disease may require systemic immunosuppressants or targeted biologics such as dupilumab and JAK inhibitors.
