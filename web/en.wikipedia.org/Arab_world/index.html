The Arab world (Arabic: العالم العربي) comprises the 22 member states of the Arab League, stretching from Morocco and Mauritania in the west across North Africa and the Levant to Iraq and the Gulf states in the east, and including Sudan, Somalia, Djibouti and the Comoros. Its defining characteristic is the Arabic language and shared cultural heritage, though the region is home to many ethnic, linguistic and religious minorities.

Geography

The Arab world spans roughly 13 million square kilometres, dominated by the Sahara and Arabian deserts, with population concentrated along the Mediterranean coast, the Nile Valley, Mesopotamia and the mountainous Levant. Its states range from small Gulf monarchies to Egypt, the most populous Arab country with over 110 million people.

History

Arabic spread from the Arabian Peninsula with the early Islamic conquests of the seventh and eighth centuries, which unified the region politically and gradually Arabised its population. Successive caliphates — Umayyad, Abbasid and Fatimid — and later Ottoman rule shaped a shared high culture in language, law and religion. Most Arab states achieved independence from European colonial rule in the mid-twentieth century, and the Arab League was founded in Cairo in 1945.

Language and culture

Modern Standard Arabic serves as the shared written and formal language, while everyday speech varies greatly by region in the form of local dialects. Islam is the majority religion, with significant Christian and other minorities. The region contains many non-Arab peoples, including Berbers (Amazigh), Kurds, Nubians and Assyrians, whose status is a recurring theme in Arab politics.
