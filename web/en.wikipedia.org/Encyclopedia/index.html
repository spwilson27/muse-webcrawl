An encyclopedia (also spelled encyclopaedia) is a reference work providing summaries of information either from all branches of knowledge or from a particular field or discipline, usually organized in articles arranged alphabetically or by subject. The word derives from the Greek enkýklios paideía, "general education". Encyclopedias have existed for roughly 2,000 years and have evolved from single-author compilations into multi-volume scholarly works and, in the twenty-first century, vast digital and collaboratively written resources.

History

The oldest encyclopedic work still surviving is the Natural History of Pliny the Elder (c. 77 CE), a 37-volume survey of the Roman world's knowledge. Medieval Europe produced compilations such as Isidore of Seville's Etymologiae, while China developed the leishu genre, culminating in the enormous Yongle Encyclopedia (1408), and the Islamic world produced works such as al-Nuwayri's encyclopedia. The term "encyclopaedia" in its modern sense was popularized in the Renaissance and given definitive form by Johann Heinrich Alsted's 1630 Encyclopaedia.

The Enlightenment brought the genre's most famous works: Ephraim Chambers' Cyclopaedia (1728), the French Encyclopédie edited by Denis Diderot and Jean d'Alembert (1751–1772) — a flagship of Enlightenment thought with some 72,000 articles — and the Encyclopaedia Britannica, first published in Edinburgh in 1768–1771. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century publishers produced national encyclopedias in many languages, alongside subject encyclopedias and children's sets, marketed door to door at their peak.

The digital era

CD-ROM encyclopedias such as Microsoft's Encarta (1993) displaced print, and print Britannica published its final edition in 2010. Online, the free, volunteer-written Wikipedia, founded in 2001, became by far the largest encyclopedia ever assembled, with tens of millions of articles in hundreds of languages and an editorial model based on verifiability and neutral point of view. Its success reshaped expectations: encyclopedias are now continuously updated, hyperlinked, and free at the point of use, though questions of reliability, systemic bias, and coverage gaps remain subjects of study.

Characteristics

Modern encyclopedias aim for breadth, accuracy, neutral tone, and synthesis rather than original research; articles are written or reviewed by subject specialists, supported by references, and organized with cross-references and indexes. Specialized encyclopedias serve fields from philosophy and religion to sports and popular culture.
