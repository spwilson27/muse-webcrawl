Disease vector

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In epidemiology, a disease vector is any living agent that carries and transmits an infectious pathogen such as a parasite or microbe, to another living organism. Agents regarded as disease vectors are predominantly blood-sucking insects, including species of mosquitoes, ticks, fleas, lice and biting flies, which ingest disease-producing microorganisms during a blood meal from an infected host (human or animal) and later transmit them into a new host after the pathogen has replicated.

Types of transmission

Vectors can transmit pathogens mechanically, simply carrying them on their body surfaces or mouthparts without the pathogen undergoing development inside the vector, or biologically, in which case the pathogen must complete part of its life cycle within the vector before it can infect a new host. Female Anopheles mosquitoes, which transmit the malaria parasite Plasmodium, are the classic example of biological vectors.

Major vector-borne diseases

Vector-borne diseases account for more than 17% of all infectious diseases and cause over 700,000 deaths annually, according to the World Health Organization. Major examples include malaria and dengue (mosquitoes), Lyme disease and tick-borne encephalitis (ticks), plague (fleas), leishmaniasis (sandflies), Chagas disease (triatomine bugs), and sleeping sickness (tsetse flies).

Control

Vector control is a central element of public health strategy against these diseases. Methods include insecticide-treated bed nets, indoor residual spraying, environmental management to remove breeding sites, larviciding, biological control using natural predators, and newer approaches such as releasing mosquitoes infected with the bacterium Wolbachia or genetically modified sterile males.
