Superantigen

Superantigens (SAgs) are a class of antigens that result in excessive activation of the immune system. Specifically, they cause non-specific activation of T-cells, resulting in polyclonal activation and massive cytokine release. Superantigens are produced by some pathogenic viruses and bacteria as a defence mechanism against the immune system.

Mechanism

Unlike conventional antigens, superantigens are not processed by antigen-presenting cells. They bind directly to the outside of MHC class II molecules and simultaneously to particular Vβ regions of the T-cell receptor, cross-linking them. Whereas a conventional antigen activates roughly 0.001–0.0001% of T-cells, a superantigen can activate up to 20%.

Clinical significance

The resulting flood of cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-2 can cause fever, rash, hypotension, and shock. Examples include staphylococcal enterotoxins and the toxic shock syndrome toxin (TSST-1) of Staphylococcus aureus, associated with toxic shock syndrome, and streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins.
