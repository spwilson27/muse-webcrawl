First-past-the-post voting

First-past-the-post (FPTP or FPP) is a plurality voting system in which voters mark one candidate on the ballot, and the candidate who receives the most votes wins. It is the system used for elections to the House of Commons of the United Kingdom, the House of Commons of Canada, the Lok Sabha of India, and, in the form of single-member districts and the Electoral College, for most elections in the United States. Under FPTP, electoral districts are single-member: each district elects one representative.

Method

Each voter casts one vote for a candidate in their district; the candidate with the plurality of votes — not necessarily a majority — is elected. Because a candidate can win with well under half the vote when the opposition is split, the system tends to produce "wasted votes" for losing candidates and for surplus votes for winners, and can produce large discrepancies between a party's share of the national vote and its share of seats.

Effects

Duverger's law observes that plurality systems tend toward two-party competition, because supporters of smaller parties may vote tactically for a leading candidate rather than "waste" their vote. FPTP is praised for its simplicity, its clear constituency link, and its tendency to produce stable single-party governments with a decisive result. It is criticised for unrepresentative outcomes, for discouraging smaller and geographically dispersed parties, for facilitating gerrymandering, and for producing "wrong winner" results in which the party winning the most votes does not win the most seats.

Use and reform

Many countries that formerly used FPTP have adopted proportional or mixed systems, including New Zealand, which switched to mixed-member proportional representation in 1996. Reform debates continue in the United Kingdom and Canada, and the system remains under scrutiny wherever questions of proportionality and representation arise.
