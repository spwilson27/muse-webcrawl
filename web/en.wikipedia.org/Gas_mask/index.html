Gas mask

A gas mask is a mask worn over the face to protect the wearer from inhaling airborne pollutants, toxic gases and chemical or biological agents, and to protect the eyes and facial skin. Gas masks typically filter the air through a cartridge containing activated charcoal and particulate filters, though some types supply clean air from an independent source.

History

Primitive respirators were used by miners and firefighters for centuries, and inventors such as John Stenhouse developed charcoal-filter respirators in the 1850s. The modern gas mask emerged urgently in the First World War after the first large-scale chlorine gas attack at Ypres in April 1915. Early improvisations — cloth pads soaked in urine or chemicals — were rapidly replaced by hood and box respirators, such as the British Small Box Respirator. Between the wars, civilian gas masks were mass-produced; during the Second World War Britain issued masks to the entire civilian population, including children, though gas was never used against them.

Notable inventors include the American Garrett Morgan, who patented a smoke hood in 1914, and the Russian chemist Nikolay Zelinsky, whose activated-charcoal mask of 1915 became a standard design.

Design

A filtering gas mask seals tightly around the face. Inhaled air passes through a filter canister: a particulate layer traps aerosols and biological agents, while activated charcoal adsorbs chemical vapours. Filters are agent-specific and have limited service life. Full-face masks also protect the eyes against irritant and blister agents. Military masks add features such as drinking tubes, voice amplifiers and compatibility with night-vision equipment.

Uses

Gas masks are standard equipment for soldiers (as protection against chemical, biological, radiological and nuclear threats), police in riot control, firefighters and industrial workers exposed to hazardous atmospheres. They do not protect against oxygen deficiency or gases such as carbon monoxide unless specifically designed to do so.
