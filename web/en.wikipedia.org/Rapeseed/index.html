Rapeseed (Brassica napus), also known as oilseed rape, is a bright-yellow flowering member of the cabbage family cultivated mainly for its oil-rich seeds. It is the third-largest source of vegetable oil worldwide, after soybean and palm, and its oil — in the low-erucic-acid form bred in Canada — is marketed as canola oil.

Cultivation and products

B. napus arose as a hybrid between turnip rape (B. rapa) and cabbage (B. oleracea). It is grown as a winter or spring annual in temperate regions; Canada, China, India and the European Union are the largest producers. Crushed seed yields oil for food, biodiesel and industrial lubricants, while the protein-rich meal is used as livestock feed. Traditional rapeseed oil contained high levels of erucic acid and glucosinolates; Canadian breeders in the 1970s developed "double-low" varieties (canola), transforming the crop's food use.

Issues

Most North American plantings are genetically modified for herbicide tolerance, which has raised debates about gene flow to wild relatives and volunteer plants. Rapeseed is also valuable to beekeepers, supports pollinators at flowering, and is used as a cover crop and in rotations to break cereal disease cycles.
