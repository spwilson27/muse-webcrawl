The Porsche 356 is a sports car produced by the German company Porsche from 1948 to 1965. It was the first production automobile to bear the Porsche name. Designed by Ferdinand "Ferry" Porsche and Erwin Komenda, the 356 was a lightweight, rear-engined two-seater sharing many components — engine, suspension, and running gear — with the Volkswagen Beetle, which Ferdinand Porsche Sr. had also designed.

Development and variants

The first 356, a mid-engined roadster prototype, was completed in Gmünd, Austria, in June 1948; production cars, mostly aluminium-bodied coupés and cabriolets built in Gmünd, followed before manufacture moved to Stuttgart in 1950. The model evolved through the 356 "pre-A" (to 1955), 356A (1955–1959), 356B (1960–1963), and 356C (1964–1965), with steadily larger flat-four engines from 1.1 to 2.0 litres. The lightweight Speedster, introduced for the American market in 1954, became an icon, while the high-revving Carrera models used the complex four-cam "Fuhrmann" racing engine.

Racing and legacy

The 356 was successful in competition from the start, winning its class at Le Mans in 1951 and triumphing in events such as the Liège–Rome–Liège rally and the Carrera Panamericana, from which the Carrera name derived. When production ended in April 1965, 76,302 cars had been built; the model had established Porsche's reputation for nimble, durable sports cars and laid the groundwork for its successor, the 911. Well-preserved examples, especially Speedsters and Carreras, are now highly valuable collector cars.
