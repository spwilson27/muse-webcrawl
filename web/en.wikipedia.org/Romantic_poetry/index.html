Romantic poetry

Romantic poetry is the poetry of the Romantic era (approximately 1785–1832 in England, with later flowering elsewhere in Europe and America), an artistic and intellectual movement that reacted against Enlightenment rationalism and rapid industrialization. Romantic poets exalted emotion, imagination, individuality, and the sublime in nature; elevated common language and marginalized subjects; and often supported political liberty and national self-determination.

English Romantic poetry

The conventional starting point is Lyrical Ballads (1798), the joint collection of William Wordsworth and Samuel Taylor Coleridge, whose Preface (1800/1802) — a landmark manifesto — defined poetry as "the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings" recollected in tranquility and advocated the language of ordinary life. Wordsworth's long autobiographical poem The Prelude and lyrics such as "Tintern Abbey" explore memory and the moral influence of nature. Coleridge contributed visionary works including "The Rime of the Ancient Mariner" and "Kubla Khan."

A second generation — Lord Byron, Percy Bysshe Shelley, and John Keats — brought greater political radicalism and formal brilliance: Byron's satirical epic Don Juan and the Byronic hero; Shelley's lyrical dramas, "Ode to the West Wind," and Prometheus Unbound; Keats's odes of 1819 ("Ode to a Nightingale," "Ode on a Grecian Urn") and his theory of "negative capability." Other significant figures include William Blake, whose self-illustrated Songs of Innocence and of Experience (1794) anticipated Romanticism; Robert Burns; John Clare; and women poets such as Charlotte Smith, Mary Robinson, Felicia Hemans, and Letitia Elizabeth Landon.

Romantic poetry in other literatures

In Germany, Romantic poetry grew from Sturm und Drang through Goethe and Schiller to Novalis, Friedrich Hölderlin, and Heinrich Heine. French Romanticism centered on Alphonse de Lamartine, Alfred de Musset, and Victor Hugo. Alexander Pushkin shaped Russian Romantic verse; Adam Mickiewicz did the same for Polish. In the United States, Romantic currents run through William Cullen Bryant, Edgar Allan Poe, and later Walt Whitman and Emily Dickinson; in the Spanish-speaking world, José de Espronceda and Gustavo Adolfo Bécquer are central.

Themes and forms

Recurrent themes include nature as a source of spiritual truth; the sublime and the picturesque; childhood and memory; dreams, the supernatural, and the Gothic; medievalism and folklore; political freedom and revolution (the French Revolution shadowed the whole movement); and the figure of the poet as prophet or "legislator of the world." Forms range from the sonnet and the ode to ballads, blank-verse meditation, and the verse tale.

Legacy

Romantic poetry permanently changed the standing of the lyric and of personal experience as poetic subject matter, and its emphases echo through Victorian, symbolist, and modern poetry.
