Gastrinoma

A gastrinoma is a neuroendocrine tumour that secretes excessive amounts of the hormone gastrin, causing the stomach to produce large quantities of acid. The resulting condition, Zollinger–Ellison syndrome, is characterised by severe, recurrent peptic ulcers, diarrhoea and reflux. Gastrinomas most often arise in the duodenum and pancreas, within the so-called "gastrinoma triangle".

Signs and symptoms

Patients typically present with abdominal pain, multiple or unusually located peptic ulcers resistant to standard treatment, chronic diarrhoea (from acid inactivating digestive enzymes and damaging the intestinal lining), and gastro-oesophageal reflux. Symptoms usually resolve dramatically with high-dose proton pump inhibitors.

Causes and associations

Most gastrinomas are sporadic, but about 25% occur as part of multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN 1), an inherited syndrome involving parathyroid, pituitary and pancreatic tumours. Many gastrinomas show malignant behaviour with spread to lymph nodes and liver, though growth is often slow.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on a markedly elevated fasting serum gastrin together with low gastric pH, confirmed by a secretin stimulation test. Tumours are localised with cross-sectional imaging, somatostatin-receptor imaging and endoscopic ultrasound. Acid hypersecretion is controlled with proton pump inhibitors; surgery may cure localised sporadic tumours, while metastatic disease is managed with somatostatin analogues and other systemic therapies.
