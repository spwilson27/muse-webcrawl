The bombing of Dresden was a series of four air raids carried out by the Royal Air Force and the United States Army Air Forces between 13 and 15 February 1945, in the closing months of the Second World War. Nearly 1,300 heavy bombers dropped more than 3,900 tons of high-explosive and incendiary bombs on the city, igniting a firestorm that destroyed the historic centre. Modern historical research, including a 2010 commission appointed by the city of Dresden, estimates the death toll at between 22,700 and 25,000.

Background

Dresden, the capital of Saxony and a major railway junction, had been largely spared heavy bombing before 1945 and was crowded with refugees fleeing the advancing Red Army. Allied planners targeted the city as part of the Thunderclap concept and to disrupt transport and communications supporting the German Eastern Front; at Yalta, the Soviets had requested air attacks on eastern German communication centres including Dresden. The city contained war-related industry and was a significant marshalling point, although the extent of its military importance has been debated.

The raids

On the night of 13–14 February, 796 RAF Lancasters attacked in two waves, dropping incendiaries that merged into a single firestorm; temperatures in the burning centre are estimated to have reached extreme levels, and many victims died of burns, asphyxiation, or carbon monoxide poisoning in cellars. Daylight raids by the USAAF Eighth Air Force followed on 14 and 15 February, aimed principally at the railway marshalling yards. Some 6.5 square miles of the city centre were devastated, and landmarks including the Frauenkirche collapsed.

Controversy and legacy

The attack became one of the most controversial Allied actions of the war. Nazi propaganda inflated casualty figures (claims of 200,000 or more deaths circulated for decades), and later debates — amplified by Kurt Vonnegut's novel Slaughterhouse-Five (1969), based on his experience as a prisoner of war in Dresden — questioned the military necessity of destroying a cultural centre so late in the war. Historians continue to debate the raids' proportionality and effect, while the rebuilt Frauenkirche, reconsecrated in 2005 with British participation, has become a symbol of reconciliation; Coventry, which had been heavily bombed in 1940, is Dresden's twin city.
