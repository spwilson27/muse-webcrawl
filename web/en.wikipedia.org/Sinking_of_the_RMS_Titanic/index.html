Sinking of the RMS Titanic

Date14–15 April 1912
LocationNorth Atlantic Ocean, ~370 mi SE of Newfoundland
CauseCollision with an iceberg
On board~2,224 passengers and crew

The British ocean liner RMS Titanic sank in the early hours of 15 April 1912 after striking an iceberg during her maiden voyage from Southampton to New York City. Of the roughly 2,224 people aboard the largest ship then in service, more than 1,500 died, making the disaster one of the deadliest peacetime maritime catastrophes in history and a defining event of the twentieth century.

Collision and sinking

At 23:40 ship's time on 14 April, lookouts sighted an iceberg directly ahead; despite evasive action, Titanic's starboard side scraped along it, buckling hull plates and opening seams below the waterline across six of her sixteen watertight compartments — one more than she could survive. Captain Edward Smith ordered the boats away under the "women and children first" protocol, but the ship carried lifeboats for only about half those aboard, and many boats left partially filled. Wireless operators sent CQD and the new SOS distress calls; the liner Carpathia raced to the scene but arrived after Titanic broke apart and sank at 02:20, two hours and forty minutes after the collision. Around 710 survivors were recovered from the boats; most victims died of hypothermia in the −2 °C water.

Inquiries and reforms

American and British inquiries criticised the ship's speed in an ice field, the shortage of lifeboats, inadequate lookout arrangements and the failure of the nearby SS Californian to respond. The disaster led directly to the first International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea (SOLAS) in 1914, requiring lifeboat capacity for everyone aboard, 24-hour radio watches, and the establishment of the International Ice Patrol.

Wreck and legacy

The wreck was located in 1985 by an expedition led by Robert Ballard, lying in two pieces at a depth of about 3,800 metres. The sinking has been the subject of innumerable books, films — most famously James Cameron's Titanic (1997) — exhibitions and memorials, and it remains a potent symbol of technological hubris, class inequality and Edwardian society.
