Operation Bagration (Russian: Операция «Багратион», romanized: Operatsiya Bagration) was the codename for the 1944 Soviet Byelorussian strategic offensive operation (Russian: Белорусская наступательная операция «Багратион»), a military campaign fought between 22 June and 19–29 August 1944 in Soviet Byelorussia in the Eastern Front of World War II, just over two weeks after the start of Operation Overlord in the west.

The operation was named after the Georgian prince Pyotr Bagration (1765–1812), a general of the Imperial Russian Army during the Napoleonic Wars. The Soviet Union destroyed 28 of the divisions of Army Group Centre and completely shattered the German front line, in what is considered the largest defeat in German military history.

Background

By mid-1944 the Red Army had driven German forces from most of Ukraine and was positioned along the borders of Byelorussia, whose salient — the "Byelorussian balcony" — threatened to unhinge both German army groups to its north and south. Soviet deception operations (maskirovka) convinced the German high command that the main summer offensive would fall in the south, leaving Army Group Centre under-strength in armour and reserves.

The offensive

The offensive began on 22 June 1944, the third anniversary of Operation Barbarossa, with partisan attacks on German rail communications. Four Soviet fronts — the 1st Baltic and the 1st, 2nd and 3rd Byelorussian Fronts, coordinated by Marshals Zhukov and Vasilevsky — broke through in multiple sectors. Vitebsk fell within days; Minsk was liberated on 3 July, trapping some 100,000 German troops east of the city. By late August Soviet forces had advanced 600 km, liberated all of Byelorussia, reached the Vistula and the borders of East Prussia.

Aftermath

Army Group Centre lost an estimated 300,000–400,000 men killed, wounded or captured, and 28 divisions were destroyed outright. The offensive opened the way to Poland and East Prussia and took place in coordination with the Allied campaign in Normandy. It is regarded by many historians as arguably the single most successful major operation of the war in operational terms.
