Horse-drawn vehicle

A horse-drawn vehicle is a wheeled conveyance pulled by one or more horses. Such vehicles were the principal means of land transport for people and goods for several thousand years, from the invention of the chariot around 2000 BC until they were largely displaced by motor vehicles in the early 20th century.

Types

Two-wheeled vehicles include the cart, the chariot, the gig, and the sulky. Four-wheeled vehicles include the wagon (for freight), the carriage (for passengers), the coach, the landau, the phaeton, the hansom cab, and the omnibus. Vehicles designed for winter use on snow include sleighs and sledges fitted with runners instead of wheels. Working vehicles ranged from farm wagons and drays to elaborate state coaches used on ceremonial occasions.

Harness and operation

Horses are attached to the vehicle by a harness, typically involving a collar or breastcollar, traces connecting to the vehicle, and reins by which the driver controls the team. Draft animals may be arranged singly, in pairs, or in larger teams of four or six. The development of effective harness, such as the padded horse collar in medieval Europe, greatly increased the loads horses could pull.

Modern use

Today horse-drawn vehicles survive in tourism, ceremonial and traditional contexts, in equestrian sport such as combined driving, and in some religious communities such as the Amish, who use horse-drawn buggies for everyday transport.
