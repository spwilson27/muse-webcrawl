Clinical lycanthropy

Clinical lycanthropy is a rare psychiatric syndrome characterised by a delusional belief that the affected person can transform, or has transformed, into an animal — or that they are an animal. Its name derives from the mythical condition of lycanthropy, in which a person transforms into a wolf (a werewolf); in the clinical syndrome the transformation is purely believed, not physical.

Symptoms

Affected individuals report feeling that they have changed into an animal, describe sensations such as growing fur, claws or teeth, and may behave like the animal they believe themselves to be — howling, crawling on all fours or eating raw food. The animal involved varies by culture: wolves and dogs are common in Europe, while case reports elsewhere feature cats, tigers, frogs and other creatures. A related broader category, sometimes called zoanthropy, covers delusions of transformation into any animal.

Causes and treatment

Clinical lycanthropy is not a standalone diagnosis in the DSM; it appears as a symptom of underlying disorders, most often schizophrenia, bipolar disorder in manic or depressive episodes, severe clinical depression with psychotic features, and occasionally neurological conditions affecting the brain's body-image representation, such as lesions of the right parietal region. Treatment targets the underlying condition, typically with antipsychotic or mood-stabilising medication, and the delusion usually resolves as the primary illness is treated. Published case numbers are small — one review found only a few dozen cases in the medical literature since 1850.
