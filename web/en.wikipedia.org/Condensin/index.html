Condensin

Condensins are large protein complexes that play a central role in the condensation and organisation of chromosomes during mitosis and meiosis. By folding and compacting chromatin, they help transform the diffuse interphase chromosomes into the compact rod-shaped structures that can be faithfully segregated to daughter cells.

Structure

Each condensin complex is built around a pair of SMC (structural maintenance of chromosomes) ATPases — SMC2 and SMC4 — whose long coiled-coil arms fold to form a V-shaped dimer joined at a hinge, with ATPase head domains at the other end. Associated non-SMC subunits, including a kleisin subunit and HEAT-repeat subunits, bridge the heads and regulate activity. Vertebrates possess two complexes, condensin I and condensin II, which share the SMC2–SMC4 core but differ in their non-SMC subunits and in their timing and location: condensin II acts in the nucleus from prophase, while condensin I gains access to chromosomes after nuclear envelope breakdown.

Mechanism and function

Condensins are thought to act as molecular motors that extrude loops of DNA, progressively enlarging loops to shorten and stiffen the chromosome axis — a mechanism supported by single-molecule experiments. They also help resolve sister chromatids and prevent entanglements, cooperating with topoisomerase II. Mutations in condensin subunits cause developmental disorders such as microcephaly, and condensin dysfunction leads to chromosome segregation errors. The related complex cohesin, by contrast, holds sister chromatids together, while condensin compacts individual chromosomes.
