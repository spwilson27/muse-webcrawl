Electron capture

Electron capture (K-electron capture, also K-capture, or L-electron capture, L-capture) is a process in which the proton-rich nucleus of an electrically neutral atom absorbs an inner atomic electron, usually from the K or L electron shells. A proton in the nucleus is thereby converted into a neutron, and an electron neutrino is emitted.

Electron capture changes the atomic number by one while leaving the mass number unchanged.

Process

The captured electron comes most often from the innermost (K) shell, which has the greatest overlap with the nucleus. The vacancy left behind is filled by an outer electron, releasing energy as characteristic X-rays or Auger electrons. Because the neutrino escapes almost undetected, the observable signature is chiefly this X-ray emission.

Examples

Electron capture is the primary decay mode of many proton-rich isotopes, such as iron-55 and potassium-40's minor branch (in which argon-40 is produced, used in potassium–argon dating). It competes with positron emission for nuclei with moderate proton excess; where the energy difference between parent and daughter atoms is small, electron capture is the only available mode.

History

The process was first discussed by Gian-Carlo Wick in 1934 and first observed by Luis Alvarez in 1937, who detected the characteristic X-rays emitted following capture in vanadium-48.
