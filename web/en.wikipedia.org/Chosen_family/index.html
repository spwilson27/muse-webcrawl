Chosen family

A chosen family (also called a found family) is a group of people who deliberately choose to play significant family-like roles in one another's lives, despite lacking blood or legal ties. The concept is especially prominent in LGBTQ communities, where individuals who face rejection from their families of origin build alternative kinship networks that provide emotional, social, and material support.

Origins

The term gained academic attention through Kath Weston's 1991 ethnography Families We Choose, based on fieldwork in the San Francisco Bay Area during the 1980s, which documented how gay and lesbian communities redefined kinship in the face of exclusion. The idea has roots in earlier traditions, including the "houses" of Black and Latino ball culture in New York City, where house mothers and fathers mentored chosen children.

Functions

Chosen families commonly provide housing, caregiving during illness (notably during the AIDS crisis), financial help, holiday companionship, and affirmation of identity. Anthropologists situate the concept within broader studies of fictive kinship found in many cultures, including compadrazgo in Latin America and blood-brotherhood customs.

In culture and law

The found-family trope is common in literature, film, and television. While chosen families generally lack legal recognition, advocates have argued for policies — hospital visitation, bereavement leave, and multi-partner or designated-family arrangements — that acknowledge non-biological kin.
