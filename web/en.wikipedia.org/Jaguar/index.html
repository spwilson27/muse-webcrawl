Jaguar

KingdomAnimalia
FamilyFelidae
GenusPanthera
SpeciesP. onca
Conservation statusNear Threatened (IUCN)

The jaguar (Panthera onca) is a large cat species and the only living member of the genus Panthera native to the Americas. It is the third-largest cat species in the world, after the tiger and the lion, and the largest in the Americas. Its range extends from the southwestern United States through Mexico and Central America to most of Amazonian South America and as far south as northern Argentina. The name derives from a Tupi–Guarani word, yaguar, meaning "he who kills with one leap".

Description

The jaguar is a compact, powerfully built animal with a broad head and the strongest bite force, relative to size, of any big cat — strong enough to pierce turtle shells and skulls. Its coat is typically tawny yellow covered with rosettes for camouflage in dappled forest light; the rosettes, unlike a leopard's, often have spots inside them. Melanistic (black) jaguars occur, especially in dense forest, and are popularly called black panthers. Adults weigh 56–96 kg, with large males exceeding 120 kg.

Behaviour and ecology

The jaguar is a solitary apex predator and a keystone species. Unlike most cats it loves water and swims readily, hunting fish, caimans and turtles as well as deer, capybaras, peccaries and tapirs. It typically kills with a bite through the skull or neck, rather than the throat suffocation used by other big cats. Jaguars are most active at dawn and dusk, and males defend large territories overlapping those of several females.

Conservation

The IUCN lists the jaguar as Near Threatened, with populations declining because of habitat loss and fragmentation — especially deforestation in the Amazon and Cerrado — and conflict with ranchers. Once hunted heavily for its pelt, it is now protected by law in most range countries and listed on CITES Appendix I, prohibiting international commercial trade. Major strongholds include the Amazon basin and the Pantanal, where jaguar-watching tourism has made live animals worth more than dead ones.

In culture

The jaguar held a central place in the religions of Mesoamerica and South America: the Olmec depicted were-jaguars, Maya rulers wore jaguar pelts and took jaguar names, and the jaguar was associated with shamans, the night sun and the underworld across many cultures.
