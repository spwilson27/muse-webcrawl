Valentinian I (Latin: Flavius Valentinianus; 321 – 17 November 375), also called Valentinian the Great, was Roman emperor from 364 to 375. A career soldier from Pannonia, he was proclaimed emperor by the army at Nicaea after the death of Jovian and ruled the western half of the empire, appointing his younger brother Valens to the East.

Military achievements

Valentinian spent most of his reign campaigning on the Rhine and Danube frontiers. He defeated the Alamanni at Solicinium, fortified the entire Rhine line with new forts and watchtowers, and sent the general Theodosius to suppress the Great Conspiracy of 367–368 in Britain and the revolt of Firmus in Africa. His energetic defence is credited with stabilising the western frontier for a generation.

Administration and religion

He reformed taxation and the currency, protected the lower classes in law courts through defensores civitatis, and separated military and civil administration in the frontier provinces. Although a Nicene Christian, he maintained broad religious tolerance toward pagans and rival Christian factions, intervening rarely.

Death and legacy

Valentinian died at Brigetio in 375, reportedly from a burst blood vessel brought on by rage during an audience with Quadi envoys. He was succeeded in the West by his sons Gratian and the infant Valentinian II. Historians such as Ammianus Marcellinus portray him as harsh and irascible but a capable and conscientious ruler.
