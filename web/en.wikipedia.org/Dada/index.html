Dada or Dadaism was an art movement of the European avant-garde in the early twentieth century, with early centres in Zurich, Switzerland, at the Cabaret Voltaire (in 1916), and in New York City from about 1915. Developed in reaction to the First World War and the nationalist and colonial interests that many Dadaists believed caused it, the movement rejected the logic, reason and aestheticism of modern capitalist society, expressing nonsense, irrationality and anti-art protest in its works.

Origins

The movement began in neutral Zurich in February 1916, when Hugo Ball and Emmy Hennings founded the Cabaret Voltaire, a venue for performances, sound poetry and exhibitions. The name "Dada" was, by the artists' own accounts, chosen arbitrarily — accounts variously credit a dictionary opened at random (French dada, a child's hobby horse) or the Slavic "da, da" ("yes, yes").

Methods and works

Dadaists pioneered collage, photomontage, assemblage and the readymade — ordinary manufactured objects designated as art, most famously Marcel Duchamp's Fountain (1917), a porcelain urinal signed "R. Mutt". Tristan Tzara's recipes for cut-up poems, Hannah Höch's photomontages, Jean Arp's chance collages and Kurt Schwitters' Merz assemblages in Hanover all rejected conventional artistic technique and taste.

Centres and decline

After the war the movement spread to Berlin, where it took a sharply political turn with the First International Dada Fair of 1920; to Cologne and Paris, where Tzara and André Breton's circles clashed; and to New York, where Duchamp, Man Ray and Francis Picabia formed a parallel group. By the mid-1920s Dada had largely dissolved into Surrealism, though its strategies — chance, appropriation, anti-art gesture — profoundly influenced later art, including Pop art, Fluxus, performance art and punk culture.
