Council of Constance

The Council of Constance (Latin: Concilium Constantiense) was a council of the Catholic Church held from 1414 to 1418 in Constance (Konstanz), Germany, convened by the Emperor Sigismund at the request of the Pisan antipope John XXIII. It is best known for ending the Western Schism by deposing or accepting the resignations of the rival popes and electing Pope Martin V in 1417.

Ending the schism

Since 1378 the Church had been divided between popes in Rome and Avignon, and after the Council of Pisa (1409) there were three claimants. At Constance, Gregory XII resigned, while John XXIII and Benedict XIII were deposed, clearing the way for the election of Martin V by a conclave that, unusually, included representatives of the five "nations" as well as cardinals.

Decrees and conciliarism

The council issued the decree Haec sancta (1415), asserting that a general council derives its authority directly from Christ and that even the pope must obey it in matters of faith and ending schism — the high-water mark of conciliarism — and Frequens (1417), requiring regular future councils.

Heresy trials

The council condemned the teachings of John Wycliffe and summoned the Bohemian reformer Jan Hus, who, despite a safe-conduct from Sigismund, was tried, condemned and burned at the stake on 6 July 1415. Jerome of Prague was burned the following year. Hus's execution helped provoke the Hussite Wars in Bohemia.
