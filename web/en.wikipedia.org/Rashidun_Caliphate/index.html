The Rashidun Caliphate (Arabic: اَلْخِلَافَةُ ٱلرَّاشِدَةُ, "the Rightly Guided Caliphate") is the first caliphate in Islamic history, ruling from the death of the Prophet Muhammad in 632 CE until 661 CE. Its name derives from the four Rashidun ("Rightly Guided") caliphs who succeeded Muhammad in turn: Abu Bakr (632–634), Umar (634–644), Uthman (644–656), and Ali (656–661). In under three decades, this state expanded from Arabia to rule Persia, the Levant, Egypt, and parts of North Africa and the Caucasus — one of the most rapid expansions in world history. Sunni Muslims regard the Rashidun as exemplary rulers who followed the Prophet's example closely; Shia Muslims recognise Ali alone as the legitimate first successor.

Abu Bakr and Umar: consolidation and conquest

Abu Bakr, chosen at the Saqifa gathering as Muhammad lay dying, first had to preserve the community: in the Ridda Wars (632–633) he subdued Arabian tribes that had renounced Medina's authority, and he ordered the first collection of the Quran. Under Umar, Muslim armies shattered the great powers of late antiquity: decisively defeating the Byzantines at Yarmouk (636) and taking Syria and Egypt, and destroying the Sasanian Empire after al-Qadisiyyah (636) and Nahavand (642). Umar organised the state — stipend registers (diwan), garrison cities such as Basra, Kufa, and Fustat, the Islamic calendar (dated from the Hijrah), and the office of judge (qadi) — before being assassinated by a Persian slave in 644.

Uthman and Ali: crisis

Uthman, elected by a council Umar had appointed, standardised the Quranic text (the "Uthmanic codex") and continued expansion into North Africa, Cyprus, and Armenia, building the first Muslim navy. Growing resentment of his nepotism and fiscal policies led rebels from Egypt and Iraq to besiege and kill him in Medina in 656 — the event that shattered Muslim unity. Ali ibn Abi Talib, Muhammad's cousin and son-in-law, succeeded him but faced revolt: the Battle of the Camel (656) against Talha, Zubayr, and the Prophet's widow Aisha, then war with Mu'awiyah, governor of Syria, inconclusively fought at Siffin (657) and poisoned by a failed arbitration. Ali was assassinated by a Kharijite in Kufa in 661; his son Hasan ceded power to Mu'awiyah, who founded the hereditary Umayyad Caliphate, closing the Rashidun era and fixing the Sunni–Shia schism.

Administration and society

The caliphate ruled a religiously plural empire: non-Muslim "protected peoples" (dhimmis — Christians, Jews, Zoroastrians) paid the jizya tax and largely kept their own institutions, and mass conversion came only gradually over later centuries. Early mosques were simple, and the caliphs governed from modest capitals, in deliberate contrast to the Byzantine and Persian courts they had overthrown. The Rashidun period remains the formative reference point for Islamic political thought — the model against which later rulers and modern revivalist movements alike measure themselves.
