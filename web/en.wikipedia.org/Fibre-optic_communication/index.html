Fiber-optic communication

Fiber-optic communication is a method of transmitting information from one place to another by sending pulses of infrared or visible light through an optical fiber. The light is a form of carrier wave that is modulated to carry information. Fiber is preferred over electrical cabling when high bandwidth, long distance, or immunity to electromagnetic interference is required.

History

The principle of guiding light by refraction was demonstrated in the 1840s by Daniel Colladon and Jacques Babinet. Practical fiber optics emerged in the 1950s with the work of Harold Hopkins and Narinder Singh Kapany. In 1966 Charles K. Kao and George Hockham proposed that sufficiently pure glass fibers could carry light over long distances; Kao received the 2009 Nobel Prize in Physics for this work. The first live telephone traffic through fiber-optic cables was achieved in 1977.

Operation

A fiber-optic system consists of a transmitter (an LED or laser diode) that converts electrical signals to light, the fiber cable itself — strands of glass or plastic in which light is confined by total internal reflection — optical amplifiers or regenerators for long spans, and a receiver photodiode that converts light back to electrical signals. Wavelength-division multiplexing allows many channels at different wavelengths to share one fiber, and modern transoceanic cables carry terabits of data per second.

Advantages and applications

Compared with copper wire, optical fibers offer far greater bandwidth, much lower attenuation, immunity to electromagnetic interference and crosstalk, and small size and weight. They form the backbone of the global Internet, telephone networks, and cable television, and are increasingly used in sensing and medicine (see Fibromyalgia for one medical topic, and Fibreboard for another use of fibres).
