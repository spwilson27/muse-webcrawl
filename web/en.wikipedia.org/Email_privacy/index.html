Email privacy

Email privacy is the set of legal, technical and social issues surrounding the confidentiality of electronic mail. By design, email is not a private medium: messages pass through multiple servers, are stored on them, and can be read by service providers, intercepted in transit, or obtained by governments and litigants.

Technical vulnerabilities

The basic email protocols (SMTP, POP3 and IMAP) were designed without encryption. Transport Layer Security can protect messages between servers that support it, but protection is opportunistic and metadata — sender, recipient, subject and timing — generally remains visible. True confidentiality requires end-to-end encryption, such as OpenPGP or S/MIME, so that only the endpoints can read the content.

Legal issues

In the United States, the Electronic Communications Privacy Act of 1986 and the "third-party doctrine" have been read to give limited constitutional protection to stored email, though case law such as United States v. Warshak recognised a reasonable expectation of privacy in emails held by a provider. Workplace email is usually monitored under employer policies, and privacy law in the European Union, including the GDPR, imposes additional constraints on processing personal data.

Provider practices

Free webmail services have historically scanned message content for advertising and spam filtering, prompting criticism and, in some cases, policy changes. Retention policies, government data requests and large-scale surveillance programmes disclosed in 2013 further shaped public debate about the privacy of email.
