Divergence theorem

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In vector calculus, the divergence theorem, also known as Gauss's theorem or Ostrogradsky's theorem, is a theorem relating the flux of a vector field through a closed surface to the divergence of the field in the volume enclosed by the surface. More precisely, if V is a region in three-dimensional space with a piecewise smooth boundary surface S oriented by outward-pointing unit normals, and F is a continuously differentiable vector field defined on a neighbourhood of V, then:

∭V (∇ · F) dV = ∬S (F · n̂) dS

In words, the volume integral of the divergence of F over V equals the surface integral of F over the boundary S — the total outward flux of F from V.

Physical interpretation

The theorem expresses a conservation principle: if F represents, for example, the flow of a fluid, the divergence at a point measures the net outflow per unit volume, and summing over the volume gives the total flux through the surface. It underlies the derivation of Gauss's law in electrostatics from its differential form, and of the continuity equation in fluid mechanics.

Generalizations

The divergence theorem is a special case of the general Stokes' theorem on manifolds, which relates the integral of the exterior derivative of a differential form over a manifold to the integral of the form over its boundary. The theorem is named for Carl Friedrich Gauss, who stated it in 1813, and was also derived independently by Mikhail Ostrogradsky in 1831.
