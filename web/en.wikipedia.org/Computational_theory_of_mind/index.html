Computational theory of mind

The computational theory of mind (CTM) states that the mind is a computational system: mental processes are computations performed over mental representations. On this view, thinking is analogous to the operation of a computer program, and the brain functions as the hardware implementing that program.

The theory was developed in the mid-twentieth century, drawing on the work of Alan Turing and the rise of cognitive science. Hilary Putnam proposed an early version under the name machine-state functionalism, and Jerry Fodor became its most prominent defender, arguing that thought takes place in a "language of thought" — an internal symbolic system sometimes called Mentalese.

Central claims

CTM holds that propositional attitudes such as beliefs and desires are relations to internal representations, and that reasoning consists of rule-governed transformations of those representations. This explains the productivity and systematicity of thought: from a finite stock of representations, thinkers can generate an unbounded range of new thoughts.

Criticisms

Critics such as John Searle, with his Chinese room argument, contend that symbol manipulation alone cannot produce understanding or intentionality. Others argue that connectionist networks, embodied cognition, or dynamical systems better capture how minds work, and that conscious experience (qualia) resists computational explanation.
