Spectral theory

In mathematics, spectral theory is the study of the spectrum of linear operators — the generalisation of eigenvalues and eigenvectors of matrices to operators on infinite-dimensional spaces. Originally motivated by systems of linear equations and differential equations, it became a central part of functional analysis in the early twentieth century through the work of David Hilbert, Frigyes Riesz and John von Neumann.

Overview

For a bounded operator T on a Banach space, the spectrum σ(T) is the set of complex numbers λ for which T − λI has no bounded inverse. For matrices the spectrum is simply the set of eigenvalues. For infinite-dimensional operators the spectrum is richer: it may include continuous spectrum (as for multiplication operators) and residual spectrum, in addition to eigenvalues (the point spectrum).

Spectral theorem

The spectral theorem states that self-adjoint operators (and more generally normal operators) can be represented as multiplication operators, allowing an integral representation analogous to diagonalising a Hermitian matrix. For compact self-adjoint operators, the operator has a countable orthonormal basis of eigenvectors with real eigenvalues accumulating only at zero.

Applications

Spectral theory is fundamental to quantum mechanics, where observables are self-adjoint operators on a Hilbert space and measurement outcomes lie in their spectra. It also underlies the analysis of differential equations, signal processing, and partial differential equations.
