On-board diagnostics

IntroducedOBD-I: 1980s; OBD-II: 1996 (US)
Connector16-pin SAE J1962 (OBD-II)
PurposeVehicle self-diagnosis and reporting

On-board diagnostics (OBD) is a term referring to a vehicle's self-diagnostic and reporting capability. OBD systems give vehicle owners and repair technicians access to information about the health of the vehicle's subsystems, most notably those related to emissions. When a fault is detected, the system stores a diagnostic trouble code (DTC) and typically illuminates the "check engine" light (malfunction indicator lamp) on the dashboard.

History

Early proprietary systems appeared in the 1980s (OBD-I). In the United States, the California Air Resources Board and then the federal Clean Air Act required a standardized system: OBD-II became mandatory for all cars and light trucks sold in the US from the 1996 model year. Europe followed with EOBD for petrol cars in 2001 and diesel cars in 2004; heavy-duty vehicles use related standards.

OBD-II

OBD-II specifies a standard 16-pin connector, a set of diagnostic trouble codes, and parameters (PIDs) that can be read with a scan tool, such as engine speed, vehicle speed, oxygen sensor readings, and fuel trim. Codes are organized by system: for example, P0xxx codes denote generic powertrain faults. Standardization allows any compliant scan tool — and inexpensive consumer adapters — to read data from any compliant vehicle.

Applications

Beyond repair, OBD data is used in emissions inspections, fleet management, telematics insurance, and consumer apps that monitor fuel economy and driving behavior.
