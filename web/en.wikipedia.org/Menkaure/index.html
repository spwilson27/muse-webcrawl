Menkaure

Menkaure (also spelled Menkaura, and in Greek Mykerinos or Mycerinus) was an ancient Egyptian king (pharaoh) of the Fourth Dynasty during the Old Kingdom, reigning in the 26th century BC (conventionally c. 2532–2503 BC). He was the son of Khafre and grandson of Khufu, and is best known as the builder of the third and smallest of the pyramids of Giza.

Pyramid and statues

The Pyramid of Menkaure stands about 65 metres (213 ft) tall, its lower courses cased in red granite rather than the limestone used higher up. In its valley temple, excavator George Reisner discovered in 1908 a series of exquisite triad statues showing Menkaure flanked by the goddess Hathor and a nome deity; a famous dyad of the king with Queen Khamerernebty II is now in the Museum of Fine Arts, Boston. Herodotus portrayed Mycerinus as a just and pious ruler who reopened the temples closed by his predecessors, though this conflicts with the monumental scale of his building works.

Legacy

Menkaure's pyramid complex remained unfinished at his death and was completed in mudbrick by his successor Shepseskaf. A later legend, recorded in the Westcar Papyrus tradition and in Herodotus, attached a prophecy to his reign: told he had only a few years to live, he supposedly had lamps lit to turn night into day and double his remaining time.
