Mascon

A mascon (from "mass concentration") is a region of a planet's or moon's crust that contains a large positive gravitational anomaly. The term was coined in 1968 by Paul Muller and William Sjogren of NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, who discovered mascons beneath the Moon's great circular maria while analyzing tracking data from the Lunar Orbiter spacecraft.

Lunar mascons

The strongest lunar mascons lie under the Imbrium, Serenitatis, Crisium, Nectaris, and Humorum basins. They are interpreted as dense basaltic lava that flooded the impact basins, combined with uplifted mantle material beneath the thinned crust. Mascons noticeably perturber low lunar orbits, causing spacecraft to drift; mission planners must account for them, and the Apollo program's subsatellites mapped them in detail.

Elsewhere

Mascons have also been identified on Mars (for example beneath Isidis and Hellas) and on Mercury and the dwarf planet Ceres, studied with data from missions such as GRAIL at the Moon and Dawn at Ceres.
