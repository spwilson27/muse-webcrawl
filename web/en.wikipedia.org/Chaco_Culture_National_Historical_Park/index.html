Chaco Culture National Historical Park is a United States National Historical Park in northwestern New Mexico, centred on Chaco Canyon. Between about 850 and 1150 CE the canyon was the ceremonial, administrative, and economic heart of the Ancestral Puebloan world, containing the largest and most complex prehistoric structures north of Mexico.

The great houses

The canyon holds a dozen monumental "great houses" — planned, multi-storey masonry complexes of hundreds of rooms, great kivas, and plazas. Pueblo Bonito, the largest, covered some 3 acres with more than 600 rooms rising four or five storeys. Chetro Ketl, Peñasco Blanco, Una Vida, and Hungo Pavi are other major sites. Construction required an estimated 200,000 timber beams hauled from mountain forests 80 km away, plus vast quantities of shaped sandstone.

Roads, astronomy, and trade

A network of remarkably straight roads, up to 9 metres wide, radiates from the canyon to outlier communities across the San Juan Basin. Chacoan buildings and features such as the Fajada Butte "Sun Dagger" record sophisticated astronomical observation of solstices and lunar standstills. Excavations have found traded goods from distant regions: turquoise in huge quantities, macaw feathers and copper bells from Mexico, shells from the Pacific and Gulf coasts, and vessels bearing traces of cacao — evidence of a far-reaching exchange network.

Decline and preservation

Construction waned after 1130 during a prolonged drought; the Chacoan system fragmented and its people migrated to areas such as the Rio Grande valley, where their Pueblo descendants live today. The canyon was declared a national monument in 1907, redesignated a national historical park in 1980, and inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987. Dark-sky designation and ongoing consultation with Pueblo and Navajo tribes guide its management.
