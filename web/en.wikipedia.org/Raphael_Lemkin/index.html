Raphael Lemkin

Raphael Lemkin (24 June 1900 – 28 August 1959) was a Polish lawyer of Jewish descent who coined the term genocide and campaigned relentlessly for it to be recognised as an international crime. His efforts led to the United Nations Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide, adopted in 1948.

Early life and education

Lemkin was born in Bezwodna in the Grodno Governorate of the Russian Empire. He studied linguistics and then law at the University of Lwów, earning a doctorate in 1926, and became a prosecutor and legal scholar in Warsaw. As a young man he was struck by the massacre of Armenians in the Ottoman Empire and by the impunity of its organisers, asking why killing a million people could be considered a lesser crime than killing one individual. In the 1930s he proposed international laws against what he called "barbarity" and "vandalism".

World War II and "genocide"

When Germany invaded Poland in 1939, Lemkin fled, eventually reaching the United States in 1941, where he taught law at Duke University and advised the War Department. Nearly fifty members of his family were murdered in the Holocaust. In his 1944 book Axis Rule in Occupied Europe, he introduced the word genocide, formed from the Greek genos (race, tribe) and the Latin -cide (killing), defining it as a coordinated plan aimed at the destruction of the essential foundations of the life of national groups.

The Genocide Convention

Lemkin served as an adviser to the United States team at the Nuremberg trials and then devoted himself to lobbying delegates at the new United Nations. On 9 December 1948 the General Assembly unanimously adopted the Genocide Convention, the first human rights treaty of the UN era, which entered into force in 1951. Lemkin spent his remaining years campaigning for ratifications, often in poverty and obscurity, and was nominated repeatedly for the Nobel Peace Prize.

Death and legacy

Lemkin died of a heart attack in New York in 1959. Only a handful of people attended his funeral, but his concept has since become foundational to international criminal law, underpinning tribunals for the former Yugoslavia and Rwanda and the International Criminal Court.
