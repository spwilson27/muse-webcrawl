Trophic cascade

Top-down effects propagate through food webs

Trophic cascades are ecological phenomena that occur when predators at the top of a food chain indirectly affect populations of organisms at lower trophic levels, often by suppressing the abundance or altering the behaviour of their prey. The effects cascade down through the food web, so that a change at one level causes alternating, opposite changes at levels below it.

Origins of the concept

The concept grew out of classic experiments by Robert Paine, who showed in the 1960s that removing the predatory starfish Pisaster ochraceus from intertidal communities caused mussels to monopolise space and reduced species diversity — the basis of the "keystone species" idea. Hairston, Smith, and Slobodkin's 1960 "green world" hypothesis similarly proposed that predators keep herbivores in check, allowing plants to flourish.

Classic examples

One of the best-known cascades involves sea otters, sea urchins, and kelp forests off the Pacific coast of North America: when otter populations declined (in part through predation by killer whales), sea urchins proliferated and destroyed kelp forests; where otters recovered, kelp returned. In lakes, adding or removing predatory fish changes zooplankton communities and thereby water clarity and algal abundance.

The 1995 reintroduction of grey wolves to Yellowstone National Park is frequently cited as a trophic cascade: wolves reduced elk numbers and changed their grazing behaviour, allowing willow, aspen, and cottonwood to recover along streams, which in turn benefited beavers, songbirds, and other species. Ecologists continue to debate the strength and relative importance of this effect compared with other factors, such as drought and other predators.

Types

Cascades may be density-mediated (predators eat herbivores) or behaviourally mediated (herbivores avoid risky areas — the "ecology of fear"). They occur in marine, freshwater, and terrestrial systems, and can also run bottom-up when changes in primary producers propagate to consumers.
