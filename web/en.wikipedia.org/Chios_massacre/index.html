Chios massacre

The Chios massacre was the killing of tens of thousands of Greeks on the island of Chios by Ottoman troops during the Greek War of Independence in 1822. Of an island population of roughly 100,000–120,000, an estimated 42,000 were hanged, butchered, or starved, and some 50,000 were enslaved, devastating one of the Aegean's most prosperous communities.

Background

Chios was a wealthy island famous for mastic production and shipping, and its inhabitants had largely stood apart from the Greek uprising that began in 1821. In March 1822, revolutionaries from Samos landed on Chios and proclaimed the revolution, prompting the Ottoman Empire to dispatch a large punitive force under Nasuhzade Ali Pasha.

The massacre

Ottoman troops landed on 11 April 1822 (Old Style) and, over the following weeks, burned villages, massacred the inhabitants, and enslaved women and children. The mastic villages were initially spared for their economic value but eventually suffered as well. Foreign consuls and missionaries documented the atrocities, and survivors scattered to Syros, Ancona, and beyond, forming a Chiot diaspora.

International reaction

Reports of the massacre shocked Europe and galvanized philhellenic sentiment. Eugène Delacroix's monumental painting The Massacre at Chios (1824) and Victor Hugo's poem "The Greek Child" became enduring artistic responses, and the event remains a powerful symbol of the Greek struggle for independence.
