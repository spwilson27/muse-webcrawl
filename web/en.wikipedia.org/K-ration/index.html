The K-ration was an individual daily combat food ration introduced by the United States Army during World War II. It was designed by physiologist Ancel Keys (from whose surname the "K" derives) at the University of Minnesota for paratroopers and other mobile, lightly equipped forces who needed compact, lightweight meals that could survive a wide range of conditions.

Composition

The ration consisted of three separate boxed meals — breakfast, dinner (lunch), and supper — packed in wax-coated cardboard cartons. A breakfast unit typically contained a small tin of eggs and ham, biscuits, a fruit bar, instant coffee, sugar, and cigarettes with matches. The dinner unit included cheese or meat spread, biscuits, a chocolate or caramels item, lemon powder or powdered coffee, and toilet paper. The supper unit featured a canned meat portion, biscuits, a chocolate bar (such as the D ration bar), bouillon powder, coffee, and sugar. Each unit included a small can opener and chewing gum.

Development and use

Keys's 1941–42 experiments sought a ration that could be bought at a grocery store and still meet nutritional needs. The K-ration was issued from 1942 and used widely in all theatres. It was intended only for short-term use — a few days at a time — but troops in prolonged combat sometimes subsisted on it for weeks, leading to weight loss and complaints about monotony and insufficient calories.

Evaluation and replacement

Veterans' opinions were mixed: the ration was light and practical, and items such as the canned cheese and chocolate were popular trade goods, but portions were judged inadequate for sustained exertion. Production ended after the war, and the K-ration was formally discontinued in 1948, its role absorbed by the C-ration. It remains one of the most recognisable artefacts of the American soldier's experience in the Second World War.
