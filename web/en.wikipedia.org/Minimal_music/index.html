Minimal music

Minimal music (also called minimalism) is a form of art music or other compositional practice that employs limited or minimal musical materials. Prominent features of the approach are repetition — especially of short musical phrases, with variations over long periods of time — and a marked reduction in the number of musical elements and their development. It emerged in the downtown New York scene of the 1960s and was initially viewed as a form of experimental music.

Origins

The movement began with the works of La Monte Young, whose sustained-tone pieces of the late 1950s stripped music down to single long-held sounds. Terry Riley's In C (1964) introduced a modular structure of 53 short phrases repeated by any number of performers over a steady pulse. Steve Reich developed "phasing", in which two identical patterns gradually slip out of synchrony, in works such as Piano Phase (1967) and Drumming (1971). Philip Glass built large-scale works, including the opera Einstein on the Beach (1976), from additive processes over repeating arpeggiated figures.

Characteristics

Minimalist works typically feature consonant harmony, a steady pulse, diatonic materials, and gradual transformation. The music often draws on non-Western influences — African drumming, Indonesian gamelan and Indian classical music — and overlaps with process music, in which the compositional process itself is audible to the listener.

Influence

Although the leading figures resisted the label "minimalist", the style became one of the most influential in later 20th-century music, feeding into postminimalism and totalism, shaping film scores, electronic dance music and the work of composers such as John Adams, whose Short Ride in a Fast Machine and opera Nixon in China brought its language to the orchestra.
