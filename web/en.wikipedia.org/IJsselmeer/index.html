The IJsselmeer (Dutch for "Lake IJssel") is a shallow artificial lake of 1,100 km² in the central Netherlands, bordering the provinces of Flevoland, North Holland and Friesland. It is named after the river IJssel, which flows into it via a smaller lake, the Ketelmeer. It is the largest lake in the Netherlands. The IJsselmeer was created in 1932 when the inland sea known as the Zuiderzee was closed by the 32 km Afsluitdijk ("Enclosure Dam"), a prime part of the Zuiderzee Works.

History

Plans to dam and partly reclaim the Zuiderzee were developed by Cornelis Lely in 1891 and enacted after the catastrophic flood of 1916. The Afsluitdijk was completed on 28 May 1932, the salt-water Zuiderzee became the fresh-water IJsselmeer, and large areas were subsequently drained as polders: the Noordoostpolder (1942), Eastern Flevoland (1957) and Southern Flevoland (1968). In 1976 the Houtribdijk split off the southern part of the lake as the Markermeer, in anticipation of a Markerwaard polder that was never built.

Functions

The lake is a major fresh-water reservoir for agriculture and drinking water, a buffer for the river IJssel (a branch of the Rhine), and a safety valve for surrounding low-lying land: water levels are regulated by discharge sluices at Den Oever and Kornwerderzand into the Wadden Sea. Artificial islands such as the Marker Wadden (2016) were built to combat silting and restore bird habitat. The lake is heavily used for recreation, commercial fishing (notably eel and perch) and sailing; historic fishing towns around its shores include Enkhuizen, Hoorn, Urk and Hindeloopen.

Ecology

The conversion from salt to fresh water transformed the ecosystem; herring and anchovy fisheries disappeared while freshwater species such as pike-perch colonised the lake. The IJsselmeer is a Natura 2000 site important for wintering waterbirds, though fish stocks and water quality have been under pressure from nutrient changes and silting.
