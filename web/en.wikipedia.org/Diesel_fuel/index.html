Diesel fuel (or simply diesel, also called diesel oil) is a liquid fuel specifically designed for use in diesel engines, in which fuel ignition takes place without a spark, as a result of compression of the inlet air and then injection of fuel. Diesel fuel is produced mainly by fractional distillation of crude oil, yielding the fraction boiling between about 200 °C and 350 °C. It powers most of the world's freight transport: trucks, trains, ships, agricultural and construction machinery, and a large share of passenger cars in Europe.

Properties

Petroleum diesel consists mostly of hydrocarbons with 8 to 21 carbon atoms per molecule. Its key quality measure is the cetane number, which rates how readily the fuel auto-ignites under compression — the counterpart of petrol's octane rating, but with the opposite virtue: high cetane (short ignition delay) is desirable. Diesel is less volatile and less flammable than petrol, with a comparatively high flash point, but its viscosity rises in cold weather and wax crystals can block filters ("gelling"), so winter grades and additives are used in cold climates. Diesel contains about 13–15% more energy per litre than petrol, one source of diesel engines' fuel economy.

Types

  • Petroleum diesel (ULSD) — ultra-low-sulfur diesel, mandated for road use in the EU, US and elsewhere since the 2000s (≤10–15 ppm sulfur), enabling particulate filters and advanced catalysts.
  • Biodiesel — fatty acid methyl esters (FAME) from vegetable oils or animal fats, blended as B5, B7, B20 or used neat (B100).
  • Renewable diesel (HVO) — hydrotreated vegetable oil, a drop-in paraffinic fuel chemically similar to fossil diesel.
  • Synthetic diesel — made by Fischer–Tropsch gas-to-liquids or biomass-to-liquids processes.
  • Marine and heating grades — heavier distillates and residual blends used in shipping and oil-fired heating.

Emissions and health

Diesel combustion produces carbon dioxide — though efficient diesel cars typically emit less CO₂ per kilometre than comparable petrol cars — as well as nitrogen oxides (NOₓ) and particulate matter (soot). The WHO's IARC classified diesel exhaust as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) in 2012. Emission standards such as Euro 6 and US EPA rules, enforced with diesel particulate filters (DPF) and selective catalytic reduction (AdBlue/DEF), have cut tailpipe pollutants sharply. The 2015 Volkswagen emissions scandal ("Dieselgate"), in which defeat devices falsified NOₓ test results, accelerated the decline of diesel passenger cars in Europe.

History

Rudolf Diesel patented his compression-ignition engine in 1892 and demonstrated it at MAN in Augsburg in the 1890s; early experiments used fuels ranging from coal dust to peanut oil. Petroleum diesel became standard as refining matured, and by the mid-twentieth century diesel dominated heavy transport, rail and shipping, spreading to passenger cars with the oil crises of the 1970s and the advent of turbocharged direct-injection engines in the 1990s.
