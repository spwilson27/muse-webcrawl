Karp's 21 NP-complete problems

Karp's 21 NP-complete problems are a famous list of combinatorial and graph-theoretic problems shown to be NP-complete in Richard Karp's landmark 1972 paper "Reducibility Among Combinatorial Problems". Building on the Cook–Levin theorem, Karp demonstrated that the Boolean satisfiability problem can be reduced to twenty other well-known problems, thereby establishing them as NP-complete and founding the modern theory of computational complexity's catalogue of hard problems.

Background

In 1971 Stephen Cook proved that the Boolean satisfiability problem (SAT) is NP-complete, meaning every problem in NP reduces to it in polynomial time. Karp's 1972 paper showed, through an elegant tree of reductions, that twenty-one natural problems from logic, graph theory, set covering and scheduling are equally hard — so that a polynomial-time algorithm for any one of them would give polynomial algorithms for all problems in NP.

The list

The twenty-one problems, in the names used by Karp, are: Satisfiability; 0-1 Integer Programming; Clique; Set Packing; Vertex Cover; Set Covering; Feedback Node Set; Feedback Arc Set; Directed Hamiltonian Circuit; Undirected Hamiltonian Circuit; 3-SAT; Chromatic Number (graph colouring); Clique Cover; Exact Cover; Hitting Set; Steiner Tree; 3-Dimensional Matching; Knapsack; Job Sequencing; Partition; and Max-Cut.

Significance

Karp's paper gave researchers a practical toolkit: to show a new problem NP-complete, it suffices to reduce one of these known problems to it. The list launched an industry of NP-completeness proofs, systematised in Garey and Johnson's 1979 book Computers and Intractability. Karp received the Turing Award in 1985 in part for this work.
