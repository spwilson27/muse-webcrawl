Emissions trading, or cap and trade, is a market-based approach used to control pollution by providing economic incentives for reducing emissions. A central authority sets a cap on the total amount of a pollutant that may be emitted and issues a corresponding number of allowances, each permitting a specified quantity of emissions. Firms may buy and sell allowances among themselves; those that can cut emissions cheaply reduce more and sell their surplus, while those facing higher abatement costs buy allowances. The total emissions stay within the cap, which is usually tightened over time.

History

The economic theory of tradable permits was developed by economists including Ronald Coase, J. H. Dales, and David Montgomery. The first large-scale implementation was the U.S. Acid Rain Program under the Clean Air Act Amendments of 1990, which from 1995 capped sulfur dioxide emissions from power plants and achieved reductions faster and more cheaply than projected. The Kyoto Protocol of 1997 introduced international emissions trading among countries, alongside project-based mechanisms such as the Clean Development Mechanism.

Major systems

The European Union Emissions Trading System, launched in 2005, is the world's largest carbon market, covering power generation, heavy industry, and aviation in the EU, EEA, and (through linkage) Switzerland. China launched its national ETS in 2021, initially covering the power sector — the largest by volume of covered emissions. Other systems operate in California and the northeastern United States (RGGI), South Korea, New Zealand, Canada, and the United Kingdom.

Design and criticism

Key design choices include whether allowances are auctioned or given away free, the rate at which the cap declines, price-stability mechanisms such as the EU's Market Stability Reserve, and protection against "carbon leakage", where production moves to jurisdictions without a carbon price. Criticisms of emissions trading include price volatility, windfall profits where free allowances are passed through into prices, fraud in early EU ETS phases, and the argument by some environmental groups that trading allows wealthy polluters to buy their way out of local reductions. Economists generally regard a declining cap with auctioning as among the most cost-effective instruments for reducing greenhouse gas emissions.
