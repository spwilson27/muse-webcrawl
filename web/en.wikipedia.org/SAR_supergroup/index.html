SAR supergroup

The SAR supergroup, also known as the Harosa, is a major clade of eukaryotes comprising three large groups: the Stramenopiles (heterokonts), the Alveolata, and the Rhizaria. The name SAR is an acronym of the initial letters of these three groups. First proposed on the basis of molecular phylogenetic analyses in 2007, the grouping is now widely accepted and accounts for a large fraction of eukaryotic diversity.

Members

Stramenopiles include the photosynthetic diatoms, brown algae and golden algae, the oomycete "water moulds" (including the potato blight organism Phytophthora infestans), and many heterotrophic flagellates. They are characterised in many lineages by flagella bearing tripartite hairs.

Alveolata include the ciliates, the dinoflagellates — important marine plankton, some of which produce harmful algal blooms — and the Apicomplexa, a wholly parasitic group including Plasmodium, the cause of malaria. Alveolates are united by cortical alveoli, flattened vesicles beneath the cell membrane.

Rhizaria are mostly amoeboid protists with thread-like or network-like pseudopodia, including the foraminifera and radiolarians, whose mineralised shells form important microfossil records, and the cercozoans.

Significance

SAR organisms dominate marine plankton communities and global primary production — diatoms alone are estimated to generate a substantial share of Earth's oxygen — and the group includes major parasites, shell-forming protists central to palaeoclimate reconstruction, and the giant kelps. Together with the Archaeplastida (including land plants) and the Amorphea (including animals and fungi), SAR forms one of the principal branches of the eukaryotic tree of life.
