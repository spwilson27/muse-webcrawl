Korean War

The Korean War (25 June 1950 – 27 July 1953) was fought between North Korea (the Democratic People's Republic of Korea), supported by China and the Soviet Union, and South Korea (the Republic of Korea), supported principally by the United States under the flag of the United Nations. It began when North Korea invaded the South and ended in an armistice that left the Korean Peninsula divided — a division that persists today.

Background

Korea, annexed by Japan in 1910, was divided at the 38th parallel in 1945 between Soviet and American occupation zones. Two rival states emerged in 1948: the communist North under Kim Il Sung and the anti-communist South under Syngman Rhee. Border clashes escalated until, with Stalin's approval, North Korea launched a full invasion on 25 June 1950.

Course of the war

North Korean forces nearly overran the South before UN forces stabilised the Pusan Perimeter. General Douglas MacArthur's amphibious landing at Inchon (15 September 1950) reversed the war; UN forces advanced toward the Yalu River. China then intervened massively from October 1950, driving the UN back. President Truman relieved MacArthur in April 1951 after public disagreements over expanding the war. The front stabilised near the 38th parallel, and two years of trench warfare and armistice talks followed while bombing devastated the North.

Armistice and aftermath

The Korean Armistice Agreement was signed at Panmunjom on 27 July 1953, creating the Korean Demilitarized Zone. No peace treaty was ever concluded. The war entrenched the division of Korea, anchored the U.S. military presence in South Korea, and is sometimes called "the Forgotten War" in the United States, overshadowed by the Second World War and Vietnam.
