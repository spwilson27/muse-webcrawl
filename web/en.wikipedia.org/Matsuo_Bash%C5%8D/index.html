Matsuo Bashō

Matsuo Bashō (1644 – 28 November 1694), born Matsuo Kinsaku and also known as Matsuo Munefusa, was the most famous Japanese poet of the Edo period. During his lifetime, Bashō was recognized for his works in the collaborative haikai no renga form; today, after centuries of commentary, he is recognized as the greatest master of haiku. His poetry is internationally renowned, and in Japan many of his poems are reproduced on monuments and traditional sites.

Life

Born to a low-ranking samurai family, Bashō served a local lord who shared his interest in haikai poetry. After his master's death he moved to Edo (Tokyo), where he became a teacher of poetry and took his pen name from the banana plant (bashō) beside his hut. He made a series of celebrated journeys around Japan, recording them in travel journals mixing prose and haiku (haibun).

Works and style

His most famous work, Oku no Hosomichi (The Narrow Road to the Deep North), recounts a journey of 1689 through northern Honshu. Bashō elevated haikai from playful verse toward ideals of sabi (loneliness, quietness) and karumi (lightness). His most famous haiku describes an old pond, a frog jumping in, and the sound of water.

Legacy

Bashō's disciples spread his school across Japan, and his travel diaries shaped Japanese literature. He remains the iconic figure of haiku worldwide.
