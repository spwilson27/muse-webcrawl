Sphygmomanometer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A sphygmomanometer is a device used to measure blood pressure, composed of an inflatable cuff to collapse and then release the artery under the cuff in a controlled manner, and a mercury or aneroid manometer, or electronic sensor, to measure the pressure. Manual sphygmomanometers are used with a stethoscope when using the auscultatory technique. The word comes from the Greek sphygmos (pulse) combined with the scientific term manometer (pressure meter).

Operation

The cuff is inflated until the brachial artery is occluded, then slowly deflated. With a manual instrument, the clinician listens with a stethoscope for Korotkoff sounds: the pressure at which sounds first appear is the systolic pressure, and the pressure at which they disappear is the diastolic pressure. Automatic devices instead use oscillometric measurement, detecting pressure oscillations in the cuff to estimate systolic, mean, and diastolic values.

History

The first practical cuff sphygmomanometer was introduced by Scipione Riva-Rocci in 1896, building on earlier instruments by von Basch and others. Nikolai Korotkoff described the auscultatory method in 1905. Mercury sphygmomanometers long served as the reference standard but have been phased out in many countries because of mercury toxicity, replaced by aneroid and validated electronic devices.

Accuracy

Accurate readings require a properly sized cuff, a rested patient seated with the arm supported at heart level, and a calibrated device. Errors arise from wrong cuff size, talking during measurement, rapid deflation, and the "white coat" effect. Regular calibration is essential, particularly for aneroid instruments.
