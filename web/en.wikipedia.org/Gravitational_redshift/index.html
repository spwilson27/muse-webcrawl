Gravitational redshift

[ Image placeholder: Diagram of light waves stretched as they climb out of a gravitational well ]

In physics and general relativity, gravitational redshift is the phenomenon that electromagnetic waves or photons travelling out of a gravitational well (seemingly) lose energy. This loss of energy corresponds to a decrease in the wave frequency and increase in the wavelength, known as a redshift. The opposite effect, in which photons gain energy when travelling into a gravitational well, is known as a gravitational blueshift.

Theory

Albert Einstein predicted the effect in 1907 from the equivalence principle, years before completing general relativity. The fractional shift is approximately z ≈ ΔΦ/c², where ΔΦ is the difference in gravitational potential. Gravitational redshift is closely tied to gravitational time dilation: clocks deeper in a gravitational well run slower, so light emitted there is measured at lower frequency by a distant observer.

Observations and tests

Walter Sydney Adams measured spectral shifts in the dense white dwarf Sirius B in 1925. The Pound–Rebka experiment of 1959–1960 first precisely measured the shift for gamma rays falling down a 22.5 m tower at Harvard, confirming the prediction. Astronomers routinely observe gravitational redshifts in spectra of white dwarfs and neutron stars, and the effect must be corrected for in GPS satellite clocks.
