A controlled burn, also known as a prescribed burn or prescribed fire, is a fire set intentionally for purposes of forest management, farming, prairie restoration, or greenhouse gas abatement. A controlled burn may also refer to the intentional burning of slash and fuels through burn piles. Fire is a natural part of both forest and grassland ecology, and controlled fire can be a tool for foresters and land managers.

Controlled burning is conducted during the cooler months to reduce fuel buildup and decrease the likelihood of serious hotter fires. It stimulates the germination of some desirable forest trees and reveals soil mineral layers, enhancing seedling survival and growth, thus renewing the forest. Some cones, such as those of lodgepole pine and giant sequoia, are serotinous and require heat from fire to open and disperse seeds.

History

Indigenous peoples in many parts of the world have used fire for land management for thousands of years. Aboriginal Australians practised "fire-stick farming", burning mosaic patches of vegetation to promote new growth that attracted game animals. In North America, Native Americans burned prairies and oak savannas to maintain open hunting grounds and encourage the growth of food plants such as camas and berries.

During the twentieth century, many forestry agencies in the United States and Australia pursued policies of total fire suppression, which led to dangerous accumulations of fuel. From the 1970s onward, scientific understanding of fire ecology led to a revival of prescribed burning as a management tool, formalised in burn plans that specify weather conditions, staffing, and ignition patterns.

Procedure

Before a burn, managers prepare a prescription describing the desired fire behaviour and acceptable weather window, including wind speed, humidity, and fuel moisture. Firebreaks — roads, rivers, or cleared lines — bound the burn unit. Ignition may be by hand with drip torches, or from helicopters using aerial ignition devices. Crews monitor the fire until it is declared out, and mop-up operations extinguish smouldering material near the perimeter.

Ecological effects

Prescribed fire reduces understory competition, recycles nutrients into the soil as ash, and creates a mosaic of habitats favoured by many species. In longleaf pine savannas of the American Southeast, frequent low-intensity burns maintain one of the most species-rich ecosystems in North America. However, poorly timed burns can harm nesting wildlife, and smoke can affect air quality in nearby communities, so burns are scheduled to minimise smoke impacts.

Risks and controversies

Occasionally a controlled burn escapes its intended boundaries. In 2000, the Cerro Grande Fire in New Mexico began as a prescribed burn at Bandelier National Monument, escaped during high winds, and destroyed more than 200 homes in Los Alamos. In 2022, the Hermits Peak Fire in New Mexico, also an escaped prescribed burn, became the largest wildfire in the state's recorded history. Such events prompt reviews of burn protocols, though practitioners note that the overall risk of wildfire is greater without fuel treatment.
