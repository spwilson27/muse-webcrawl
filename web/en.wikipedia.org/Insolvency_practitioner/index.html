Insolvency practitioner

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In the United Kingdom, only an authorised or licensed insolvency practitioner (IP) may be appointed in relation to formal insolvency procedures. Insolvency practitioners are licensed to advise on and undertake appointments in all formal insolvency procedures, including liquidations, administrations, company voluntary arrangements, and bankruptcies.

Licensing

Under the Insolvency Act 1986, practitioners must be licensed by a recognised professional body, such as the Institute of Chartered Accountants in England and Wales (ICAEW), the Insolvency Practitioners Association, or the Institute of Chartered Accountants of Scotland. Most IPs originally train and qualify as accountants, though solicitors and others may also qualify by passing the Joint Insolvency Examination Board (JIEB) examinations and satisfying experience requirements.

Roles

An insolvency practitioner may act as liquidator of a company in liquidation, administrator of a company in administration, trustee in bankruptcy of an individual's estate, supervisor of a voluntary arrangement, or receiver. In these capacities the IP takes control of assets, investigates the affairs and conduct of directors or the bankrupt, realises assets, and distributes funds to creditors in the statutory order of priority.

Duties and regulation

Practitioners owe duties to creditors as a whole rather than to the appointing party, and must report on the conduct of directors to the Insolvency Service. Their work is regulated through Statements of Insolvency Practice (SIPs), monitoring visits by their licensing bodies, and the complaints gateway operated by the Insolvency Service.
