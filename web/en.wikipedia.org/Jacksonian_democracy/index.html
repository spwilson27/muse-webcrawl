Jacksonian democracy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Andrew Jackson, seventh president of the United States]

Jacksonian democracy was a 19th-century political philosophy in the United States that expanded suffrage to most white men over the age of 21 and restructured a number of federal institutions. Originating with the seventh president, Andrew Jackson, and his supporters, it became the nation's dominant political worldview for a generation.

Principles

Jacksonian democracy championed the "common man" against what Jacksonians called a corrupt aristocracy of wealth and privilege. Its tenets included expanded white male suffrage, Manifest Destiny, patronage (the spoils system), strict constructionism, and laissez-faire economics. Jacksonians opposed the Second Bank of the United States, which Jackson vetoed and dismantled in the Bank War, viewing it as a monopoly that favored the rich.

Era and policies

The era, roughly 1828 to 1854, saw the rise of mass political parties, popular campaigning, and the modern Democratic Party. Jackson's presidency also pursued the forced removal of Native American tribes from the southeastern United States under the Indian Removal Act of 1830, resulting in the Trail of Tears — a policy now widely condemned. Jacksonian democracy excluded women, Native Americans, and African Americans from its democratic promises.

Legacy

Historians debate the era's meaning: it democratized American politics for white men while entrenching slavery and dispossessing Native peoples. The Jacksonian coalition reshaped the presidency into a popular office claiming a direct mandate from the people.
