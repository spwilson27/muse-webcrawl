Malcom McLean

McLean, whose standardised steel container transformed world trade.

Malcom Purcell McLean (born McLean; November 14, 1913 – May 25, 2001) was an American businessman and trucking magnate who developed the modern intermodal shipping container, which revolutionised transport and international trade in the second half of the twentieth century. By standardising the container and redesigning ships, ports and handling equipment around it, McLean dramatically reduced the cost of loading and unloading cargo and is often called the "father of containerization".

Early life and trucking

McLean was born in Maxton, North Carolina, and during the Great Depression built McLean Trucking Company from a single truck into one of the largest trucking firms in the United States. The story often told of the container's origin is that in 1937, while waiting for hours at a New Jersey dock for his truck's cotton bales to be loaded piece by piece onto a ship, McLean realised that loading whole truck trailers — or standardised boxes — would be far more efficient.

Sea-Land and the container

In 1955 McLean sold his trucking company and bought the Pan-Atlantic Steamship Company, renaming it Sea-Land Service. On 26 April 1956 his converted tanker, the Ideal X, sailed from Newark, New Jersey, to Houston, Texas, carrying 58 metal containers. Loading costs fell from several dollars per ton for loose break-bulk cargo to a few cents per ton. Container shipping initially served American coastal routes, but in the 1960s Sea-Land and competitors began transatlantic and transpacific services, and containers proved vital to supplying American forces in Vietnam.

McLean championed industry-wide standards for container dimensions and corner fittings, releasing his patents royalty-free to help secure the ISO standard that made containers interchangeable worldwide. In 1969 he sold Sea-Land to R.J. Reynolds Industries for a large fortune; Maersk acquired Sea-Land's international business in 1999.

Later ventures and legacy

McLean later founded Trailer Bridge, Inc., and continued experimenting with shipping and energy ventures. In 2001 he was named Man of the Century by the International Maritime Hall of Fame. Economists credit containerisation with slashing freight costs so deeply that it enabled modern global supply chains and the offshoring of manufacturing; some studies argue it boosted world trade more than all trade agreements of the postwar era combined.
