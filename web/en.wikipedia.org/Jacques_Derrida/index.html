Jacques Derrida (born Jackie Élie Derrida; July 15, 1930 – October 9, 2004) was a French philosopher best known for developing the approach known as deconstruction, a method of critical analysis of texts and concepts that influenced literary theory, philosophy, law, anthropology, and architecture.

Life and career

Born in French Algeria to a Sephardic Jewish family, Derrida moved to Paris in 1949 and studied at the École Normale Supérieure, where he later taught. In 1967 he published three landmark books — Of Grammatology, Writing and Difference, and Speech and Phenomena — that established his reputation. He taught at the École des Hautes Études en Sciences Sociales and held visiting posts at American universities including Yale and the University of California, Irvine.

Deconstruction

Deconstruction examines the binary oppositions (speech/writing, presence/absence, nature/culture) that structure Western thought, showing how texts privilege one term while depending on the other. Derrida coined the term différance to capture how meaning arises through differences and deferral. His work provoked strong reactions: celebrated in the humanities, it was attacked by many analytic philosophers as obscure — most famously in the 1992 controversy over his honorary doctorate at Cambridge.

Later work

In his later writings Derrida turned increasingly to ethics and politics — hospitality, forgiveness, justice, the gift, and the "democracy to come" — in works such as Specters of Marx (1993). He published more than 40 books before his death from pancreatic cancer in 2004.
