Enamel

Enamel, in the context of teeth, is the hard, crystalline outer layer that covers the crown of a tooth. It is the hardest substance in the human body — even harder than bone — and serves to protect the underlying dentine and pulp from mechanical wear, temperature, and chemical attack during chewing.

Composition and structure

Tooth enamel consists of about 96 per cent mineral by weight, principally hydroxyapatite, a crystalline calcium phosphate, with most of the remainder being water and a small fraction of organic material. The mineral is organized into tightly packed rods, or prisms, roughly 4–8 micrometres across, woven in a pattern that gives enamel great strength and resistance to fracture.

Formation

Enamel is formed by cells called ameloblasts during tooth development, in the process known as amelogenesis. Once a tooth erupts, the ameloblasts are lost, so mature enamel is acellular and cannot regenerate; damage from decay, erosion, or abrasion is therefore permanent, although early demineralization can be reversed by remineralization from saliva and fluoride.

Clinical significance

Acid produced by oral bacteria demineralizes enamel in dental caries (tooth decay), one of the most common diseases worldwide. Fluoride strengthens enamel by promoting fluorapatite formation, which is more acid-resistant. Conditions such as amelogenesis imperfecta and dental fluorosis arise from disturbed enamel formation.
