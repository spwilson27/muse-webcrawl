Germanic law

Germanic law refers to the customary legal traditions of the early Germanic peoples, which were first written down in the early Middle Ages as the law codes of the successor kingdoms established on former Roman territory. These codes — often called the leges barbarorum ("laws of the barbarians") — include the Salic law of the Franks, the Visigothic Code, the Burgundian laws, the Lombard laws, and the Anglo-Saxon dooms of England.

Characteristics

Germanic law was originally unwritten custom, declared at public assemblies (things) by law-speakers. It was strongly personal rather than territorial: a person was judged by the law of his own people. Wrongs were frequently settled by compensation rather than punishment — most famously the wergild, a fixed price payable to the kin of a slain person according to the victim's rank. Other distinctive institutions included the oath with compurgators (oath-helpers), trial by ordeal, the blood feud regulated by composition payments, and detailed tariffs of fines for particular injuries.

The written codes

From the fifth century onward, Germanic kings issued written codes, usually in Latin and increasingly influenced by Roman law. The Lex Salica, promulgated under Clovis around 500, became one of the most influential. The Visigothic Liber Iudiciorum (654) was notable for applying a single territorial law to Goths and Romans alike. In England, the laws of Æthelberht of Kent (c. 602) are the earliest surviving Germanic laws written in a vernacular language.

Influence

Germanic legal customs contributed significantly to medieval European law and, through it, to modern legal systems — influences traced in the jury, in feudal tenure, and in the common law of England. Tacitus's Germania (c. 98 AD) remains an early, if idealised, written source on Germanic legal customs.
