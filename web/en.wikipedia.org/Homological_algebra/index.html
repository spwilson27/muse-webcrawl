Homological algebra

Homological algebra is the branch of mathematics that studies homology in a general algebraic setting. It arose from the combination of algebraic topology, where homology and cohomology groups were first defined, and abstract algebra, particularly the theory of modules and categories. Its central tools are chain complexes, derived functors, and spectral sequences.

History

The subject coalesced in the 1940s and 1950s. Henri Cartan and Samuel Eilenberg's 1956 book Homological Algebra systematised the field, introducing projective and injective resolutions and the Ext and Tor functors. Alexander Grothendieck's 1957 "Tôhoku paper" recast the theory in terms of abelian categories, enabling sheaf cohomology.

Basic concepts

A chain complex is a sequence of abelian groups or modules connected by homomorphisms such that the composition of any two consecutive maps is zero; the quotient of the kernel of one map by the image of the previous one is its homology. Derived functors measure the failure of a functor to be exact: Tor arises from tensor products and Ext from the Hom functor. The snake lemma and the five lemma are workhorse diagram-chasing results.

Applications

Homological methods underpin algebraic topology, algebraic geometry (via sheaf cohomology), group cohomology, representation theory, and commutative algebra, where homological characterisations of rings such as regular local rings are fundamental.
