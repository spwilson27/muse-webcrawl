Diversion airport

Diversion airport

Also called: Alternate airport

Role: Backup landing site in flight planning

Chosen for: Weather, runway length, services, proximity

Planning rules: ICAO / FAA / EASA regulations

A diversion airport, or alternate airport, is an airport to which a flight can proceed when it becomes impossible or inadvisable to land at the intended destination. Flights may divert because of bad weather, medical emergencies, mechanical problems, airport closures, or unruly passengers. Under aviation regulations, pilots must plan for suitable alternates before departure and carry enough fuel to reach one.

Types of alternate

Regulations distinguish several roles. A take-off alternate is available if an aircraft must land soon after departure and cannot return. An en-route alternate serves for emergencies along the route, while a destination alternate is nominated in case the destination becomes unusable. Extended-range twin-engine operations (ETOPS) impose strict requirements on en-route alternates, which must remain within a specified flying time on one engine.

Selection criteria

A suitable alternate must have adequate runway length and strength for the aircraft, acceptable forecast weather above specified minima at the expected arrival time, and appropriate rescue and fire-fighting services. Airlines also consider ground handling, maintenance support, passenger accommodation, and whether the airport can accept international arrivals if border formalities are required.

Operations

Unexpected diversions are costly and disruptive: passengers may need hotels and rebooking, aircraft and crew end up out of position, and remote airports may lack facilities for large aircraft. Notable diversion airports include Gander in Newfoundland, historically a transatlantic stepping stone — which received 38 flights during the September 11 attacks — and Shannon in Ireland.
