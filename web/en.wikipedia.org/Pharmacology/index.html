Pharmacology

Pharmacology is the science of drugs and medications, including a substance's origin, composition, pharmacokinetics, therapeutic use and toxicology. More specifically, it is the study of the interactions that occur between a living organism and chemicals that affect normal or abnormal biochemical function. If substances have medicinal properties, they are considered pharmaceuticals.

Branches

The field divides chiefly into pharmacodynamics, the study of what a drug does to the body — its mechanism of action at receptors, enzymes and ion channels and the resulting dose–response relationships — and pharmacokinetics, what the body does to the drug: absorption, distribution, metabolism and excretion (ADME). Related subdisciplines include clinical pharmacology, toxicology, pharmacogenomics, neuropharmacology and chemotherapy.

History

The use of medicinal plants is ancient, documented in the Egyptian Ebers Papyrus and the Chinese Shennong Bencaojing. Scientific pharmacology emerged in the 19th century as chemists isolated pure active principles such as morphine (1805), quinine and atropine, and as experimentalists like Rudolf Buchheim and Oswald Schmiedeberg established the first institutes of pharmacology. The 20th century brought receptor theory, the antibiotic era and systematic clinical trials, regulated after disasters such as the thalidomide tragedy.

Drug development

Modern drug discovery moves from target identification and screening through preclinical testing to phased clinical trials and regulatory approval, followed by post-marketing surveillance (pharmacovigilance). Understanding pharmacology underpins rational prescribing, dosing in special populations, and the management of drug interactions and adverse effects.
