The 1964 Alaskan earthquake, also known as the Great Alaskan earthquake and Good Friday earthquake, occurred at 5:36 p.m. AKST on Good Friday, 27 March 1964. It struck the Prince William Sound region of south-central Alaska with a moment magnitude of 9.2, making it the most powerful earthquake ever recorded in North America and the second most powerful in world history, after the 1960 Valdivia earthquake in Chile.

Effects

Ground shaking lasted approximately four and a half minutes. In Anchorage, about 120 km northwest of the epicentre, large areas were devastated by landslides and liquefaction; the Turnagain Heights neighbourhood slid into Cook Inlet, and Fourth Avenue dropped by up to 2.6 metres along a graben. Property damage was estimated at about $311 million (over $3 billion in modern terms).

The earthquake generated tsunamis that destroyed the villages of Chenega, Valdez (the original town site), Whittier and Seward, and caused deaths as far away as Crescent City, California, where 12 people were killed. The maximum run-up, 67 metres, was recorded at Shoup Bay near Valdez. Of the 139 deaths, 124 were caused by tsunamis.

Geology

The earthquake was a megathrust event caused by subduction of the Pacific Plate beneath the North American Plate along the Aleutian Trench. The rupture caused enormous vertical deformation: parts of the seafloor and coastline were uplifted by up to 11 metres, while other areas subsided by more than 2 metres, permanently reshaping the coastline and drowning coastal forests, whose "ghost forests" of salt-killed trees remain visible as evidence used to date earlier prehistoric megathrust earthquakes. The event was pivotal in the acceptance of plate tectonics, as its deformation pattern confirmed the subduction model. It also led to the founding of modern tsunami warning systems and lasting changes to building codes.
