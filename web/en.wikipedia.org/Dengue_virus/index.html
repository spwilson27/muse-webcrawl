Dengue virus

Dengue virus (DENV) is the cause of dengue fever. It is a mosquito-borne, single positive-stranded RNA virus of the family Flaviviridae. Four serotypes of the virus have been found: DENV-1, DENV-2, DENV-3, and DENV-4; a fifth serotype associated with a sylvatic (jungle) cycle has also been reported. Infection with one serotype confers lifelong immunity to that serotype but only transient protection against the others.

Transmission and disease

The virus is transmitted primarily by female mosquitoes of the species Aedes aegypti, and secondarily by Aedes albopictus, which bite during the day. Most infections are asymptomatic or cause a self-limited febrile illness with headache, muscle and joint pain, and rash. A minority progress to severe dengue (formerly dengue hemorrhagic fever and dengue shock syndrome), characterized by plasma leakage and potentially fatal shock. The risk of severe disease is higher in secondary infection with a different serotype, a phenomenon attributed to antibody-dependent enhancement.

Genome and replication

The roughly 11-kilobase genome encodes a single polyprotein that is cleaved into three structural proteins (capsid, membrane, and envelope) and seven non-structural proteins. The virus replicates in the cytoplasm of host cells, including monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells.

Prevention

Control relies mainly on mosquito control and personal protection, since no specific antiviral treatment exists; treatment is supportive. Vaccines have been developed, including Dengvaxia and Qdenga, with recommendations restricted by serostatus and region because of the enhancement phenomenon.
