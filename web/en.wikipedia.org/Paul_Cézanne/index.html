Paul Cézanne (19 January 1839 – 22 October 1906) was a French Post-Impressionist painter whose work introduced new modes of representation, influenced many twentieth-century artists, and bridged the late nineteenth-century Impressionism and early twentieth-century Cubism. Although his paintings were often mocked or ignored during his lifetime, both Henri Matisse and Pablo Picasso are said to have remarked that Cézanne "is the father of us all".

Life and career

Cézanne was born in Aix-en-Provence in southern France, the son of a prosperous banker. At school he formed a close friendship with the writer Émile Zola. Against his father's wishes — his father wanted him to study law and join the family bank — Cézanne pursued painting, moving between Paris and Provence. In the 1860s his dark, expressive early works were repeatedly rejected by the Paris Salon.

In the early 1870s, working alongside Camille Pissarro at Pontoise and Auvers-sur-Oise, Cézanne adopted the brighter palette and broken brushwork of Impressionism; he exhibited in the first (1874) and third (1877) Impressionist exhibitions. Thereafter he withdrew increasingly to Provence, developing his mature method: patient, constructive strokes of modulated colour that build form through tonal relationships rather than traditional linear perspective — treating nature, in his famous phrase, "by the cylinder, the sphere, the cone".

Mature work

From the 1880s Cézanne painted the Provençal landscape obsessively, above all Mont Sainte-Victoire, which he depicted in more than eighty paintings. His major figure compositions include the five large versions of The Card Players (1890s) — one version of which sold in 2011 for a record price reported at over $250 million — and the monumental Large Bathers series of his final years. In 1895 the dealer Ambroise Vollard mounted Cézanne's first solo exhibition in Paris, which finally brought serious critical attention from younger artists.

Cézanne died of pneumonia in Aix in October 1906, days after being caught in a storm while painting outdoors. A retrospective at the Salon d'Automne in 1907 revealed the full scope of his achievement to the Paris avant-garde and profoundly shaped the development of modern art.
