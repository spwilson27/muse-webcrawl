Artificial cardiac pacemaker

An artificial cardiac pacemaker, commonly known simply as a pacemaker, is an implantable medical device that generates electrical pulses delivered by electrodes to one or more chambers of the heart, causing the targeted chambers to contract and pump blood. It is used when the heart's natural pacemaker, the sinoatrial node, produces an abnormally slow or irregular rhythm.

Indications

Pacemakers are implanted mainly to treat bradycardia — a slow heart rate — caused by sinus node dysfunction or heart block, and in selected patients with heart failure as cardiac resynchronisation therapy (CRT). Temporary pacing may also be used in emergencies or after cardiac surgery.

How it works

A modern pacemaker consists of a pulse generator — a small hermetically sealed titanium case containing a lithium battery and circuitry — and one or more thin insulated leads threaded through a vein into the heart. The device senses the heart's intrinsic activity and delivers a pacing pulse only when needed. Leadless pacemakers, tiny self-contained capsules implanted directly in the right ventricle, have become an option for some patients.

History

The first fully implantable pacemaker was designed by engineer Rune Elmqvist and implanted by surgeon Åke Senning in Stockholm in 1958; the patient, Arne Larsson, outlived both and received more than twenty replacement devices. Wilson Greatbatch independently developed a practical implantable design in the United States in 1960. Later advances added programmability, rate responsiveness, remote monitoring and MRI-conditional designs.

Living with a pacemaker

Most patients return to normal activities, though strong magnetic fields and certain medical procedures require precautions. Battery life is typically six to fifteen years, after which the pulse generator is replaced in a minor procedure.
