Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Risk Evaluation and Mitigation Strategy (REMS) is a drug safety programme that the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) can require for certain medications with serious safety concerns. Its purpose is to ensure that the benefits of a drug outweigh its risks, through measures that go beyond the information contained in professional labelling.

Legal basis

The FDA's authority to require a REMS derives from the Food and Drug Administration Amendments Act of 2007. The agency may require a REMS before approval, or after approval if it becomes aware of new safety information. It is estimated that roughly a third of new drugs approved by the FDA are subject to some form of REMS.

Elements

Depending on the risks involved, a REMS may include a medication guide or patient package insert, a communication plan directed at healthcare providers, and "elements to assure safe use" (ETASU). ETASU may require prescribers to complete special training or certification, pharmacies to be specially certified, dispensing only in certain settings such as hospitals, or patient monitoring and enrolment in a registry.

Well-known examples include the programme for isotretinoin (iPLEDGE), designed to prevent fetal exposure, and the shared system REMS governing opioid analgesics. Clozapine is subject to a REMS requiring monitoring of absolute neutrophil counts because of the risk of severe neutropenia.

Assessment and criticism

Manufacturers must submit REMS assessments at specified intervals, typically 18 months, three years, and seven years after approval. Critics argue the programmes can impose substantial administrative burdens and delay patient access, while supporters hold that they allow medicines to remain available that might otherwise be withdrawn.
