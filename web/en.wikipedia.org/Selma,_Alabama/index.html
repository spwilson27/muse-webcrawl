Selma, Alabama

Selma is a city in and the county seat of Dallas County, Alabama, United States, on the banks of the Alabama River. Its population was about 17,971 at the 2020 census, down from a peak above 27,000. Founded in 1820 and incorporated in 1855, Selma was an important Confederate manufacturing and supply centre during the American Civil War, and a century later became a focal point of the civil rights movement.

History

The site was known to Native American peoples for centuries before European settlement; Hernando de Soto's expedition passed through the region in 1540. The town of Selma — its name derived, at the suggestion of William R. King, from Ossian's "Songs of Selma" — grew as a cotton-shipping port. During the Civil War its foundries and naval yard produced iron and munitions, including material for the ironclad CSS Tennessee; Union forces under General James H. Wilson captured and largely burned the industrial works in April 1865.

Civil rights movement

In the early 1960s, despite a population that was majority Black, only a small fraction of eligible Black citizens in Dallas County were registered to vote. Activists of the Dallas County Voters League, joined by SNCC and later Martin Luther King Jr.'s SCLC, mounted a voting-rights campaign in early 1965. The killing of activist Jimmie Lee Jackson in nearby Marion intensified the effort. On 7 March 1965 — "Bloody Sunday" — state troopers and a sheriff's posse violently attacked some 600 marchers crossing the Edmund Pettus Bridge. Televised images shocked the nation; after a second, symbolic march, a court-sanctioned third march reached Montgomery on 25 March with thousands of participants. The campaign directly propelled the Voting Rights Act of 1965, signed that August.

Geography and economy

Selma lies in Alabama's Black Belt, named for its rich dark soil, about 50 miles west of Montgomery. Its economy has long rested on agriculture and manufacturing, and the city has faced sustained population decline and high poverty rates. Historic preservation and civil-rights heritage tourism are significant: the Selma to Montgomery National Historic Trail, administered by the National Park Service, commemorates the 1965 marches, and the bridge remains a national symbol — and a subject of debate, since it still bears the name of a Confederate general and Ku Klux Klan leader.
