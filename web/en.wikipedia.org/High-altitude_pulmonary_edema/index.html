High-altitude pulmonary edema

SpecialtyEmergency medicine, pulmonology
SymptomsBreathlessness at rest, cough, frothy sputum, cyanosis
CausesRapid exposure to high altitude, hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction
TreatmentImmediate descent, oxygen, nifedipine

High-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE) is a life-threatening form of non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema (fluid accumulation in the lungs) that occurs in otherwise healthy people at altitudes typically above 2,500 metres (8,200 ft). It is the most common cause of death related to high-altitude exposure, and usually appears within 2 to 4 days of arrival at altitude.

Signs and symptoms

Early signs include a dry cough, decreased exercise tolerance, and breathlessness on exertion out of proportion to companions. As fluid accumulates, sufferers develop dyspnea at rest, orthopnea, gurgling respirations, pink frothy sputum, tachycardia, low-grade fever, and bluish discoloration of the lips and nail beds (cyanosis). Chest examination reveals crackles, often worst in the right middle lobe.

Mechanism

HAPE results from an exaggerated hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction. In susceptible individuals the constriction is uneven, diverting blood flow into unconstricted vessels at high pressure; the resulting stress failure of pulmonary capillaries allows protein-rich fluid to leak into the alveoli. Impaired alveolar fluid clearance at altitude worsens the accumulation.

Treatment and prevention

Descent by at least 1,000 metres and supplemental oxygen are the mainstays of treatment; a portable hyperbaric bag and nifedipine are adjuncts when descent is delayed. Prevention involves slow ascent (no more than 300–500 metres per day of sleeping altitude above 3,000 metres), and prophylactic nifedipine or salmeterol for those with a history of HAPE. Unlike some altitude illnesses, HAPE can strike fit, experienced climbers, and recurrence in susceptible individuals is common.
