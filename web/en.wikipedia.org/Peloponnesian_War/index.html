Peloponnesian War

Athenian triremes, the backbone of Athens' naval empire

The Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC) was an ancient Greek war fought between Athens and Sparta and their respective allies for hegemony of the Greek world. The war reshaped the ancient Greek world, ending the golden age of Athens—the most powerful city-state in Greece prior to the war—and reducing it from the height of its empire to a state of complete subjection, while Sparta emerged as the leading power in Greece.

The war is traditionally divided into three phases. In the first phase, the Archidamian War, Sparta launched repeated invasions of Attica, while Athens took advantage of its naval supremacy to raid the coast of the Peloponnese. This period ended in 421 BC with the Peace of Nicias.

The Sicilian Expedition and the Decelean War

The uneasy peace was broken by Athenian operations in Sicily, culminating in the disastrous Sicilian Expedition (415–413 BC), in which Athens' massive invasion force was destroyed at Syracuse. In the final phase, the Decelean (or Ionian) War, Sparta—now receiving financial support from Persia—fortified Decelea in Attica, built a navy of its own, and fomented revolts among Athens' subject states in Ionia. Athens replaced its losses again and again, winning the battles of Cyzicus and Arginusae, but in 405 BC the Spartan admiral Lysander destroyed the Athenian fleet at Aegospotami. Athens, starved by blockade, surrendered in 404 BC; its walls were demolished and its empire dissolved.

Sources and significance

The war is documented in extraordinary detail by two contemporaries: Thucydides, an Athenian general who wrote a rigorous, unfinished history of the conflict, and Xenophon, whose Hellenica continues the story. Thucydides' analysis of power, fear, and interest—exemplified by the Melian Dialogue—remains foundational to the study of international relations. The war devastated much of Greece, weakened the city-states, and set the stage for the ascendancy of Macedon in the following century.
