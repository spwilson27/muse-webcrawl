Tel Megiddo

Tel Megiddo (Hebrew: מגידו; Arabic: Tell el-Mutesellim) is the site of the ancient city of Megiddo, the remains of which form a tell (archaeological mound) in northern Israel near the modern kibbutz of Megiddo. Megiddo is known for its historical, geographical, and theological importance, especially under its Greek name Armageddon. During the Bronze Age, Megiddo was an important Canaanite city-state, and during the Iron Age it was a royal city of the Kingdom of Israel.

Location and history

Megiddo guarded the western branch of the Via Maris, the great trade route linking Egypt with Mesopotamia and Anatolia, where it passed through the Wadi Ara pass into the Jezreel Valley. Because of this strategic position, the plain around Megiddo was the scene of numerous battles over the millennia, including the Battle of Megiddo in 1457 BC, in which Pharaoh Thutmose III defeated a Canaanite coalition—one of the earliest battles recorded in reliable detail.

Archaeology

The mound contains some 20–26 superimposed layers of settlement spanning roughly 7,000 years, from the Neolithic to the Persian period. Major excavations were conducted by the University of Chicago's Oriental Institute in the 1920s and 1930s and have continued under Tel Aviv University. Notable finds include monumental gates, palaces, temples, stables (long attributed to King Solomon, though their dating is debated), and an elaborate water system cut through bedrock to a spring outside the walls.

Armageddon

In the New Testament Book of Revelation, Armageddon (from Har Megiddo, "Mount Megiddo") is prophesied as the site of the final battle between the forces of good and evil. The name has since become a general term for any catastrophic end-of-the-world scenario.
