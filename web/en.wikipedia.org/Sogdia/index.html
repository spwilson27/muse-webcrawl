Sogdia

Sogdia (Sogdian: Suγδ) was an ancient Iranian civilization in Central Asia between the Oxus (Amu Darya) and the Jaxartes (Syr Darya) rivers, located in present-day Uzbekistan, Tajikistan and parts of Kazakhstan. It encompassed the fertile Zeravshan valley and flourishing city-states such as Samarkand, Bukhara, Khujand and Panjakent.

History

Sogdia was incorporated into the Achaemenid Empire under Darius I and later conquered by Alexander the Great, whose campaign was fiercely resisted by the Sogdian leader Spitamenes; Alexander founded Alexandria Eschate ("Alexandria the Furthest") on the Jaxartes. Thereafter Sogdia passed through Hellenistic, Kushan, Hephthalite, Turkic and Sasanian spheres of influence, while remaining in practice a constellation of semi-independent principalities.

Trade and culture

The Sogdians became the dominant merchant intermediaries of the Silk Road, establishing trading colonies from Mongolia and China to the Byzantine world. The Sogdian language, an Eastern Iranian tongue written in an Aramaic-derived script, served as a lingua franca of commerce, and Sogdian letters, contracts and religious texts survive from Turfan and Dunhuang. Sogdians transmitted Buddhism, Manichaeism, Nestorian Christianity and Zoroastrianism along the trade routes, and their vivid wall paintings at Panjakent and Afrasiab testify to a rich artistic culture.

Decline

The Arab conquests of the early 8th century, culminating in the capture of Samarkand and Bukhara under Qutayba ibn Muslim, gradually absorbed Sogdia into the Islamic world. The Sogdian language declined, replaced in daily use by Persian (Tajik), though its descendant, Yaghnobi, survives in a mountain valley of Tajikistan.
