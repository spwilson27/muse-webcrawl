Edmund Spenser (c. 1552 – 13 January 1599) was an English poet best known for The Faerie Queene, an epic poem and fantastical allegory celebrating the Tudor dynasty and Elizabeth I. He is recognised as one of the premier craftsmen of nascent Modern English verse, and is often considered one of the greatest poets in the English language.

Life

Spenser was born in London to a modest family, educated at Merchant Taylors' School and at Pembroke College, Cambridge. In 1580 he went to Ireland as secretary to the Lord Deputy, Arthur Grey, Baron Grey of Wilton. He was granted an estate at Kilcolman in County Cork during the Munster Plantation. The castle was burned by Irish rebels in 1598 during Tyrone's Rebellion; Spenser fled to London, where he died in poverty the following year and was buried near Geoffrey Chaucer in Westminster Abbey's Poets' Corner.

Works

The Shepheardes Calender (1579), a pastoral sequence of eclogues, made his reputation. The Faerie Queene, published in instalments in 1590 and 1596, was planned as twelve books, one for each virtue of a perfect gentleman; six books and part of a seventh were completed. Spenser invented the Spenserian stanza — nine lines of alternating iambic pentameter closing with an alexandrine — which later influenced Byron, Keats, Tennyson and many others. His prose tract A View of the Present State of Ireland (written c. 1596) advocated harsh measures against Irish resistance and remained unpublished until 1633.
