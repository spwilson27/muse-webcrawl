Amblyopsidae

The Amblyopsidae are a family of small freshwater fishes, commonly called cavefishes, blindfishes and swampfishes, in the order Percopsiformes. The family contains about seven species in five genera, all native to the eastern and central United States.

Characteristics

Members of the family show a gradient of adaptation to dark habitats. The surface-dwelling swampfish (Chologaster cornuta) of Atlantic coastal swamps retains functional eyes and pigment, while the spring cavefish (Forbesichthys agassizii) lives in springs and caves. The fully cave-dwelling species — the northern cavefish (Amblyopsis spelaea) of the Mammoth Cave region and the Ozark cavefish (Amblyopsis rosae) — are blind and unpigmented, with enlarged sensory papillae and lateral-line systems that compensate for the loss of sight. Peculiarly, amblyopsids have the anus positioned far forward on the throat (jugular position) in adults, and females brood eggs in their gill chambers.

Conservation

Several cave species have small ranges and are vulnerable to groundwater pollution and habitat disturbance; the Ozark cavefish is listed as threatened under US law, and others are of conservation concern. The family is a classic model for studying regressive evolution — the loss of eyes and pigment in darkness.
