The Congo Crisis (French: Crise congolaise) was a period of political upheaval and conflict between 1960 and 1965 that constituted the first major crisis of the newly independent Republic of the Congo, and a major episode of the Cold War in Africa. It began almost immediately after independence from Belgium on 30 June 1960, with an army mutiny, the secession of the mineral-rich province of Katanga under Moïse Tshombe (backed by Belgian interests), and interventions by Belgium, the United Nations, the United States, and the Soviet Union.

Course of the crisis

Prime Minister Patrice Lumumba appealed for UN help; the United Nations Operation in the Congo (ONUC) was deployed, but refused to suppress the Katanga secession. Lumumba's turn to Soviet aid precipitated his dismissal by President Kasa-Vubu and his arrest by army chief of staff Mobutu; Lumumba was murdered in Katanga in January 1961 with Belgian complicity, an act later also linked to CIA plotting. UN Secretary-General Dag Hammarskjöld died in a suspicious plane crash at Ndola in September 1961 while travelling to negotiate with Tshombe. UN forces ended the Katanga secession in January 1963. A leftist rebellion in the east (the Simba rebellion, 1964) was crushed with Belgian and U.S. assistance.

Aftermath

On 25 November 1965, Lieutenant-General Joseph-Désiré Mobutu seized power in a coup, ending the crisis and beginning his 32-year rule over the country he renamed Zaire.
