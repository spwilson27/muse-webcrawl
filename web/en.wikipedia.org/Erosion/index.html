Erosion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Erosion is the action of surface processes — such as flowing water, wind, ice, or gravity — that remove soil, rock, or dissolved material from one location on the Earth's crust and transport it to another location where it is deposited. Erosion is distinct from weathering, which breaks rock down in place without movement, though the two processes usually act together.

Agents of erosion

  • Water erosion is the most important agent in most landscapes. It ranges from the splash of raindrops and thin sheet wash, through rill and gully erosion on slopes, to the cutting of river valleys and the retreat of sea cliffs by waves.
  • Wind erosion dominates in arid regions, deflating fine sediment and abrading rock surfaces; it produced the dust storms of the 1930s American Dust Bowl.
  • Glacial erosion by moving ice scours bedrock, plucking and abrading it to carve U-shaped valleys, fjords, and cirques.
  • Mass movement — landslides, creep, and rockfalls — moves material downslope under gravity.

Rates and human impact

Natural ("geological") erosion is generally slow, balancing soil formation over time. Human activities — deforestation, overgrazing, intensive cultivation, and construction — can accelerate soil erosion by one or two orders of magnitude, leading to loss of farmland, siltation of reservoirs, and water pollution. Global losses of topsoil are estimated in the tens of billions of tonnes per year. Countermeasures include contour ploughing, terracing, cover crops, shelter belts, and reforestation.

Landforms

Erosion sculpts many characteristic landforms: canyons and gorges, sea arches and stacks, badlands, hoodoos, yardangs, and glacial troughs. The Grand Canyon, carved by the Colorado River over millions of years, is among the largest products of erosion on Earth.
