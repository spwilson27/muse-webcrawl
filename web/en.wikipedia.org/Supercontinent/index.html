Supercontinent

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In geology, a supercontinent is the assembly of most or all of Earth's continental blocks or cratons to form a single large landmass. Supercontinents have assembled and dispersed repeatedly in Earth history, in a pattern known as the supercontinent cycle, driven by plate tectonics.

Known supercontinents

The most recent and best-known supercontinent is Pangaea, which existed during the late Paleozoic and early Mesozoic eras and began breaking apart about 200 million years ago into Laurasia and Gondwana. Earlier supercontinents reconstructed from geological and paleomagnetic evidence include Rodinia (about 1.1 billion years ago), Pannotia, Columbia (Nuna), Kenorland, and possibly Ur and Vaalbara in the Archaean.

The supercontinent cycle

Continents drift together along subduction zones and collide, welding into a supercontinent; over tens to hundreds of millions of years the landmass breaks up through rifting as new ocean basins open. The cycle takes roughly 300–500 million years and profoundly affects global climate, sea level, ocean circulation, and the evolution of life — supercontinent assembly is linked to icehouse climates and evolutionary radiations following break-up.

Future supercontinents

Models of future plate motion project a new supercontinent in about 200–300 million years. Scenarios include Pangaea Proxima (or Pangaea Ultima), formed by the closing of the Atlantic; Novopangaea; Aurica; and Amasia, in which the Americas merge with Asia around the North Pole.
