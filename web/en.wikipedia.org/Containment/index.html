Containment

Containment was a geopolitical strategic foreign policy pursued by the United States during the Cold War to prevent the spread of communism after the end of World War II. The name was loosely related to the term cordon sanitaire, the strategy of restricting the spread of a contagious ideology or disease. Containment represented a middle-ground position between detente and rollback.

Origins

The policy's intellectual foundation was the "Long Telegram" sent in February 1946 by American diplomat George F. Kennan from Moscow, and his anonymous 1947 Foreign Affairs article "The Sources of Soviet Conduct" (the "X Article"), which argued that Soviet expansionism should be met by "a policy of firm containment, designed to confront the Russians with unalterable counter-force at every point." President Harry S. Truman adopted the approach in the Truman Doctrine of March 1947, pledging support to peoples resisting subjugation.

Implementation

Containment underpinned the Marshall Plan for European recovery (1948), the creation of NATO (1949), and American interventions in the Korean War (1950–53) and Vietnam War. NSC-68 (1950) globalized and militarized the doctrine. Critics such as Walter Lippmann warned that containment would oblige the United States to defend indefensible positions worldwide, while later administrations debated whether Vietnam reflected the doctrine's logic or its distortion.

End and legacy

The policy framed American strategy, with modifications through détente and the Reagan Doctrine, until the dissolution of the Soviet Union in 1991. The term has since been applied by analogy to other states and challenges, and Kennan himself later protested against what he saw as the excessive militarization of his concept.
