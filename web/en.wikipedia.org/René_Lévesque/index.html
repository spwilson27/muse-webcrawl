René Lévesque (August 24, 1922 – November 1, 1987) was a Canadian journalist and politician. He founded the Parti Québécois and served as the 23rd premier of Quebec from 1976 to 1985. Lévesque was the central figure of the Quebec sovereignty movement and led the province's "Yes" side in the 1980 referendum on sovereignty-association.

Early life and broadcasting career

Born in Campbellton, New Brunswick, Lévesque was raised in New Carlisle, in Quebec's Gaspé Peninsula. He studied law at Université Laval but left before graduating to pursue journalism. During the Second World War he served as a war correspondent with the United States Army's liaison and broadcasting service, reporting from Europe. After the war he became one of Quebec's best-known broadcasters at Radio-Canada, notably as host of the public-affairs program Point de mire, and he played a prominent role in the 1959 producers' strike that became a milestone of the Quiet Revolution era.

Political career

Elected to the National Assembly in 1960 as a Liberal under Jean Lesage, Lévesque became minister of natural resources and led the nationalization of Quebec's private electricity companies, creating the modern Hydro-Québec in 1962–63 — summed up in the slogan "Maîtres chez nous" ("masters in our own house").

After the Liberal defeat in 1966, Lévesque concluded that Quebec's future lay in independence. His manifesto Option Québec (1968) proposed "sovereignty-association": a politically independent Quebec retaining an economic association with Canada. Expelled from the Liberal Party, he founded the Mouvement souveraineté-association, which merged into the Parti Québécois in 1968.

Premier of Quebec

The Parti Québécois won the election of November 15, 1976, making Lévesque premier. His government enacted the Charter of the French Language (Bill 101, 1977), which made French the language of instruction (with exceptions), government, and commerce; reformed election financing; and introduced no-fault automobile insurance. In the referendum of May 20, 1980, 59.6% of voters rejected sovereignty-association. Lévesque accepted the result with his famous words to supporters: "À la prochaine fois" — "until next time."

Re-elected in 1981, Lévesque clashed with Ottawa over the patriation of the Canadian Constitution in 1982, which Quebec never formally endorsed. Divided over strategy and exhausted, he resigned as party leader and premier in 1985.

Death and legacy

Lévesque died of a heart attack in Montreal in 1987. He is widely regarded as one of the most significant Quebec political figures of the twentieth century; Boulevard René-Lévesque in Montreal and numerous institutions bear his name.
