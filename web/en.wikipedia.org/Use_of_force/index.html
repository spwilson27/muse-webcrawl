Use of force

The use of force, in the context of law enforcement, is the amount of effort required by police to compel compliance by an unwilling subject. More broadly, the term describes the legally sanctioned application of physical coercion by state agents, including the military, under defined rules and limitations.

Use of force continuum

Many agencies organise force options along a continuum, from mere officer presence and verbal commands, through empty-hand control and chemical or conducted-energy devices, to impact weapons and finally lethal force. Officers are generally expected to use the least force reasonably necessary, escalating or de-escalating as the subject's behaviour changes.

Legal standards

In the United States, the constitutional standard derives from Graham v. Connor (1989), which requires force to be objectively reasonable in light of the severity of the crime, the immediate threat posed, and resistance or flight. International human rights law requires force to be lawful, necessary and proportionate, with lethal force reserved for protection against an imminent threat of death or serious injury.

Deadly force

Deadly force is force likely to cause death or serious bodily harm. It is typically authorised only to prevent death or serious injury to the officer or another person, or to stop a fleeing suspect who poses a significant threat. Policies on warning shots, shooting at vehicles, and chokeholds vary widely by jurisdiction.

Criticism and reform

High-profile deaths during police encounters have driven public debate about force statistics, racial disparities, body-worn cameras, duty-to-intervene rules and de-escalation training. In military contexts, the use of force is governed by the law of armed conflict and rules of engagement.
