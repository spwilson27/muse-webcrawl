Nanotechnology (often shortened to nanotech) is the manipulation of matter with at least one dimension sized from 1 to 100 nanometres (nm). At this scale, materials frequently display properties — optical, electrical, mechanical, and chemical — that differ markedly from their bulk behaviour, because of increased surface-area-to-volume ratios and quantum mechanical effects. Nanotechnology spans many fields, including surface science, materials science, molecular biology, semiconductor physics, energy storage, and medicine.

History

The conceptual origins of nanotechnology are often traced to Richard Feynman's 1959 lecture "There's Plenty of Room at the Bottom," which imagined manipulating individual atoms. The term "nanotechnology" was coined by Norio Taniguchi in 1974 and popularised by K. Eric Drexler, whose 1986 book Engines of Creation described molecular nanotechnology with self-replicating assemblers — a vision later debated (notably the "fat fingers / sticky fingers" exchange with Richard Smalley). Practical milestones included the invention of the scanning tunnelling microscope (1981), the discovery of fullerenes (1985), and the characterisation of carbon nanotubes (1991). In 2000 the United States launched the National Nanotechnology Initiative.

Materials and approaches

Nanomaterials are commonly built by two strategies: top-down methods that carve nanoscale structures from bulk material (as in lithography for microelectronics) and bottom-up methods that assemble structures atom by atom or molecule by molecule, including self-assembly and DNA nanotechnology. Notable nanomaterials include carbon nanotubes and graphene (exceptional strength and conductivity), quantum dots (size-tunable fluorescence, now used in displays and imaging), dendrimers, and metal nanoparticles such as gold and silver colloids.

Applications

  • Electronics: transistor features in modern processors are fabricated at nanometre scales.
  • Medicine: targeted drug delivery (e.g., liposomal formulations), diagnostics, and nanoparticle imaging agents.
  • Materials: nanocomposites, scratch-resistant and self-cleaning coatings, sunscreens using zinc oxide nanoparticles.
  • Energy: catalysts, battery electrodes, and photovoltaic materials.

Concerns

The possible toxicity of engineered nanoparticles — their ability to penetrate cells and persist in the environment — is an active research area, and regulators have debated whether existing chemical-safety frameworks suffice. Public discussion has also featured speculative scenarios such as Drexler's "grey goo," which most researchers consider implausible but which shaped early policy debate about the field.
