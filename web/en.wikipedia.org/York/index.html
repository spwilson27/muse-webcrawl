York is a historic cathedral city in North Yorkshire, England, at the confluence of the rivers Ouse and Foss. Founded by the Romans in 71 CE as Eboracum, it became a capital of Roman Britain, then the Viking city of Jorvik, and one of medieval England's greatest cities. Its preserved walls, Gothic minster and medieval streets make it a major tourist destination.

History

Constantine the Great was proclaimed emperor at York in 306 CE. After Roman rule the city passed to Anglian Northumbria — where Edwin was baptised in 627 — and in 867 was captured by the Great Heathen Army, becoming the Viking trading city of Jorvik. In the Middle Ages York was England's second city, a wool and ecclesiastical centre, seat of an archbishop, and namesake of the House of York in the Wars of the Roses. Later it became a railway and confectionery centre (Rowntree's, Terry's).

Landmarks

York Minster, one of the largest Gothic cathedrals in northern Europe, dominates the skyline. The Shambles, a medieval butchers' street, the largely complete circuit of city walls, Clifford's Tower and the National Railway Museum draw millions of visitors. The Jorvik Viking Centre recreates the excavated Viking settlement at Coppergate.

Modern city

Today York's economy centres on tourism, education (the University of York, founded 1963), railways and services. The city gives its name to New York and to Toronto's earlier name.
