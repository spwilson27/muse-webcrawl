Wolfpack (naval tactic)

A wolfpack (German: Rudeltaktik, "pack tactic") is a coordinated submarine attack tactic in which a group of submarines converges on a convoy and attacks together, typically at night on the surface, overwhelming the escorts. It was developed by Germany's U-boat arm and used extensively in the Battle of the Atlantic during World War II.

Used byGerman Kriegsmarine (U-boats); adapted by the U.S. Navy in the Pacific
Key figureKarl Dönitz
Principal conflictWorld War II

Development

Karl Dönitz, commander of the U-boat force, had proposed massed attacks on convoys during World War I and formalized the idea between the wars. U-boats spread out in a patrol line across expected convoy routes; the first to sight the enemy shadowed it and radioed position reports so headquarters could vector the rest of the pack in.

Operations

Wolfpacks inflicted devastating losses in 1940–43, especially where air cover was absent in the mid-Atlantic "air gap." Famous pack attacks struck convoys SC 7 and HX 79 in October 1940. The tactic depended on radio communication — which Allied codebreakers exploited through Ultra decrypts of Enigma — and on surface running at night, which became suicidal once escort radar, long-range aircraft, escort carriers, and hunter-killer groups matured. By "Black May" 1943 the packs were defeated. The United States Navy employed its own coordinated attack groups, also called wolfpacks, against Japanese shipping in the Pacific.

Legacy

The wolfpack remains the classic example of concentrating dispersed forces by radio control, and the term is now applied metaphorically to coordinated group attacks in sport, business, and other navies' submarine doctrine.
