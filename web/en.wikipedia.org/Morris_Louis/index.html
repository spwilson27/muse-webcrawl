Morris Louis Bernstein (November 28, 1912 – September 7, 1962), known professionally as Morris Louis, was an American painter. During the 1950s he became one of the earliest exponents of Color Field painting. While living in Washington, D.C., Louis, along with Kenneth Noland and other Washington painters, formed an art movement known as the Washington Color School.

Career

Louis studied at the Maryland Institute College of Art and worked on the Federal Art Project during the Depression. In 1953 he visited New York with Kenneth Noland and saw Helen Frankenthaler's stain paintings, an encounter that proved decisive. Working mainly with Magna, a thinned oil-based acrylic resin paint, Louis poured pigment down unprimed canvas so that color soaked into the support rather than sitting on it.

Major series

His best-known works are the Veils (1954, and 1958–59), in which overlapping curtains of translucent color flow down the canvas; the monumental Unfurleds (1960–61), where streams of color run along the sides of largely bare canvases; and the late Stripes (1961–62). The art critic Clement Greenberg championed Louis's work and brought it to wide attention.

Louis died of lung cancer in 1962, aged 49. A major retrospective was organized by the Museum of Modern Art in 1986–87. His works are held by major museums including the National Gallery of Art, MoMA and Tate.
