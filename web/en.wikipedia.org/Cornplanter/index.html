Cornplanter

Cornplanter (c. 1736 – February 18, 1836), also known by his Seneca name Gaiänt'wakê and his Dutch given name John Abeel, was a Seneca war chief and diplomat of the Iroquois Confederacy. Of mixed Seneca and Dutch ancestry — his father was the Albany trader John Abeel and his mother a Seneca woman of the Wolf clan — he rose to prominence during the era of the French and Indian War and the American Revolution.

Life

Cornplanter fought on the British side during the Revolutionary War, taking part in campaigns including the Wyoming Valley expedition of 1778, and his warriors were among those defeated in the Sullivan Expedition's retaliatory devastation of Iroquois towns in 1779. In a curious incident of 1780, his party captured his own father, whom Cornplanter recognised and released with gifts and an apology.

After the war he became the principal intermediary between the Seneca and the new United States, negotiating at Fort Stanwix (1784) and later visiting Philadelphia and meeting George Washington in 1790 to protest land frauds. Pennsylvania granted him a tract of land on the Allegheny River — the Cornplanter Tract — where he settled with his followers. He was the elder half-brother of the prophet Handsome Lake, whose religious revival he initially resisted but later tolerated.

Legacy

Cornplanter died in 1836, reportedly around one hundred years old, reputedly the last surviving war chief of the Revolution era. The Cornplanter Tract remained in his descendants' hands until the 1960s, when its flooding by the Kinzua Dam — in breach, Seneca argued, of treaty guarantees — forced the relocation of his grave and monument. The flooding of the Cornplanter grant became a symbol of broken federal promises to Native nations.
