The Torino scale is a method for categorizing the impact hazard associated with near-Earth objects (NEOs) such as asteroids and comets. It is intended to allow a combination of impact probability and kinetic energy to be communicated to the public as a single integer ranging from 0 to 10, analogous to the Richter scale for earthquakes. An object is assigned a value based on its collision probability and the kinetic energy (expressed in megatons of TNT) of the possible collision.

Overview

The scale was created by Professor Richard P. Binzel of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. A preliminary version was presented in 1995 and the scale was adopted by the International Astronomical Union in 1999 at a conference held in Turin (Torino), Italy — hence its name. Its colour-coded zones group the values into five categories: white (0, no hazard), green (1, normal), yellow (2–4, meriting attention by astronomers), orange (5–7, threatening), and red (8–10, certain collisions with consequences ranging from localised destruction to global climatic catastrophe).

Scale values

A value of 0 indicates an object has a negligibly small chance of collision, or is too small to reach the surface intact. A value of 1 indicates a routine discovery whose predicted pass near Earth poses no unusual level of danger. Values 2 to 4 describe discoveries meriting attention by astronomers but not yet public concern; 5 to 7 describe close encounters posing serious but still uncertain threats; and 8 to 10 describe certain collisions, with 10 reserved for an impact capable of causing a global climatic catastrophe.

Notable ratings

No object has ever retained a high rating after follow-up observations. The asteroid 99942 Apophis briefly reached level 4 in December 2004 — the highest rating ever assigned — when early orbit solutions gave it a significant probability of impact in 2029; further observations ruled out an impact in 2029 and reduced its rating to 0. The asteroid (144898) 2004 VD17 and 1950 DA have also held ratings of 1–2 at times. The Torino scale complements the more technical Palermo Technical Impact Hazard Scale used by specialists.
