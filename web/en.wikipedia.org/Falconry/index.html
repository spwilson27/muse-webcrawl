Falconry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Falconry is the hunting of wild animals in their natural state and habitat by means of a trained bird of prey. Small animals are hunted; squirrels and rabbits often fall prey to these birds. Two traditional terms are used to describe a person involved in falconry: a "falconer" flies a falcon; an "austringer" (of French origin) flies a hawk (Accipiter and some buteos, similar to a "hawker"). In modern falconry, the red-tailed hawk and the Harris's hawk are often used.

Falconry is practised in many countries worldwide and has a history of some 4,000 years, with origins often traced to Mesopotamia and the Eurasian Steppe. In 2010, UNESCO inscribed falconry on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, in one of the largest multinational nominations in the list's history.

History

Falconry spread along trade routes from Central Asia to China, Korea, Persia, the Middle East and Europe. It flourished at medieval courts: the Holy Roman Emperor Frederick II wrote De arte venandi cum avibus ("On the Art of Hunting with Birds"), one of the most famous treatises on the subject. In Britain, strict social codes assigned particular species to particular ranks, from the gyrfalcon for a king down to the kestrel for a servant. The sport declined with the spread of firearms but revived in the 20th century as a regulated field sport and a tool of raptor conservation.

Practice

Birds used include falcons (flown at quarry in open country and stooping from height), short-winged hawks such as goshawks (flown in woodland), broad-winged hawks and eagles. Equipment includes jesses, leashes, bells, telemetry transmitters, hoods and the falconer's glove. Training proceeds through manning (accustoming the bird to people), creance work, and free flight, with the bird's weight carefully managed to keep it keen but healthy. In most countries falconry requires licences, and captive-bred birds are now the norm; falconry techniques also underpin raptor rehabilitation and the use of raptors for bird control at airports and vineyards.
