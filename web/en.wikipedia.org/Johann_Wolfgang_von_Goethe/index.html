Johann Wolfgang von Goethe

Johann Wolfgang von Goethe (28 August 1749 – 22 March 1832) was a German polymath, poet, playwright, novelist, scientist and statesman, regarded as the greatest figure of German literature. His works include the epistolary novel The Sorrows of Young Werther, the Bildungsroman Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship, and the two-part poetic drama Faust, on which he worked for most of his life.

Life

Goethe was born in Frankfurt into a prosperous family and studied law at Leipzig and Strasbourg, where Herder introduced him to Shakespeare and folk poetry. The drama Götz von Berlichingen (1773) and Werther (1774) made him famous across Europe as a leader of the Sturm und Drang. In 1775 the young Duke Carl August invited him to Weimar, where he spent the rest of his life as minister, court official and director of the court theatre. His journey to Italy (1786–88) transformed his art towards classical forms; his friendship with Schiller from 1794 shaped Weimar Classicism.

Works

Faust, begun in the 1770s, published in Part One in 1808 and completed only in 1831, retells the legend of the scholar who sells his soul to Mephistopheles and is generally considered the greatest work of German literature. Goethe's lyric poetry, the West–Eastern Divan (1819), the autobiography Poetry and Truth, and the Maxims and Reflections display the range of a mind that also pursued science: works on plant morphology, anatomy (he helped establish the existence of the intermaxillary bone in humans) and a controversial Theory of Colours (1810) directed against Newton.

Legacy

Goethe's influence on German and European culture is immense; the Goethe-Institut, Germany's cultural institute, bears his name. He died in Weimar in 1832; his reported last words, "Mehr Licht!" ("More light!"), became legendary.
