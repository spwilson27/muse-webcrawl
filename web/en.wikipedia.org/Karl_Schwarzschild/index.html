Karl Schwarzschild

Karl Schwarzschild (9 October 1873 – 11 May 1916) was a German physicist and astronomer, a leading figure in theoretical astrophysics. He is remembered above all for finding, in 1916, the first exact solution to Einstein's field equations of general relativity — the Schwarzschild metric describing spacetime around a spherical, non-rotating mass — which laid the mathematical foundation for the theory of black holes.

Career

A child prodigy who published papers on celestial mechanics at sixteen, Schwarzschild studied in Strasbourg and Munich, directed the Göttingen Observatory from 1901, and from 1909 the Astrophysical Observatory at Potsdam. His wide-ranging work covered stellar motions and structure, photographic photometry, radiative transfer (the Schwarzschild–Milne equation), quantum theory and the theory of atomic spectra.

The Schwarzschild solution

Serving as an artillery officer on the Russian front in World War I, Schwarzschild read Einstein's November 1915 paper and within weeks derived the exact exterior solution, writing to Einstein that he had not expected an exact solution to be formulable so simply. The solution contains the Schwarzschild radius — the size of the event horizon of a non-rotating black hole. He also derived the interior solution for a constant-density star. Contracting an autoimmune skin disease (pemphigus) at the front, he died in Potsdam in May 1916, aged 42.

Legacy

His name attaches to the Schwarzschild metric, radius, coordinates and black hole. His son Martin Schwarzschild became a prominent astrophysicist at Princeton, and the Karl Schwarzschild Observatory in Germany and a lunar crater commemorate him.
