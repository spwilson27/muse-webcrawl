Insider trading

Insider trading is the trading of a public company's stock or other securities (such as bonds or stock options) based on material, nonpublic information about the company. In many countries, trading based on insider information is illegal, as it is seen as unfair to other investors who do not have access to the information.

Legal framework

In the United States, insider trading rules are enforced by the Securities and Exchange Commission under the Securities Exchange Act of 1934 and rules such as Rule 10b-5. Corporate officers, directors and major shareholders — "insiders" — must disclose their trades, and trading on material nonpublic information can lead to civil and criminal penalties. The classical theory holds insiders liable because of their fiduciary duty to shareholders; the misappropriation theory extends liability to outsiders who wrongfully obtain such information.

Debate

Some economists and legal scholars have defended insider trading, arguing that it helps prices reflect information more quickly and efficiently. Critics respond that it undermines confidence in the fairness of markets and can discourage outside investment.

Notable cases

High-profile prosecutions have included Ivan Boesky and Michael Milken in the 1980s, Martha Stewart's 2004 conviction for obstruction connected to an insider trading investigation, and the 2011 conviction of hedge fund manager Raj Rajaratnam.
