Proton-exchange membrane fuel cell

A PEM fuel cell combines hydrogen and oxygen to produce electricity and water.

Proton-exchange membrane fuel cells (PEMFC), also known as polymer electrolyte membrane fuel cells, are a type of fuel cell that converts the chemical energy of hydrogen (or another fuel) directly into electricity. They are distinguished by a solid polymer electrolyte membrane — commonly Nafion — that conducts protons from anode to cathode while insulating electrically and separating the reactant gases.

Operation

At the anode, hydrogen is split by a platinum catalyst into protons and electrons. The protons pass through the membrane; the electrons travel through an external circuit, delivering power, and recombine with protons and oxygen at the cathode to form water. The overall reaction is 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O. PEM cells operate at relatively low temperatures, typically 60–80 °C, which allows quick start-up but makes waste-heat recovery modest and the catalyst sensitive to carbon monoxide poisoning.

Applications

PEM fuel cells were first used in NASA's Gemini space programme in the 1960s. Their high power density and low operating temperature suit them to vehicles — cars such as the Toyota Mirai and Hyundai Nexo, buses and forklifts — and to portable and backup power. Challenges include the cost of platinum catalysts, hydrogen storage and distribution infrastructure, and durability over thousands of operating hours.
