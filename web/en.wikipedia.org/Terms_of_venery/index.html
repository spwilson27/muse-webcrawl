Terms of venery

Terms of venery are the collective nouns and specialised expressions used for groups of animals, originating in the hunting vocabulary of late-medieval England. The tradition is associated above all with The Book of Saint Albans (1486), attributed to Dame Juliana Berners, which lists "terms of association" — a pride of lions, a gaggle of geese, a murder of crows — as part of the knowledge expected of a gentleman. Many listed terms were playful or satirical coinages (a "superfluity of nuns") that were probably never used in actual hunting speech.

Origins

Venery derives from Old French venerie (hunting), itself from Latin venari, to hunt. In the 15th century, hunting at the great courts of Burgundy, France, and England was a ritualised art whose technical jargon signalled gentility; Edward of Norwich's The Master of Game (c. 1413) is an earlier English source. The Book of Saint Albans extended the system beyond game animals to birds, fish, and even professions, and its lists were copied, expanded, and joked about for centuries.

Famous examples

Well-known terms still in everyday use include a school of fish, a flock of sheep, a pack of wolves or dogs, a herd of cattle, and a swarm of bees. The more picturesque survivors in popular usage include a parliament of owls (popularised by C. S. Lewis), an ostentation of peacocks, a tower of giraffes, a bloat of hippopotamuses, and a crash of rhinoceroses. Linguists point out that some modern favourites are recent inventions: many "traditional" terms were coined or revived in the 20th century, spurred by James Lipton's best-selling 1968 compilation An Exaltation of Larks (itself an authentic medieval term, for larks).

Modern play

The genre continues as a parlour game: modern coinages such as "a click of photographers" or "an absence of professors" imitate the medieval pattern. Because the original lists mixed genuine hunting usage with wit, reference works treat individual terms with caution, but the tradition remains the principal source of English collective nouns for animals.
