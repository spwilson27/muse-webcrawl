A hinny is a domestic equine hybrid, the offspring of a male horse (a stallion) and a female donkey (a jenny). It is the reciprocal cross of the more common mule, which is sired by a male donkey (a jack) out of a female horse (a mare).

Why hinnies are rarer

Hinnies are harder to produce than mules. Stallions and jennies are less willing partners than jacks and mares, conception rates are lower, and the resulting foals tend to be smaller because a donkey's uterus is smaller than a mare's. Breeders seeking a large working hybrid therefore favour the mule cross.

Characteristics

Hinnies resemble mules in most respects — long ears, a short mane, slender limbs and hard hooves — but often look slightly more horse-like in the head and tend to be somewhat smaller and quieter than mules of comparable breeding. Like mules they are sure-footed, hardy, long-lived and resistant to many equine diseases, and they have been used as pack and draught animals.

Fertility

Horses have 64 chromosomes and donkeys 62; the hinny has 63, an odd number that cannot pair evenly in meiosis. Hinnies are therefore almost always sterile, a classic illustration of hybrid incompatibility between species. A handful of verified fertile female hinnies and mules are documented, but no fertile male has ever been confirmed.
