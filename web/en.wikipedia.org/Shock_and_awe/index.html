Shock and awe

Shock and awe (technically known as rapid dominance) is a military doctrine based on the use of overwhelming power, dominant battlefield awareness and spectacular displays of force to paralyse the enemy's perception of the battlefield and destroy its will to fight. The term became famous during the 2003 invasion of Iraq, when US officials used it to describe the planned opening air campaign over Baghdad.

The doctrine

The doctrine was set out in a 1996 study by a team led by Harlan Ullman and James Wade at the National Defense University in Washington. Drawing on Sun Tzu's dictum that the supreme art of war is to subdue the enemy without fighting, the authors proposed controlling the whole "environment of conflict" — through near-total knowledge of one's own and the enemy's forces, rapidity and timeliness, and the "operational brilliance" of force application — so that the adversary is rendered powerless and unwilling to resist. Historical touchstones cited include the Roman legions' aura, the Mongol campaigns, the German Blitzkrieg, and, at the extreme, the psychological effect of the atomic bombings of Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

Iraq 2003

In the run-up to the Iraq War, US Defense Secretary Donald Rumsfeld and CENTCOM planners spoke of applying "shock and awe" to Baghdad. On the night of 21–22 March 2003, coalition aircraft and cruise missiles struck hundreds of targets across the Iraqi capital in a televised bombardment intended to shatter the regime's command structure and morale. A planned much larger strike package (the "Nixon option" of thousands of cruise missiles) was scaled back. Critics argued that the actual campaign was far short of the doctrine's stated mass, that the regime's forces largely melted away to fight a guerrilla war the doctrine had not addressed, and that "shock and awe" became a propaganda label rather than a decisive method: Baghdad fell on 9 April 2003 to armoured ground advance, while the insurgency that followed showed the limits of psychological paralysis as a war-winning strategy.

Reception

The phrase entered popular culture, spawning books, headlines and parodies, and is still used loosely for any sudden overwhelming attack or display. Military analysts remain divided on whether rapid dominance is a distinct doctrine or a rebranding of older ideas about morale, manoeuvre and the indirect approach.
