Open-field system

The open-field system was the prevalent agricultural system in much of Europe from the Middle Ages until the 20th century. Under it, each manor or village had two or three large open fields divided into numerous narrow strips of land, farmed by individual peasants but subject to communal decisions about cropping and grazing.

Organisation

A peasant's holding was scattered as strips (often grouped in furlongs and measured in acres and roods) across the different fields, so that each family held a mix of good and poor soil. In the classic three-field system, one field grew autumn-sown wheat or rye, one grew spring crops such as barley, oats or legumes, and one lay fallow each year in rotation. After harvest, and on the fallow, the community's livestock grazed in common on the stubble. Decisions were made collectively and enforced by manorial courts and village bylaws; common land, meadow and waste provided hay, pasture and fuel.

Decline and enclosure

The system suited heavy ploughs and shared ox teams but made individual innovation difficult. It was gradually replaced by consolidated, hedged farms through enclosure — first by agreement and later, in England, by thousands of parliamentary Inclosure Acts between about 1750 and 1850. Open-field systems survived longer in parts of France, Germany, Russia (the obshchina) and Scandinavia; a few open-field villages, such as Laxton in Nottinghamshire, still preserve the system today.
