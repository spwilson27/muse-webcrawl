Suture (joint)

The major sutures of the human skull seen from above.

In anatomy, a suture is a fairly rigid joint between two or more hard elements of an organism, with or without significant overlap of the elements. The best-known examples are the fibrous joints between the flat bones of the skull (cranium). Suture lines are also found in the shells of animals such as ammonites and turtles, and between the plates of some arthropod exoskeletons.

Cranial sutures

The bones of the human skull are joined by sutures of dense fibrous connective tissue. The principal named sutures are:

  • Coronal suture — between the frontal bone and the two parietal bones.
  • Sagittal suture — between the two parietal bones along the midline.
  • Lambdoid suture — between the parietal bones and the occipital bone.
  • Squamosal suture — between the parietal and temporal bones.

In infancy the sutures are wide and the fontanelles remain open, allowing the skull to deform during childbirth and to grow with the brain. With age the sutures interlock increasingly and may ossify (synostosis). Premature fusion of one or more sutures causes craniosynostosis, altering skull shape.

Function and growth

Sutures are growth sites for the skull: new bone is deposited at the suture margins as the brain expands. They also absorb and distribute mechanical stresses. Forensic anthropologists use the progressive closure of sutures as a rough indicator of age at death, though the correlation is weak.

In other organisms

In ammonites, the suture pattern where internal septa meet the shell wall — ranging from simple (goniatitic) to highly frilled (ammonitic) — is a key taxonomic character. In turtles, sutures join the bony scutes' underlying plates of the carapace and plastron.
