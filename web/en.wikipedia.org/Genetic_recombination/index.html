Genetic recombination

Genetic recombination (also known as genetic reshuffling) is the exchange of genetic material between different organisms which leads to production of offspring with combinations of traits that differ from those found in either parent. In eukaryotes, genetic recombination during meiosis can lead to a novel set of genetic information that can be further passed on from parents to offspring.

Meiotic recombination

During meiosis in eukaryotes, homologous chromosomes pair and exchange segments in a process called chromosomal crossover. Recombination is initiated by programmed double-strand breaks created by the enzyme Spo11, which are repaired using the homologous chromosome as a template. Repair can yield either crossover products, which reciprocally exchange chromosome arms, or non-crossover products accompanied by short tracts of gene conversion. In most organisms at least one crossover per chromosome pair is required for proper segregation.

Types

Homologous recombination occurs between sequences of high similarity and underpins DNA repair as well as meiosis. Site-specific recombination, by contrast, is mediated by dedicated recombinases acting at specific sequences, as in the integration of bacteriophage lambda into the bacterial chromosome and in V(D)J recombination of the immune system. Illegitimate or non-homologous recombination occurs between dissimilar sequences and can produce translocations and other rearrangements.

Recombination frequency and mapping

The frequency with which two loci recombine is proportional to the distance between them, a relationship exploited by Sturtevant and Morgan to construct the first genetic maps. One percent recombination corresponds to one centimorgan. Recombination is not uniformly distributed: most events concentrate in narrow hotspots, whose positions in mammals are largely determined by the zinc-finger protein PRDM9.

In pathogens and technology

Recombination shuffles variation in viruses and bacteria, contributing to antigenic change — for example, antigenic shift in influenza. In the laboratory, homologous recombination is the basis of gene targeting in model organisms and of many genome engineering strategies.
