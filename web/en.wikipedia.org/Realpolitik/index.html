Realpolitik

Realpolitik (from German Realpolitik, "realistic politics") is the approach to politics and diplomacy based primarily on considerations of power, practical circumstances, and material interests, rather than on explicit ideological, moral, or ethical premises. It holds that political action should be judged by its results in advancing the interests of the state or actor pursuing it.

Origins

The term was coined by the German writer and politician August Ludwig von Rochau, whose 1853 treatise Grundsätze der Realpolitik ("Principles of Realpolitik") argued that the law of power governs states as the law of gravity governs the physical world. In practice the approach is much older: it is associated with Machiavelli's The Prince, Cardinal Richelieu's raison d'état, and the diplomacy of the balance of power.

Practice

Realpolitik is most famously associated with Otto von Bismarck, whose flexible alliances and limited wars unified Germany (1864–1871) and who thereafter built a shifting alliance system to preserve the peace. In the 20th century, the term was applied to the diplomacy of Henry Kissinger, including the opening to China and détente with the Soviet Union, and more broadly to the realist school of international relations theory associated with Hans Morgenthau. Critics charge that Realpolitik excuses the sacrifice of principles and human rights to expediency; proponents reply that it recognises the world as it is and can limit conflict by avoiding crusading wars.
