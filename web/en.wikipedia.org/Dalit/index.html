Dalit

Dalit (from Sanskrit dalita, "broken" or "scattered") is a name for people belonging to the lowest stratum castes in the traditional caste system of India, historically regarded as "untouchables" and placed outside the four varnas. Dalits themselves chose the term as an assertive political identity; it was popularized in the 1970s by the Dalit Panthers movement, echoing the Black Panthers in the United States.

History and social position

Communities now called Dalit were traditionally assigned occupations considered ritually polluting, such as leather work, sanitation work, and removal of carcasses, and faced severe social exclusion, including restrictions on temple entry, water sources, and education. Reformers and leaders such as Jyotirao Phule, and above all B. R. Ambedkar — himself born into a Dalit community — campaigned against caste discrimination. Ambedkar, as the principal architect of the Indian Constitution, secured the abolition of untouchability under Article 17 and a system of reservations (affirmative action quotas) in legislatures, government jobs, and education for Scheduled Castes, the official designation for most Dalit communities.

Demographics and politics

Dalits number roughly 200 million in India, about 16–17 percent of the population, and Dalit communities also exist in Nepal, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and Sri Lanka, as well as in the South Asian diaspora. Dalit political parties and leaders, such as Kanshi Ram and Mayawati of the Bahujan Samaj Party, have played a major role in modern Indian politics. Despite legal protections, caste-based discrimination and violence remain subjects of ongoing debate and activism.
