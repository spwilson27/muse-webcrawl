German student movement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The German student movement (German: Deutsche Studentenbewegung), sometimes called the 1968 movement in West Germany, was a protest movement that took place during the late 1960s in West Germany. It was largely a reaction against the perceived authoritarianism and hypocrisy of the West German government and other Western governments, and the poor living conditions of students. The movement is closely associated with the Socialist German Student Union (SDS) and its most prominent spokesman, Rudi Dutschke.

Background

West Germany in the 1960s was governed by politicians many of whom had served under the Nazi regime; Chancellor Kurt Georg Kiesinger, who led the grand coalition of CDU and SPD from 1966, had been a member of the Nazi Party. The younger generation accused their parents' generation of silence about the crimes of the Third Reich. The grand coalition left the country with almost no parliamentary opposition, fuelling the growth of an "extra-parliamentary opposition" (Außerparlamentarische Opposition, APO). Students also protested against the Vietnam War, the Shah of Iran's regime, overcrowded universities, and the emergency laws (Notstandsgesetze) passed in 1968, which many feared echoed the Enabling Act of 1933.

Key events

Death of Benno Ohnesorg

On 2 June 1967, during demonstrations against a visit by Shah Mohammad Reza Pahlavi to West Berlin, the unarmed student Benno Ohnesorg was shot and killed by police officer Karl-Heinz Kurras. The shooting radicalised the movement; Ohnesorg's death is often regarded as the turning point that transformed protest into a broader confrontation with the state.

Attempt on Rudi Dutschke

On 11 April 1968, Dutschke was shot and seriously wounded by Josef Bachmann, a young labourer influenced by anti-communist propaganda. Students blamed the Springer press empire, whose newspapers such as Bild had campaigned against the movement, and the ensuing Easter riots were among the most violent disturbances in postwar West German history. Dutschke never fully recovered and died in 1979 from the long-term effects of his injuries.

Decline and legacy

After 1969 the movement fragmented. The SDS dissolved itself in 1970. Some activists pursued Dutschke's strategy of a "long march through the institutions," entering professions and politics; many later joined the Green Party, founded in 1980. A small minority turned to armed struggle, forming groups such as the Red Army Faction and the 2 June Movement, whose terrorism overshadowed the movement's legacy in the 1970s.

Historians credit the student movement with liberalising West German society: it forced a public confrontation with the Nazi past, transformed education and family life, and contributed to the feminist, environmental, and peace movements of the following decades.
