The Third Wave: Democratization in the Late Twentieth Century

The Third Wave: Democratization in the Late Twentieth Century is a 1991 book by American political scientist Samuel P. Huntington. It analyses the wave of transitions from authoritarian rule to democracy that swept more than sixty countries between 1974 and 1990, beginning with Portugal's Carnation Revolution and extending through Latin America, East Asia, and eventually Eastern Europe.

Argument

Huntington frames democratisation as occurring in historical waves, each followed by a partial reverse wave: a first long wave from the 1820s to 1926, a second after the Second World War, and the "third wave" after 1974. He examines the causes of the third wave — the legitimacy crisis of authoritarian regimes, economic growth, the Catholic Church's shift toward reform, changes in US and European policy, and demonstration ("snowballing") effects — and considers how new democracies might be consolidated, warning that a third reverse wave remained possible.

Influence

The book became one of the most influential works in comparative politics, popularising the vocabulary of democratization "waves" and shaping debates about transitions, consolidation, and democratic backsliding for decades.
