Anamorphic widescreen

Anamorphic widescreen is a technique for storing and displaying widescreen pictures within a standard 4:3 aspect-ratio frame by horizontally compressing ("squeezing") the image. On playback, a widescreen television expands the image back to its correct proportions, while a standard 4:3 set displays it letterboxed or distorted depending on the player settings.

On DVD and broadcasting

The format became common with DVD-Video, whose frame is 720×480 (NTSC) or 720×576 (PAL). An anamorphically encoded 16:9 film uses the full resolution of that frame rather than wasting lines on black bars, then relies on aspect-ratio flags to tell the player to stretch the picture horizontally on output. Properly made anamorphic DVDs therefore contain noticeably more vertical detail than letterboxed non-anamorphic transfers of the same film. Anamorphic encoding was also used in widescreen standard-definition broadcasting and in formats such as DV.

Relation to anamorphic lenses

The term derives from anamorphic cinematography (e.g. CinemaScope), in which special lenses squeeze a wide image onto 35 mm film. The home-video usage is analogous but purely a matter of image scaling and aspect-ratio signaling; no special lens is involved.
