Jump cut

A jump cut is a cut in film editing in which a single continuous sequential shot of a subject is cut into two parts, with a piece of footage removed in order to render a jump in time or the subject's position. Camera positions slightly change in relation to the subject in an attempt to replicate the effect of a jump in the film. This kind of cut abruptly communicates the passing of time, as opposed to the more seamless dissolve heavily favoured in Hollywood in the 1930s and 1940s.

History

An early use occurs in Georges Méliès's trick films, where stopping and restarting the camera made objects appear or vanish. For mainstream cinema, jump cuts long violated the conventions of continuity editing, such as the 30-degree rule. They were famously embraced by Jean-Luc Godard in Breathless (1960), where cuts within continuous shots created a jarring, energetic rhythm that became a hallmark of the French New Wave.

Modern use

Jump cuts are now used to indicate the passage of time within a scene, to create a frenetic pace, or for comic effect, and they have become a standard stylistic device in online video, where creators routinely cut out pauses and mistakes within a single take, making the speaker appear to jump forward in time.
