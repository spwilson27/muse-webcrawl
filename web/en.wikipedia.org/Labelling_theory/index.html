Labelling theory

Labelling theory examines how social reactions create and reinforce deviance

Labelling theory (also spelled labeling theory) is a theory in sociology and criminology concerned with how the self-identity and behaviour of individuals may be determined or influenced by the terms used to describe or classify them. It is associated with the concepts of self-fulfilling prophecy and stereotyping, and holds that deviance is not an intrinsic quality of an act but a consequence of the social reaction to it: an act becomes deviant when others label it as such.

Origins

The theory developed within symbolic interactionism. George Herbert Mead and Charles Horton Cooley laid its foundations by arguing that the self emerges from social interaction and from imagining how one appears to others. Frank Tannenbaum, in Crime and the Community (1938), described the "dramatization of evil", by which a young offender, once tagged as delinquent, comes to be treated — and to see himself — as a criminal. Edwin Lemert distinguished primary deviance (rule-breaking with little effect on identity) from secondary deviance, which follows from societal reaction and reorganises a person's life around the deviant role.

Howard Becker

Howard S. Becker's Outsiders (1963) gave the theory its classic statement: "deviance is not a quality of the act the person commits, but rather a consequence of the application by others of rules and sanctions to an 'offender'. The deviant is one to whom that label has successfully been applied." Becker emphasised the role of "moral entrepreneurs" who create and enforce rules, and studied groups such as marijuana users and dance musicians to show how deviant careers develop through stages of labelling and exclusion.

Applications and criticism

Labelling theory influenced debates over juvenile justice, mental illness (notably Thomas Scheff's work on the labelling of the mentally ill), education, and the decriminalisation of minor offences. Critics argue that it neglects the causes of primary deviance, overstates the passivity of labelled people, and lacks strong empirical support for the claim that labelling increases later offending; nonetheless, concepts such as stigma, master status and deviancy amplification remain central to the sociology of deviance.
