Interfaith dialogue

Interfaith dialogue, also called interreligious dialogue, refers to cooperative, constructive and positive interaction between people of different religious traditions (i.e. "faiths") and/or spiritual or humanistic beliefs, at both the individual and institutional levels. Distinct from syncretism or alternative religion, it aims to promote understanding between different religions in order to increase acceptance of others, rather than to synthesize new beliefs.

History

While encounters between traditions are ancient — from the dialogues of ancient India to the court of Akbar the Great — the modern interfaith movement is usually dated to the Parliament of the World's Religions, held in Chicago in 1893 alongside the World's Columbian Exposition, where Swami Vivekananda's addresses made a lasting impression. A second Parliament was held in 1993, and the event now recurs periodically. Milestones of the twentieth century include the founding of the World Congress of Faiths (1936), the Second Vatican Council's declaration Nostra aetate (1965), which transformed Roman Catholic engagement with Judaism and other faiths, and the growth of the World Conference of Religions for Peace (founded 1970).

Forms

Scholars commonly distinguish the dialogue of life (everyday coexistence), the dialogue of action (joint work for peace and justice), the dialogue of theological exchange, and the dialogue of religious experience (shared prayer and contemplation). Dialogue may be bilateral — such as Jewish–Christian or Muslim–Christian dialogue — or multilateral through bodies like the World Council of Churches, the Pontifical Council for Interreligious Dialogue, and KAICIID.

Debates

Proponents argue dialogue reduces conflict and prejudice; critics within some traditions worry it relativizes truth claims or masks proselytism. The 2007 open letter A Common Word Between Us and You, signed by 138 Muslim scholars and addressed to Christian leaders, is a notable recent initiative proposing common ground in the love of God and neighbour.
