The Falkland Islands (Spanish: Islas Malvinas) are an archipelago in the South Atlantic Ocean on the Patagonian Shelf, about 480 km east of Argentina's Atlantic coast. The principal islands are East Falkland and West Falkland, separated by Falkland Sound, with 776 smaller islands. The capital and largest settlement is Stanley on East Falkland. The islands are a self-governing British Overseas Territory; the United Kingdom is responsible for defence and foreign affairs.

History

Uninhabited when Europeans first sighted them in the 16th–17th centuries, the islands were settled by France (1764, Port Louis), Britain (1765, Port Egmont), and Spain, which took over the French settlement. After Spanish withdrawal, the United Provinces of the Río de la Plata claimed the islands; Britain reasserted control in 1833, a foundational grievance in Argentina's continuing sovereignty claim. Argentina invaded on 2 April 1982, sparking the Falklands War; British forces liberated the islands after 74 days of fighting. A 2013 referendum found 99.8% of islanders voting to remain a UK Overseas Territory.

Geography and wildlife

The terrain is hilly and moor-like, with the highest point Mount Usborne (705 m) on East Falkland. The climate is cool and windy. The islands and surrounding waters support large colonies of penguins (gentoo, king, Magellanic, rockhopper, macaroni), albatrosses, sea lions, and elephant seals; the Falkland Islands wolf (warrah), the only native land mammal, was hunted to extinction by 1876.

Economy

Historically based on sheep farming and wool, the economy since the 1980s has been dominated by fishing — especially Illex squid — licensed in the islands' exclusive fishing zone, alongside tourism (including cruise ships and wildlife tourism) and a growing hydrocarbons exploration sector offshore.
