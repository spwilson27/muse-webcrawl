Christopher Columbus (Latin: Christophorus Columbus; Italian: Cristoforo Colombo; Spanish: Cristóbal Colón; between 25 August and 31 October 1451 – 20 May 1506) was an Italian explorer and navigator from the Republic of Genoa who completed four Spanish-based voyages across the Atlantic Ocean sponsored by the Catholic Monarchs of Spain, opening the way for the widespread European exploration and colonisation of the Americas.

Columbus believed that a westward sea route to the Indies of East Asia existed, and underestimated the circumference of the Earth. After lobbying several European courts, he won support from Ferdinand II of Aragon and Isabella I of Castile. His ships, the Niña, the Pinta and the Santa María, departed Spain on 3 August 1492 and made landfall in the Bahamas on 12 October 1492, on an island the native Lucayan people called Guanahani. He went on to visit Cuba and Hispaniola, believing throughout that he had reached the outskirts of Asia, and called the inhabitants indios ("Indians").

Subsequent voyages

Columbus made three further voyages, in 1493, 1498 and 1502, exploring the Caribbean, the coast of South America and Central America. As governor of the Indies he was accused of tyranny and mismanagement and was arrested and returned to Spain in chains in 1500, though he was freed and allowed a final voyage. He died in Valladolid in 1506, still maintaining that he had reached Asia.

Legacy

Columbus's expeditions inaugurated the Columbian exchange of plants, animals, diseases and peoples between the Old and New Worlds, with transformative and often catastrophic consequences for Indigenous populations. He is commemorated in place names such as Colombia and the District of Columbia, and his legacy remains the subject of scholarly and public debate.
