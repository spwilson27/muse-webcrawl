In topology, the Jordan curve theorem, formulated by Camille Jordan in 1887, asserts that every Jordan curve — a plane simple closed curve, that is, a non-self-intersecting continuous loop in the plane — divides the plane into an "interior" region bounded by the curve and an "exterior" region containing all points far away, so that any continuous path connecting a point of one region to a point of the other intersects the curve somewhere.

Statement and intuition

More precisely, the theorem states that the complement of a Jordan curve J in the plane consists of exactly two connected components, one of which (the interior) is bounded and the other (the exterior) unbounded, and that J is the common boundary of both components. Although the statement seems intuitively obvious, its proof is surprisingly difficult: continuity alone permits highly pathological curves — a Jordan curve can be nowhere differentiable and can even have positive area, as with Osgood curves — so arguments appealing to smooth geometry are insufficient.

History of the proof

Jordan's original proof in his Cours d'analyse (1887) was for decades considered incomplete by many mathematicians, who felt the polygonal case had not been rigorously extended to general curves. Oswald Veblen gave the first proof widely accepted as fully rigorous in 1905. Since then many proofs have appeared, using tools from algebraic topology (homology, the Brouwer fixed-point theorem, winding numbers) and elementary combinatorial methods, and the theorem was an early milestone in the development of formal machine-verified proof: Thomas C. Hales led a formalisation in HOL Light in 2005, and a second formalisation in Mizar also exists.

Generalisations

The Jordan–Brouwer separation theorem extends the result to higher dimensions: an embedded (n−1)-sphere in n-dimensional Euclidean space separates its complement into two components. The Jordan–Schoenflies theorem strengthens the planar result, stating that any two Jordan curves are equivalent under a homeomorphism of the plane. By contrast, Alexander's horned sphere shows that the analogous statement about the interior being a ball fails in three dimensions.
