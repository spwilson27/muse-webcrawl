Antisthenes

Bust of Antisthenes, founder of the Cynic school

Antisthenes (Greek: Ἀντισθένης; c. 445 – c. 365 BC) was a Greek philosopher and a pupil of Socrates. He is regarded as the founder of Cynic philosophy, having taught that the purpose of life is to live a life of virtue in agreement with nature, rejecting conventional desires for wealth, power, and fame.

Life

Born in Athens to an Athenian father and a Thracian mother, Antisthenes first studied rhetoric under the sophist Gorgias before becoming a devoted follower of Socrates, whose endurance and self-mastery deeply impressed him. After Socrates' death he taught at the Cynosarges gymnasium outside Athens—its name ("White Dog") is one proposed origin for the label "Cynic" applied to his school. His most famous pupil was Diogenes of Sinope.

Philosophy

Antisthenes held that virtue is sufficient for happiness and requires only the strength of a Socrates; pleasure, luxury, and social convention were to be despised. He wrote extensively on ethics, logic, and literature—including commentaries on Homer—though his works survive only in fragments and reports by later authors such as Diogenes Laertius and Xenophon. In logic he is associated with the view that definition of essences is impossible and that a thing can only be described, not defined.

Legacy

Through Diogenes, Antisthenes' teaching shaped the Cynic tradition, which in turn influenced Stoicism. His emphasis on self-sufficiency (autarkeia) and indifference to fortune became central themes of later Hellenistic philosophy.
