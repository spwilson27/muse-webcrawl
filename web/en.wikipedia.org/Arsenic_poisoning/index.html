Arsenic poisoning

Arsenic poisoning is a medical condition that occurs when arsenic levels in the body rise to toxic amounts. Arsenic is a metalloid element present in groundwater, some industrial processes, pesticides and (historically) medicines and pigments. Poisoning may be acute — a single large exposure — or chronic, from long-term ingestion of contaminated water or food.

Signs and symptoms

Acute poisoning causes severe gastroenteritis ("rice-water" diarrhoea), vomiting, garlic-odour breath, shock, and multi-organ failure. Chronic poisoning (arsenicosis) develops over years: mottled skin pigmentation, palmoplantar keratoses, peripheral neuropathy, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and cancers of the skin, lung and bladder. Transverse white lines on the nails (Mees' lines) may appear after acute exposure.

Causes and scale

The largest source worldwide is naturally contaminated groundwater. Tens of millions of people, especially in Bangladesh and West Bengal — where tube wells tapped arsenic-rich aquifers — have been exposed, in what the World Health Organization has called the largest mass poisoning in history. Arsenic trioxide ("white arsenic") was a classic homicidal poison of the 19th century, detectable after 1836 by the Marsh test.

Mechanism and treatment

Trivalent arsenicals bind sulfhydryl groups and inhibit enzymes such as pyruvate dehydrogenase, while pentavalent arsenate substitutes for phosphate, uncoupling energy production. Management consists of removing the exposure, aggressive supportive care, and chelation with agents such as dimercaprol (BAL), succimer (DMSA) or unithiol (DMPS).
