Pachacuti Inca Yupanqui (Quechua: Pachakutiq Inka Yupanki), often simply Pachacuti, was the ninth Sapa Inca (ruler) of the Kingdom of Cusco (reigned c. 1438–1471/1472). During his reign Cusco grew from a modest city-state into the vast Inca Empire, Tawantinsuyu, stretching along the Andes. For this transformation he is often compared to Alexander the Great or Napoleon, and the name Pachacuti — "he who overturns space and time", or "earth-shaker" — reflects his reputation as a world-changer.

Accession

According to the chronicle tradition, Pachacuti was a younger son of the Inca Viracocha. When the Chanka people attacked Cusco around 1438, Viracocha and his designated heir fled, but Pachacuti stayed, organised the defence, and defeated the invaders — in legend, aided by stones that rose up as soldiers. He then took the throne, taking the name Pachacuti.

Expansion and administration

Pachacuti conquered or incorporated much of the central Andes, from Lake Titicaca to the northern highlands of modern Ecuador, using a combination of warfare, diplomacy, and gifts. He reorganised the empire into four quarters (suyu) meeting at Cusco, systematised the census-and-tribute order recorded on quipus, expanded the road network and storehouse (qullqa) system, and rebuilt Cusco in monumental stone, including the Coricancha temple of the sun. The great fortress-temple of Sacsayhuamán above the city and the royal estate of Machu Picchu are traditionally attributed to his building programme. Late in his reign he associated his son Tupac Inca Yupanqui in command, and the son succeeded him and carried expansion further.

Sources and assessment

Pachacuti's life is known mainly from Spanish chronicles written after the conquest — including those of Pedro Cieza de León, Juan de Betanzos, and Pedro Sarmiento de Gamboa — which drew on Inca oral tradition, so details and dates remain debated. Historians credit him not merely with conquest but with creating the administrative and ideological template of the empire encountered by the Spanish in 1532, less than a century after his accession.
