Hearth

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A hearth (/hɑːrθ/) is the place in a home where a fire is or was traditionally kept for heating and cooking, usually constituted by a horizontal hearthstone and often enclosed to varying degrees by a fireside, a fireplace, an oven, a smoke hood or a chimney. Hearths are typically composed of masonry such as brick or stone, materials able to withstand high temperatures. For millennia, the hearth was an integral part of a dwelling, often its central or most important feature; in archaeology, hearth features are among the key evidences of human occupation of a site.

Archaeology and early history

Controlled use of fire was a landmark of early human evolution, providing warmth, protection from predators, light and a means of cooking. Preserved hearths — concentrations of ash, charcoal, burnt bone and fire-cracked rock — appear at sites such as Wonderwerk Cave in South Africa (about one million years old) and Qesem Cave in Israel. In Neolithic and later dwellings the central open hearth defined the living space; smoke escaped through the roof until flues and chimneys developed in medieval Europe.

Structure and parts

A domestic fireplace hearth consists of the hearthstone (the fireproof floor of the fireplace, extending into the room), the firebox, a mantel or overmantel, and the chimney breast above. In industrial settings, "hearth" also names the floor of a furnace, as in the open-hearth furnace used in steelmaking, or the raised hearth of a forge. Building regulations commonly specify hearth dimensions and clearances to protect surrounding combustible materials.

Cultural significance

The hearth became a potent symbol of home and family — "hearth and home" — in many cultures. In ancient Greek religion, Hestia was goddess of the hearth, and in Rome her counterpart Vesta's perpetual flame was tended by the Vestal Virgins; the Roman focus (hearth) gave language the word for a centre of attention. "Hearth taxes" were levied on households in various countries — including England's hearth tax of 1662–1689 — counting hearths as a proxy for wealth. In modern homes, central heating has displaced the working hearth, but fireplaces remain as decorative and social focal points.
