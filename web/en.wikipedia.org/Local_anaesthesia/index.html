Local anaesthesia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Local anaesthesia
DefinitionReversible loss of sensation in a limited body region, with consciousness maintained
MechanismBlockade of voltage-gated sodium channels in nerve fibres
Common agentsLidocaine, bupivacaine, articaine, prilocaine; topical benzocaine, EMLA cream
TechniquesInfiltration, nerve block, topical, (related: spinal/epidural = regional anaesthesia)

Local anaesthesia is any technique that produces an absence of sensation in a specific part of the body while the patient remains conscious, typically by administering a local anaesthetic drug around the nerves supplying that area. It contrasts with general anaesthesia, in which the patient is rendered unconscious, and with regional anaesthesia (such as spinal or epidural blocks), which numbs a larger region; the boundary between "local" and "regional" is one of extent, and the same drugs are used for both.

Mechanism

Local anaesthetics are weak bases that block voltage-gated sodium channels from the inside of the nerve cell membrane, preventing initiation and conduction of action potentials. Small, lightly myelinated fibres carrying pain and temperature are blocked before larger fibres for touch, pressure, and motor function, so patients may feel pressure and movement while pain is abolished. Adding a vasoconstrictor such as adrenaline (epinephrine) reduces local blood flow, prolonging the block and reducing bleeding and systemic absorption.

Techniques

Common techniques include surface (topical) anaesthesia with sprays, gels, or creams such as EMLA on skin or mucous membranes; infiltration, injecting drug directly into the tissues to be treated, as for suturing wounds or skin surgery; field blocks surrounding an area; and peripheral nerve blocks, in which drug is placed beside a named nerve—for example the inferior alveolar nerve block used in dentistry, or digital blocks for finger injuries. Ultrasound guidance is increasingly used to place blocks accurately.

Uses

Local anaesthesia is used for dental procedures, minor skin surgery and biopsy, wound repair, cataract surgery (usually topical), vasectomy, and many emergency department procedures, and to provide postoperative pain relief. Because it avoids the risks and recovery time of general anaesthesia, it is often preferred for frail or elderly patients.

Risks

Complications are uncommon and include failure or patchiness of the block, bruising or bleeding, infection, temporary or rarely persistent nerve injury, and allergic reactions (more often to preservatives or to ester-type agents). The most serious risk is local anaesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST), from overdose or accidental intravascular injection, which can cause seizures and cardiovascular collapse; it is treated with intravenous lipid emulsion, so maximum safe doses are calculated by patient weight.
