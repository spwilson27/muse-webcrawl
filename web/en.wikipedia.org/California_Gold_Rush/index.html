The California Gold Rush (1848–1855) was a mass migration driven by the discovery of gold at Sutter's Mill in January 1848. News of the discovery brought some 300,000 people to California from the rest of the United States and abroad, transforming the territory's population, economy and politics within a few years.

Discovery

On 24 January 1848, James W. Marshall, building a sawmill for John Sutter on the American River at Coloma, found flakes of gold in the mill's tailrace. Sutter tried to keep the find secret, but word spread after San Francisco merchant Sam Brannan publicised it, and President James K. Polk confirmed the discovery in his December 1848 address to Congress.

The forty-niners

Those who arrived in 1849 became known as forty-niners. They came overland on the California Trail and by sea around Cape Horn or across the Panama isthmus. San Francisco exploded from a village of about 1,000 into a city of 25,000 within two years. Mining began with simple placer techniques — panning and sluicing — and evolved into capital-intensive hydraulic mining that scarred whole hillsides.

Effects

California's non-native population rose from about 14,000 to some 380,000 by 1860, and California entered the Union as a free state in 1850 under the Compromise of 1850. Merchants such as Levi Strauss grew rich supplying the miners. The rush was disastrous for Native Californians, whose population fell catastrophically through disease, displacement and violence, and it brought severe discrimination against Latin American and Chinese miners, including the Foreign Miners' Tax.
