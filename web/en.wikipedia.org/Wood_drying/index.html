Wood drying

Wood drying, also called seasoning, is the process of reducing the moisture content of timber before use. Freshly cut ("green") wood may contain 30–200 percent water by dry weight; drying brings it to a target moisture content — typically 6–8 percent for interior joinery and 15–19 percent for framing lumber — making it lighter, stronger, more stable, and resistant to decay.

Principles

Water in wood exists as free water in cell cavities and bound water in cell walls. Drying first removes free water; below the fibre saturation point (about 28–30 percent) the wood begins to shrink as bound water leaves. Uneven shrinkage between the surface and core causes defects such as checking, warping, splitting, and case hardening if drying is too fast. Moisture content is expressed as a percentage of oven-dry weight and measured with pin or pinless moisture meters.

Methods

Air drying stacks boards outdoors with spacers ("stickers") between layers for months to years; it is cheap but cannot reach low moisture levels in humid climates. Kiln drying — in conventional steam kilns, dehumidification kilns, solar kilns, or vacuum kilns — controls temperature, humidity, and airflow to dry lumber in days to weeks with fewer defects. Specialty methods include microwave and radio-frequency drying. Hardwoods for furniture are often air-dried first, then finished in a kiln, and may be steamed or conditioned to relieve drying stresses.

Importance

Properly dried wood resists fungal decay and insects, holds fasteners and finishes better, machines more cleanly, and stays dimensionally stable in service — essential for furniture, flooring, musical instruments, and structural timber grading.
