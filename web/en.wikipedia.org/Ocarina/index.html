The ocarina is a wind musical instrument — a type of vessel flute. Variations exist, but a typical ocarina is an enclosed space with four to twelve finger holes and a mouthpiece, which projects from the body. It is traditionally made from clay or ceramic, but other materials may be used, such as plastic, wood, glass, stone, or metal. An ocarina-like instrument was used in many ancient cultures, including the Chinese xun, which dates back thousands of years.

History

Vessel flutes appeared independently in many regions. The modern ocarina is credited to Giuseppe Donati of Budrio, Italy, who in 1853 developed the transverse "sweet potato" form with a systematic fingering system; the name derives from the Italian ocarina, "little goose." Ocarinas became popular as inexpensive folk instruments, were carried by soldiers in both World Wars, and were standard issue or popular pastimes in several armies.

Types

Ocarinas are classified by shape (transverse, pendant, inline, and multi-chambered) and by hole count. Multi-chambered (double and triple) ocarinas extend the range well beyond the roughly one-and-a-half octaves of a single-chamber instrument. Pendant ocarinas use fingering systems such as the 4-hole "English" system devised by John Taylor.

In popular culture

The instrument gained wide recognition through the video game The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time (1998), which significantly boosted worldwide interest and sales. The ocarina also appears in films and anime, and has an international community of players and makers.
