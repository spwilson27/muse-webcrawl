West Slavic languages

The West Slavic languages are a subdivision of the Slavic language family, spoken in Central Europe by roughly 60 million people. Together with the East Slavic languages (Russian, Ukrainian, Belarusian) and the South Slavic languages they descend from Proto-Slavic, from which they diverged roughly in the 7th–9th centuries as Slavic-speaking peoples spread and their dialect continuum broke apart.

Languages

The group is conventionally divided into three subgroups:

  • Czech–Slovak: Czech and Slovak, closely related and largely mutually intelligible.
  • Lechitic: Polish (the largest West Slavic language, with around 45 million native speakers), Kashubian, and the extinct Polabian and Pomeranian languages.
  • Sorbian: Upper Sorbian and Lower Sorbian, spoken by a Slavic minority in Lusatia, eastern Germany.

Features

West Slavic languages are written in the Latin alphabet — reflecting the region's historical orientation toward Roman Catholicism and Western Europe — in contrast to the Cyrillic used by most East and some South Slavic languages. Shared innovations include the preservation of the Proto-Slavic nasal vowels in Polish (*ę, *ą), the development of *tj/*dj clusters into sounds such as Polish c, dz and Czech c, z, fixed word stress patterns (initial stress in Czech and Slovak; penultimate stress in Polish), and large vocabularies of German and Latin loanwords. Like other Slavic languages they are highly inflected, with (typically) seven grammatical cases and three genders, and verbs marked for aspect.

History

Literary traditions developed from the later Middle Ages — Czech and Polish both have substantial literatures from the 14th–16th centuries. The 19th-century national revivals standardised modern literary forms; Czech and Slovak standards diverged further after the dissolution of Czechoslovakia in 1993. Kashubian and the Sorbian languages are recognised minority languages today.
