Saros (astronomy)

The saros is a period of exactly 223 synodic months — approximately 6,585.32 days, or about 18 years, 11 days, and 8 hours — that can be used to predict eclipses of the Sun and Moon. One saros after an eclipse, the Sun, Earth, and Moon return to approximately the same relative geometry, and a nearly identical eclipse will occur; a series of such eclipses is called a saros series. Because the period is not a whole number of days, successive eclipses in a series are visible from different parts of the Earth, shifting westward by about 120° of longitude each cycle.

The saros arises from the near-commensurability of the synodic, draconic, and anomalistic months.

History

The cycle was known to Babylonian astronomers by the last centuries BC and was used by Greek, Islamic, and medieval astronomers for eclipse prediction. The name "saros" was applied to the period by Edmond Halley in 1686, drawing on a Byzantine lexicon, though the Babylonian word šār originally denoted a much longer numerical unit. The mechanism of the Antikythera, an ancient Greek analogue computer, includes a dial that tracks the saros cycle to predict eclipses.

Saros series

Each saros series begins with a partial eclipse near one of Earth's poles and evolves over 1,226 to 1,550 years through dozens of eclipses — typically 69 to 87 — before ending at the opposite pole. At any time about 40 saros series are in progress. Individual eclipses are conventionally identified by their saros series number and position within it; for example, the total solar eclipse of 11 August 1999 belonged to Solar Saros 145, the same series as the North American eclipse of 21 August 2017.
