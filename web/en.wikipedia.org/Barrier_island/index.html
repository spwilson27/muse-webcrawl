Barrier island

Barrier islands are coastal landforms — narrow, elongated ridges of sand running parallel to the mainland coast — separated from the mainland by a lagoon, bay, or marsh and from each other by tidal inlets. They are dynamic systems that migrate, erode, and accrete in response to waves, tides, currents, and storms.

Formation and distribution

Theories of barrier island formation include the emergence of offshore bars, the growth of spits cut off by inlets, and the submergence of coastal dunes as sea level rose after the last glacial period. Barrier islands occur on about 15% of the world's coastlines, most famously along the Atlantic and Gulf coasts of the United States — including the Outer Banks of North Carolina, Padre Island in Texas (the world's longest barrier island), and the Sea Islands — as well as the Frisian Islands of northern Europe.

Ecology and human use

Barrier islands protect mainland coasts from storm surge and provide habitats including dunes, salt marshes, and maritime forests for migratory birds and sea turtles. Many are heavily developed for resorts and housing, which has led to controversy over beach nourishment, erosion, and hurricane risk as sea levels rise.
