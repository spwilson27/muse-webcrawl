Lynn Margulis (5 March 1938 – 22 November 2011) was an American evolutionary biologist whose work transformed understanding of the origin of complex cells. Her 1967 paper on endosymbiosis — rejected by many journals before publication — proposed that mitochondria and chloroplasts evolved from free-living bacteria engulfed by host cells, a theory now central to biology and confirmed by molecular evidence.

Career

Margulis studied at the University of Chicago, the University of Wisconsin–Madison and UC Berkeley (PhD 1965). She taught at Boston University and then, from 1988, at the University of Massachusetts Amherst. She developed her ideas into Serial Endosymbiotic Theory and argued that symbiosis, not mutation alone, was a major engine of evolutionary novelty. She also championed James Lovelock's Gaia hypothesis and, with her son Dorion Sagan, wrote widely read books on evolution, including Microcosmos.

Honours

Margulis was elected to the National Academy of Sciences in 1983 and received the National Medal of Science in 1999. She was the first wife of astronomer Carl Sagan.
