Bean sprout

Bean sprouts are the young shoots of germinated beans, cultivated by sprouting seeds in moist, warm conditions for several days. The term most commonly refers to mung bean sprouts and soybean sprouts, both staples of East and Southeast Asian cooking. Sprouting has been practiced in China for thousands of years and increases the availability of some vitamins and amino acids compared with the dry seed.

Types

Mung bean sprouts (Vigna radiata) are thick, crisp, silvery-white shoots with small yellow-green seed heads, used in stir-fries, spring rolls, salads, and noodle dishes such as pad thai. Soybean sprouts (Glycine max) have larger yellow heads and a nuttier flavor; in Korea they are the base of the soup-and-side dish kongnamul. Sprouts are also grown from adzuki beans, lentils, peas, and other legumes.

Cultivation

Beans are soaked, then kept moist and dark at warm temperatures and rinsed regularly; commercial growers use rotating drums or trays and harvest after roughly three to seven days, when the root and shoot have elongated but before true leaves expand. Growing in darkness keeps the sprouts pale and tender.

Food safety

The warm, humid conditions that sprouts require also favor bacteria such as Salmonella and E. coli, and raw sprouts have caused several foodborne illness outbreaks, including a major 2011 outbreak in Europe traced to fenugreek sprouts. Health authorities advise cooking sprouts thoroughly and caution that young children, the elderly, pregnant women, and immunocompromised people avoid eating them raw.
