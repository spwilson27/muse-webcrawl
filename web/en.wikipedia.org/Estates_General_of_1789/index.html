The Estates General of 1789 was a general assembly representing the French estates of the realm—the clergy (First Estate), the nobility (Second Estate), and the commoners (Third Estate)—summoned by King Louis XVI to address France's financial crisis. It was the first meeting of the Estates General since 1614.

Background

Bankrupt after costly wars and unable to push tax reform past the privileged estates and the parlements, the monarchy announced in 1788 that the Estates General would convene. Elections produced cahiers de doléances (lists of grievances) from communities across France. Controversy erupted over whether voting would proceed by order (giving each estate one vote) or by head, which the doubled representation of the Third Estate would make decisive.

From assembly to revolution

The Estates opened at Versailles on 5 May 1789 and deadlocked over verification of credentials. On 17 June the Third Estate declared itself the National Assembly; locked out of its hall three days later, it swore the Tennis Court Oath not to disperse until France had a constitution. The events of June 1789 marked the opening of the French Revolution.
