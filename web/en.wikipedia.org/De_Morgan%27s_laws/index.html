De Morgan's laws

In propositional logic and Boolean algebra, De Morgan's laws, also known as De Morgan's theorem, are a pair of transformation rules that are both valid rules of inference. They are named after Augustus De Morgan, a 19th-century British mathematician.

Statement

The laws state that the negation of a conjunction is the disjunction of the negations, and the negation of a disjunction is the conjunction of the negations. In symbols: ¬(P ∧ Q) ⇔ ¬P ∨ ¬Q, and ¬(P ∨ Q) ⇔ ¬P ∧ ¬Q. Intuitively: "not (A and B)" is the same as "(not A) or (not B)", and "not (A or B)" is the same as "(not A) and (not B)".

Set theory and other forms

In set theory the laws state that the complement of a union is the intersection of the complements, and vice versa. Corresponding dualities appear in predicate logic, where negation moves across quantifiers by exchanging ∀ and ∃, and in electrical engineering, where they guide the transformation of logic gate circuits (for example, a NAND gate is equivalent to an OR of inverted inputs).

History

Versions of the principles were described by medieval logicians including William of Ockham, but they were first formally stated and proved by De Morgan in the mid-19th century as part of his work on the algebra of logic.
