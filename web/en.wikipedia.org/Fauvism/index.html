Fauvism is a style of painting and an art movement that emerged in France around 1904 and flourished from 1905 to 1907. It emphasised painterly qualities and strong, arbitrary colour over realistic representation: wild brushwork and vivid, non-naturalistic hues applied straight from the tube. The name comes from the critic Louis Vauxcelles, who, seeing the group's work exhibited alongside a classical sculpture at the 1905 Salon d'Automne in Paris, called the artists les fauves — "the wild beasts."

Artists and works

The leaders of the movement were Henri Matisse and André Derain, who had painted together at Collioure in the summer of 1905; other Fauves included Maurice de Vlaminck, Raoul Dufy, Albert Marquet and, briefly, Georges Braque. Famous Fauvist works include Matisse's Woman with a Hat and The Green Stripe, and Derain's views of London. The movement drew on the expressive colour of Van Gogh and Gauguin and the pointillist technique of Neo-Impressionism.

Legacy

Fauvism was short-lived — by 1908 most of its artists had moved on, Braque towards Cubism with Picasso — but it was the first of the major avant-garde movements of the twentieth century and established colour liberation as a central concern of modern art.
