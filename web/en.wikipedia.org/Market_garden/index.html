Market garden

A market garden is the relatively small-scale production of fruits, vegetables and flowers as cash crops, frequently sold directly to consumers and restaurants. It is distinguishable from other types of farming by the diversity of crops grown on a small area of land, typically from under one acre (0.4 ha) to a few acres, during a single growing season.

Characteristics

Market gardening is generally done with hand labour and gardening techniques rather than large-scale mechanised farming, although small tractors and specialised hand tools — broadforks, wheel hoes, precision seeders and tilthers — are common. Production is intensive: beds are closely spaced, crops are succession-planted, and high-value produce such as salad greens, tomatoes, herbs and cut flowers predominates. Many market gardens sell through farmers' markets, community-supported agriculture (CSA) subscriptions, farm stands and local restaurants.

History

Market gardens historically clustered on the outskirts of growing cities, where fresh produce could be carted quickly to urban markets; the maraîchers of 19th-century Paris were famous for intensive raised-bed production using horse manure for heat and fertility. In the United States and Canada such operations were once called "truck farms", the produce being taken to market by truck or wagon. The late 20th and early 21st centuries saw a revival of small-scale market gardening, associated with writers and growers such as Eliot Coleman and Jean-Martin Fortier, whose book The Market Gardener popularised profitable small-plot intensive methods.
