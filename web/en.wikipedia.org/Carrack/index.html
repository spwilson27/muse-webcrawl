Carrack

A large Portuguese carrack (nau) of the Age of Discovery

A carrack is a three- or four-masted ocean-going sailing ship that was developed in the 14th to 15th centuries in Europe, most notably in Portugal, from the Mediterranean cog. It was widely used for trade in the Atlantic and Indian oceans during the Age of Discovery, carrying explorers such as Christopher Columbus — whose flagship Santa María was a small carrack — and the fleets of the Portuguese India Armadas and the Spanish treasure fleets.

Design

Carracks were carvel-built, with a high rounded stern and a large aftcastle, and an equally prominent forecastle projecting forward over the bow. They combined square sails on the fore- and mainmasts with a lateen sail on the mizzenmast, an arrangement that gave them both running power and reasonable maneuverability. Early carracks displaced around 200–600 tons; the great Portuguese naus of the India run grew to 1,000 tons and beyond, and a few exceptional examples, such as the Portuguese Flor de la Mar and the English Henry Grace à Dieu (1514), exceeded 1,500 tons.

Use and development

The carrack's size and seaworthiness made it the principal European vessel of the first century of oceanic expansion: Portuguese carracks sailed the Cape route to India, Spanish carracks served in the Manila galleon trade across the Pacific, and carracks fought as warships in fleets such as those of the Anglo-Spanish war, though their high castles made them slow and weatherly compared with later designs.

By the late 16th and early 17th centuries the carrack was superseded by the galleon, which lowered the forecastle, lengthened the hull and improved sailing qualities, becoming the dominant large ship of the following century.

Famous carracks

  • Santa María — Columbus's flagship, 1492
  • Victoria — the first ship to circumnavigate the globe (1519–1522), under Magellan and Elcano
  • Mary Rose — Henry VIII's warship, sunk 1545, now preserved at Portsmouth
  • Flor de la Mar — Portuguese treasure carrack lost off Sumatra in 1511
