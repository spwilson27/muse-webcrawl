Cooperative education

Cooperative education (or co-operative education) is a structured method of combining classroom-based education with practical work experience. A cooperative education experience, commonly known as a "co-op", provides academic credit for structured work experiences, helping young people in making the school-to-work transition. It falls under the umbrella of work-integrated learning (alongside internships, service learning, and clinical placements) but is distinct from these other approaches in that it is designed to align the student's academic study with relevant career experience.

History

The concept was developed by Herman Schneider, dean of the College of Engineering at the University of Cincinnati, who founded the first cooperative education program in 1906. Schneider reasoned that engineering students would benefit from alternating periods of classroom study with periods of paid work in their field. The model spread to other institutions, including Northeastern University (1909), Drexel University, and the University of Waterloo in Canada, which operates one of the largest co-op programs in the world.

Models

Common models include the alternating model, in which students alternate terms of full-time study with terms of full-time work, and the parallel model, in which students work part-time while studying. In alternating programs a degree may take five years rather than four, but students graduate with a year or more of professional experience.

Benefits and criticism

Proponents note that co-op students gain practical skills, professional contacts, and earnings that offset tuition costs, and that they often receive job offers from co-op employers after graduation. Criticisms include the extended time to degree and variability in placement quality.
