Rainforest

Rainforest
Forest characterised by high, generally constant rainfall
Tropical and temperate types; home to over half of terrestrial species

Rainforests are forests characterized by a closed and continuous tree canopy, moisture-dependent vegetation, the presence of epiphytes and lianas, and the absence of wildfire. A rainforest may be described as a tall, dense jungle — the term actually derives from an old interpretation of a Sanskrit term for forest. The largest rainforests are tropical, centred on the Amazon Basin, the Congo Basin and Southeast Asia.

Structure

Tropical rainforests have a layered structure: an emergent layer of very tall trees, a main canopy some 30–45 m up that intercepts most sunlight, an understorey of young trees and shrubs, and a shaded forest floor where decomposition is rapid. Rainforests receive typically 175–200 cm or more of rain per year, with high humidity and stable warm temperatures.

Biodiversity and climate role

Though covering under 10% of land area, rainforests harbour well over half of the world's plant and animal species. They store immense amounts of carbon and generate atmospheric moisture and rain downwind. See also Rafflesia, one of their most famous plants.

Threats

Logging, conversion to cattle pasture, oil palm and soy plantations, and mining have destroyed large areas — historically about half the original extent — releasing carbon and driving species extinction. Temperate rainforests, such as those of the Pacific Northwest, face similar pressures.
