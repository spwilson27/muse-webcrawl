Rose window

A rose window (or Catherine window) is a circular window divided into segments by stone mullions and tracery, filled with stained glass, characteristic of Gothic church architecture. The term derives from the resemblance of the radiating pattern to a rose.

Development

Circular oculi existed in Roman and Romanesque building, but the great rose windows emerged in French Gothic of the 12th and 13th centuries — at Chartres, Notre-Dame de Paris, Reims, Amiens and Sainte-Chapelle — reaching over 12 metres in diameter. Tracery evolved from plate forms to bar tracery, and in Flamboyant Gothic to curvilinear, flame-like patterns.

Symbolism

Set typically on the west façade and in transept ends, rose windows poured coloured light into the nave and embodied medieval number symbolism and Marian devotion — Mary as the "rose without thorns". Their geometry, built from overlapping circles, is a showcase of medieval stone-cutting craft. See also Gothic architecture and stained glass.
