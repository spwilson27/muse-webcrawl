Prescription drug

A prescription drug (also prescription medication, prescription medicine or Rx-only drug) is a pharmaceutical drug that is permitted to be dispensed only to those with a medical prescription. In contrast, over-the-counter drugs can be obtained without a prescription. The ability to prescribe drugs is restricted to certain qualified medical professionals, such as physicians, dentists, and in some jurisdictions nurse practitioners and pharmacists with prescribing rights.

Regulation

Regulatory authorities decide whether a medicine may be sold over the counter or requires a prescription, based largely on its safety profile, potential for misuse, and the need for professional supervision. In the United States, the Federal Food, Drug, and Cosmetic Act of 1938 and the Durham-Humphrey Amendment of 1951 established the modern distinction between prescription and non-prescription drugs, administered by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA). In the United Kingdom, prescription-only medicines (POM) are regulated by the Medicines and Healthcare products Regulatory Agency (MHRA).

Prescribing and dispensing

A prescription is a written, electronic or verbal order from an authorised prescriber to a pharmacist to prepare and dispense a medicine for a particular patient. Prescriptions typically specify the drug, dose, route, frequency and duration of treatment. Pharmacists verify prescriptions, counsel patients, and check for interactions before dispensing.

Costs and access

Prescription drug prices and the extent of public subsidies vary widely between countries and are a recurring subject of health policy debate, particularly in the United States, where patients often face substantial out-of-pocket costs. Generic drugs, sold after patent expiry under their chemical names, generally cost much less than brand-name products.
