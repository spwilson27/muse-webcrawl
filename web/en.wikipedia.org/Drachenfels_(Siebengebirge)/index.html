Drachenfels (Siebengebirge)

The Drachenfels ("Dragon's Rock") is a hill (321 metres) in the Siebengebirge uplands of North Rhine-Westphalia, Germany, situated between Königswinter and Bad Honnef, near Bonn. Crowned by the ruins of Burg Drachenfels, built in the 12th century, it is one of the most famous landmarks of the Rhine valley and a celebrated destination of Rhine romanticism.

Geology and history

The hill is of volcanic origin, formed from trachyte lava of the Siebengebirge volcanic field. Its stone was quarried from Roman times onwards; Drachenfels trachyte was used in the construction of Cologne Cathedral. The castle at the summit was begun about 1138 by Archbishop Arnold I of Cologne and played a role in regional conflicts before being destroyed by Swedish troops in the Thirty Years' War in 1634.

Legend

The hill's name is bound up with dragon legends. According to one tale, the hero Siegfried of the Nibelungenlied slew the dragon Fafnir at the Drachenfels and bathed in its blood to become invulnerable — a local tradition attaching the saga to the cave known as the Drachenhöhle on the hill's flank. Other legends tell of a dragon kept in a cave that received sacrifices.

Tourism

The Drachenfels was an early star of Rhine tourism; Lord Byron celebrated it in Childe Harold's Pilgrimage ("The castled crag of Drachenfels"). The Drachenfels Railway, a rack railway opened in 1883, carries visitors from Königswinter to the summit, making it one of Germany's oldest rack railways. The modernist Drachenburg Castle, a villa built in 1882–84 on a terrace below the summit, is another major attraction. The Siebengebirge around it is one of Germany's oldest nature reserves, protected since the 1920s.
