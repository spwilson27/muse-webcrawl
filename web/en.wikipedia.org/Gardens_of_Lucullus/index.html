Gardens of Lucullus

The Gardens of Lucullus (Latin: Horti Lucullani) were an ancient patrician villa and gardens on the Pincian Hill in Rome, laid out by the wealthy Roman general and consul Lucius Licinius Lucullus (118–57/56 BC) after his campaigns in the east. They were among the earliest and most luxurious of the great horti (pleasure gardens) of ancient Rome and set a model for later imperial gardens.

History

Lucullus returned to Rome laden with the spoils of the Third Mithridatic War and spent lavishly on building and gardening. At his estate on the Pincian Hill (and at villas near Tusculum and Naples) he constructed pavilions, libraries, menageries, and fish ponds, imported exotic plants — he is credited with introducing the cherry and the apricot to Italy from Asia Minor — and collected Greek art and literature. His banquets became proverbial for their extravagance, giving rise to the adjective "Lucullan."

After Lucullus's death the gardens passed through several hands and eventually became imperial property. It was in these gardens that the emperor Claudius's wife Messalina, who had coveted them and engineered the downfall of their owner Valerius Asiaticus, was executed in AD 48 after her marriage to Gaius Silius was exposed. The area is now occupied by the Villa Medici and the gardens above the Spanish Steps.
