Verner's law

Verner's law is a sound change in the Germanic languages discovered by the Danish philologist Karl Verner and published in 1875. It explained a systematic class of apparent exceptions to Grimm's law, the consonant shift that distinguishes Germanic from other Indo-European languages.

The problem

Grimm's law predicts that Proto-Indo-European voiceless stops became voiceless fricatives in Proto-Germanic. Yet in many words the Germanic outcome was a voiced fricative instead: compare the differing consonants in cognates of "father" and "brother" across Germanic languages, or within verb paradigms where, for example, some forms showed s and others r (rhotacism of an earlier voiced z).

The law

Verner showed that the outcome depended on the position of the Proto-Indo-European accent, which was preserved in Sanskrit and Ancient Greek. Voiceless fricatives produced by Grimm's law became voiced when the preceding syllable had not carried the Indo-European accent; otherwise they remained voiceless. Later, Germanic fixed its stress on the initial syllable, obscuring the conditioning environment, but the alternations (Grammatischer Wechsel) survive in Germanic strong verbs and in related word pairs such as English seethe/sodden and was/were.

Significance

Verner's law was a landmark in establishing the Neogrammarian principle that sound laws operate without exceptions — apparent irregularities merely reflect conditioning factors not yet identified. It remains a standard textbook example of the comparative method in historical linguistics.
