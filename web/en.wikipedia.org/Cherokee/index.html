The Cherokee (Cherokee: ᎠᏂᏴᏫᏯ, Aniyvwiyaʔi) are an Indigenous people of the Southeastern Woodlands of the United States. Prior to the 18th century, they were concentrated in what is now the southern Appalachians, in present-day western North Carolina, Tennessee, Georgia and Alabama. Today three Cherokee tribes are federally recognised: the Cherokee Nation and the United Keetoowah Band in Oklahoma, and the Eastern Band of Cherokee Indians in North Carolina.

History

The Cherokee speak an Iroquoian language, suggesting ancient migration from the Great Lakes region. In the early 19th century the Cherokee adopted a written constitution and, through Sequoyah's invention of the Cherokee syllabary (1821), achieved widespread literacy, publishing the bilingual newspaper Cherokee Phoenix from 1828. Despite these adaptations, the Indian Removal Act of 1830 and the disputed Treaty of New Echota led to the forced removal of some 16,000 Cherokee to Indian Territory in 1838–39 — the Trail of Tears, on which thousands died.

Modern nations

The Cherokee Nation, headquartered in Tahlequah, Oklahoma, is the largest federally recognised tribe in the United States, with several hundred thousand enrolled citizens. The Eastern Band descends from Cherokee who remained in or returned to North Carolina and occupy the Qualla Boundary. Language revitalisation efforts seek to preserve Cherokee, now critically endangered among first-language speakers.

Culture

Traditional Cherokee society was matrilineal and organised into seven clans. Ceremonies such as the Green Corn Ceremony, stickball games, basketry, and clan and town governance remain central to Cherokee identity.
