Inuit clothing

Inuit clothing is the traditional dress of the Inuit peoples of the Arctic regions of Canada, Alaska and Greenland, developed over centuries to protect against extreme cold, wind and moisture. Made principally from caribou hide and sealskin, sewn with sinew thread, it is regarded as one of the most effective cold-weather clothing systems ever devised and strongly influenced modern polar expedition gear.

Materials

Caribou skin, with its hollow hairs, provides exceptional insulation and was the preferred material for winter garments. Sealskin is waterproof and was used for boots, mittens and summer clothing; other materials included fox, wolf and polar bear fur for trims and ruffs, bird skins, and fish skin. Garments were traditionally made by women, whose sewing skill was essential to survival; waterproof seams were sewn with sinew using closely spaced stitches that did not pierce fully through the skin.

Garments

The central garment is the parka (anorak/qulittaq), a hooded pullover coat often worn in two layers in winter, fur-in against the body and fur-out outside. The women's amauti has an enlarged hood and pouch (amaut) in which a baby is carried on the back. Lower garments include fur trousers, stockings, and boots (kamik) of sealskin with hair-side-out soles of bearded seal or caribou. Mittens, often attached by a cord, and a large ruff of wolverine or wolf fur around the face — which sheds frost from breath — complete the ensemble.

Contemporary practice

Today Inuit wear a mixture of modern manufactured clothing and traditional skin garments, which remain preferred for hunting and winter travel and are important cultural symbols. Contemporary Inuit designers adapt traditional forms and materials in modern fashion, while debates over seal-product trade restrictions have made skin clothing a politically significant issue.
