In commutative algebra, localization is a systematic way of adding multiplicative inverses to a ring. Given a commutative ring R and a multiplicatively closed subset S (containing 1 and not containing 0), the localization S−1R is a new ring whose elements are fractions r/s with r in R and s in S, subject to the usual rules of fraction arithmetic. It comes with a canonical ring homomorphism from R that makes every element of S a unit, and it is universal with this property. The construction generalizes the passage from the integers to the rational numbers.

Motivation and examples

Localization lets algebraists focus on the behaviour of a ring "near" a chosen set of points, inverting everything else. If R is an integral domain and S is all nonzero elements, the localization is the field of fractions of R. If P is a prime ideal and S = R \ P, the localization RP is a local ring whose unique maximal ideal is generated by P; for example, localizing the integers at the prime ideal (p) gives the ring of rational numbers whose denominators are not divisible by p. If instead S consists of the powers of a single element f, the localization Rf corresponds geometrically to removing the vanishing locus of f.

Construction

Formally, elements of the localization are equivalence classes of pairs (r, s), written r/s, where (r, s) is equivalent to (r′, s′) if some element t of S annihilates the difference: t(r s′ − r′ s) = 0. Addition and multiplication are defined as for fractions. When R is a domain, the extra factor t is unnecessary. The canonical map R → S−1R sends r to r/1; its kernel consists of elements annihilated by some element of S.

Properties and use

Localization is an exact functor: it preserves exactness of sequences of modules, so properties such as flatness and many finiteness conditions can be checked locally at each prime ideal—the "local–global principle." Prime ideals of the localization correspond exactly to prime ideals of R disjoint from S. In algebraic geometry, localizing the coordinate ring of an affine variety at a point yields its local ring, the algebraic counterpart of the ring of germs of functions at that point, and the rings Rf provide the standard affine open covering used to build schemes.
