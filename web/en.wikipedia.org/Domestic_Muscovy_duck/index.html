Domestic Muscovy duck

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The domestic Muscovy duck (Cairina moschata domestica) is a domesticated form of the Muscovy duck, native to the Americas from Mexico southwards. Domesticated by Indigenous peoples in pre-Columbian times, it is one of only two ancestors of domestic ducks worldwide, the other being the mallard. It is kept for meat, eggs and feathers, and as an ornamental and pest-control bird.

Characteristics

Domestic Muscovies are heavy, broad-bodied birds. Drakes weigh 4.5–6.3 kg and females about half as much, making the breed strongly sexually dimorphic. Both sexes have bare red or blackish facial skin with fleshy caruncles, most developed in males. Plumage is most commonly black-and-white pied, but many colour varieties exist, including white, blue, chocolate and lavender. Unlike mallard-derived ducks, Muscovies hiss rather than quack, perch readily in trees, and have strong clawed feet. Hens are devoted mothers and may incubate eggs for about 35 days — longer than other domestic ducks.

History and use

The Spanish brought the bird to Europe in the 16th century. It is widely raised in Latin America, Africa, Asia and Australasia, valued for lean, flavourful meat and for its appetite for insects and snails. Crossed with mallard-derived ducks it produces the sterile hybrid known as the mulard, important in foie gras production. Feral populations are established in many countries, and the species is considered invasive in some regions.
