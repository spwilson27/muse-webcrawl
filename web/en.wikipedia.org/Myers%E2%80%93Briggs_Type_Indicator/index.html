The Myers–Briggs Type Indicator (MBTI) is an introspective self-report questionnaire indicating differing psychological preferences in how people perceive the world and make decisions. It assigns respondents to one of sixteen personality types based on four dichotomies: extraversion/introversion (E/I), sensing/intuition (S/N), thinking/feeling (T/F), and judging/perceiving (J/P).

History

Katharine Cook Briggs began research into personality differences in the 1910s and, after reading Carl Jung's Psychological Types (1921), developed her own typology with her daughter Isabel Briggs Myers. During the Second World War Myers constructed a pencil-and-paper test intended to help women entering the workforce find suitable jobs. The assessment was published for educational use in 1962 and became widely used in corporate training, education and personal development.

Criticism

The MBTI is popular — millions of assessments are taken each year — but it is controversial among psychologists. Critics point to dichotomising continuous traits, modest test–retest reliability, and limited evidence that type predicts job performance or other outcomes. The administering company maintains that the indicator is intended for development and self-understanding rather than selection decisions. Many researchers favour trait models such as the Big Five instead.
