Black migration

Families migrating northward during the Great Migration era.

Black migration refers broadly to the large-scale movements of African Americans within and into the United States, most prominently the Great Migration, in which roughly six million Black Southerners moved to cities in the North, Midwest, and West between about 1916 and 1970.

The Great Migration

Driven by Jim Crow discrimination, racial violence, and limited economic opportunity in the rural South, and drawn by industrial jobs created by the two World Wars and immigration restrictions, migrants settled in cities such as Chicago, Detroit, Cleveland, New York, Pittsburgh, Los Angeles, and Oakland. The migration transformed American culture and politics, fueling movements such as the Harlem Renaissance, reshaping urban demographics, and shifting Black voting power into Northern states.

Earlier and later movements

Earlier movements included the flight of enslaved people via the Underground Railroad, the Exoduster movement to Kansas in 1879, and migration to Oklahoma's all-Black towns. Since the 1970s, a "New Great Migration" has seen many African Americans move back to the South — particularly to metropolitan areas such as Atlanta, Charlotte, Houston, and Dallas — attracted by jobs, lower costs, family ties, and growing Black political influence, reversing the net flow out of the region.

International dimensions

The term also covers Black migration beyond the United States, including postwar Caribbean and African migration to Britain, Canada, and France, and contemporary African immigration to the United States and Europe.
