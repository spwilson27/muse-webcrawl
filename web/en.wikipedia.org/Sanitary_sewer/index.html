Sanitary sewer

A sanitary sewer is an underground pipe or tunnel system for transporting sewage from houses and commercial buildings (but not stormwater) to a sewage treatment plant or disposal site. Sanitary sewers are a type of gravity sewer and are part of an overall system called a "sewage system" or sewerage.

Design

A sanitary sewer system typically consists of building connections (laterals), smaller branch sewers, larger trunk sewers and interceptors, manholes for access, and, where gravity flow is not possible, pumping (lift) stations and force mains. Pipes are commonly made of vitrified clay, PVC, cast iron, or reinforced concrete. Unlike combined sewers, sanitary sewers are designed to exclude stormwater, although infiltration and inflow through cracks and illicit connections can overload them.

History

Ancient cities such as Mohenjo-daro and Rome (with its Cloaca Maxima) had drainage systems, but modern sanitary sewerage dates from the mid-19th century, when cities like Hamburg, London, and later American cities built comprehensive underground systems in response to cholera epidemics and the sanitary reform movement. Joseph Bazalgette's London sewer network, built after the Great Stink of 1858, is a celebrated example.

Maintenance and problems

Common problems include blockages caused by fats, oils and grease ("fatbergs"), tree root intrusion, corrosion from hydrogen sulfide, and sanitary sewer overflows during wet weather. Modern maintenance uses CCTV inspection, jetting, and trenchless rehabilitation such as cured-in-place pipe lining.
