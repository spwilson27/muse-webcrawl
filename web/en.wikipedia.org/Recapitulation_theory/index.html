Recapitulation theory

The recapitulation theory, also called the biogenetic law or embryological parallelism, is a biological hypothesis that in developing from embryo to adult, an animal goes through stages resembling or representing successive stages in the evolution of its remote ancestors. It is often summarized by the phrase "ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny", coined by Ernst Haeckel in 1866.

History

Earlier versions of the idea were expressed by Johann Friedrich Meckel and Étienne Serres in the early 19th century. Haeckel formalized the theory and supported it with his famous, and controversial, embryo drawings, which appeared to show the embryos of different vertebrates passing through similar stages.

Criticism and modern status

The strong form of the theory has been rejected by modern biology. Karl Ernst von Baer's laws of embryology already pointed to a more nuanced picture: embryos of different species resemble each other in general features early on but diverge, rather than literally replaying adult ancestral forms. However, concepts derived from the theory, such as heterochrony, remain important in evolutionary developmental biology.
