Conifer

Conifers are a group of cone-bearing, woody seed plants, the division Pinophyta (also called Coniferophyta or Coniferae). Most are trees, some are shrubs, and nearly all are evergreen with needle-like or scale-like leaves. Living conifers number roughly 600–650 species in about 70 genera and seven or eight families, dominated by the pine family (Pinaceae), which includes pines, spruces, firs, larches, cedars and hemlocks.

Distribution and ecology

Conifers dominate vast forests of the Northern Hemisphere, especially the boreal forest (taiga) of North America and Eurasia — the largest terrestrial biome on Earth — and mountain forests at high altitude. Southern Hemisphere conifers, such as the araucarias and podocarps, are more scattered but locally important. The group includes the tallest, largest and longest-lived organisms on Earth: coast redwoods exceed 115 metres, giant sequoias are the most massive trees, and bristlecone pines live for nearly 5,000 years.

Reproduction

Conifers are gymnosperms: their seeds are not enclosed in an ovary but borne, usually, on the scales of cones. Pollen from male cones is carried by wind to female cones, where fertilisation may take more than a year; the mature woody cone then opens to release winged seeds. Some conifers, such as junipers and yews, bear fleshy, berry-like structures instead of woody cones.

Economic importance

Conifers supply most of the world's softwood timber and paper pulp, and are widely planted in forestry plantations (notably Monterey pine, Pinus radiata) and as ornamentals and hedges. They are also culturally prominent as Christmas trees.
