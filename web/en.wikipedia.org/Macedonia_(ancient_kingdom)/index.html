Macedonia (Greek: Μακεδονία, Makedonía), also Macedon, was an ancient kingdom on the periphery of Archaic and Classical Greece, later the dominant state of the Hellenistic world. It emerged during the eighth or seventh century BC under the Argead dynasty and rose to supremacy under Philip II (359–336 BC), who unified Greece under Macedonian hegemony. His son Alexander the Great overthrew the Persian Empire and carried Macedonian arms to Egypt and India, creating one of the largest empires of the ancient world.

Rise and peak

Early Macedonia was a relatively loose kingdom of the Lower Macedonian plain and the Upper Macedonian highland cantons. Philip II reformed the army — introducing the sarissa phalanx and combined-arms tactics — secured the gold mines of Mount Pangaeum, and defeated the Greek city-states at Chaeronea (338 BC). After Philip's assassination, Alexander conquered the Achaemenid Empire (334–330 BC), founded cities across Asia, and died at Babylon in 323 BC.

Wars of the Diadochi and Antigonid kingdom

Alexander's generals, the Diadochi, fought over his empire for decades. In Macedonia itself, the Antigonid dynasty established by Antigonus II Gonatas ruled from the 270s BC until Rome defeated Perseus at the Battle of Pydna in 168 BC, ending the kingdom, which became a Roman province in 146 BC.

Society and culture

The Macedonian court at Pella patronised Greek art and letters — Euripides wrote his final plays there, and Aristotle served as tutor to the young Alexander. Royal tombs at Vergina (ancient Aigai), identified in 1977, yielded treasures including gold larnakes bearing the sixteen-rayed "Vergina Sun" and the probable tomb of Philip II.
