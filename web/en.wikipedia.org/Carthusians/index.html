Carthusians

The Grande Chartreuse in the Chartreuse Mountains, France

The Carthusians, also known as the Order of Carthusians (Latin: Ordo Cartusiensis), are a Latin enclosed religious order of the Catholic Church. The order was founded by Bruno of Cologne in 1084 and includes both monks and nuns. The name derives from the Chartreuse Mountains in the French Alps, where Bruno built the first hermitage, the Grande Chartreuse.

Way of life

The Carthusian life combines solitary hermit living with a small measure of community life. Each monk lives in a small individual dwelling (a "cell") with a garden, where he spends most of the day in prayer, study, and manual work, receiving meals through a hatch. The monks come together for the night office, morning Mass, and vespers, and share a common meal only on Sundays and feast days, eaten in silence. The order is regarded as one of the most austere in the Catholic Church; the monks generally do not engage in outside ministry, and their houses are not open to casual visitors.

Unlike most other orders, the Carthusians never produced a formal reform movement; their traditional boast is numquam reformata, quia numquam deformata — "never reformed, because never deformed." Their statutes were first codified by the fifth prior, Guigo I, in the Consuetudines Cartusiae (c. 1127).

Chartreuse liqueur

The monks of the Grande Chartreuse are also known for producing Chartreuse, a herbal liqueur made from a secret recipe of 130 plants, first given to the order in 1605. Sales of the liqueur have historically provided much of the order's income.
