Lewis Fry Richardson

Lewis Fry Richardson, FRS (11 October 1881 – 30 September 1953) was an English mathematician, physicist, meteorologist, psychologist, and pacifist who pioneered modern mathematical techniques of weather forecasting, and the application of similar techniques to studying the causes of wars and how to prevent them. He is noted for his pioneering work in fractals — his study of coastlines anticipated the concept of fractal dimension — and for the Richardson extrapolation method in numerical analysis and the modified Richardson iteration. He was also an early developer of the mathematics of turbulence, commemorated in the Richardson number.

Weather forecasting

While working at the Meteorological Office and as an ambulance driver in France during the First World War, Richardson computed by hand a six-hour forecast from the equations of atmospheric motion — the work published as Weather Prediction by Numerical Process (1922). The forecast failed badly because of unbalanced initial data, but the book laid out the entire framework of numerical weather prediction decades before computers existed, including his famous vision of a "forecast factory" of tens of thousands of human computers. Modern forecasting vindicated his approach once electronic computers and data assimilation arrived.

War and peace research

A committed Quaker and pacifist — he was a conscientious objector in the First World War — Richardson applied statistics to conflict, modelling arms races with coupled differential equations (the Richardson model) and compiling data on wars in Statistics of Deadly Quarrels (published 1960) and Arms and Insecurity. He is regarded as a founder of the quantitative study of international conflict within peace and conflict studies.

Fractals and turbulence

Measuring coastlines and borders at different scales, Richardson found that measured length grows without bound as the measuring unit shrinks, following a power law; Benoit Mandelbrot later used Richardson's data in formulating the concept of fractals and the "coastline paradox". Richardson's whimsical rhyme — "Big whirls have little whirls that feed on their velocity, and little whirls have lesser whirls and so on to viscosity" — summarised the energy cascade in turbulence, an idea formalised in his 1922 work and later by Kolmogorov.
