Jewish ghettos in German-occupied Poland


Over 1,100 Jewish ghettos were established by Nazi Germany in occupied Poland during World War II.

Jewish ghettos in German-occupied Poland were established by Nazi Germany during World War II in order to segregate, exploit, and ultimately exterminate the Jewish population of Poland. More than 1,100 ghettos of varying size were created across the occupied territories between 1939 and 1942, holding roughly two million Jews in total.

Establishment

Following the invasion of Poland in September 1939, German authorities began concentrating Jews into designated districts of cities and towns. The first ghetto was established at Piotrków Trybunalski in October 1939. The largest were the Warsaw Ghetto, which at its peak held some 400,000–460,000 people in 3.4 square kilometres, and the Łódź Ghetto, which held around 200,000. Ghettos were sealed with walls, fences, and barbed wire; leaving without permission was punishable by death.

Conditions

Conditions inside were deliberately starvation-level: official rations in Warsaw in 1941 amounted to about 184 calories per day for Jews. Overcrowding, disease (especially typhus), cold, and hunger killed tens of thousands — in Warsaw, roughly 100,000 people died of starvation and disease before deportations to the death camps even began. Jews were administered by Jewish Councils (Judenräte) forced to implement German orders, and policed by the Jewish Ghetto Police.

Liquidation and resistance

Beginning in 1942, under Operation Reinhard, the ghettos were liquidated: their inhabitants were deported to extermination camps such as Treblinka, Bełżec, Sobibór, and Auschwitz, or shot in mass executions. In several ghettos Jews resisted — most famously in the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising of April–May 1943, but also in Białystok, Częstochowa, Będzin, and elsewhere. By the end of 1943 nearly all the ghettos had been destroyed; the Łódź Ghetto, the last major one, was liquidated in August 1944.
