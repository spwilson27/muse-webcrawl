Congress of Industrial Organizations

Founded9 November 1935 (as Committee for Industrial Organization)
Dissolved1955 (merged into AFL–CIO)

The Congress of Industrial Organizations (CIO) was a federation of unions that organised workers in industrial unions in the United States and Canada from 1935 to 1955. Created as the Committee for Industrial Organization within the American Federation of Labor (AFL) in 1935, it became a rival federation after its unions were expelled in 1936.

Founding and growth

Led by John L. Lewis of the United Mine Workers, the CIO championed industrial unionism — organising all workers in an industry regardless of craft — in mass-production sectors such as steel, automobiles, rubber and electrical manufacturing that the craft-oriented AFL had neglected. Landmark victories included the Flint sit-down strike of 1936–37, which forced General Motors to recognise the United Auto Workers, and the 1937 agreement with U.S. Steel. The Steel Workers Organizing Committee and "Operation Dixie" extended organising to the South with mixed success.

Politics and internal conflict

The CIO supported Franklin D. Roosevelt and the New Deal, creating the first modern political action committee, CIO-PAC, in 1943. After the Second World War, under Philip Murray, the CIO expelled eleven communist-led unions in 1949–50 during the early Cold War, losing roughly 900,000 members.

Merger

After the deaths of both federation presidents in 1952, George Meany (AFL) and Walter Reuther (CIO) negotiated reunification; the two federations merged in December 1955 to form the AFL–CIO.

Legacy

The CIO transformed American labour, raising wages and standards in basic industry and anchoring a labour–liberal political coalition for a generation.
