Direct current

Direct current (red) contrasted with alternating current (green)

Direct current (DC) is one-directional flow of electric charge. An electrochemical cell is a prime example of DC power; direct current may flow through a conductor such as a wire, but also through semiconductors, insulators, or even a vacuum, as in electron or ion beams. The electric current flows in a constant direction, distinguishing it from alternating current (AC).

History

The first commercial electric power transmission, developed by Thomas Edison in the early 1880s, used direct current. Because AC could be transformed to high voltages for efficient long-distance transmission, it won the “war of the currents” for general distribution. Nevertheless, DC remained standard for batteries, electronics, and many specialized applications.

Sources and conversion

DC is produced by batteries, fuel cells, solar cells, and dynamo-type generators with commutators. Alternating current can be converted to direct current by a rectifier, and DC can be converted to AC by an inverter. High-voltage direct current (HVDC) is used for bulk long-distance power transmission and submarine cables, where its lower losses outweigh conversion costs.

Applications

Virtually all electronic devices run internally on DC. Other applications include electroplating, railway traction, telecommunications power plants, electric vehicles, and charging portable devices via USB.
