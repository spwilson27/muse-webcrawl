United States v. Schooner Amistad, 40 U.S. (15 Pet.) 518 (1841), was a United States Supreme Court case resulting from a revolt by enslaved Africans aboard the Spanish schooner La Amistad in 1839. The Court held that the Africans had been illegally enslaved and transported in violation of international treaties, and that they were therefore free and entitled to resist their captors. Former president John Quincy Adams argued the Africans' case before the Court.

Background

In 1839, a group of Mende captives led by Sengbe Pieh (Joseph Cinqué) seized control of La Amistad off the coast of Cuba, killing the captain and the cook. The ship was intercepted by the USS Washington and brought to Connecticut. Spain demanded the return of the ship, cargo, and Africans as property under treaty; abolitionists in the United States defended the Africans, arguing they were free people kidnapped in Africa.

Decision

Justice Joseph Story delivered the opinion of the Court. The Court held that the Africans were not slaves under Spanish law, because the transatlantic slave trade had been banned by treaty and by Spanish law; they had been "kidnapped" and unlawfully transported. As free people, they were entitled to assert their liberty, and the United States had no obligation to return them to Spain. The Court ordered them discharged from custody.

Legacy

The decision was a landmark victory for the abolitionist movement, though the Court did not challenge the institution of slavery within the United States itself. The surviving Africans returned to Sierra Leone in 1842 with the help of the Amistad Committee, a precursor of the American Missionary Association. The story was dramatized in the 1997 film Amistad, directed by Steven Spielberg.
