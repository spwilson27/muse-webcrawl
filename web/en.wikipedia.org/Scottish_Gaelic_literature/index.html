Scottish Gaelic literature

Scottish Gaelic literature refers to literature composed in the Scottish Gaelic language, a Goidelic language spoken in Scotland. It shares its earliest origins with Irish literature, from which it gradually diverged after the Middle Ages.

Early literature

Gaelic was the language of the Scottish court and of much of the country from the early medieval period. Early works include heroic tales, religious verse, and genealogies, much of it transmitted orally and shared with the Gaelic tradition of Ireland. The Book of the Dean of Lismore, compiled in the early sixteenth century, is a major anthology of Gaelic poetry from Scotland and Ireland.

The age of the bards

Professional bardic poets, trained in classical Gaelic metres, served the clan chiefs of the Highlands and Islands. Notable figures include Mary MacLeod (Màiri Nighean Alasdair Ruaidh) and Iain Lom. Vernacular poets of the eighteenth century, such as Alasdair mac Mhaighstir Alasdair, who published the first secular book in Scottish Gaelic in 1751, and Duncan Ban MacIntyre, brought new vigour to the tradition; Rob Donn Mackay chronicled the society of Sutherland.

Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

The nineteenth century saw Gaelic prose, religious writing, and an expanding Gaelic press, even as the language declined under pressure from clearance, emigration, and anglicising education. The twentieth century brought a major revival led by Sorley MacLean, whose Dàin do Eimhir (1940) transformed Gaelic poetry, alongside Derick Thomson, Iain Crichton Smith, and George Campbell Hay.

Contemporary literature

Modern Gaelic literature includes poetry, novels, drama, and children's books, supported by publishers, the Gaelic Books Council, and awards such as the Highland Book Prize. Writers such as Aonghas MacNeacail and Christopher Whyte have extended Gaelic writing into new forms.
