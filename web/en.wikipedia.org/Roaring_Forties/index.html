Roaring Forties

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Roaring Forties are strong westerly winds that occur in the Southern Hemisphere, generally between the latitudes of 40° and 50° south. The strong west-to-east air currents are caused by the combination of air being displaced from the Equator towards the South Pole, Earth's rotation, and the scarcity of landmasses to serve as windbreaks at those latitudes.

The Roaring Forties were a major aid to ships sailing the clipper route from Europe to the East Indies or Australia and New Zealand, and in modern usage are favoured by yachtsmen sailing round-the-world races and competitions. The boundaries of the Roaring Forties are not consistent; they shift with the seasons and with the position of the subtropical ridge.

Mechanism

Warm air rising at the Equator moves poleward in the upper atmosphere, cools, and descends around latitude 30° south, forming the subtropical high-pressure belt. Surface air flowing back toward the Equator is deflected, but a substantial portion continues poleward between the subtropical high and the subpolar low near 60° south; the Coriolis effect deflects this flow to the left in the Southern Hemisphere, producing prevailing winds from the west. Because the Southern Ocean offers an almost unbroken stretch of water around Antarctica, there is little friction from land to slow the winds, and they reach exceptional average speeds.

Terminology

Sailors extended the scheme with further terms for the even more violent winds found further south: the Furious Fifties between 50° and 60° south, and the Shrieking or Screaming Sixties below 60° south. Conditions in these regions remain among the most demanding encountered by mariners.

Effect on climate

The Roaring Forties drive the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, the world's largest ocean current, and bring frequent rain-bearing depressions to southern New Zealand, Tasmania, southern Chile and Argentina, and the Cape region of South Africa.
