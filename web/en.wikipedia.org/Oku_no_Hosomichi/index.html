Oku no Hosomichi

Oku no Hosomichi (奥の細道), often translated as The Narrow Road to the Deep North or The Narrow Road to the Interior, is a major work of haibun by the Japanese poet Matsuo Bashō. It is considered one of the masterpieces of Japanese literature and one of the great travel writings of world literature.

The journey

In the spring of 1689, Bashō, then in his mid-forties, set out from Edo (modern Tokyo) with his disciple Kawai Sora on a journey of some 2,400 kilometres (1,500 miles) through the northern provinces of Honshu. The journey lasted about 150 days. Bashō visited sites famous in earlier poetry — the utamakura of the Tōhoku region, including Matsushima, Hiraizumi and Kisakata — measuring his own perceptions against the verses of poets such as Saigyō.

The text

The work alternates prose passages with haiku, in the mixed form known as haibun. Bashō revised the text over several years; it was published posthumously in 1702. The opening lines are among the most famous in Japanese:

"The months and days are the travellers of eternity. So too are the years that come and go..."

The work's episodes move between melancholy reflections on impermanence, accounts of weather and hardship on the road, and haiku crystallising a scene — for example, at the ruins of Hiraizumi, where the Fujiwara clan had fallen: "Summer grasses — / all that remains / of warriors' dreams."

Legacy

Oku no Hosomichi fixed the itinerary of a literary pilgrimage still retraced today, and established the model of the poetic travel diary in Japan. Numerous English translations exist, including those by Nobuyuki Yuasa, Donald Keene and Sam Hamill.
