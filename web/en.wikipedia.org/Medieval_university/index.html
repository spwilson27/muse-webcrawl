A medieval university was an institution of higher learning founded in Europe during the High and Late Middle Ages. The word universitas originally referred to the guild-like corporation of teachers or students — the universitas magistrorum et scholarium — rather than to the institution itself. The earliest universities, at Bologna, Paris, and Oxford, emerged in the late 11th and 12th centuries from cathedral schools and private teaching, and they established the degree structure of bachelor, master, and doctor that persists today.

Origins

At Bologna, students — many of them mature laymen studying Roman law after the rediscovery of Justinian's Digest — organized themselves into guilds ("nations") to protect their interests against the town and hired the professors. At Paris, by contrast, the masters formed the ruling corporation around the cathedral school of Notre Dame. Pope Gregory IX's bull Parens scientiarum (1231) granted Paris a measure of self-governance and is often called the Magna Carta of the university. Oxford developed after 1167, when Henry II recalled English scholars from Paris; Cambridge arose from an exodus of Oxford scholars in 1209.

Organization and curriculum

Every student began in the faculty of arts, studying the trivium (grammar, rhetoric, dialectic) and quadrivium (arithmetic, geometry, music, astronomy), largely through Aristotle's logic and natural philosophy. Graduates could proceed to the higher faculties of law, medicine, or theology — the most prestigious but longest course, sometimes requiring a decade or more. Teaching took the form of the lecture (lectio) on a set text and the disputation (disputatio), in which questions were argued on both sides before a determination was given.

Student life

Students, who might begin the arts course as young as fourteen, were tonsured clerics enjoying benefit of clergy. They lodged in rented halls and later in endowed colleges such as the Sorbonne (founded 1257). Relations with townspeople were frequently tense — "town and gown" riots could lead to strikes, migrations, and the founding of new universities. By 1500 there were some eighty universities in Europe, and the institution had spread from Iberia to Scandinavia and Poland.

Legacy

The medieval university created a self-governing, degree-granting corporation devoted to teaching and disputation, preserved and transmitted classical and Arabic learning, and trained the clerks, lawyers, and administrators of church and state. Many of its features — faculties, examinations, academic dress, and Latin terminology — survive in universities worldwide.
