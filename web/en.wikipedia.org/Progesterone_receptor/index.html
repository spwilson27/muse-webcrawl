Progesterone receptor

The progesterone receptor (PR), also known as NR3C3 (nuclear receptor subfamily 3, group C, member 3), is a protein found inside cells that is activated by the steroid hormone progesterone. It is encoded by the PGR gene in humans, located on chromosome 11q22. As a member of the nuclear receptor superfamily, the progesterone receptor acts as a ligand-activated transcription factor regulating the expression of genes involved in female reproduction, the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and embryogenesis.

Isoforms

The PGR gene produces two main isoforms from alternate promoters: PR-A and the longer PR-B. The isoforms have distinct and sometimes opposing transcriptional activities; their relative expression varies between tissues and is altered in diseases such as breast and endometrial cancer. A third, truncated isoform, PR-C, has also been described.

Mechanism of action

In the absence of hormone, the receptor resides in the cell (predominantly the nucleus) in a complex with heat-shock proteins. Binding of progesterone causes a conformational change, dissociation of chaperones, dimerisation, and binding to progesterone response elements in the DNA, recruiting coactivators or corepressors to modulate transcription. Progesterone also acts through membrane-associated receptors to produce rapid, non-genomic effects.

Clinical significance

Progesterone receptor status is routinely measured in breast cancer, together with estrogen receptor and HER2 status, to guide endocrine therapy. Synthetic progestins (agonists) are used in contraception and hormone therapy, while antagonists (antiprogestogens) such as mifepristone are used medically to terminate pregnancy and in the treatment of certain hormone-sensitive tumours. Selective progesterone receptor modulators (SPRMs) combine agonist and antagonist activity.
