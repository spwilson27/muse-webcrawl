The Himalayas, or Himalaya (from Sanskrit Himālaya, "abode of snow"), is a mountain range in Asia separating the plains of the Indian subcontinent from the Tibetan Plateau. The range contains some of the planet's highest peaks, including the highest, Mount Everest (8,848.86 m). More than one hundred Himalayan peaks exceed 7,200 m (23,600 ft) in elevation.

The Himalayan range runs for about 2,400 km (1,500 mi) from Nanga Parbat in the west to Namcha Barwa in the east, in an arc through Pakistan, India, Nepal, Bhutan and China. Together with the adjacent Karakoram and Hindu Kush, the system is sometimes called the Hindu Kush–Karakoram–Himalaya region. The range is the source of three of the world's major river systems — the Indus, the Ganges and the Brahmaputra — whose combined drainage basin is home to some 600 million people. Because its glaciers hold the largest store of ice outside the polar regions, the Himalaya are sometimes called the "Third Pole".

Geology

The Himalayas began to form about 50 million years ago when the Indian Plate, moving northwards, collided with the Eurasian Plate, closing the ancient Tethys Ocean. The collision continues today: India still converges with Asia at roughly 40–50 mm per year, and the range is uplifted by several millimetres annually, making it geologically young and seismically active. The range consists of parallel belts: the Greater Himalaya (containing the highest peaks), the Lesser Himalaya, and the outermost Siwalik foothills, separated by major thrust faults including the Main Central Thrust and the Main Boundary Thrust.

Climate and ecology

The range forms a barrier to the monsoon winds from the Indian Ocean, producing heavy rainfall on the southern slopes and arid conditions on the Tibetan Plateau to the north. Altitudinal zonation runs from tropical forest at the foothills through temperate and alpine zones to permanent snow above about 5,000–6,000 m. The region is a biodiversity hotspot, home to snow leopards, red pandas, Bengal tigers in the Terai lowlands and the one-horned rhinoceros.

Peaks

Notable peaks include Everest (8,849 m), K2 (8,611 m, in the Karakoram), Kangchenjunga (8,586 m), Lhotse (8,516 m), Makalu (8,485 m), Cho Oyu (8,188 m), Dhaulagiri (8,167 m), Manaslu (8,163 m), Nanga Parbat (8,126 m) and Annapurna I (8,091 m) — ten of the world's fourteen eight-thousanders lie in the Himalaya proper. Everest was first climbed in 1953 by Edmund Hillary and Tenzing Norgay.

Culture and religion

The Himalayas have profound significance in Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism and Sikhism; Mount Kailash is sacred in four religions, and the range is personified in Hindu tradition as Himavat, father of the goddess Parvati. Monasteries such as Tengboche, pilgrimage circuits such as the Char Dham, and the kingdom-turned-democracy of Nepal and the kingdom of Bhutan give the region a rich cultural landscape.
