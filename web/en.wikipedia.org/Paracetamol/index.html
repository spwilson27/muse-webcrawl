Paracetamol

Paracetamol, also known as acetaminophen, is a widely used non-opioid analgesic and antipyretic agent used to treat fever and mild to moderate pain. It is commonly sold under brand names such as Tylenol and Panadol. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines and is available as a generic medication.

Paracetamol is generally safe at recommended doses — up to about 4 grams per day for adults — but overdose can cause potentially fatal liver damage; paracetamol poisoning is one of the most common causes of acute liver failure in the Western world. The antidote is acetylcysteine. Unlike non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), paracetamol has only weak anti-inflammatory effects in peripheral tissues and does not significantly irritate the stomach.

History

Paracetamol was first synthesized in 1877 by Harmon Northrop Morse at Johns Hopkins University. It came into widespread medical use in the 1950s after it was identified as the active metabolite of acetanilide and phenacetin, and it gradually displaced those older, more toxic drugs. Its precise mechanism of action remains debated, but it is thought to act mainly in the central nervous system, inhibiting cyclooxygenase enzymes and modulating endocannabinoid and serotonergic pathways.

Medical uses

Paracetamol is a first-line treatment for fever in children and adults and is used for headaches, muscle aches, backache, toothache, osteoarthritis pain, and the symptoms of colds and influenza. It is frequently combined with opioid analgesics for more severe pain and with other ingredients in cold and flu preparations.
