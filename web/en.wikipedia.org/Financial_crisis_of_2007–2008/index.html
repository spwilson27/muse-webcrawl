The financial crisis of 2007–2008, also known as the global financial crisis (GFC), was a severe worldwide economic crisis that occurred in the late 2000s. It was the most serious financial crisis since the Great Depression. Predatory lending in the form of subprime mortgages targeted at low-income homebuyers, excessive risk-taking by global financial institutions, and the bursting of the United States housing bubble culminated in a "perfect storm".

Mortgage-backed securities (MBS) tied to American real estate, as well as a vast web of derivatives linked to those MBS, collapsed in value. Financial institutions worldwide suffered severe damage, reaching a climax with the bankruptcy of the investment bank Lehman Brothers on 15 September 2008 and an international banking crisis that followed.

Causes

The crisis was precipitated by the bursting of the United States housing bubble, which peaked in early 2006, causing the values of securities tied to U.S. real estate prices to plummet and damaging financial institutions globally. Questions regarding bank solvency, declines in credit availability, and damaged investor confidence had an impact on global stock markets, where securities suffered large losses during late 2008 and early 2009. The crisis played a significant role in the failure of key businesses, declines in consumer wealth estimated in trillions of U.S. dollars, and a downturn in economic activity leading to the Great Recession of 2008–2012 and contributing to the European sovereign-debt crisis.

Subprime mortgage crisis

The proliferation of subprime lending — high-risk loans to borrowers with poor credit — was enabled by government policies, competitive pressure between lenders, and rising housing prices that made refinancing seem safe. When house prices began to fall in 2006, mortgage delinquencies soared, and the securities backed by those mortgages, held by investors around the world, lost most of their value.

Policy responses

Governments and central banks responded with unprecedented fiscal and monetary policy measures, including the $700 billion Troubled Asset Relief Program (TARP) in the United States, near-zero interest rates, and quantitative easing by the Federal Reserve and other central banks. The crisis led to major regulatory reforms, most notably the Dodd–Frank Wall Street Reform and Consumer Protection Act of 2010 in the United States and the Basel III international banking standards.

Aftermath

The active phase of the crisis, taking the form of a liquidity crisis, can be dated from August 2007, when BNP Paribas halted withdrawals from three hedge funds citing "a complete evaporation of liquidity". The crisis was followed by the Great Recession, which drove unemployment in the United States to 10 percent in October 2009 and caused millions of home foreclosures.
