The Ute Mountain Ute Tribe is a federally recognized tribe of Ute people, descendants principally of the Weeminuche band, headquartered in Towaoc, Colorado. Its reservation, the Ute Mountain Reservation, lies in the Four Corners region of southwestern Colorado, northwestern New Mexico and southeastern Utah, beneath Sleeping Ute Mountain, which is sacred to the tribe. A separate community, White Mesa, in Utah, is also part of the tribe.

History

The Utes were the dominant people of the Colorado Plateau and central Rockies for centuries. Nineteenth-century treaties (1849, 1863, 1868) were followed by gold and silver rushes, war and pressure for removal. After the 1879 Meeker incident, most Colorado Utes were removed to Utah. The Weeminuche, under Chief Ignacio, refused removal eastward and in 1895 settled the western strip of the Southern Ute Reservation; in 1900 this became a separate reservation, and the community formed its own tribal government under the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934.

In the 20th century the tribe pursued land and water claims; the Colorado Ute Indian Water Rights Settlement of 1988 secured water delivered in part through the Dolores Project and McPhee Reservoir. Management of ancient sites is a major responsibility: the reservation includes the Ute Mountain Tribal Park, an archaeologically rich area adjacent to Mesa Verde National Park with Ancestral Puebloan cliff dwellings, which the tribe opens through guided tours led by Ute guides.

Government and economy

The tribe is governed by an elected Tribal Council and chairman under its constitution. Enterprises include the Ute Mountain Casino Hotel near Towaoc, farming and ranching operations, construction, and oil and gas development. The tribal pottery tradition, carried on by Ute Mountain Pottery, continues an ancient craft of the region.

Culture

The Ute language, a Southern Numic language of the Uto-Aztecan family, is taught in tribal programmes; annual events include the Bear Dance, one of the oldest Ute ceremonies, and the tribe's traditional ties to Sleeping Ute Mountain remain central to its identity.
