On Death and Dying

On Death and Dying is a 1969 book by the Swiss-American psychiatrist Elisabeth Kübler-Ross. Based on interviews with terminally ill patients, it introduced the model popularly known as the five stages of grief: denial, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance. The book transformed public discussion of death in Western medicine and helped launch the modern hospice and palliative care movement.

Contents

Working at the University of Chicago's Billings Hospital, Kübler-Ross organised seminars in which dying patients spoke to medical students and staff about their experience — an unusual practice at a time when death was largely taboo in clinical settings. The book presents the patients' voices and describes five responses she observed in people confronting their own terminal diagnosis:

  1. Denial — refusing to accept the diagnosis.
  2. Anger — resentment directed at others, fate or God.
  3. Bargaining — attempts to negotiate for more time.
  4. Depression — grief over losses already suffered and anticipated.
  5. Acceptance — a quiet readiness, neither happy nor bitter.

Reception and criticism

The book was a bestseller and remains widely influential in nursing, medicine and counselling. Subsequent research has questioned whether the stages form a universal sequence: studies have found that acceptance is often present from the start, that the stages do not occur in a fixed order, and that the model is frequently misapplied — notably, it was derived from the dying themselves, not from bereaved people, though it is now most often invoked about bereavement. Kübler-Ross herself cautioned that the stages were not a linear checklist.
