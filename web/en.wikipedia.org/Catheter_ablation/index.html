Catheter ablation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Catheter ablation is a minimally invasive procedure used to treat cardiac arrhythmias by destroying (ablating) small areas of heart tissue that cause or sustain abnormal electrical signals. A thin, flexible catheter is threaded through a blood vessel — usually from the groin — into the heart under X-ray and electroanatomic guidance, and energy is delivered to the target tissue via its tip.

Procedure

An electrophysiology study first maps the heart's electrical activity to locate the abnormal pathway or focus. Ablation energy may be radiofrequency (heating the tissue to form a scar), cryoablation (freezing), or, more recently, pulsed-field ablation, which uses short high-voltage pulses that selectively destroy myocardial cells. Success is confirmed by re-testing whether the arrhythmia can still be induced.

Conditions treated

  • Atrial fibrillation — the most common indication; isolation of the pulmonary veins electrically disconnects the usual source of triggers.
  • Supraventricular tachycardias — AV nodal reentrant tachycardia, atrioventricular reentrant tachycardia via accessory pathways (as in Wolff–Parkinson–White syndrome), and atrial flutter, with success rates often above 95%.
  • Ventricular arrhythmias — premature ventricular contractions and scar-related ventricular tachycardia.

Risks and outcomes

Serious complications are uncommon (roughly 1–3%) and include bleeding or vessel injury, stroke, cardiac tamponade, pulmonary vein stenosis, and heart block occasionally requiring a pacemaker. For many arrhythmias ablation offers a cure that frees patients from lifelong medication; atrial fibrillation ablation reduces symptoms and recurrence but may need repeat procedures.
