Sericulture

Silkworms and cocoons in sericulture

Sericulture, or silk farming, is the cultivation of silkworms to produce silk. Although there are several commercial species of silkworms, Bombyx mori (the caterpillar of the domestic silkmoth) is the most widely used and intensively studied silkworm. Silk was first produced in China as early as the Neolithic period, and sericulture remained a closely guarded Chinese industry for millennia before spreading along the Silk Road.

Production process

Sericulture involves cultivating mulberry trees, whose leaves are the sole food of Bombyx mori larvae. Female moths lay several hundred eggs, which are incubated until they hatch into tiny larvae. The larvae feed continuously for several weeks, moulting four times, before spinning cocoons of a single continuous silk filament up to about a kilometre long. The cocoons are treated with hot air or water to kill the pupa and soften the sericin gum, allowing the filament to be reeled off, twisted into yarn and woven into fabric.

History

According to Chinese tradition, silk production was discovered by the empress Leizu in the 27th century BCE. Archaeological evidence of silk fabrics dates back several thousand years in China. The industry spread to Korea, Japan, India and eventually to the Mediterranean and Europe; the Byzantine Empire acquired silkworm eggs, by tradition smuggled from China by monks in the sixth century CE, breaking the Chinese monopoly.

Modern industry

Today China and India are the world's largest silk producers, together accounting for the great majority of global output. Sericulture is a labour-intensive rural industry employing millions of households. Wild silks such as tussah, eri and muga are produced from other moth species. The pupae left after reeling are often used as food or animal feed.
