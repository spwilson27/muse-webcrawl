A chordate is a deuterostome animal belonging to the phylum Chordata. All chordates possess, at some point during their larval or adult stages, five distinctive physical characteristics (synapomorphies) that distinguish them from other animals: a notochord, a hollow dorsal nerve cord, an endostyle or thyroid, pharyngeal slits, and a post-anal tail. Chordates include the vertebrates — fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals, including humans — as well as the invertebrate lancelets and tunicates.

Anatomy

The notochord is a flexible rod of vacuolated cells that provides skeletal support; in most vertebrates it is largely replaced by the vertebral column during development, persisting as the nucleus pulposus of intervertebral discs. The dorsal nerve cord, unlike the ventral cord of most invertebrates, lies above the gut and in vertebrates develops into the brain and spinal cord. Pharyngeal slits, used for filter feeding in early chordates, persist in modified form in fishes (gill slits) and in embryos of tetrapods.

Classification

  • Cephalochordata (lancelets/amphioxus): small, fish-like filter feeders retaining all five traits as adults.
  • Tunicata (tunicates/sea squirts): adults are mostly sessile filter feeders that lose the notochord and tail; molecular evidence places them as the vertebrates' closest relatives.
  • Vertebrata (craniates): possess a skull and usually vertebrae; includes hagfish, lampreys, cartilaginous and bony fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds, and mammals.

Evolution

Chordates first appear in the Cambrian explosion, about 525 million years ago, in fossils such as Pikaia and the Chengjiang fish-like Haikouichthys and Myllokunmingia. Together with echinoderms and hemichordates, chordates form the deuterostomes. Their body plan — dorsal cord, segmented musculature, and post-anal tail — underlies the anatomy of all vertebrate life on land and sea.
