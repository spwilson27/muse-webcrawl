Marian reforms

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Roman legionaries carrying their own equipment — "Marius's mules"]

The Marian reforms were changes traditionally attributed to the Roman statesman and general Gaius Marius, consul in 107 BC and repeatedly thereafter, that transformed the Roman army from a citizen militia of property owners into a professional standing force recruited from the landless poor.

Principal changes

The reforms are conventionally said to include: abolishing the property qualification for enlistment and recruiting the capite censi (head-count poor); state provision of equipment; organising the legion into ten cohorts of about 480 men instead of the manipular system; making the eagle (aquila) the sole standard of the legion; requiring soldiers to carry their own kit (earning them the nickname "Marius's mules"); and promising land grants on discharge, which tied veterans' welfare to their generals.

Scholarship

Modern historians debate how much Marius personally introduced, with many changes now seen as gradual developments over the 2nd century BC rather than a single reform package; the term "Marian reforms" as a discrete event has been questioned.

Consequences

Whatever their precise origin, the changes produced long-service professional legions loyal to their commanders, a development often linked to the civil wars of the late Republic—Marius himself, Sulla, Pompey and Caesar all marched armies drawn from this new model against Rome or its enemies.
