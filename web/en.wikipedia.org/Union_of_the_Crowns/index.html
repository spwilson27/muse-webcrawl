Union of the Crowns

The Union of the Crowns refers to the accession in 1603 of James VI of Scotland to the thrones of England and Ireland as James I, uniting the three kingdoms under a single monarch. It was a dynastic or personal union only: Scotland and England remained separate sovereign states with their own parliaments, laws, churches, and currencies until the political union of 1707 created the Kingdom of Great Britain.

Background

The union became possible through the marriage in 1503 of James IV of Scotland to Margaret Tudor, daughter of Henry VII of England — the "Marriage of the Thistle and the Rose". A century later their great-grandson, James VI, was the closest Protestant heir of the childless Elizabeth I. On her death on 24 March 1603, James was proclaimed king in London and travelled south, the first Scottish king to rule England since the succession was settled.

James's project of union

James styled himself "King of Great Britain" by proclamation in 1604 and pressed for a fuller incorporating union, including a common name, flag, and free trade. The English Parliament resisted many of these proposals, though a union flag combining the crosses of St George and St Andrew was adopted in 1606, and Scots born after 1603 were eventually recognised as naturalised in England following the legal case known as Calvin's Case (1608).

Legacy

The personal union lasted through the Stuart period, interrupted by the Commonwealth, until the Acts of Union 1707 joined the parliaments. The crowns of the United Kingdom and the other Commonwealth realms are still united in one person today, and the events of 1603 remain a landmark in the constitutional history of Britain.
