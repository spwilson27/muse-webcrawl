Political realism

SchoolInternational relations theory
Key thinkersNiccolò Machiavelli, Thomas Hobbes, Hans Morgenthau, Kenneth Waltz
Core assumptionsAnarchy; states seek power and survival

Political realism, or simply realism, is a school of thought in international relations that views the world as an anarchic arena in which sovereign states, lacking any authority above them, must pursue power and security to survive. It is one of the oldest and most influential paradigms in the study of world politics.

Assumptions

Realists hold that the international system is anarchic, that states are its principal actors, and that states act rationally to advance their national interest, defined largely in terms of power. Because no world government can protect them, states rely on self-help, balancing, and deterrence.

Branches

Classical realism, exemplified by Hans Morgenthau's Politics Among Nations (1948), roots conflict in human nature. Neorealism or structural realism, founded by Kenneth Waltz in Theory of International Politics (1979), instead derives state behaviour from the structure of the system, and divides into defensive realism, which sees states as security maximizers, and offensive realism (John Mearsheimer), which sees them as power maximizers. Neoclassical realism combines systemic and domestic-level variables.

History and criticism

Realists claim a lineage from Thucydides' account of the Peloponnesian War through Machiavelli and Hobbes. Liberal and constructivist scholars criticize realism for underplaying international institutions, economic interdependence, and ideas, and for failing to predict the peaceful end of the Cold War.
