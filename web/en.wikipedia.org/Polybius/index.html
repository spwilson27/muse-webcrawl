Polybius

Bornc. 200 BC, Megalopolis, Achaea
Diedc. 118 BC
OccupationHistorian, statesman
Major workThe Histories (40 books; 6 substantially survive)

Polybius (Greek: Πολύβιος, c. 200 – c. 118 BC) was a Greek historian of the Hellenistic period, famous for his work The Histories, which covered the period 264–146 BC and explained how Rome came to dominate the Mediterranean world in a little over fifty years.

Life

The son of the Achaean statesman Lycortas, Polybius became hipparch (cavalry commander) of the Achaean League. After Rome's victory in the Third Macedonian War he was taken to Italy as one of a thousand Achaean hostages in 167 BC. There he befriended the general Scipio Aemilianus, whom he later accompanied at the destruction of Carthage in 146 BC, and after his release he helped settle Greek affairs under Roman rule.

The Histories

Polybius advocated a pragmatic, evidence-based history: he interviewed witnesses, travelled widely, and criticized armchair historians such as Timaeus. Book 6 presents his influential theory of anacyclosis, the cycle of constitutions, and praises Rome's mixed constitution — combining consuls, Senate, and people — as the secret of its stability and success.

Influence

Polybius' constitutional analysis influenced Cicero, Machiavelli, Montesquieu, and the framers of the United States Constitution. Only the first five books survive complete, with substantial fragments of the rest.
