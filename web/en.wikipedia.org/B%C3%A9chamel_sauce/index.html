Béchamel sauce

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Béchamel sauce is a white sauce made from a roux of butter and flour cooked in milk. It is one of the five "mother sauces" of French cuisine codified by Auguste Escoffier, and forms the base for many other sauces and dishes, including lasagne, gratins, macaroni and cheese, and moussaka.

TypeMother sauce
Main ingredientsMilk, butter, flour
CuisineFrench

Preparation

A white roux is prepared by cooking equal parts butter and flour briefly without colouring, then hot or cold milk is whisked in gradually and the sauce simmered until it thickens and loses any taste of raw flour. Classic seasoning is salt, white pepper and a pinch of nutmeg; Escoffier's version includes an onion studded with cloves steeped in the sauce. Consistency is adjusted for use — thinner for saucing, thicker as a binder for croquettes or soufflés.

History

The sauce is traditionally named for Louis de Béchameil, marquis de Nointel, a financier and steward in the court of Louis XIV, though milk-thickened sauces existed earlier, and Italian besciamella has a parallel history. Antonin Carême and then Escoffier classified béchamel among the foundational sauces from which derivative "small sauces" — such as Mornay (with cheese), soubise (with onion) and crème — are made.

Uses

Béchamel binds the layers of lasagne alla bolognese and Greek moussaka, tops croque-monsieur, and enriches gratins and pasta bakes. In home cooking its role is often played by simple white sauce.
