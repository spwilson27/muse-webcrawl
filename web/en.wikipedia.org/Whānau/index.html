Whānau (Māori pronunciation: [ˈfaːnaʉ]) is a word in te reo Māori meaning an extended family or family group — the basic unit of traditional Māori society. A whānau typically consists of several generations descended from a common ancestor, including grandparents, parents, children and more distant relatives, bound together by kinship, shared responsibilities and mutual support.

Traditional structure

In traditional Māori social organisation, whānau form part of larger descent groups: several whānau make up a hapū (subtribe), and hapū in turn form an iwi (tribe). The whānau was the unit that held and worked land, raised children collectively — with grandparents playing a central role — and cared for the elderly. Elders (kaumātua) are accorded particular respect within the whānau.

Modern usage

The word has entered New Zealand English in a broader sense, referring to family and close kin-like networks, and appears in government and social-service contexts — for example the "Whānau Ora" policy approach, which delivers health and social support through whānau-centred services. Concepts of whānau may also extend, in modern usage, to groups bound by a common purpose rather than descent alone (kaupapa whānau).
