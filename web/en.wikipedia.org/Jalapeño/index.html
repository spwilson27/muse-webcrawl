Jalapeño

The jalapeño (Spanish: [xalaˈpeɲo]) is a medium-sized chili pepper pod type of the species Capsicum annuum. It is named after Xalapa (also spelled Jalapa), the capital of the Mexican state of Veracruz, where it was traditionally grown. A mature jalapeño is typically 5–10 cm (2–4 in) long, firm and smooth, and is usually harvested and eaten while still green, though it ripens to red, orange or yellow. Jalapeños are among the most widely recognised hot peppers in the world and a staple of Mexican and Tex-Mex cuisine.

Cultivation

Jalapeños are grown commercially in Mexico — especially Veracruz, Chihuahua and the Papaloapan basin — and extensively in the United States, notably in New Mexico, Texas and California. The plants grow about 60–90 cm tall and produce 25–35 pods each over a season. Pods are picked green for fresh and pickled markets; fruit left to ripen red may be smoke-dried to produce chipotle peppers, the morita and meco types. Mexico's annual jalapeño crop is measured in the hundreds of thousands of tonnes, and large acreage also exists in China, Thailand and Spain.

Heat and flavour

The pungency comes from capsaicin concentrated in the pale placental tissue inside the pod. Jalapeños rate around 3,500 to 8,000 Scoville heat units — hotter than a poblano but much milder than a habanero — though heat varies greatly with growing conditions and cultivar. Some modern varieties have been bred for little or no heat.

Culinary use

Jalapeños are eaten fresh in salsas such as pico de gallo, sliced over nachos and tacos, pickled (often with carrots and onions as jalapeños en escabeche), stuffed with cheese and grilled or breaded as "poppers", and blended into hot sauces. In Vietnam they commonly appear in phở as a fresh garnish. The peppers provide good amounts of vitamin C and provitamin A.
