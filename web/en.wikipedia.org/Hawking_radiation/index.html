Hawking radiation is black body radiation that is predicted to be released by black holes because of quantum effects near the black hole event horizon. It is named after the physicist Stephen Hawking, who provided a theoretical argument for its existence in 1974. The radiation implies that black holes have a temperature — the Hawking temperature, inversely proportional to their mass — and slowly lose mass and energy, eventually evaporating completely.

Overview

Hawking's calculation combined quantum field theory with the curved spacetime of general relativity. In the popular (though simplified) picture, pairs of virtual particles and antiparticles continually appear near the event horizon; if one partner falls into the hole with negative energy relative to an outside observer while the other escapes, the escaping particle carries away energy, reducing the black hole's mass. More accurately, Hawking showed that the quantum vacuum state near a forming black hole produces a steady flux of particles at infinity with a thermal spectrum.

Temperature and evaporation

The Hawking temperature is T = ħc³ / (8πGMkB), where M is the black hole's mass. For a solar-mass black hole this is about 60 nanokelvin — far colder than the cosmic microwave background — so astrophysical black holes currently absorb more energy than they emit. A black hole's evaporation time scales with the cube of its mass; a solar-mass hole would take around 1067 years to evaporate, while a primordial black hole of about 1012 kg would be completing its evaporation today in a burst of gamma rays, a signature searched for but not conclusively observed.

Black hole thermodynamics

Hawking radiation completed the analogy between black hole mechanics and thermodynamics developed by Jacob Bekenstein and others: the event horizon's area plays the role of entropy (Bekenstein–Hawking entropy), and Hawking's result fixed the temperature, making the analogy exact. The result also raised the black hole information paradox: if a black hole evaporates into featureless thermal radiation, what happens to the information about matter that fell in — apparently violating quantum mechanics' principle of unitarity? The paradox remains a central problem in quantum gravity research, addressed by proposals including black hole complementarity, holography and the AdS/CFT correspondence, firewalls, and Hawking's own later work with collaborators on "soft hair".

Experimental status

Direct detection of Hawking radiation from astrophysical black holes is beyond current instruments because of its extreme faintness. Analogue systems — such as sonic horizons in Bose–Einstein condensates and optical analogues — have reported observations of correlated emissions consistent with Hawking-like radiation, supporting the underlying physics.
