3D film

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Modern 3D cinema audiences wear lightweight circular-polarized glasses.

3D films are motion pictures made to give the illusion of three-dimensional solidity, usually with the help of special viewing devices such as polarized or active-shutter glasses. Stereoscopic cinema exploits binocular disparity: two images, photographed from slightly different viewpoints corresponding to the separation of the eyes, are presented separately to the left and right eyes, and the brain fuses them into a single image with depth.

History

Early systems

Stereoscopic photography dates to the 19th century. William Friese-Greene filed a patent for a 3D movie process in the 1890s. The earliest confirmed 3D film shown to a paying audience was The Power of Love in 1922, using the red-green anaglyph system. The 1939 New York World's Fair featured notable stereoscopic shorts.

The 1950s "golden era"

The first major boom came in 1952–1954 with the success of Bwana Devil and House of Wax, the first 3D feature from a major studio. Studios released dozens of stereoscopic features to compete with television, but cumbersome dual-projector systems and eye strain caused the fad to fade within a few years. Alfred Hitchcock's Dial M for Murder (1954) was shot in 3D but mostly shown flat.

Revival and the digital era

Single-strip systems such as StereoVision revived the format briefly in the 1970s and 1980s. IMAX 3D theatres popularised large-format stereoscopy in the 1990s. The decisive revival came with digital projection in the 2000s: systems such as RealD 3D used a single projector with circular polarization. James Cameron's Avatar (2009), shot with the Fusion Camera System, became the highest-grossing film of all time and triggered a wave of 3D releases, including many post-converted titles of variable quality.

Techniques

  • Anaglyph — the two views are encoded in complementary colours (usually red and cyan) and separated by coloured filters.
  • Polarization — the dominant cinema system; images are projected through orthogonal polarizing filters, either linear or circular.
  • Active shutter — glasses with liquid-crystal shutters alternately block each eye in sync with rapidly alternating images; common in home 3D television.
  • Autostereoscopy — glasses-free displays using lenticular lenses or parallax barriers, used in devices such as the Nintendo 3DS.

Production

Native 3D productions shoot with dual-camera rigs whose interaxial separation and convergence can be adjusted. Alternatively, 2D films can be converted to 3D in post-production by creating depth maps for each element of every frame — a labour-intensive process whose results vary widely in quality.

Decline in the 2010s

Audience enthusiasm cooled after the post-Avatar boom; surcharges, dimmer images, and indifferent conversions reduced demand. Manufacturers discontinued 3D televisions by 2017, and 3D releases became concentrated in animated and spectacle-driven films, though the format remained popular in some markets, particularly China.
