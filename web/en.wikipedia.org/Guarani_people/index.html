Guarani people

The Guarani are an Indigenous people of South America whose traditional territory spans present-day Paraguay, northeastern Argentina, southern Brazil, and parts of Bolivia and Uruguay. They speak Guarani, a Tupian language that is exceptional among Indigenous American languages in being spoken by a large non-Indigenous population: in Paraguay it is co-official with Spanish and is spoken by the great majority of the country's inhabitants.

History

Before European contact, the Guarani were semi-nomadic farmers and hunters organized in extended-family communities led by chiefs and shamans, cultivating maize, manioc, and sweet potatoes. From the early seventeenth century, Jesuit missionaries gathered many Guarani into mission settlements (reducciones) in the region known as the Jesuit Missions of the Guaranis, where tens of thousands lived, practiced crafts and music, and served in militias defending the missions from Portuguese slave raiders (bandeirantes). The Guaraní War of the 1750s followed the Treaty of Madrid's attempt to transfer mission lands to Portugal, and the Jesuits were expelled from Spanish America in 1767, after which the missions declined.

Subgroups and present day

Major Guarani subgroups include the Ava-Guarani (Chiriguano), the Mbyá, the Kaiowá (Pai Tavytera), and the Ñandeva. In Brazil, particularly in Mato Grosso do Sul, Kaiowá and Ñandeva communities have faced prolonged conflicts over ancestral lands largely converted to cattle ranching and sugarcane, with very high rates of violence and suicide drawing international attention. Land demarcation, cultural survival, and the preservation of the Guarani language remain central issues for Guarani organizations across the four countries.

Culture

Guarani religion centres on the figure of Ñamandu, the creator, and on the search for yvy marã e'ỹ, the "Land Without Evil", a paradise believed reachable through prayer, dance, and in some historical movements by migration. Oral tradition, the ritual use of the mbaraka rattle and takuapu rhythm sticks, and the cultivation of yerba mate — a plant the Guarani first domesticated for the infusion now consumed throughout the Southern Cone — are enduring cultural markers.
