Johann Christoph Friedrich von Schiller (10 November 1759 – 9 May 1805) was a German playwright, poet, philosopher and historian. Along with Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, with whom he formed a celebrated friendship and collaboration, he is regarded as a central figure of Weimar Classicism and one of the most significant dramatists in German literature.

Early life and The Robbers

Schiller was born in Marbach am Neckar, the son of an army surgeon, and studied medicine at the Karlsschule in Stuttgart. His first play, The Robbers (1781), a fiery drama of rebellion against tyranny associated with the Sturm und Drang movement, made him famous overnight — and got him arrested and forbidden to write by the Duke of Württemberg, prompting his flight to Mannheim.

Major works

Schiller's mature dramas include Don Carlos, the Wallenstein trilogy, Mary Stuart, The Maid of Orleans, and William Tell (1804). His poetry includes the ballads and the "Ode to Joy" (1785), which Beethoven set in the finale of the Ninth Symphony and which became the anthem of the European Union. As a philosopher he wrote the Letters on the Aesthetic Education of Man (1795), and as historian he produced a history of the Thirty Years' War.

Friendship with Goethe and death

From 1794 Schiller and Goethe worked closely in Weimar, exchanging the famous correspondence that shaped German classical literature, and jointly publishing the satirical Xenien. Schiller suffered from chronic ill health, probably tuberculosis, and died in Weimar in 1805 at the age of 45. His plays remain staples of the German stage, and his ideal of freedom through art has made him a national cultural symbol in Germany.
