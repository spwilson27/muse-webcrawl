Mental model

A mental model is an internal representation of external reality: a way of representing reality within one's mind. Such models are hypothesized to play a major role in cognition, reasoning and decision-making. The term was coined by the Scottish philosopher Kenneth Craik in 1943, who suggested that the mind constructs "small-scale models" of reality that it uses to anticipate events.

Characteristics

Mental models can be constructed from perception, imagination, or the comprehension of discourse. They are similar in structure to the situation or state of affairs they represent, unlike formal logic, whose rules are based on syntactic forms. Mental models are often incomplete, unstable, and unscientific in the sense that their boundaries are fuzzy and their workings are not always consistent with formal rules; people maintain separate models for different aspects of their lives.

In psychology and cognitive science

Philip Johnson-Laird developed a theory of mental models as the basis of human reasoning: rather than manipulating logical forms, reasoners construct models of possibilities and test conclusions against them. In human–computer interaction, Donald Norman emphasized that users form models of the systems they interact with, and good design makes the correct model easy to form — the designer's model, the system image, and the user's model should coincide.

In business and systems thinking

The investor Charlie Munger popularized the idea of a "latticework of mental models" — drawing models from many disciplines (psychology, economics, physics, mathematics) to improve judgment. Charles Duhigg and Shane Parrish have written popular treatments of mental models as tools for clearer thinking.
