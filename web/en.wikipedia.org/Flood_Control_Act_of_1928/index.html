Flood Control Act of 1928

The Flood Control Act of 1928 (officially the act of May 15, 1928, ch. 569, 45 Stat. 534) was a United States federal law enacted by the 70th United States Congress in response to the Great Mississippi Flood of 1927, the most destructive river flood in American history, which had inundated some 27,000 square miles, displaced hundreds of thousands of people, and killed at least 246.

Provisions

The Act authorized the Mississippi River and Tributaries Project, a comprehensive federal program of flood control for the lower Mississippi River administered by the United States Army Corps of Engineers under the Mississippi River Commission. Departing from the previous "levees only" policy, the plan — based largely on the report of Chief of Engineers Major General Edgar Jadwin — combined higher and stronger levees with floodways, spillways (including the Bonnet Carré Spillway above New Orleans) and channel improvements designed to pass a "project flood" greater than that of 1927.

The Act was landmark legislation in several respects: the federal government assumed the full cost of flood control on the Mississippi, with no requirement for local contributions toward levees; it immunized the federal government from liability for flood damage, a provision later codified at 33 U.S.C. § 702c; and it authorized a major federal program for tributary basins including the Arkansas and Red rivers. It also authorized flood control surveys that laid the groundwork for later acts, notably the Flood Control Act of 1936, which made flood control a federal responsibility nationwide.
