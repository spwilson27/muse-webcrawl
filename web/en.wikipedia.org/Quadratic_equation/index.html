In algebra, a quadratic equation is an equation that can be rearranged in standard form as

ax² + bx + c = 0,

where x represents an unknown value, and a, b and c represent known numbers, where a ≠ 0. (If a = 0 and b ≠ 0 then the equation is linear, not quadratic.) The numbers a, b and c are the coefficients of the equation and may be distinguished by respectively calling them the quadratic coefficient, the linear coefficient and the constant coefficient or free term.

Quadratic formula

Because the quadratic equation involves only one unknown, it is called "univariate". Its solutions, also called roots, are given by the quadratic formula:

x = (−b ± √(b² − 4ac)) / 2a

The expression under the square root, Δ = b² − 4ac, is the discriminant. If Δ > 0 there are two distinct real roots; if Δ = 0 there is one repeated real root; if Δ < 0 there are two complex conjugate roots.

Solution methods

  • Factoring (inspection of factor pairs of ac)
  • Completing the square — the method from which the formula is derived
  • Graphing the related quadratic function and reading the x-intercepts
  • Vieta's formulas relating the roots to the coefficients: the roots sum to −b/a and multiply to c/a

History

Babylonian mathematicians solved problems equivalent to quadratic equations geometrically by about 2000 BC. Euclid gave a geometric treatment in the Elements; Brahmagupta (628 AD) stated an explicit rule for one root, and the general formula was articulated in Islamic and later European algebra, with al-Khwārizmī's Algebra (c. 820) systematising solution by completing the square.
