Isostasy (Greek ísos "equal", stásis "standstill") is the state of gravitational equilibrium between Earth's crust (or lithosphere) and mantle, such that the crust "floats" at an elevation that depends on its thickness and density. The concept explains why the elevations of continents and ocean floors differ, why high mountain ranges have deep crustal roots, and why the ground in formerly glaciated regions is still rising today.

History

The idea arose from geodesy. During the survey of India in the 19th century, plumb lines near the Himalayas were deflected less than the mountains' visible mass predicted — suggesting the mountains were underlain by a deficit of mass. Archdeacon John Pratt proposed that mountain crust is less dense, expanded like rising bread; George Airy instead proposed that the crust floats on a denser substratum, with high mountains supported by deep "roots" of light crust, as an iceberg is supported by its submerged bulk. The American geologist Clarence Dutton introduced the word "isostasy" in 1882 to name the general condition of equilibrium.

Models

Airy model

Crustal blocks of uniform density float on the denser mantle: the higher a block rises, the deeper its root extends. This describes mountain ranges well — seismic studies confirm, for example, that the crust beneath the Himalayas and Andes is roughly twice the normal continental thickness.

Pratt model

All blocks reach a common depth of compensation, but their densities vary: mountains stand high because their crust is less dense. This better describes mid-ocean ridges and regions of hot, buoyant mantle. In reality, both thickness and density variations combine, and the lithosphere also has elastic strength, so loads are partly supported by regional flexure — the basis of modern "flexural isostasy".

Isostatic movements

Because the mantle flows only slowly, the crust responds to changes in load over thousands of years. When the great Pleistocene ice sheets melted, the unloaded crust began rising — post-glacial rebound — and continues to rise by up to about a centimetre per year around Hudson Bay and the Gulf of Bothnia, recorded in raised beaches and measured today by GPS. Conversely, erosion of mountains lightens the crust and causes uplift, while sedimentation in deltas and the growth of volcanoes depress it. Isostatic adjustments also produce characteristic gravity anomalies used to probe crustal structure.
