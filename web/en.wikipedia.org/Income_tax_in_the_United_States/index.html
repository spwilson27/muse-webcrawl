Income tax in the United States

The income tax in the United States is imposed by the federal government and by most state and some local governments on the income of individuals, corporations, estates, and trusts. The federal individual income tax is progressive, with marginal rates (as of recent years) ranging from 10 percent to 37 percent, and is administered by the Internal Revenue Service (IRS) under the Internal Revenue Code.

History

The first federal income tax was levied in 1861 to finance the Civil War and was repealed a decade later. An 1894 income tax was struck down by the Supreme Court in Pollock v. Farmers' Loan & Trust Co. (1895) as an unapportioned direct tax. The Sixteenth Amendment, ratified in 1913, removed that obstacle, and a modern income tax followed that year. Initially applying to few taxpayers, the tax was vastly expanded during World War II, when wage withholding was introduced in 1943, transforming it into a mass tax.

Structure

Taxable income is gross income minus deductions, including a standard deduction or itemized deductions. Preferential rates apply to most long-term capital gains and qualified dividends. Tax credits — such as the child tax credit and the earned income tax credit — reduce tax liability directly. Corporations pay a flat federal rate of 21 percent (set by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017). Most states levy their own income taxes, a few (such as Texas and Florida) levy none on wages, and some cities, including New York City, impose local income taxes.

Administration and filing

Employers withhold tax from wages and remit it to the IRS; individuals file annual returns, principally Form 1040, generally due April 15. Payroll taxes for Social Security and Medicare (FICA) are separate from the income tax. Disputes may be heard by the U.S. Tax Court.
