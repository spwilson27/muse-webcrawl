The Alcatraz Federal Penitentiary was a maximum-security federal prison on Alcatraz Island, 1.25 miles off San Francisco, California. Operating from 1934 to 1963, it housed some of the most notorious criminals in American history and gained a reputation as America's most escape-proof prison, surrounded by the cold, swift currents of San Francisco Bay.

The Alcatraz cell house, seen from San Francisco Bay.

History

The island had served military purposes since the 1850s, first as a fort and then as a military prison. The main cell house, built by military prisoners between 1910 and 1912, was taken over by the Department of Justice in 1933, and the Federal Bureau of Prisons opened the penitentiary in August 1934 as a showcase of the new federal prison system — a place for the most incorrigible inmates from other federal prisons.

Under Warden James A. Johnston, Alcatraz imposed a strict regime of silence, routine and minimal privileges: food, clothing, shelter and medical care were guaranteed, everything else had to be earned. The population never exceeded about 320 inmates, watched by an unusually high ratio of guards.

Notable inmates

Alcatraz held Al Capone, convicted Prohibition-era gangster; George "Machine Gun" Kelly; Alvin Karpis, the last of the Depression-era "public enemies"; Roy Gardner; and Robert Stroud, the "Birdman of Alcatraz", who in fact kept his birds at Leavenworth before his transfer. James "Whitey" Bulger also served time there in the 1950s.

Escape attempts

During 29 years of operation, 36 prisoners made 14 separate escape attempts; 23 were recaptured, 6 were shot and killed, and 5 are listed as "missing and presumed drowned". The most violent attempt, the 1946 "Battle of Alcatraz", left two guards and three inmates dead. The most famous came on 11 June 1962, when Frank Morris and brothers John and Clarence Anglin escaped through tunnels dug with spoons, leaving dummy heads in their bunks and launching a homemade raft. They were never found; the case remains officially unresolved and inspired the film Escape from Alcatraz (1979).

Closure and legacy

Attorney General Robert F. Kennedy ordered Alcatraz closed in 1963, as its buildings crumbled from salt air and its operating costs far exceeded those of mainland prisons. After a landmark occupation by Native American activists (1969–1971), the island became part of the Golden Gate National Recreation Area in 1972 and opened to the public in 1973. It is now one of San Francisco's most popular tourist attractions, drawing well over a million visitors a year.
