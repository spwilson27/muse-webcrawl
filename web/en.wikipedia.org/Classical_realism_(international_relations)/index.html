Classical realism (international relations)

Classical realism is a theory of international relations that explains state behaviour by a human drive for power. Rooted in the writings of Thucydides, Machiavelli, and Hobbes, it was formulated as an academic theory in the mid-20th century, above all in Hans Morgenthau's Politics Among Nations (1948).

Core claims

Morgenthau grounded politics in "objective laws" derived from human nature: political man seeks power (the animus dominandi), and statesmen therefore "think and act in terms of interest defined as power." Unlike the later structural realism (neorealism) of Kenneth Waltz, which derives competition from the anarchic structure of the international system, classical realism locates the source of conflict in human nature and allows room for statesmanship, prudence, diplomacy, ethics, and the character of regimes—including the distinction between status quo and imperialist policies.

Thinkers

Key figures include E. H. Carr (The Twenty Years' Crisis, 1939), Reinhold Niebuhr, George Kennan, and Raymond Aron. Niebuhr's theology-informed pessimism about collective egoism and Carr's critique of interwar utopianism helped define the school against liberal idealism.

Influence and criticism

Classical realism shaped early Cold War policy debates and remains influential in diplomatic practice and in critiques of ideological foreign policy. Behavioural and structural critics charged it with imprecision and unfalsifiability; recent scholarship has revived interest in the classical texts for their attention to prudence, tragedy, and moral judgment.
