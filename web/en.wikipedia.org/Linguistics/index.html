Linguistics
The world's major language families

Linguistics is the scientific study of language: its structure, meaning, acquisition, history and use in society. Linguists analyse language at several levels — sounds (phonetics and phonology), word structure (morphology), sentence structure (syntax), meaning (semantics) and use in context (pragmatics) — and study how languages change over time, vary between communities, and are learned by children and processed by the brain.

Subfields

  • Phonetics and phonology: speech sounds and sound systems.
  • Morphology: the internal structure of words.
  • Syntax: the rules governing sentence structure.
  • Semantics and pragmatics: meaning, and meaning in context.
  • Historical linguistics: language change and the classification of languages into families.
  • Sociolinguistics: language in society — variation, dialect, register and identity.
  • Psycholinguistics and neurolinguistics: language acquisition, processing and the brain.
  • Computational linguistics: modelling language for natural language processing and speech technology.

History

Grammatical analysis began in ancient India with Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī (c. 4th century BC), a remarkably formal grammar of Sanskrit, and continued through Greek, Roman, Arabic and Chinese traditions. Comparative philology in the 18th–19th centuries (William Jones, the Grimms, Bopp) established the Indo-European language family. Ferdinand de Saussure's structuralism and his distinction between langue and parole shaped the 20th century, as did the descriptive schools of Boas and Sapir. Noam Chomsky's generative grammar, beginning with Syntactic Structures (1957), redirected the field toward the innate structures underlying language, a programme later complemented by cognitive and usage-based approaches.

Key ideas

Linguists distinguish descriptive study of language as it is used from prescriptive rules about how it "should" be used. Central concepts include the arbitrariness of the sign, the rule-governed creativity of grammar, universal grammar debates, and language universals (Greenberg). Some 7,000 languages are spoken today, many of them endangered.
