Hygroscopy

Hygroscopy is the phenomenon of attracting and holding water molecules via either absorption or adsorption from the surrounding environment, which is usually at normal or room temperature. If water molecules become suspended among the substance's molecules, the substance may become physically changed in volume, stickiness or other properties. Substances that absorb so much water that they dissolve in it are called deliquescent.

Examples

Common hygroscopic materials include calcium chloride, silica gel, honey, glycerol, ethanol, methanol, concentrated sulfuric acid and sodium hydroxide. Silica gel is widely used as a desiccant in packaging to protect goods from moisture damage. Calcium chloride is spread on roads both for dust control and de-icing because it draws moisture from the air.

Applications

Hygroscopic properties are exploited in desiccants, humidity buffers in museum storage, water harvesting from air, and the self-healing behaviour of some materials. In biology, hygroscopic movements — such as the opening and closing of pine cones and the dispersal structures of many mosses — are driven by differential absorption of water in cell walls. Hygroscopicity must also be controlled in pharmaceuticals, explosives and electronics, where moisture uptake degrades performance.
