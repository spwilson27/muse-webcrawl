Old quantum theory

The old quantum theory is a collection of results from the years 1900–1925 which predate modern quantum mechanics. It was never complete or self-consistent, but was a set of heuristic corrections to classical mechanics which is now understood as the semiclassical approximation to modern quantum mechanics.

History

The theory began with Max Planck's 1900 solution to the black-body radiation problem, which introduced quantised energy E = hν. Albert Einstein extended the idea in 1905 with the photon explanation of the photoelectric effect and, in 1907, a quantum model of atomic specific heats. Niels Bohr's 1913 model of the hydrogen atom introduced stationary states and quantum jumps between them, explaining the Rydberg formula for spectral lines.

Basic principles

The central tool was the Bohr–Sommerfeld quantisation rule: the classical motion of a system is restricted to orbits for which the action integral over each period is an integer multiple of Planck's constant, ∮p dq = nh. Arnold Sommerfeld extended this to elliptical orbits, adding relativistic corrections that reproduced the fine structure of hydrogen. The correspondence principle, articulated by Bohr, required that quantum predictions merge with classical ones for large quantum numbers.

Successes and failures

The old quantum theory accounted for the hydrogen spectrum, the Stark and normal Zeeman effects, and Moseley's law. It failed, however, for helium and more complex atoms, for the intensities of spectral lines, and for the anomalous Zeeman effect; it also gave a wrong value for the ground-state angular momentum. These failures motivated the creation of matrix mechanics by Heisenberg, Born and Jordan and of wave mechanics by Schrödinger in 1925–1926.
