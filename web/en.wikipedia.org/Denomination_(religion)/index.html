Denomination (religion)

A religious denomination is a subgroup within a religion that operates under a common name, tradition, and identity. Members of a denomination share beliefs and practices that distinguish them from other subgroups of the same religion, while typically recognizing a shared core heritage.

Usage

The term is most often applied within Christianity, where denominations such as the Catholic Church, the Eastern Orthodox Church, and the many Protestant traditions (Lutheran, Baptist, Methodist, Presbyterian, and others) represent organized branches with their own governance, doctrine, and worship. The term is also used for branches of Judaism (Orthodox, Conservative, Reform), schools and movements within Islam (Sunni and Shia), and divisions of Hinduism and Buddhism.

Formation

Denominations commonly arise through schism — a split over doctrine, practice, or authority — or gradually, as communities in different regions develop distinct customs. Over time a denominational identity may be formalized through creeds, confessions of faith, ordination, and institutional structures.

Distinctions

A denomination differs from a sect, which in sociological usage denotes a smaller, often newer group that has broken away, and from a cult in the technical sense of a system of religious devotion. Scholars of religion classify such groupings using typologies such as the church–sect continuum developed in the sociology of religion.
