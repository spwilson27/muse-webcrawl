Cromarty

Cromarty (Scottish Gaelic: Cromba) is a town, civil parish and former royal burgh in Ross and Cromarty, in the Highland area of Scotland. It stands on the tip of the Black Isle peninsula at the mouth of the Cromarty Firth, a deep, sheltered inlet of the Moray Firth renowned for its dolphins and, in the twentieth century, for North Sea oil fabrication yards on the opposite shore.

History

Cromarty grew as a fishing and trading port around its fine natural harbour. In the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries it was developed by the laird George Ross into a small industrial town with a hemp factory, brewery and nail works. The geologist and writer Hugh Miller was born in Cromarty in 1802; his cottage is preserved as a museum. The town was the county town of the historic county of Cromartyshire, notable for consisting of numerous small enclaves scattered within Ross-shire, until the counties were merged in 1889.

Geography

The entrance to the firth is guarded by two headlands known as the Sutors of Cromarty, North Sutor and South Sutor. A small vehicle ferry, one of the smallest in Britain, runs in summer from Cromarty to Nigg across the firth. Bottlenose dolphins are regularly seen from the shore at nearby Chanonry Point.
