Arsenic is a chemical element with the symbol As and atomic number 33. It is a metalloid occurring in many minerals, usually in combination with sulfur and metals, and also as native crystals. Arsenic is notorious for the toxicity of its compounds — particularly arsenic trioxide, historically a favoured poison — yet it also has important industrial uses, notably in the semiconductor gallium arsenide.

Properties

Arsenic occurs in several allotropes. The common grey form is a brittle, steel-grey metalloid that sublimes at 615 °C; yellow arsenic is a soft, waxy molecular solid (As4). Arsenic burns in air to arsenic trioxide and readily tarnishes. It is in group 15 of the periodic table, between phosphorus and antimony.

History

Arsenic compounds were known in antiquity (the mineral orpiment and realgar); Albertus Magnus is credited with isolating the element around 1250. In the Victorian era, arsenic was ubiquitous — in pigments such as Scheele's green, medicines, cosmetics and flypapers — and its use as a homicidal poison ("inheritance powder") prompted the Marsh test (1836), the first reliable chemical test for it.

Applications

Gallium arsenide is a key semiconductor for high-frequency electronics, lasers and photovoltaics. Arsenic compounds have been used in wood preservatives (chromated copper arsenate), historically in pesticides and pigments, and arsenic trioxide is an effective chemotherapy drug for acute promyelocytic leukaemia.

Toxicity and environment

Inorganic arsenic is a confirmed human carcinogen (IARC Group 1) associated with skin, lung and bladder cancers. Contamination of groundwater by natural arsenic — affecting tens of millions of people using tube wells in Bangladesh and West Bengal — has been called the largest mass poisoning in history. The WHO guideline for drinking water is 10 μg/L.
