Blood-oxygen-level-dependent imaging

Blood-oxygen-level-dependent imaging, or BOLD-contrast imaging, is a method used in functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) to observe different areas of the brain that are active at any given time. The method exploits the fact that neuronal activity locally increases cerebral blood flow and changes the relative concentration of oxygenated and deoxygenated haemoglobin, which differ in their magnetic properties.

Principle

Deoxyhaemoglobin is paramagnetic, whereas oxyhaemoglobin is diamagnetic. Deoxygenated blood therefore distorts the local magnetic field and reduces the MRI signal in T2*-weighted images. When a brain region becomes active, blood flow increases more than oxygen consumption, so the local deoxyhaemoglobin fraction falls and the signal rises. This haemodynamic response peaks about 4–6 seconds after a brief burst of neural activity.

History

The phenomenon was described by Seiji Ogawa at Bell Laboratories in 1990, building on Linus Pauling's 1930s observations about the magnetic properties of haemoglobin. Ogawa and others demonstrated BOLD contrast first in rodents and then in humans, and within a few years it became the dominant technique in functional neuroimaging.

Applications and limitations

BOLD fMRI is used to map sensory, motor, and cognitive functions, in resting-state connectivity studies, and clinically for presurgical planning. The signal is an indirect and temporally blurred measure of neural activity, and interpretation requires care: BOLD reflects input and local processing more than spiking output, and draining veins can displace the apparent site of activity.
