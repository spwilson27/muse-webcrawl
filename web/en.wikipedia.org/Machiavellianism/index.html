Machiavellianism

Machiavellianism denotes, in political discourse, the doctrine associated with the Italian Renaissance writer Niccolò Machiavelli (1469–1527), especially the view expressed in The Prince (written 1513, published 1532) that rulers may legitimately use cunning, deceit, and force to acquire and maintain power, subordinating conventional morality to political necessity — summarised in the maxim that "the ends justify the means".

Political sense

Machiavelli analysed power realistically, arguing that a prince must learn "how not to be good" and must be prepared to act against faith, charity, and religion when necessity demands, while avoiding the hatred of the people. Whether The Prince is a sincere handbook for tyrants, a satire, or a republican work in disguise remains debated; Machiavelli's Discourses on Livy reveal strong republican sympathies. The adjective "Machiavellian" acquired its sinister meaning largely through Elizabethan stage villains and Counter-Reformation polemic, which depicted Machiavelli as a teacher of evil.

Psychological sense

In personality psychology, Machiavellianism is a trait characterised by manipulativeness, cynical disregard for morality, and strategic self-interest. Psychologists Richard Christie and Florence Geis developed the Mach IV scale in the 1970s to measure it. Together with narcissism and subclinical psychopathy it forms the "Dark Triad" of personality traits, associated with low agreeableness and empathy.
