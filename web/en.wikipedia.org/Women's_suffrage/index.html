Women's suffrage

Women's suffrage is the right of women to vote in elections. Historically, voting was generally restricted to men, and in many places further limited by property, wealth or literacy requirements. Campaigns to extend the franchise to women gathered force in the 19th century and succeeded country by country, mostly in the late 19th and 20th centuries.

History

New Zealand became the first self-governing country to grant women the vote in national elections in 1893, followed by Australia in 1902 (though Indigenous Australians were largely excluded). Finland, then part of the Russian Empire, introduced full suffrage including eligibility to stand for office in 1906. In Britain, the women's suffrage movement split between the constitutional suffragists of the National Union of Women's Suffrage Societies and Emmeline Pankhurst's more militant suffragettes; British women over 30 gained the vote in 1918 and equal voting rights with men followed in 1928. In the United States, the Seneca Falls Convention of 1848 launched the organised movement, and the Nineteenth Amendment, ratified in 1920, prohibited denying the vote on account of sex.

Later extensions

Many countries enfranchised women only later: France in 1944, Italy in 1946, and Switzerland at federal level only in 1971. Some states restricted women's voting into the 21st century. Universal women's suffrage is now affirmed in international conventions, although women's political representation continues to lag behind men's in most countries.
