George Gerbner, founder of cultivation theory in media studies.

George Gerbner (August 8, 1919 – December 24, 2005) was a Hungarian-American professor of communication and the founder of cultivation theory, the study of the long-term effects of television on viewers' perceptions of the world. He served as dean of the Annenberg School for Communication at the University of Pennsylvania from 1964 to 1989.

Early life and career

Gerbner was born in Budapest and emigrated to the United States in 1939. He studied journalism at the University of California, Berkeley, served in the U.S. Army during the Second World War, and later earned a doctorate from the University of Southern California.

Cultivation theory

Beginning in the late 1960s, Gerbner's Cultural Indicators research project tracked trends in television content and related them to viewers' attitudes. His studies found that people who watched large amounts of television — "heavy viewers" — tended to perceive the world as more dangerous and violent than it actually is, a phenomenon he called the Mean World Syndrome. Gerbner argued that television, as the dominant storyteller of modern culture, cultivates shared assumptions about reality across otherwise diverse audiences.

Later work

In 1990 Gerbner founded the Cultural Environment Movement, an advocacy coalition concerned with the concentration of media ownership and the quality of the media environment. He continued writing and lecturing until shortly before his death in 2005.
