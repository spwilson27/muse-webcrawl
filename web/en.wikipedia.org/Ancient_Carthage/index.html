Ancient Carthage was a Phoenician city-state and maritime empire centred on the city of Carthage, on the coast of modern Tunisia. Founded, by tradition, in 814 BC by settlers from Tyre under Queen Dido (Elissa), it grew into the dominant commercial power of the western Mediterranean before its destruction by Rome in 146 BC at the end of the Punic Wars.

History

Carthage began as a trading post but by the sixth century BC controlled a network of colonies and dependencies in North Africa, Sardinia, Corsica, the Balearic Islands, Malta and western Sicily, later extending into southern Iberia under the Barcid family. Its wealth rested on trade — purple dye, metals, agricultural produce — and on a powerful navy and mercenary armies.

Government and society

Aristotle praised Carthage's constitution, a mixed system with two annually elected chief magistrates called suffetes, a powerful senate and council of elders, and a popular assembly. Religion centred on Baal Hammon and Tanit; the tophet precinct, where urns of cremated infants were buried, has fuelled long debate over child sacrifice. The city's twin harbours — a rectangular merchant port and a circular naval port (the cothon) able to shelter some 220 warships — were engineering marvels.

The Punic Wars and destruction

Carthage fought Rome in three Punic Wars (264–241, 218–201 and 149–146 BC). In the second, Hannibal crossed the Alps and won crushing victories at Cannae and elsewhere, yet Rome prevailed, stripping Carthage of its empire and fleet. In the Third Punic War Rome besieged and systematically destroyed the city in 146 BC, selling its surviving inhabitants into slavery. A Roman Carthage was later refounded under Julius Caesar and Augustus and became a great city of the empire.
