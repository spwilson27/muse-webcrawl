Delphic maxims

The Delphic maxims are a set of moral precepts inscribed in the pronaos of the Temple of Apollo at Delphi. Ancient tradition attributed the sayings to Apollo himself or to the Seven Sages of Greece. Three maxims are most famous and were reportedly inscribed on the temple itself: "Know thyself" (gnōthi seauton), "Nothing in excess" (mēdén ágan), and "Surety brings ruin" (engýa pára d'átē). A much longer list of 147 maxims is preserved in the anthology of the 5th-century writer Stobaeus and attributed there to Sosiades.

The 147 maxims

The maxims preserved by Stobaeus cover the whole of ancient Greek practical ethics: worship of the gods, respect for parents and the laws, self-control, justice, friendship, and caution in speech and action. Examples include "Follow God", "Obey the law", "Honour your parents", "Control yourself", "Help your friends", "Restrain your anger", "Exercise prudence", "Love learning", "Do not make fun of the dead", and "Beget children from noble stock". Together they give a picture of the conventional moral wisdom of the Greek world, emphasising moderation, piety, and social harmony.

"Know thyself"

The most influential of the maxims, "Know thyself", was discussed by numerous ancient authors. Plato's dialogues return to it repeatedly, and Socrates is portrayed as interpreting it as a call to recognise one's own ignorance and limitations. Later readings treated it as advice to know one's place in the cosmos, one's mortal nature, or one's soul. The phrase has remained a touchstone of Western philosophy down to the present day.
