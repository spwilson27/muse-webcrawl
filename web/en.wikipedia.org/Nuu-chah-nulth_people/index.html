Nuu-chah-nulth people

The Nuu-chah-nulth (also Nuučaan̓uɫ, formerly called Nootka) are an Indigenous people of the Pacific Northwest Coast of Canada. Their traditional territories lie along the west coast of Vancouver Island, British Columbia, and they number about fifteen related First Nations represented by the Nuu-chah-nulth Tribal Council.

Culture

Traditional Nuu-chah-nulth society was ranked and centred on hereditary chiefs (ha'wiih), and on the potlatch, a ceremonial system of feasting and gift-giving that validated rights and status. The economy rested on salmon, halibut, herring, shellfish, and marine mammals. Whaling held exceptional prestige: whaling chiefs prepared through elaborate ritual at shrines such as the Yuquot Whalers' Shrine, using specialised harpoons and floats. Their language belongs to the southern Wakashan family.

History

In 1778 Captain James Cook landed at Nootka Sound, beginning the maritime fur trade in sea otter pelts. The Nootka Crisis of 1789–90 nearly brought Britain and Spain to war. Chief Maquinna of the Mowachaht was a prominent early leader. Colonisation, disease, residential schools — including the notorious Alberni Indian Residential School — and the suppression of the potlatch devastated the population and culture in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Modern era

Nuu-chah-nulth nations were central to landmark Aboriginal rights litigation, including the Calder case (1973) and fisheries cases such as Ahousaht (2009), which affirmed their rights to commercial fisheries. Several nations signed the Maa-nulth Final Agreement (2011), a modern treaty. Language and cultural revitalisation programmes are ongoing.
