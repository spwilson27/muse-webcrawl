Levada

A levada is an irrigation channel or aqueduct specific to the Portuguese Atlantic region of Madeira. Levadas were built to carry water from the wetter mountains of the island's interior and north coast to the drier, cultivated south, where bananas, sugar cane and vineyards demanded irrigation. Over roughly 2,170 kilometres of channels — including tunnels dug through volcanic rock — now cross the island, and the paths maintained alongside them have become one of Madeira's most famous networks of walking routes.

History

The first levadas date to the earliest Portuguese settlement of Madeira in the 15th–16th centuries, as settlers cut channels to irrigate sugar-cane fields. Construction expanded steadily, with major networks built in the 19th and 20th centuries to tap streams in the high central massif and the laurisilva forest, sometimes involving long tunnels (furados) hewn by hand. From the mid-20th century many levadas also fed hydroelectric stations on their way downslope to the fields.

Construction and maintenance

Levadas are typically narrow channels of stone or concrete, often only 20–50 cm wide, engineered to follow the contour of the land with an extremely gentle gradient so that water flows slowly without eroding the channel. Alongside most runs a narrow maintenance path, the caminho, used by the levadeiros — the levada keepers who clear debris, operate the sluices and distribute water to farmers according to traditional shares of time.

Tourism

The maintenance paths give walkers access to Madeira's most dramatic scenery — the levadas of the Rabaçal (25 Fontes), the Levada do Caldeirão Verde, and the high routes between Pico do Areeiro and Pico Ruivo are among the island's classic hikes. Because the paths can be narrow with steep drops, guidebooks grade routes by difficulty and exposure.

Note

The word also names an unrelated Russian polling organisation, the Levada Center, founded by sociologist Yuri Levada; the same spelling occurs in dressage, where the levade is a classical haute école movement in which the horse rears at about 45 degrees.
