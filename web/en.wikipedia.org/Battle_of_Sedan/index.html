The Battle of Sedan was fought during the Franco-Prussian War on 1 September 1870. The Prussian-led German armies encircled and destroyed the French Army of Châlons in the loop of the Meuse at Sedan; Emperor Napoleon III was taken prisoner with over 100,000 of his men. The disaster ended the Second French Empire and is commemorated in Germany as Sedantag.

Background

After defeats on the frontier, Marshal MacMahon's Army of Châlons marched northeast in a politically driven attempt to relieve Metz, where Bazaine's army was besieged. Moltke pivoted the German Third Army and the new Army of the Meuse to intercept; the French were caught against the Belgian frontier at Sedan after the preliminary fight at Beaumont (30 August).

The battle

On 1 September Bavarian troops seized Bazeilles after savage house-to-house fighting, while Prussian corps crossed the Meuse and closed the ring from the north and east. MacMahon was wounded early in the day; command passed chaotically between Ducrot and Wimpffen, whose orders countermanded each other. French cavalry under General Margueritte made repeated heroic but futile charges at Floing—prompting Wilhelm I's admiring (attributed) remark about "these brave fellows." By afternoon the French were compressed into the fortress and woods around Sedan under converging artillery fire. Napoleon III, ill and seeking death in vain, ordered the white flag raised.

Aftermath

On 2 September the Emperor surrendered himself and his army. In Paris the Empire collapsed: on 4 September the Third Republic was proclaimed and a Government of National Defense continued the war, but the road to Paris lay open. Sedan became the founding victory myth of the German Empire proclaimed at Versailles in January 1871. (This battle should not be confused with the breakthrough at Sedan in May 1940 during the Second World War.)
