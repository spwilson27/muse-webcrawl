The dromedary camel (Camelus dromedarius), also known as the Arabian camel, is a large, even-toed ungulate with one hump on its back. It is the tallest of the three camel species and has been domesticated for roughly 3,000–4,000 years. Dromedaries make up about 94% of the world's camel population, concentrated in the Middle East, North and East Africa, South Asia and Australia, where a large feral population descends from imported transport animals.

Adaptations

The dromedary is adapted to hot deserts: its hump stores fat that can be metabolised for energy and water; it tolerates losing a quarter of its body water; its oval red blood cells keep flowing when dehydrated; and it can let its body temperature swing from about 34 °C to over 40 °C before sweating. Long legs, closable nostrils, double rows of eyelashes and a split upper lip suit it to sand and thorny forage. Contrary to legend, the hump stores no water directly.

Uses

Dromedaries serve as pack and riding animals, sources of milk and meat, and racing animals in the Gulf states, where camel racing with robot jockeys is a major sport. Their milk is a staple for pastoralist peoples such as the Somali and Bedouin.
