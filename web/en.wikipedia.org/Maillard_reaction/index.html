Maillard reaction

The Maillard reaction is a form of non-enzymatic browning that occurs between amino acids and reducing sugars, usually when food is heated. Named after French chemist Louis Camille Maillard, who described it in 1912, it generates hundreds of flavour and aroma compounds and the golden-brown colours of seared meat, bread crusts, roasted coffee, and fried onions. It is distinct from caramelisation, the pyrolysis of sugars alone.

The crust on seared meat is a product of the Maillard reaction.

Chemistry

The reaction begins when the carbonyl group of a sugar condenses with the amino group of an amino acid to form a Schiff base, which rearranges into Amadori or Heyns products. These intermediates fragment and recombine through a web of pathways — dehydration, fission, and Strecker degradation — producing volatile aromatics (pyrazines give roasted, nutty notes; sulfur compounds give meaty notes) and brown nitrogenous polymers called melanoidins that colour the food. The reaction rate increases with temperature, lower water activity, and higher pH; it proceeds slowly even at room temperature over long storage.

Culinary significance

Cooks exploit the reaction by drying food surfaces, preheating pans, and using high heat for brief times, since boiling water caps temperatures near 100 °C and slows browning. It underlies the flavour of grilled and roasted meats, toast, pretzels (helped by an alkaline lye bath), soy sauce, and beer malts. Liquid marinades and crowding a pan (which traps steam) inhibit it.

Health aspects

Advanced glycation end-products (AGEs) formed by Maillard chemistry in foods and in the body are studied for possible roles in ageing and diabetic complications. At very high temperatures, the amino acid asparagine can react with sugars to form acrylamide, a probable carcinogen monitored in fried potato and cereal products since its discovery in foods in 2002.
