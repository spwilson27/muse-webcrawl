Genetic privacy

Genetic privacy involves the concept of personal privacy concerning the storing, repurposing, provision to third parties, and display of information pertaining to an individual's genetic information. Because DNA is uniquely identifying, is shared with biological relatives, and can reveal predispositions to disease, genetic data raises privacy concerns that go beyond those of ordinary medical records.

Re-identification

Studies have shown that supposedly anonymised genomic data can often be re-identified. In 2013, researchers demonstrated that participants in public genomic projects could be identified by combining Y-chromosome data with genealogical databases and public records. Even aggregate statistics released from genome-wide association studies can leak whether a particular individual participated.

Familial implications

An individual's genome reveals information about their relatives, who did not consent to its release. Consumer genetic genealogy databases have been used by law enforcement to identify suspects through their distant relatives — most famously in the 2018 arrest of the Golden State Killer — prompting debate about consent and the limits of investigative genetic genealogy.

Legal frameworks

In the United States, the Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act of 2008 restricts use of genetic information by health insurers and employers but leaves life, disability, and long-term care insurance uncovered. In the European Union, genetic data is a special category of personal data under the General Data Protection Regulation, subject to heightened protection. Many jurisdictions also regulate police DNA databases and the retention of samples from arrestees.

Research ethics

Large biobanks and direct-to-consumer testing companies hold genetic data on millions of people. Ethical debate centres on informed consent for future uses, data sharing with commercial partners, the return of incidental findings, and the 2013 controversy over publication of the HeLa cell line genome without the Lacks family's consent, which led to a controlled-access agreement with the US National Institutes of Health.
