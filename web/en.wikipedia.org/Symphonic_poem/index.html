Symphonic poem

A symphonic poem or tone poem is a piece of orchestral music, usually in a single continuous movement, that illustrates or evokes the content of a poem, short story, novel, painting, landscape, or other non-musical source. The form was the emblematic genre of musical Romanticism and of programme music — music intended to depict an extra-musical narrative — as opposed to absolute music.

Origins and Liszt

The genre was invented and named by Franz Liszt, who composed thirteen symphonic poems between 1848 and 1882, beginning with Ce qu'on entend sur la montagne (after Victor Hugo) and including Les Préludes, Mazeppa, Prometheus, and Hamlet. Liszt developed the technique of thematic transformation, in which a single theme recurs in altered character throughout the work, and he intended the form to update the classical symphony with poetic content.

Development

The tone poem flourished especially in the late nineteenth century: Bedřich Smetana's cycle Má vlast (including Vltava), Antonín Dvořák's fairy-tale poems, Pyotr Ilyich Tchaikovsky's fantasy-overtures, Camille Saint-Saëns's Danse macabre, and the large-scale poems of Richard Strauss — Don Juan, Till Eulenspiegel's Merry Pranks, Also sprach Zarathustra, Ein Heldenleben, and the Alpine Symphony — which brought the genre to its peak. Jean Sibelius (Finlandia, The Swan of Tuonela), Sergei Rachmaninoff (The Isle of the Dead), and in the twentieth century Ottorino Respighi (Pines of Rome) and George Gershwin (An American in Paris) continued the tradition, though after about 1920 the genre waned with modernism's turn toward neoclassicism and abstraction.
