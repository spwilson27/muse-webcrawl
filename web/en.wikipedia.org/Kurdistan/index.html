Kurdistan

Kurdistan (Kurdish: کوردستان, "land of the Kurds") is a roughly defined geo-cultural region in Western Asia where the Kurds form a prominent majority and Kurdish culture, language, and national identity have historically been centred. Divided among Turkey, Iraq, Iran, and Syria, it is the homeland of the world's largest ethnic group without a sovereign state of its own.

Geography

Kurdistan covers approximately 190,000–500,000 km² depending on definition — commonly around 392,000 km² — centred on the Zagros and eastern Taurus mountain ranges and the upper basins of the Tigris and Euphrates. Major cities include Erbil (Hewlêr), Diyarbakır (Amed), Kirkuk, Sulaymaniyah, and Mahabad.

History

Kurds are generally considered descendants of diverse ancient peoples of the region; Kurdish principalities flourished under Islamic empires, and the geographer Sharaf Khan Bidlisi's Sharafnama (1597) gives an early history. The Ottoman–Persian Treaty of Zuhab (1639) first split Kurdistan between two empires. After the First World War, the Treaty of Sèvres (1920) contemplated Kurdish autonomy, but the Treaty of Lausanne (1923) divided the territory among Turkey, British-mandated Iraq, French-mandated Syria, and Persia.

Modern politics

Twentieth-century Kurdish history includes repeated revolts — Sheikh Mahmud in Iraq, Sheikh Said (1925) and Dersim (1937–38) in Turkey, the short-lived Republic of Mahabad in Iran (1946) — and repression, most notoriously Iraq's Anfal campaign and the Halabja chemical attack of 1988. The Kurdistan Region of Iraq gained constitutional autonomy after 2003; its 2017 independence referendum passed overwhelmingly but was rejected by Baghdad and followed by federal retaking of disputed territories. In Syria, Kurdish-led forces established the Autonomous Administration of North and East Syria during the civil war, and in Turkey the Kurdish question remains intertwined with the decades-long conflict with the PKK.
