In mathematics, the ring of integers of an algebraic number field K is the ring of all algebraic integers contained in K. An algebraic integer is a root of a monic polynomial with integer coefficients. The ring of integers is usually denoted 𝒪K. It generalises the ordinary integers ℤ, which are exactly the ring of integers of the rational field ℚ.

Properties

The ring 𝒪K is a Dedekind domain of Krull dimension one: every non-zero ideal factorises uniquely into prime ideals, even though elements themselves may fail to factorise uniquely into irreducibles — the failure measured by the ideal class group. As an abelian group, 𝒪K is a free ℤ-module whose rank equals the degree of K over ℚ; a basis is called an integral basis. The discriminant of the field can be computed from any integral basis.

Examples

For a square-free integer d, the ring of integers of ℚ(√d) consists of numbers (a + b√d)/2 with a, b integers of the same parity when d ≡ 1 (mod 4), and of a + b√d otherwise. The Gaussian integers ℤ[i] are the ring of integers of ℚ(i), and the Eisenstein integers ℤ[ω] belong to ℚ(√−3). Both rings enjoy unique factorisation, a fact used in classical proofs about sums of two squares and Fermat's Last Theorem for small exponents.
