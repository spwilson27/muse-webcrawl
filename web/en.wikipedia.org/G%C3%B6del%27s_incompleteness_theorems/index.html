Gödel's incompleteness theorems are two theorems of mathematical logic, proved by Austrian logician Kurt Gödel in 1931, that demonstrate inherent limitations of every formal axiomatic system capable of modelling basic arithmetic.

The first theorem

The first incompleteness theorem states that no consistent formal system whose theorems can be listed by an effective procedure, and which is capable of expressing elementary arithmetic, is complete: there exist statements of the system that are true but can neither be proved nor disproved within the system. Gödel's proof constructs such a statement by encoding statements and proofs as numbers (Gödel numbering), producing a sentence that effectively asserts its own unprovability.

The second theorem

The second incompleteness theorem shows that such a system cannot prove its own consistency, provided it is in fact consistent. This struck directly at Hilbert's program, which had sought a finitary proof of the consistency of all of mathematics.

Significance

The theorems are regarded as among the most important results in 20th-century logic, with wide influence on mathematics, computer science, and philosophy of mind. They are often summarized, loosely, as showing that truth in arithmetic outruns provability in any single formal system.
