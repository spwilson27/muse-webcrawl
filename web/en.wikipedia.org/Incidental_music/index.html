Incidental music

Incidental music is music composed to accompany a play, radio drama, television program, or other work that is not primarily musical. It typically consists of overtures, entr'actes, songs, dances, and underscoring that create atmosphere, mark scene changes, and heighten emotion — an ancestor of the modern film score.

History

Music accompanied drama from Greek tragedy onward. The nineteenth century produced famous concert-hall survivals: Beethoven's music for Egmont, Mendelssohn's for Shakespeare's A Midsummer Night's Dream (including the celebrated Wedding March), Bizet's for L'Arlésienne, Grieg's for Ibsen's Peer Gynt, and Sibelius's extensive stage scores. Suites extracted from such scores often outlived the plays they accompanied.

Modern usage

In film and television, the corresponding craft is the score; in theatre the term still denotes music written for a production, increasingly delivered by small ensembles or electronics. "Incidental music" also names the recurring musical cues and stingers used in broadcast programs and video games to accompany action that is not itself a musical number.
