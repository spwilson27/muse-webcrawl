Fraction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A fraction (from Latin fractus, "broken") represents a part of a whole or, more generally, any number of equal parts. A simple or common fraction consists of a numerator, displayed above a line, and a non-zero denominator, displayed below that line: for example, 3/4 denotes three equal parts of a whole divided into four.

Forms of fractions

A proper fraction has numerator smaller than its denominator (e.g. 2/3), while an improper fraction has numerator greater than or equal to the denominator (e.g. 7/4) and can be written as a mixed number, such as 1 3/4. Fractions with the same value, like 1/2, 2/4 and 50/100, are equivalent; reducing a fraction to lowest terms by dividing numerator and denominator by their greatest common divisor gives its simplest form.

Other forms include unit fractions (numerator 1, as used in ancient Egyptian mathematics), dyadic fractions (denominators powers of two), decimal fractions (denominators powers of ten, the basis of decimal notation and percentages), and continued fractions.

Arithmetic

Fractions are added and subtracted by finding a common denominator; they are multiplied by multiplying numerators and denominators separately; and divided by multiplying by the reciprocal of the divisor. Comparing fractions can be done by cross-multiplication or by conversion to a common denominator.

History

The earliest fractions were the unit fractions of ancient Egypt, recorded in sources such as the Rhind Mathematical Papyrus (c. 1550 BC). Babylonian mathematics used sexagesimal fractions; Greek and Indian mathematicians developed general fractions, and the horizontal fraction bar entered widespread use through medieval Arabic and European texts, including the work of Fibonacci in the Liber Abaci (1202).

In other contexts

Beyond arithmetic, "fraction" appears in algebra as a rational expression, in chemistry for quantities such as mole fraction, and in everyday usage for any small part of a whole.
