The Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee (SNCC, pronounced "snick") was the principal channel of student participation in the civil rights movement in the United States during the 1960s. Emerging in 1960 from the student-led sit-ins protesting segregated lunch counters in the American South, SNCC quickly became one of the movement's most important organizations, known for its grassroots organizing, voter registration drives, and willingness to confront segregation directly in some of the most dangerous areas of the Deep South.

History

SNCC was founded in April 1960 at a conference at Shaw University in Raleigh, North Carolina, organized by veteran activist Ella Baker. Baker urged the students to form an independent organization rather than become a youth wing of an existing group, and she championed a model of group-centered leadership rather than reliance on a single charismatic leader.

In 1961 SNCC members joined and sustained the Freedom Rides after the original riders from the Congress of Racial Equality were violently attacked. The organization then turned to voter registration, most famously in Mississippi, where field secretaries such as Bob Moses built local leadership in communities facing economic reprisal and violence.

Freedom Summer and the MFDP

In 1964 SNCC was the driving force behind Freedom Summer, which brought hundreds of mostly white northern volunteers to Mississippi to register Black voters and run Freedom Schools. That summer saw the murders of three civil rights workers — James Chaney, Andrew Goodman, and Michael Schwerner — and the founding of the Mississippi Freedom Democratic Party (MFDP), which challenged the seating of the all-white regular Mississippi delegation at the 1964 Democratic National Convention. Fannie Lou Hamer's televised testimony became one of the movement's defining moments.

Later years

Under chairman Stokely Carmichael from 1966, SNCC popularized the slogan "Black Power" and grew increasingly critical of nonviolence and of white participation in the organization. John Lewis, the previous chairman, later served decades in the U.S. House of Representatives. Financial strain, internal conflict, and surveillance and disruption by the FBI's COINTELPRO program contributed to SNCC's decline; it effectively dissolved by the mid-1970s.

Legacy

Historians credit SNCC with developing a generation of organizers and with pushing the civil rights movement toward participatory democracy. Many alumni went on to careers in electoral politics, education, and community organizing.
