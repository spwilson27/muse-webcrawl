Copernican heliocentrism is the astronomical model developed by Nicolaus Copernicus and published in 1543 in his book De revolutionibus orbium coelestium (On the Revolutions of the Celestial Spheres). The model positioned the Sun at the centre of the universe, motionless, with Earth and the other planets orbiting it in circular paths modified by epicycles. It displaced the geocentric Ptolemaic system, which had placed Earth at the centre for over 1,400 years.

The Copernican system, with the Sun at the centre and the planets, including Earth, revolving around it.

The model

Copernicus retained many elements of Ptolemaic astronomy, including uniform circular motion and epicycles, but rearranged the cosmos: the Moon orbits Earth; Earth rotates daily on its axis, explaining the apparent daily motion of the heavens; and Earth revolves annually around the Sun, so that retrograde motion of the planets becomes an apparent effect of Earth's own motion rather than real loops in planetary paths. The ordering of the planets by orbital period — Mercury fastest and nearest the Sun, Saturn slowest and farthest — fell out naturally from the geometry.

Precursors and development

Heliocentric ideas had been proposed in antiquity by Aristarchus of Samos (3rd century BC) and were discussed by medieval Islamic and European scholars. Copernicus circulated a short summary, the Commentariolus, before 1514, but delayed publishing the full work until the end of his life; legend holds he received a printed copy on his deathbed. The book was published in Nuremberg with an unauthorised preface by Andreas Osiander presenting the model as a mere computational hypothesis.

Reception and the Copernican Revolution

Initially the model attracted limited attention and some theological criticism, but it launched what historians call the Copernican Revolution. Tycho Brahe's precise observations, Johannes Kepler's laws of planetary motion (1609–1619), which replaced circles with ellipses, Galileo Galilei's telescopic discoveries such as the phases of Venus and the moons of Jupiter, and Isaac Newton's law of universal gravitation (1687) together established heliocentrism as physical reality. The Roman Catholic Church placed De revolutionibus on the Index of Forbidden Books "until corrected" in 1616, and Galileo was tried in 1633, yet by the end of the 17th century the heliocentric picture was generally accepted among astronomers.
