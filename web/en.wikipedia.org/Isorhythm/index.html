Isorhythm

In isorhythm, a rhythmic pattern (talea) repeats independently of the melodic pattern (color).

Isorhythm (from the Greek for "the same rhythm") is a musical composition technique that uses a repeating rhythmic pattern, characteristic of the motets of the 14th and early 15th centuries, especially those of Philippe de Vitry and Guillaume de Machaut.

Technique

In an isorhythmic motet, the tenor voice is built from two independent patterns: the talea, a repeating sequence of rhythms, and the color, a repeating sequence of pitches. Because the two patterns usually have different lengths, they realign at different points on each repetition, generating complex large-scale structure. In later works the technique was extended to all voices, and patterns were often subjected to diminution, with note values halved or otherwise reduced on repetition.

History and influence

Isorhythm flourished in the French Ars nova and remained in use into the early Renaissance, notably in masses and motets of Guillaume Du Fay. The term itself was coined by musicologists around 1900. Related repeating-pattern ideas appear in the ostinato, in Indian tala, and in some 20th-century serial and minimalist music.
