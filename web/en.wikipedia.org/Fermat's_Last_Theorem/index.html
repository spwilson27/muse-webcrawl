Fermat's Last Theorem

Fermat's Last Theorem
FieldNumber theory
Conjectured byPierre de Fermat, c. 1637
First proofAndrew Wiles, 1994 (published 1995)

Fermat's Last Theorem states that no three positive integers a, b, and c satisfy the equation

an + bn = cn

for any integer value of n greater than 2. The cases n = 1 and n = 2 have infinitely many solutions (the latter given by Pythagorean triples), which makes the theorem's threshold at n = 2 striking.

History

Around 1637, Pierre de Fermat wrote in the margin of his copy of Diophantus's Arithmetica that he had "a truly marvelous proof of this, which this margin is too narrow to contain." Whether Fermat actually possessed a valid general proof is doubted by most historians; he himself proved only the case n = 4, by infinite descent.

Over the following centuries, proofs were found for individual exponents: Euler for n = 3, Dirichlet and Legendre for n = 5, Lamé for n = 7, and Ernst Kummer for all regular primes, introducing ideal numbers and founding much of algebraic number theory. By 1993 the theorem had been verified for exponents up to four million, yet no general proof existed.

Wiles's proof

In 1986 Gerhard Frey, Jean-Pierre Serre, and Ken Ribet connected the theorem to the modularity conjecture for elliptic curves: if a counterexample existed, the associated elliptic curve could not be modular. British mathematician Andrew Wiles, working in secrecy for six years, proved enough of the modularity theorem for semistable elliptic curves to settle the question. He announced the proof in June 1993; a gap found in review was repaired with Richard Taylor in 1994, and the proof was published in 1995.

Consequences

The effort to prove Fermat's Last Theorem drove the creation of algebraic number theory and, through the modularity theorem it helped establish, reshaped modern arithmetic geometry. It remains the most famous example of a simply stated problem resisting proof for over 350 years.
