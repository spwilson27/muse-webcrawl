Morning sickness

[Image: Illustration related to nausea in pregnancy]

Morning sickness, also called nausea and vomiting of pregnancy (NVP), is a symptom complex of nausea and sometimes vomiting experienced by a majority of pregnant women. Despite its name, symptoms may occur at any time of day. It usually begins around the sixth week of pregnancy, peaks around weeks nine to eleven, and resolves by around the twelfth to fourteenth week, though in some women it persists longer.

Causes

The cause is not fully understood, but rising levels of human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) and estrogen are strongly implicated, as symptoms track the rise and fall of hCG in early pregnancy. A 2023 line of research identified the hormone GDF15, produced largely by the fetus, as a key driver: sensitivity to GDF15 - influenced by a woman's pre-pregnancy exposure - appears to determine severity. Risk factors include a previous history of NVP, carrying twins, migraine and motion sickness. Mild NVP has been associated with somewhat lower rates of miscarriage.

Severe form and management

A severe form, hyperemesis gravidarum, involves persistent vomiting, dehydration, weight loss of more than five percent and electrolyte disturbance, and may require hospital treatment with intravenous fluids and antiemetics. For ordinary morning sickness, dietary measures (small frequent meals, ginger), vitamin B6 (pyridoxine), doxylamine, and various antiemetic medications are used. Thalidomide was infamously marketed for morning sickness in the late 1950s before its teratogenic effects caused its withdrawal.
