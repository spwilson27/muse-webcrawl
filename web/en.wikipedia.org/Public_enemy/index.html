Public enemy

A public enemy is a person considered a danger or menace to society. The term is best known from 1930s United States law enforcement, when it was applied to notorious gangsters and bank robbers of the Prohibition and Great Depression era, and from the FBI's ranked lists of "public enemies," which evolved into the Ten Most Wanted Fugitives list.

Origins and use in the United States

The phrase entered American usage in 1930, when the Chicago Crime Commission, led by Frank J. Loesch, published a list of 28 "public enemies" of Chicago headed by Al Capone. The term was popularized nationally during the FBI's "War on Crime" of 1933–34, when J. Edgar Hoover's Bureau publicized its pursuit of criminals such as John Dillinger — designated "Public Enemy No. 1" — along with "Pretty Boy" Floyd, "Baby Face" Nelson, and Bonnie and Clyde. The designation helped build public support for expanding federal law enforcement powers. In Roman law, a similar concept, hostis publicus, denoted an enemy of the state who could be killed with impunity.

Later and figurative uses

The label has since been applied rhetorically to corrupt officials, drug traffickers, and terrorists, and it appears extensively in popular culture: the 1931 James Cagney film The Public Enemy dramatized the gangster era, and the hip hop group Public Enemy, formed in 1985, adopted the name to signify a voice for communities they felt were treated as threats. In the twenty-first century the phrase "Public Enemy No. 1" is also used informally for the most pressing problem in a field, such as a disease or pollutant.
