Scaffolding (education)

In education, scaffolding refers to the temporary support a teacher provides to help a student accomplish a task or understand a concept that they could not manage independently. Like the scaffolding on a building, the support is gradually removed as the learner gains competence.

Origins

The term derives from research by David Wood, Jerome Bruner, and Gail Ross (1976), who studied how adults tutor children in problem-solving. It is closely linked to Lev Vygotsky's concept of the zone of proximal development — the gap between what a learner can do alone and what they can do with guidance.

Methods

Scaffolding strategies include modelling, breaking tasks into smaller steps, using graphic organisers, pre-teaching vocabulary, prompting and questioning, providing worked examples and sentence starters, and giving feedback. Effective scaffolding is contingent (responsive to the learner's current performance), involves gradual fading of support, and aims at transferring responsibility to the student.
