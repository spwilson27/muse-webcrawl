The appendix (pl.: appendices or appendixes; also called the vermiform appendix; caecal [or cecal] appendix; vermify; or vermiform process) is a finger-like, blind-ended tube connected to the cecum, from which it develops in the embryo. The cecum is a pouch-like structure of the large intestine, located at the junction of the small and large intestines. The term "vermiform" comes from Latin and means "worm-shaped." The appendix used to be considered a vestigial organ, but this view has largely changed since the early 2000s.

Structure

The human appendix averages 9 cm (3.5 in) in length but can range from 2 to 20 cm (0.8 to 7.9 in). The diameter is usually between 7 and 8 mm. It hangs down from the cecum, most commonly in a retrocecal or pelvic position, though its precise location varies considerably between individuals. Its position on the abdominal wall corresponds to McBurney's point. The appendix contains lymphoid tissue similar to that of the tonsils and Peyer's patches.

Function

Although Charles Darwin proposed that the appendix was a useless remnant of a larger cecum used by ancestors to digest leaves, modern research suggests functions. The appendix is rich in immune tissue and biofilms, and a leading hypothesis holds that it serves as a "safe house" for beneficial gut bacteria, allowing recolonization of the colon after diarrheal illness. Comparative studies show the appendix has evolved independently many times among mammals, which argues against it being purely vestigial.

Clinical significance

The most common disease of the appendix is appendicitis, an inflammation that typically causes pain migrating to the right lower quadrant, nausea, and fever; it is treated by appendectomy or, in selected cases, antibiotics. Less common conditions include appendiceal tumors such as carcinoid (neuroendocrine) tumors and mucinous neoplasms.
