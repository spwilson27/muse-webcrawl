History of Israel

The history of Israel covers the region of the southern Levant from prehistory through the ancient Israelite kingdoms, successive foreign empires, and the modern State of Israel established in 1948. The land — known in different eras as Canaan, Israel and Judah, Judaea, Palestine and the Holy Land — holds central significance in Judaism, Christianity and Islam.

Ancient period

The region was among the earliest centres of agriculture and urban life. The name "Israel" first appears on the Egyptian Merneptah Stele (c. 1208 BCE). According to biblical tradition, united Israelite monarchy under Saul, David and Solomon gave way to the kingdoms of Israel (north) and Judah (south). Assyria destroyed Israel in 722 BCE; Babylon destroyed Jerusalem and the First Temple in 586 BCE and exiled Judah's elite. Under Persian rule the exiles returned and built the Second Temple. The Hasmonean dynasty (from 140 BCE) restored Jewish independence until the Roman conquest; revolts against Rome (66–73 and 132–135 CE) ended with the Temple's destruction in 70 CE, the fall of Masada, and the renaming of the province as Syria Palaestina. Rabbinic Judaism developed in Galilee, where the Mishnah and the Jerusalem Talmud were compiled.

Medieval and Ottoman periods

Byzantine rule gave way in the 630s–640s to Muslim conquest; the Dome of the Rock and Al-Aqsa Mosque made Jerusalem a holy city of Islam. Crusader states held the coast and Jerusalem between 1099 and 1187, when Saladin retook the city. Mamluk and then Ottoman rule (1517–1917) followed; Jewish communities persisted in Jerusalem, Safed, Hebron and Tiberias, and Safed became a centre of Kabbalah.

Zionism, the Mandate and statehood

In the late 19th century the Zionist movement, led by Theodor Herzl, organised Jewish return to the land in successive waves of immigration (aliyot). Britain's Balfour Declaration (1917) promised a Jewish national home, and Britain governed under a League of Nations Mandate from 1920. Jewish immigration and land purchase, Arab resistance, and revolts (1936–39) marked the Mandate decades. After the Holocaust, the UN voted in 1947 to partition Palestine into Jewish and Arab states. On 14 May 1948 David Ben-Gurion proclaimed the State of Israel; neighbouring Arab states invaded, and the ensuing war ended in 1949 armistice lines, Israeli independence, and the flight and expulsion of some 700,000 Palestinian Arabs (the Nakba).

The modern state

Israel absorbed mass immigration from Europe and the Middle East, fought further wars in 1956, 1967 (the Six-Day War, after which it occupied the West Bank, Gaza Strip, Sinai and Golan Heights), 1973 and Lebanon in 1982. It signed peace treaties with Egypt (1979) and Jordan (1994), the Oslo Accords with the PLO (1993–95), and normalisation agreements with several Arab states in 2020 (the Abraham Accords). The Israeli–Palestinian conflict — through two intifadas, the Gaza wars and settlement expansion — remains unresolved, as do questions of borders and Jerusalem's status.
