Firebreak

A firebreak (sometimes called a fire line, fuel break or firetrail) is a gap in vegetation or other combustible material that acts as a barrier to slow or stop the progress of a bushfire or wildfire. A firebreak may occur naturally where there is a lack of vegetation or fuel, such as a river, lake or canyon. Firebreaks may also be man-made, and many of these also serve as roads, such as a logging road, four-wheel drive trail, secondary road, or a highway.

Construction and use

Firefighters construct firebreaks by clearing vegetation down to mineral soil with hand tools, bulldozers or other machinery, often in combination with backburning — deliberately burning the fuel between the break and the approaching fire. The width required depends on fuel type, slope and expected flame length; a break too narrow can be jumped by radiant heat, convection or wind-blown embers (spotting).

Effectiveness and ecology

Firebreaks are most effective under moderate fire conditions and as anchor points for suppression operations; under extreme weather, long-range spotting can render them ineffective. Permanent fuel breaks can fragment habitat and promote erosion or invasive species, so land managers increasingly favour shaded fuel breaks, in which undergrowth is removed but a canopy of fire-resistant trees is retained.
