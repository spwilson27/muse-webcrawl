John Steinbeck

John Ernst Steinbeck (February 27, 1902 – December 20, 1968) was an American writer and the 1962 Nobel Prize in Literature recipient. He has been called "a giant of American letters." His works, including his Pulitzer Prize-winning novel The Grapes of Wrath (1939) and the novella Of Mice and Men (1937), are frequently read in school curriculums throughout the United States and the United Kingdom.

Early life

Steinbeck was born in Salinas, California, and spent his summers working on ranches in the Salinas Valley, whose landscape and people deeply shaped his fiction. He attended Stanford University intermittently without graduating, then worked various jobs, including as a manual laborer, while beginning to write.

Major works

Steinbeck's breakthrough came with Tortilla Flat (1935). Of Mice and Men (1937) tells of two migrant ranch workers, George Milton and Lennie Small, during the Great Depression. The Grapes of Wrath (1939) follows the Joad family, Oklahoma sharecroppers driven west to California by the Dust Bowl; it won the Pulitzer Prize for Fiction and became a landmark of American social realism. Later works include Cannery Row (1945), East of Eden (1952), which Steinbeck considered his masterpiece, and Travels with Charley (1962), a travelogue of a road trip across America with his poodle.

Legacy

Steinbeck wrote 33 books, including 16 novels, six non-fiction books, and two collections of short stories. Many of his works were adapted into films. He received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1962 "for his realistic and imaginative writings, combining as they do sympathetic humour and keen social perception." He died in New York City in 1968.
