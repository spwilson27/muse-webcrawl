Borobudur

Borobudur is a 9th-century Mahayana Buddhist temple in Magelang Regency, Central Java, Indonesia. It is the world's largest Buddhist temple and one of the greatest monuments of Buddhist architecture. Built during the reign of the Sailendra dynasty of the Mataram Kingdom (c. 780–825 CE), the temple is designed as a stepped pyramid and three-dimensional mandala: pilgrims ascend through nine stacked platforms, passing some 2,672 relief panels and 504 Buddha statues, in a journey from the world of desire to enlightenment.

Architecture

Borobudur is built as a single large stupa on a square base about 123 metres on each side. Six square terraces rise to three circular platforms crowned by a central dome (stupa). The square levels' walls carry narrative reliefs illustrating the Lalitavistara (life of the Buddha), the Jataka tales of his previous lives, and the Gandavyuha, the pilgrim Sudhana's quest for enlightenment. On the circular upper terraces, 72 openwork stupas each enclose a Buddha statue. The whole structure has been interpreted as a map of the Buddhist cosmos, a mandala, and an artificial mountain in the tradition of Mount Meru.

History

No written records name Borobudur's builder; inscriptions suggest construction began under the Sailendra king Indra and was completed under Samaratungga around 825 CE. The temple was abandoned as Central Javanese power shifted east in the 10th century and as Islam spread in later centuries, and it lay buried under jungle and volcanic ash until 1814, when the British lieutenant governor of Java, Thomas Stamford Raffles, ordered it cleared. Dutch-led restorations followed in 1907–1911, and a full UNESCO-assisted dismantling and reconstruction between 1973 and 1983 stabilised the monument. In 1985 a bombing by extremists damaged nine stupas, which were repaired.

Pilgrimage and tourism

Borobudur is Indonesia's most visited tourist attraction and the centre of the national Vesak (Waisak) celebrations, when monks process from Mendut temple to Borobudur and release lanterns at night. Together with the nearby temples of Pawon and Mendut — aligned on a single axis — it forms a single sacred complex.
