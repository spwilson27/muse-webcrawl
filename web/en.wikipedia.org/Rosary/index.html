The Rosary (from Latin rosarium, "rose garden") is a set of prayers in the Catholic tradition, prayed using a string of beads, and by extension the beads themselves. The prayers combine vocal recitation — chiefly the Hail Mary, the Lord's Prayer, and the Glory Be — with meditation on episodes ("mysteries") from the lives of Jesus Christ and the Virgin Mary.

Structure

A standard rosary consists of five decades, each comprising one Our Father, ten Hail Marys, and one Glory Be, counted on beads arranged in a loop with a crucifix and centrepiece medal. Each decade is devoted to a mystery. Traditionally fifteen mysteries were divided into the Joyful, Sorrowful, and Glorious sets; in 2002 Pope John Paul II added the five Luminous Mysteries, drawn from Christ's public ministry, bringing the total to twenty.

History

Prayer beads have ancient precedents in many religions, and repetitive Marian prayer developed in medieval monasticism as a "Psalter of Mary" for those who could not read the 150 psalms. Dominican tradition attributes the rosary's promotion to Saint Dominic in the 13th century; the Carthusians and Dominicans shaped its modern form in the 15th century. Pope Pius V standardised the devotion in 1569, and the victory at the Battle of Lepanto in 1571 was credited to it, giving rise to the Feast of Our Lady of the Rosary on 7 October.

Practice and significance

The rosary remains one of the most widespread Catholic devotions, prayed privately, in families, and in parish groups. Popes have repeatedly commended it, notably Leo XIII, who wrote numerous encyclicals on the rosary, and John Paul II in his apostolic letter Rosarium Virginis Mariae (2002). Analogous bead prayers exist in Eastern Christianity (the prayer rope), Anglicanism, and other faiths.
