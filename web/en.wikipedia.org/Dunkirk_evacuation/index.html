Dunkirk evacuation

British troops awaiting evacuation at Dunkirk, 1940 (placeholder image)

The Dunkirk evacuation, codenamed Operation Dynamo, was the evacuation of Allied soldiers during the Second World War from the beaches and harbour of Dunkirk, in northern France, between 26 May and 4 June 1940. After Germany invaded France and the Low Countries, the British Expeditionary Force and French troops were cut off and surrounded, and a large-scale sea evacuation to England became necessary.

Background

The German breakthrough at Sedan and the drive to the Channel in May 1940 trapped the BEF and the French First Army around Dunkirk. A controversial German halt order gave the Allies time to organize a defensive perimeter. Vice-Admiral Bertram Ramsay, based in Dover Castle, directed the naval evacuation.

The evacuation

Planners hoped to rescue about 45,000 men. In the event, 338,226 soldiers — about 198,000 British and 140,000 French and Belgian — were lifted from Dunkirk. Alongside Royal Navy destroyers and transports, some 800–900 civilian "little ships" — pleasure craft, fishing boats and lifeboats — crossed the Channel, many ferrying men from the beaches to larger vessels offshore. The RAF fought to protect the beaches from the Luftwaffe, often out of sight of the troops, who sometimes felt abandoned.

Aftermath

Although nearly all heavy equipment was left behind and the operation followed a major defeat, the rescue preserved the core of the British Army and became a defining symbol of British resolve — the "Dunkirk spirit". Winston Churchill cautioned in his 4 June 1940 speech that "wars are not won by evacuations", even as he praised the deliverance. French rearguard troops who held the perimeter were largely captured. The evacuation has been depicted in films including Dunkirk (2017).
