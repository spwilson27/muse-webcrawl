Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1

Other namesWermer's syndrome, MEN1
SpecialtyEndocrinology, medical genetics
CauseMutation in the MEN1 gene (menin)
InheritanceAutosomal dominant
FrequencyAbout 1 in 30,000 people

Multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1), also called Wermer's syndrome, is a rare inherited disorder that causes tumours in multiple endocrine glands, most characteristically the parathyroid glands, the endocrine pancreas (and duodenum), and the anterior pituitary gland — a classic triad sometimes remembered as the "three Ps". The condition is caused by inactivating mutations in the MEN1 tumour-suppressor gene on chromosome 11q13, which encodes the protein menin, and is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern.

MEN1 principally affects the parathyroid glands, enteropancreatic neuroendocrine tissue, and anterior pituitary.

Signs and symptoms

Primary hyperparathyroidism is the most common manifestation, occurring in roughly 90–95% of patients, usually by early adulthood; overactivity of the parathyroid glands raises blood calcium, causing kidney stones, bone pain, and fatigue. Enteropancreatic neuroendocrine tumours occur in a large proportion of patients and include gastrinomas (causing Zollinger–Ellison syndrome), insulinomas (causing hypoglycaemia), and often non-functioning tumours. Anterior pituitary adenomas, most frequently prolactinomas, occur in about a third of patients. Other features include adrenal cortical tumours, thymic and bronchial carcinoids, lipomas, angiofibromas, and collagenomas.

Genetics and diagnosis

MEN1 follows Knudson's two-hit model: patients inherit one defective copy of the gene and tumours arise when the second copy is lost in a susceptible cell. Diagnosis is made by finding two or more characteristic tumours, by the combination of one tumour and an affected first-degree relative, or by genetic testing identifying a pathogenic MEN1 variant.

Management

There is no cure; management is lifelong surveillance with periodic biochemical screening and imaging, beginning in childhood for known mutation carriers. Hyperparathyroidism is usually treated surgically, hormone-secreting tumours with medication and surgery as appropriate, and pancreatic neuroendocrine tumours according to size and behaviour, since malignant pancreatic tumours and thymic carcinoids are the leading causes of death in MEN1.
