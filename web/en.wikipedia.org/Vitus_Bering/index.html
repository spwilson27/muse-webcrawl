Vitus Bering

Vitus Jonassen Bering (August 1681 – December 19, 1741), also known as Ivan Ivanovich Bering in Russia, was a Danish cartographer and explorer in Russian service, and an officer in the Russian Navy. He is known for his two Kamchatka expeditions, which explored the northeastern coast of Asia and the northwestern coast of North America. The Bering Strait, the Bering Sea, Bering Island, the Bering Glacier and the Bering land bridge are all named after him.

First Kamchatka expedition

Bering joined the Russian navy in 1704 and served in the Great Northern War. In 1725, Tsar Peter the Great charged him with determining whether Asia and North America were connected by land. Sailing from Kamchatka in 1728 in the St. Gabriel, Bering passed through the strait that now bears his name and reached about 67°N, proving that the two continents were separated by water, although — in fog — he never saw the American coast.

Great Northern Expedition

The second, far larger expedition (1733–1743), sometimes called the Great Northern Expedition, mapped much of Siberia's Arctic coast and in 1741 reached Alaska. Bering's ship St. Peter, separated from Alexei Chirikov's St. Paul, sighted Mount Saint Elias and explored the Aleutian Islands on the return voyage. Beset by storms and scurvy, the St. Peter was wrecked on the uninhabited island later named Bering Island, where Bering and 28 of his men died. The survivors, under Sven Waxell, built a vessel from the wreck and returned in 1742 with sea otter pelts that sparked Russia's fur trade in Alaska.

Legacy

Bering's voyages established that a sea passage separated Asia from America and launched the Russian colonization of Alaska. In 1991 a Danish-Russian expedition exhumed his remains on Bering Island, allowing forensic reconstruction of his appearance, which disproved the portly portrait long attributed to him.
