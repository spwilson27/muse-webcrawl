Condition-of-England novel

The Condition-of-England novel (also called the industrial novel or social problem novel) is a genre of Victorian literature that sought to examine the social and economic problems created by the Industrial Revolution in Britain, and to foster sympathy between the classes. The phrase derives from Thomas Carlyle's essay "Chartism" (1839), which raised "the Condition-of-England question".

Major works

Key examples include Benjamin Disraeli's Sybil, or The Two Nations (1845), Elizabeth Gaskell's Mary Barton (1848) and North and South (1854–55), Charles Kingsley's Alton Locke (1850), Charles Dickens's Hard Times (1854), and George Eliot's Felix Holt, the Radical (1866). These novels depict factory life, trade unions, Chartism, poverty, and public health crises, often drawing on parliamentary reports and journalism.

Themes

Condition-of-England novels typically juxtapose the "two nations" of rich and poor, criticise laissez-faire political economy and utilitarianism, and propose reconciliation through mutual understanding, Christian charity, or paternalist reform rather than revolution. Critics have debated the genre's politics, noting its reformist sympathy alongside anxiety about working-class collective action.
