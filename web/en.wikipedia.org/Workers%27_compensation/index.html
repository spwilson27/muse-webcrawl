Workers' compensation

Workers' compensation or workers' comp is a form of insurance providing wage replacement and medical benefits to employees injured in the course of employment, in exchange for mandatory relinquishment of the employee's right to sue the employer for negligence. This "compensation bargain" is a hallmark of no-fault systems adopted in most industrialised countries.

History

The first modern system was introduced in Prussia in 1884 under Chancellor Otto von Bismarck. In the United States, Upton Sinclair's 1906 novel The Jungle drew attention to workplace dangers, and Wisconsin passed the first effective workers' compensation law in 1911. By 1949 all U.S. states had enacted schemes, administered at state level.

Benefits

Systems typically cover medical treatment, temporary and permanent disability payments, rehabilitation, and death benefits for dependants. Coverage and benefit levels vary widely by jurisdiction, and exclusions commonly apply to injuries caused by intoxication or wilful misconduct.
