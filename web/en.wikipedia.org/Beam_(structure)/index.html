Beam (structure)

A beam is a structural element that primarily resists loads applied laterally across its axis, mainly by bending. Beams are characterised by their cross-sectional shape, length, material and manner of support, and they are fundamental components of buildings, bridges and machines, carrying loads to columns, walls or other supports.

Classification

By support conditions, common types include the simply supported beam (resting on supports at each end), the cantilever (fixed at one end and free at the other), the continuous beam (spanning multiple supports) and fixed beams restrained against rotation at both ends. By cross-section, common forms are the I-beam, rectangular timber or concrete beams, box beams, and T-beams.

Mechanics

When a beam bends, its top fibres are compressed and its bottom fibres stretched (for a downward load), with a neutral axis between them where stress is zero. Beam theory, developed from the work of Galileo, Bernoulli and Euler, relates bending moment to curvature and stress; the classic Euler–Bernoulli beam equation underpins most structural design. Designers check beams for bending stress, shear, deflection and buckling, and choose materials — timber, steel, reinforced or prestressed concrete, or composites — accordingly.

Applications

Beams appear as joists and girders in floors, lintels over openings, bridge spans, crane rails and airframe spars. Their efficiency comes from placing material far from the neutral axis, as in the flanges of an I-beam, maximising stiffness and strength for a given weight.
