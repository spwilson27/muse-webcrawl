The Health and Social Care Act 2012 (c. 7) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that introduced the most extensive reorganisation of the structure of the National Health Service in England since the service's creation. Championed by Health Secretary Andrew Lansley under the Conservative–Liberal Democrat coalition government, it received royal assent on 27 March 2012, with its main provisions taking effect on 1 April 2013.

Provisions

The Act abolished primary care trusts (PCTs) and strategic health authorities, transferring commissioning of most hospital and community services to clinical commissioning groups (CCGs) led by general practitioners. It created NHS England (the NHS Commissioning Board) as an arm's-length body, moved public health responsibilities to local authorities under Public Health England, established Healthwatch as a patient voice body, and converted Monitor into an economic regulator for health care. It removed the cap on the proportion of income NHS foundation trusts could earn from private patients, increasing it to 49%.

Reception and aftermath

The reforms were fiercely controversial: most medical royal colleges, nursing unions, and health professional bodies opposed the bill, which underwent an unusual government "listening pause" in 2011 and hundreds of amendments. Critics argued it fragmented the NHS and increased marketisation and privatisation; supporters said it put clinicians in charge of budgets and cut bureaucracy. From 2016 onwards, NHS policy (Sustainability and Transformation Plans, then Integrated Care Systems) increasingly worked around the Act's competition framework, and the Health and Care Act 2022 reversed key elements, replacing CCGs with Integrated Care Boards and removing compulsory competitive tendering.
