Eucalyptus

Eucalyptus regnans (mountain ash), among the tallest flowering plants in the world.

Eucalyptus is a genus of more than 700 species of flowering plants in the family Myrtaceae, most native to Australia, with a small number also found in New Guinea and nearby islands. Known commonly as eucalypts or gum trees, they dominate the Australian landscape, occurring in almost every habitat from arid inland plains to alpine areas and wet coastal forests; they are absent only from the driest deserts and dense rainforests.

Description

Eucalypts range from small shrubs and multi-stemmed "mallees" to very tall single-trunked forest trees; Eucalyptus regnans can exceed 85 metres (280 ft) in height, making it one of the tallest angiosperms on earth. Many species shed bark annually in ribbons, plates or fibrous strips (stringybarks, ironbarks, boxes and smooth-barked "gums" are named for bark type). Leaves of most species are dimorphic: broad, round juvenile leaves give way to long, sickle-shaped adult leaves, which typically hang vertically and are rich in volatile eucalyptus oil. Flowers have no petals; their showy appearance comes from numerous stamens, and the fused sepals form a cap (operculum) that gives the genus its name — from Greek eu ("well") and kalyptos ("covered"). The woody fruit is commonly called a gumnut.

Ecology and fire

Eucalyptus forests are highly fire-adapted: many species resprout from epicormic buds beneath thick bark or from an underground lignotuber, and some have seeds whose release or germination is promoted by fire. Their oils make foliage flammable, and crown fires are a natural feature of many eucalypt landscapes, with consequences for Australian bushfire regimes. Eucalypts are the principal food trees of koalas, and their hollows provide habitat for many birds and mammals.

Uses and cultivation abroad

Eucalypts are grown worldwide in plantations for pulpwood, timber, poles and honey production, and eucalyptus oil is used in pharmaceuticals and flavourings. They are among the fastest-growing hardwoods, but introductions have sometimes been controversial: heavy water use, fire risk and invasiveness have led to restrictions in parts of California, South Africa, Spain and elsewhere, while in countries such as Brazil, China and Portugal they are major plantation species.
