Dopamine receptor

Dopamine receptors are a class of G protein-coupled receptors that are prominent in the vertebrate central nervous system. The neurotransmitter dopamine is the primary endogenous ligand for dopamine receptors. These receptors are involved in many neurological processes, including motivation, pleasure, cognition, memory, learning, and fine motor control, as well as modulation of neuroendocrine signaling.

Subtypes

Five subtypes of dopamine receptors have been identified, labeled D1 through D5. They are divided into two families: the D1-like family (D1 and D5), which generally stimulate adenylyl cyclase via Gs proteins, and the D2-like family (D2, D3, and D4), which inhibit adenylyl cyclase via Gi proteins.

Signaling

Activation of D1-like receptors increases intracellular cyclic AMP, while D2-like receptor activation decreases it. Dopamine receptors also signal through non-canonical pathways, including beta-arrestin recruitment.

Role in disease

Abnormal dopamine receptor signaling has been implicated in schizophrenia, Parkinson's disease, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, and addiction. Most antipsychotic drugs act as antagonists at D2 receptors, while Parkinson's disease is treated with dopamine receptor agonists such as pramipexole and ropinirole.
