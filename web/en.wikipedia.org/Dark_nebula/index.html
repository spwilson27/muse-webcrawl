Dark nebula

A dark nebula or absorption nebula is a type of interstellar cloud that is so dense that it obscures the visible wavelengths of light from objects behind it, such as background stars and emission or reflection nebulae. The extinction of the light is caused by interstellar dust grains in the coldest, densest parts of molecular clouds.

Characteristics

Dark nebulae are cold clouds of gas and dust, with typical temperatures of 10–20 K and masses from a few solar masses to over a million. The smallest, roundish, isolated dark nebulae are called Bok globules, after astronomer Bart Bok, and are dense cores where stars may be forming. Because the dust blocks visible light, dark nebulae are studied in infrared and radio wavelengths, which penetrate the dust. On large scales, the dark lanes visible in the Milky Way — such as the Great Rift — are caused by dark nebulae between the Sun and the galactic centre.

Notable dark nebulae

The Horsehead Nebula (Barnard 33) in Orion is silhouetted against the emission nebula IC 434. The Coalsack Nebula, near the Southern Cross, is the most prominent dark nebula in the sky, visible to the naked eye. Other examples include the Pipe Nebula in Ophiuchus, the Snake Nebula, and the extensive Taurus Molecular Cloud, one of the nearest star-forming regions.

History

Astronomer Edward Emerson Barnard catalogued over 300 dark nebulae in the early 20th century, establishing that they were obscuring clouds rather than gaps in the stars. Beverly Lynds' 1962 catalogue listed 1,802 dark nebulae.
