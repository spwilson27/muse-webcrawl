Box–Behnken design

Box–Behnken designs are experimental designs for response surface methodology, devised by George E. P. Box and Donald Behnken in 1960. They are used to fit second-order (quadratic) models to a response as a function of several quantitative factors, while requiring fewer experimental runs than a full three-level factorial design or a central composite design.

Structure

In a Box–Behnken design, each factor is run at three coded levels (−1, 0, +1). The design points are formed by taking pairs of factors at their extreme combinations (±1, ±1) while all remaining factors sit at their centre value (0), together with several replicated centre points. Consequently, the design contains no points at the corners of the experimental cube and none at the extreme axial positions — a useful property when those combinations are physically impossible, unsafe, or excessively costly. For three factors, the design needs only 12 factorial-style points plus centre replicates, compared with 27 runs for a full 3³ factorial.

Properties and use

Box–Behnken designs are rotatable (or nearly so) for certain numbers of factors, giving prediction variance that depends mainly on distance from the centre. They are widely applied in chemistry, engineering, pharmaceutical formulation, and process optimisation, where fitting a quadratic response surface identifies optimal factor settings with modest experimental effort.
