The waterfall illusion is a form of the motion aftereffect: after watching a moving stimulus for some time — classically, the falling water of a waterfall — a stationary scene viewed immediately afterwards appears to move in the opposite direction. If you stare at a waterfall for a minute and then look at the rocks beside it, the rocks seem to drift upwards.

History

The phenomenon was remarked on by Aristotle and by Lucretius, who noticed that after watching a river flow, stationary objects seemed to move. The effect takes its name from a letter published in 1834 by Robert Addams, who described experiencing it at the Falls of Foyers in Scotland: after gazing at the falls, the adjacent cliffs seemed to ascend. The psychologist Silvanus P. Thompson coined related demonstrations (such as the rotating spiral aftereffect) in the 19th century.

Explanation

The waterfall illusion is explained by adaptation in direction-selective neurons of the visual system. Neurons tuned to downward motion respond vigorously during viewing of the waterfall and become "fatigued" (adapted). When a stationary scene is then viewed, the reduced activity of downward-tuned neurons is interpreted relative to the unadapted upward-tuned population, producing apparent upward motion. The effect demonstrates that motion is computed by opponent channels and was historically used as evidence for labelled motion detectors in the brain. Variants include the spiral aftereffect, the motion aftereffect for expanding/contracting patterns, and contingent aftereffects such as the McKay effect and colour-contingent motion aftereffects studied in the 20th century.

Uses

The motion aftereffect remains a standard tool in vision science for probing motion processing, is exploited in art and animation, and appears in perceptual demonstrations widely reproduced in museums and textbooks.
