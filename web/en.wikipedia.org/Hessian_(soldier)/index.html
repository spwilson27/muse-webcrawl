Hessian was the term given to the 18th-century German auxiliaries contracted for military service by the British government, who took part in the American Revolutionary War. The term derives from the Landgraviate of Hesse-Kassel, which supplied the largest contingent, though troops also came from Hesse-Hanau, Brunswick, Ansbach-Bayreuth, Waldeck and Anhalt-Zerbst.

Background

German princes routinely subsidised their budgets by hiring regiments to foreign powers. When Britain needed troops for America, it concluded treaties with six German states. In total about 30,000 German soldiers served in North America between 1776 and 1783, roughly a third of the Crown's forces there.

In the Revolutionary War

Hessians fought in most major campaigns, including the battles of Long Island, White Plains and Brandywine. They are best remembered in American history for the garrison at Trenton, New Jersey, surprised and defeated by George Washington's troops after the crossing of the Delaware on the night of 25–26 December 1776. Though commonly called mercenaries, they were in fact state auxiliaries paid through subsidy treaties rather than individual hirelings. Discipline and drill were respected, but desertion was frequent, encouraged by American offers of land.

Legacy

Some 5,000–6,000 Hessians remained in North America after the war, settling in the United States and Canada. In American folklore the "Hessian" became a stock villain, immortalised as the headless horseman in Washington Irving's "The Legend of Sleepy Hollow" (1820).
