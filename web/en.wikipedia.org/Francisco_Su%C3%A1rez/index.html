Francisco Suárez (1548–1617) was a Spanish Jesuit priest, philosopher, and theologian, regarded as one of the greatest scholastics after Thomas Aquinas and a leading figure of the School of Salamanca. He was known in his lifetime as Doctor Eximius ("the exceptional doctor").

Life and work

Suárez joined the Society of Jesus in 1564 and taught philosophy and theology at Segovia, Valladolid, Rome, Alcalá, Salamanca, and finally Coimbra in Portugal, where he held the principal chair of theology for twenty years.

Philosophy and law

His Disputationes Metaphysicae (1597) was the first systematic treatise on metaphysics independent of commentary on Aristotle and influenced Descartes, Leibniz, and German university philosophy for two centuries. In De Legibus (1612) he developed theories of natural law, the law of nations (ius gentium), and popular sovereignty — arguing that political authority derives from the community — which made him a foundational figure in the history of international law. His defence of the indirect power of the papacy and his theories on tyrannicide brought his books into conflict with James I of England, who had them publicly burned.

Influence

Suárez's synthesis shaped Catholic theology and early modern philosophy; his works were standard textbooks in European universities, Protestant as well as Catholic, into the 18th century.
