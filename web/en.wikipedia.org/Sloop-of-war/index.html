Sloop-of-war

TypeSailing warship
ArmamentUp to 18 guns on a single deck (Royal Navy rating)
Era18th–19th centuries (sail); steam sloops into the 20th century
RolesCruising, convoy escort, dispatch, commerce raiding

In the age of sail, a sloop-of-war was a warship with a single gun deck mounting up to 18 guns. In the British Royal Navy rating system, a sloop-of-war was an unrated vessel commanded by an officer with the rank of commander, larger than a cutter or brig but smaller than a sixth-rate frigate.

Design

Despite the name, sloops-of-war were not necessarily rigged as sloops. They could carry any rig: many were ship-rigged (three masts, square sails), while others were rigged as brigs or snows. Two main hull forms existed — flush-decked vessels and those with a raised quarterdeck and forecastle. Typical weapons were 6- or 9-pounder guns, later supplemented or replaced by carronades.

Roles

Sloops-of-war were workhorses of empire: they escorted convoys, carried dispatches, patrolled against pirates and smugglers, surveyed coasts, and raided enemy commerce. Famous examples include HMS Beagle, the survey sloop that carried Charles Darwin around the world (1831–1836), and the sloops of the West Africa Squadron that suppressed the Atlantic slave trade.

Steam sloops

In the mid-19th century the term was applied to small steam-powered warships, first paddle-driven and then screw-propelled. Steam sloops and their descendants served through both world wars — the Flower-class sloops of the Second World War escorted Atlantic convoys — before the designation fell out of use in favour of frigates and corvettes.
