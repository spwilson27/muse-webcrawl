Heuristics in judgment and decision-making are the mental shortcuts people use to form judgments and make decisions quickly, especially under uncertainty. They are generally useful — most judgments they produce are accurate — but they can lead to systematic errors called cognitive biases. The research programme launched by Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman in the 1970s transformed the psychology of judgment and helped found behavioral economics.

The heuristics-and-biases programme

In their landmark 1974 Science paper "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases", Tversky and Kahneman described three heuristics. Representativeness: people judge probability by similarity, neglecting base rates and sample size — the source of the conjunction fallacy and insensitivity to prior probability. Availability: people estimate frequency or probability by the ease with which examples come to mind, so dramatic or recent events (plane crashes, shark attacks) are overweighted. Anchoring and adjustment: estimates are pulled toward an initial value, even an arbitrary one, because adjustment from the anchor is insufficient.

Later developments

Subsequent work added heuristics including affect (judging by feelings), fluency (preferring easily processed options), simulation and recognition. Daniel Kahneman's dual-process account ("System 1" and "System 2") treats heuristics as products of fast, intuitive processing. Prospect theory (Kahneman and Tversky, 1979) extended the programme to risky choice, describing loss aversion and reference dependence.

Criticism and the fast-and-frugal programme

Gerd Gigerenzer and colleagues argue that heuristics are often ecologically rational — well adapted to the environments in which they are used — and that biases demonstrated in laboratories can reflect mismatches between task format (single-event probabilities) and natural reasoning (frequencies). Their "fast and frugal" heuristics, such as the recognition heuristic and take-the-best, model decisions as simple, information-lean rules that can outperform complex optimization. The debate between the two programmes has shaped modern judgment research, and applications range from medical diagnosis and legal judgment to finance, public policy and "nudging".
