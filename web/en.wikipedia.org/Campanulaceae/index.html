The family Campanulaceae (from the Latin campana, "bell") — the bellflower family — is a family of flowering plants in the order Asterales, comprising roughly 94 genera and about 2,400 species. Most are herbaceous plants or shrubs, rarely small trees, and many exude a milky latex when cut. Members are found nearly worldwide but are most diverse in the Northern Hemisphere, with notable centres of diversity in the Mediterranean, the mountains of Asia, and South Africa.

Description

Leaves are usually alternate and simple, without stipules. The flowers are typically showy and bell-shaped, with a fused corolla of five lobes and an inferior ovary. A distinctive feature is the pollen-presentation mechanism, in which pollen is shed onto hairs of the style before the stigma lobes open. The fruit is usually a capsule or berry. The family includes the large and popular genus Campanula (bellflowers and harebells), as well as Lobelia, Platycodon (balloon flower), and Adenophora.

Classification and uses

Modern classifications recognise several subfamilies, including Campanuloideae and Lobelioideae; the lobelioids, with their strongly bilaterally symmetric flowers, were once treated as a separate family. Many Campanulaceae are grown as garden ornamentals for their blue, violet, or white bell-shaped flowers. A few species have edible roots or leaves, and some Lobelia species contain alkaloids such as lobeline and have been used in traditional medicine.

The family also includes remarkable island radiations, such as the giant Hawaiian lobeliads, and the South African genera Prismatocarpus and Wahlenbergia, the latter among the largest genera of the family in the Southern Hemisphere.
