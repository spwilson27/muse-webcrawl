Lincoln–Douglas debate format

Lincoln–Douglas debate (commonly abbreviated "LD") is a one-on-one competitive debate format practised primarily in American high schools, governed by organisations including the National Speech and Debate Association (NSDA). Named after the historic 1858 Lincoln–Douglas debates, the event focuses on competing values and the application of philosophy and ethics to a resolution.

Structure

A round lasts about 45 minutes and follows a set structure: Affirmative Constructive (6 minutes), Negative cross-examination (3), Negative Constructive (7), Affirmative cross-examination (3), Affirmative Rebuttal (4), Negative Rebuttal (6), and Affirmative Rebuttal (3), with a small amount of preparation time for each side. Resolutions change every two months (for example, topics on civil disobedience, plea bargaining, or living wages).

Style and theory

Traditional LD emphasises a value premise and criterion by which the resolution is judged. Since the 1990s, parts of the national circuit have adopted "progressive" practices borrowed from policy debate — rapid delivery ("spreading"), counterplans and critical (Kritik) arguments — producing ongoing debate within the community about accessibility and the educational purpose of the event.
