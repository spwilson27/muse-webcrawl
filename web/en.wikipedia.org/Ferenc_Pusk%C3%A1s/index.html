Ferenc Puskás (1927–2006) was a Hungarian footballer and manager, widely regarded as one of the greatest players of all time. Nicknamed "the Galloping Major", he was the star forward of Hungary's legendary "Golden Team" (Mighty Magyars) of the early 1950s and later of Real Madrid's European Cup-winning side.

Career

Puskás scored 84 goals in 85 internationals for Hungary, winning Olympic gold in 1952 and reaching the 1954 World Cup final, where Hungary surprisingly lost to West Germany after beating them 8–3 in the group stage — their famous 6–3 victory over England at Wembley in 1953 had ended England's long unbeaten home record against continental opposition. After the Hungarian Revolution of 1956, Puskás emigrated to Spain, joining Real Madrid in 1958. There he formed a famed partnership with Alfredo Di Stéfano, winning three European Cups (1959, 1960, 1966) and scoring four goals in the 1960 final against Eintracht Frankfurt. He later played briefly for Spain, including at the 1962 World Cup, and as a manager led Panathinaikos to the 1971 European Cup final.

Legacy

FIFA created the Puskás Award in 2009 for the most aesthetically significant goal of the year. Hungary's national stadium in Budapest is named the Puskás Aréna. Puskás was famous for his devastating left foot and extraordinarily prolific scoring: over 700 goals in official matches.
