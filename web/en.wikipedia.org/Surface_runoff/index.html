Surface runoff

Surface runoff (also known as overland flow) is the flow of water that occurs when excess rainwater, stormwater, meltwater, or water from other sources flows over the Earth's surface. It is a major component of the water cycle and the primary agent of soil erosion by water.

Generation

Runoff occurs when the rate of precipitation exceeds the rate at which water can infiltrate the soil (infiltration-excess or Hortonian overland flow), or when the soil is already saturated and can absorb no more (saturation-excess overland flow). Impervious surfaces such as roads, roofs and compacted soils greatly increase runoff, as do steep slopes, sparse vegetation and frozen ground.

Effects

Surface runoff collects in rills, streams and rivers and eventually reaches lakes and oceans. Along the way it can erode topsoil from agricultural land and carry pollutants — fertilisers, pesticides, petroleum products, sediment and pathogens — into water bodies, a phenomenon known as nonpoint source pollution. In urban areas, rapid runoff is a major cause of flash flooding, and stormwater management systems such as retention ponds, swales and permeable pavements are designed to slow and infiltrate it.

Measurement and modelling

Hydrologists estimate runoff using methods such as the rational method and the curve number method, and measure it directly with stream gauges. The proportion of rainfall that becomes runoff is expressed as the runoff coefficient, which varies with land use, soil type and antecedent moisture.
