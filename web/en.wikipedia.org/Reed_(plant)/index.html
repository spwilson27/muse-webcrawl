Reed (plant)

The common reed, Phragmites australis, the plant most widely known as "reed".

Reed is a common name for several tall, grass-like plants of wetlands. The name is most often applied to the common reed (Phragmites australis), a large perennial grass found in marshes, lake margins and riverbanks on every continent except Antarctica, but it is also used for species of Arundo, Typha (bulrushes or cattails, sometimes called reedmace), bur-reeds (Sparganium) and reed sweet-grass (Glyceria maxima).

Ecology

Reeds grow in shallow water or waterlogged soil, spreading by rhizomes to form dense stands called reed beds. Reed beds are valuable wildlife habitat, sheltering nesting birds such as the Eurasian bittern and bearded reedling, and they filter water, trap sediment and protect banks from erosion. Some reed species are aggressive invaders outside their native ranges; an introduced lineage of Phragmites australis has displaced native wetland vegetation across much of North America.

Uses

Humans have used reeds since prehistory. Stems are cut for thatching roofs — Norfolk reed is prized for durability — and for weaving mats, baskets, fences and screens. The hollow stems provided tubes for wind instruments, including the ancient Greek aulos and the modern clarinet and oboe reeds cut from Arundo donax cane; Egyptian scribes wrote with reed pens on papyrus. Today reeds are planted in constructed wetlands for wastewater treatment and used as biomass and as a source of cellulose.

Species called reed

Plants commonly called reeds include the common reed (Phragmites australis), giant reed (Arundo donax), reed canary grass (Phalaris arundinacea), reed sweet-grass (Glyceria maxima), papyrus sedge (Cyperus papyrus) and the bur-reeds (Sparganium species).
