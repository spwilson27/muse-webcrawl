Anti-ship ballistic missile

An anti-ship ballistic missile (ASBM) is a military ballistic missile designed to hit a warship at sea. Combining the range and speed of a ballistic missile with a manoeuvrable re-entry vehicle carrying terminal guidance, an ASBM can in principle strike moving ships hundreds or thousands of kilometres away — a capability long sought as a counter to aircraft carrier battle groups.

Development

The Soviet Union experimented with the concept during the Cold War (the R-27K) but did not deploy it operationally. China brought the idea to fruition with the DF-21D, fielded from about 2010 and dubbed a "carrier killer", and the longer-ranged DF-26, which can also carry nuclear warheads. Other states, including Iran with its Khalij Fars missile, have developed shorter-range ASBMs, and air-launched variants such as China's CM-401 and Russia's Kinzhal have been promoted as related systems.

Challenges

An effective ASBM kill chain requires ocean-surveillance satellites and radars to find and track a moving fleet, secure data links to update the missile in flight, and a terminal seeker able to pick out the target amid decoys and electronic countermeasures — all while the defending fleet's interceptors, such as the SM-3 and SM-6, attempt to shoot the missile down. No ASBM has been used against a ship in combat, so their real-world effectiveness remains unproven; their existence nonetheless shapes naval planning and anti-access/area-denial strategy.
