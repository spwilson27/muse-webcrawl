Capital (architecture)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In architecture, a capital (from Latin caput, "head") forms the topmost member of a column (or a pilaster). It mediates between the column and the load thrusting down upon it, broadening the area of the column's supporting surface. The capital, projecting on each side as it rises to support the abacus, joins the usually square abacus and the usually round shaft of the column.

Classical orders

The form of the capital is a defining feature of each classical order: the plain cushion-like Doric capital, the Ionic capital with its spiral volutes, and the elaborate Corinthian capital carved with acanthus leaves. The Roman Composite order combines Ionic volutes with Corinthian foliage, while the Tuscan order uses a simplified form.

Other traditions

Egyptian architecture used capitals shaped as lotus, papyrus, and palm forms. Byzantine, Romanesque, and Gothic architecture developed their own capital types, including basket and cushion capitals and richly foliated crocket capitals, while Islamic architecture developed stalactite (muqarnas) capitals.
