Saffir–Simpson scale

The scale classifies hurricanes by sustained wind speed into five categories.

The Saffir–Simpson hurricane wind scale (SSHWS) classifies hurricanes in the Atlantic and northern Pacific east of the International Date Line into five categories distinguished by the intensities of their sustained winds. The scale was developed in 1971 by civil engineer Herbert Saffir and meteorologist Robert Simpson, then director of the U.S. National Hurricane Center, and came into public use in 1973.

Storms reaching Category 3 and higher are designated major hurricanes. The scale is now based solely on wind speed: earlier versions incorporated central pressure and storm surge, but those elements were removed in a 2010 revision (and the scale renamed the Saffir–Simpson Hurricane Wind Scale) because surge depends heavily on local coastal geography — as demonstrated when Hurricane Katrina (Category 3 at landfall) produced a far larger surge than Category 5 Hurricane Camille.

Criticism

Meteorologists have criticized the scale for ignoring rainfall, flooding, storm size, and surge — often the deadliest hazards — and proposals for supplementary indices or a "Category 6" recur after extreme storms, though the National Hurricane Center maintains the five-category wind scale for public communication.
