Retrovirus

A retrovirus is a type of RNA virus that inserts a DNA copy of its genome into the DNA of a host cell in order to replicate, using the enzyme reverse transcriptase. Once integrated into the host genome as a provirus, the virus can persist for the life of the cell and be copied into every descendant cell. The family Retroviridae includes the human immunodeficiency viruses (HIV-1 and HIV-2), which cause AIDS, and the human T-lymphotropic viruses (HTLV).

Replication cycle

The retrovirus particle carries two copies of its positive-sense single-stranded RNA genome, together with reverse transcriptase, integrase, and protease enzymes. After the virus binds a specific receptor and enters the cell, reverse transcriptase transcribes the RNA into double-stranded DNA — reversing the usual flow of the central dogma of molecular biology. The viral DNA enters the nucleus and is integrated into the host chromosome by integrase. The host's transcription machinery then produces viral RNA and messenger RNA for viral proteins; new particles assemble and bud from the cell, maturing as the viral protease cleaves precursor proteins.

Genome organization

All retroviruses contain three principal genes: gag (structural proteins of the core), pol (reverse transcriptase, integrase, and protease), and env (envelope glycoproteins). The genome is flanked by long terminal repeats (LTRs) that contain promoter and enhancer sequences. "Complex" retroviruses such as HIV encode additional regulatory and accessory proteins (for example tat and rev), while "simple" retroviruses carry only the three core genes.

Classification and disease

Retroviridae is divided into two subfamilies and multiple genera, including alpharetroviruses and gammaretroviruses of birds and mammals, deltaretroviruses (HTLV), and lentiviruses — slow viruses such as HIV, simian immunodeficiency virus, and feline immunodeficiency virus. Some retroviruses cause cancer: Rous sarcoma virus, discovered by Peyton Rous in 1911, was the first tumor virus identified, and its src oncogene revealed that viral oncogenes derive from normal cellular genes.

History

Howard Temin proposed the provirus hypothesis in the 1960s; reverse transcriptase was demonstrated independently by Temin and David Baltimore in 1970, earning them a share of the 1975 Nobel Prize. HIV was identified as the cause of AIDS in 1983–84 by teams led by Luc Montagnier and Robert Gallo.

Endogenous retroviruses and uses

About 8% of the human genome derives from ancient retroviral integrations inherited through the germline — endogenous retroviruses. In research and medicine, engineered retroviral and lentiviral vectors are widely used to deliver genes into cells, including in approved gene therapies.
