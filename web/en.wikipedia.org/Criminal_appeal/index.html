Criminal appeal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A criminal appeal is a legal proceeding in which a defendant convicted of a criminal offense asks a higher court to review the judgment of a trial court for errors of law, procedure, or fact. Appeals are a central feature of most modern legal systems and serve both to correct miscarriages of justice and to ensure the consistent development of criminal law.

Grounds for appeal

Typical grounds include errors by the trial judge in admitting or excluding evidence, misdirection of the jury, prosecutorial misconduct, ineffective assistance of counsel, and sentences that are unlawful or manifestly excessive. In some jurisdictions an appeal may also be brought on the ground that fresh evidence has emerged that was not available at trial.

Procedure

An appeal usually begins with a notice of appeal filed within a strict time limit after conviction or sentence. The appellate court generally reviews the written record of the trial rather than hearing witnesses anew. It may affirm the conviction, quash it and order a retrial, substitute a different verdict or sentence, or, in limited circumstances, acquit the defendant outright.

Appeals by the prosecution

In many common-law jurisdictions the prosecution historically had no right to appeal an acquittal, a rule connected with the protection against double jeopardy. Some systems now permit prosecution appeals against unduly lenient sentences or against certain terminating rulings, and a small number allow retrial after acquittal in serious cases where compelling new evidence emerges.
