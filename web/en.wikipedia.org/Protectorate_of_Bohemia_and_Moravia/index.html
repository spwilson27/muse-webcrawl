The Protectorate of Bohemia and Moravia (German: Protektorat Böhmen und Mähren; Czech: Protektorát Čechy a Morava) was a partially annexed territory of Nazi Germany that was established on 16 March 1939 after the German occupation of the Czech lands. It followed the Munich Agreement of September 1938, under which Czechoslovakia had already ceded the Sudetenland, and the declaration of independence by Slovakia on 14 March 1939.

The Protectorate was formally autonomous, with a Czech puppet government under State President Emil Hácha, but in practice it was ruled by the Reich Protector appointed from Berlin. Its industry — including the Škoda works — was integrated into the German war economy, and its population was subjected to Germanisation policies, forced labour and terror.

History

On 15 March 1939, German troops occupied the remainder of Bohemia and Moravia. Hácha, summoned to Berlin and threatened with the bombing of Prague, signed a declaration placing the Czech lands under German "protection". The first Reich Protector, Konstantin von Neurath, governed with a mixture of conciliation and repression, but after student demonstrations in October 1939 the Czech universities were closed and hundreds of students deported.

In September 1941 Neurath was effectively replaced by SS-Obergruppenführer Reinhard Heydrich, whose regime combined wage concessions for armaments workers with mass executions. Heydrich was assassinated in Prague in May 1942 by Czechoslovak soldiers trained in Britain (Operation Anthropoid). In reprisal, the villages of Lidice and Ležáky were destroyed and their inhabitants murdered. Kurt Daluege and then Wilhelm Frick succeeded as Reich Protector. The Protectorate ended with the Prague uprising and liberation in May 1945.

Administration and resistance

The Protectorate retained a Czech government and administration, but all key decisions required German approval, and a parallel German administration supervised it. A small collaborationist government army existed, while actual power lay with the Gestapo and the office of the Reich Protector. Resistance ranged from intelligence networks reporting to the Czechoslovak government-in-exile in London to the assassination of Heydrich, one of the most senior Nazi officials killed by resistance action during the war.
