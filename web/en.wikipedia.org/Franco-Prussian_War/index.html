The Franco-Prussian War, or Franco-German War, often referred to in France as the War of 1870, was a conflict between the Second French Empire and the North German Confederation led by the Kingdom of Prussia. Lasting from 19 July 1870 to 28 January 1871, the conflict was caused primarily by France's determination to restore its dominant position in continental Europe, which it had lost following Prussia's victory over Austria in 1866.

A pretext for war arose over a Prussian prince's candidacy for the Spanish throne; Chancellor Otto von Bismarck's edited publication of the Ems Dispatch goaded France into declaring war. The German states, bound by treaty, mobilised swiftly under Helmuth von Moltke's planning, while French mobilisation descended into confusion.

Course of the war

German armies won a series of frontier battles in August 1870 — Wissembourg, Wörth, Spicheren, and Gravelotte — driving one French army under MacMahon to surrender at Sedan on 1 September, where Emperor Napoleon III was captured. A second army under Bazaine was trapped and eventually surrendered at Metz. The Second Empire collapsed; a Government of National Defence proclaimed the French Republic and continued the war, improvising new armies while Paris endured a four-month siege. Léon Gambetta escaped the city by balloon to raise forces in the provinces, but relief attempts failed, guerrilla francs-tireurs harassed German supply lines, and Paris capitulated on 28 January 1871.

Consequences

In the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, the German princes proclaimed Wilhelm I German Emperor on 18 January 1871, unifying Germany. The Treaty of Frankfurt (10 May 1871) ceded Alsace-Lorraine to Germany and imposed an indemnity of five billion francs. The bitterness of the annexation fuelled French revanchism and shaped the alliance system that led to the First World War. In Paris, the revolutionary Paris Commune briefly seized power in spring 1871 before being bloodily suppressed by the national government.
