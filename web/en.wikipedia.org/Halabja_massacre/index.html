The Halabja massacre (Kurdish: كیمیابارانی هه‌ڵه‌بجه Kîmyabarana Helebce), also known as the Halabja chemical attack, was a massacre of Kurdish civilians that took place on 16 March 1988, during the closing days of the Iran–Iraq War. Iraqi government forces attacked the Kurdish town of Halabja, in the Kurdistan Region of Iraq, with chemical weapons, killing between 3,200 and 5,000 people and injuring between 7,000 and 10,000 more, most of them civilians. It remains the largest chemical weapons attack directed against a civilian-populated area in history.

The attack was carried out during the Al-Anfal campaign, a counterinsurgency operation led by Ali Hassan al-Majid — later nicknamed "Chemical Ali" — on the orders of Iraqi President Saddam Hussein, against Kurdish rebels and the largely Kurdish civilian population of rural northern Iraq.

Background

Northern Iraq was a theatre of fighting during the Iran–Iraq War. Kurdish peshmerga guerrillas, allied with Iran, had taken control of Halabja and the surrounding area with Iranian support. On 15 March 1988, Iranian forces and Kurdish fighters captured the town in Operation Zafar 7. Iraqi officials resolved to retake it and to punish its population.

The attack

On the morning of 16 March 1988, Iraqi aircraft began dropping conventional bombs and napalm on Halabja. Later waves of aircraft — including MiG and Mirage jets — dropped chemical bombs containing mustard gas and the nerve agents sarin and tabun; survivors and investigators also suspected the use of VX and of cyanide compounds. The bombardment lasted for about five hours. Witnesses described clouds of white, black and yellow smoke rising to heights of over 150 feet, and victims dying within minutes, many with no visible injuries; others succumbed to burns, blistering and respiratory failure.

Aftermath and investigation

Photographs taken by Iranian journalists who entered the town shortly afterwards — showing streets littered with the bodies of men, women and children, many killed still sheltering in doorways or beside vehicles — were published around the world and remain iconic images of the atrocity. The Iraqi government initially denied responsibility and blamed Iran; the United States initially also suggested Iranian culpability, a position later discredited. Studies by Human Rights Watch and by medical teams documented the agents used and the scale of the deaths, and long-term surveys recorded elevated rates of cancer, respiratory disease, congenital birth defects and miscarriage among survivors.

Trials and recognition

After the 2003 invasion of Iraq, Ali Hassan al-Majid was tried by the Iraqi Special Tribunal; he was convicted of genocide for the Anfal campaign and the Halabja attack, sentenced to death, and executed in January 2010. Dutch businessman Frans van Anraat was convicted in the Netherlands in 2005 of complicity in war crimes for supplying Iraq with the chemical precursors used in such attacks. The Iraqi High Criminal Court and several national parliaments have formally recognised the Halabja massacre as an act of genocide. A memorial and museum in Halabja commemorate the victims, and the 16th of March is observed as a day of remembrance in the Kurdistan Region.
