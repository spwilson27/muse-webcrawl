Legal anthropology

Legal anthropology, also known as the anthropology of law, is a sub-field of anthropology that uses ethnographic methods to explore the social processes by which norms, rules, and legal orders are created, maintained, and transformed. It studies dispute resolution and social control in societies with and without centralized legal institutions.

History

Early work include Henry Maine's Ancient Law (1861), and the field was shaped by classics such as Bronisław Malinowski's Crime and Custom in Savage Society (1926), which showed how reciprocal obligations bound Trobriand Islanders without a state legal system, and Max Gluckman's studies of Barotse courts in The Judicial Process Among the Barotse of Northern Rhodesia (1955). Karl Llewellyn and E. Adamson Hoebel's The Cheyenne Way (1941) introduced the "trouble-case" method.

Key themes

Legal anthropologists examine legal pluralism — the coexistence of multiple legal orders, such as state law, customary law, and religious law — as well as dispute processes, property, human rights, and the effects of colonialism on indigenous legal systems. Contemporary work addresses international law, transitional justice, and global institutions.
