Twenty-first Amendment to the United States Constitution

The Twenty-first Amendment repealed national Prohibition in 1933.

The Twenty-first Amendment (Amendment XXI) to the United States Constitution repealed the Eighteenth Amendment, which had mandated nationwide prohibition of alcohol. Proposed by Congress on February 20, 1933, it was ratified on December 5, 1933, when Utah became the 36th state to approve it. It remains the only amendment to repeal another amendment and the only one ratified by state ratifying conventions rather than state legislatures.

Text

Section 1. The eighteenth article of amendment to the Constitution of the United States is hereby repealed.

Section 2. The transportation or importation into any State, Territory, or possession of the United States for delivery or use therein of intoxicating liquors, in violation of the laws thereof, is hereby prohibited.

Section 3. This article shall be inoperative unless it shall have been ratified as an amendment to the Constitution by conventions in the several States, as provided in the Constitution, within seven years from the date of the submission hereof to the States by the Congress.

Background

By the early 1930s, Prohibition had become widely unpopular: enforcement was costly and uneven, organised crime had flourished around bootlegging, and the Great Depression created pressure for jobs and tax revenue from a restored legal liquor industry. The Democratic Party's 1932 platform called for repeal, and President Franklin D. Roosevelt supported the measure.

Effect

Section 2 gives states broad authority to regulate alcohol within their borders, a power the Supreme Court has repeatedly balanced against other constitutional provisions such as the Commerce Clause and the Fourteenth Amendment. Many states retained prohibition for a time — Mississippi did not repeal statewide prohibition until 1966 — and a number of counties and municipalities remain "dry" today.
