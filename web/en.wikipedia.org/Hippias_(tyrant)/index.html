Hippias (Greek: Ἱππίας; c. 570 – 490 BC) was the last tyrant of Athens. The eldest son of Peisistratus, he succeeded his father in 527 BC and ruled, initially alongside his younger brother Hipparchus, until his expulsion in 510 BC. The assassination of Hipparchus in 514 BC by the tyrannicides Harmodius and Aristogeiton turned Hippias's rule repressive, and his overthrow, engineered with Spartan help, cleared the way for the reforms of Cleisthenes and the birth of Athenian democracy. Hippias later took refuge with Persia and, according to Herodotus, accompanied the Persian expedition that landed at Marathon in 490 BC, guiding it to the landing place in hopes of being restored.

Reign

Peisistratus died in 528/527 BC, leaving power to Hippias, who had already been the more politically active of his sons. The brothers continued their father's policies: maintaining the forms of the constitution, patronising religion and the arts (the poets Simonides and Anacreon were at their court), and extending Athenian influence abroad. Hippias arranged alliances by marriage with powerful families, including the tyrant of Lampsacus, whose daughter Archedice married Hippias's son.

The tyrannicide and the hardening of the regime

In 514 BC, during the Panathenaic festival, Harmodius and Aristogeiton murdered Hipparchus, provoked by a personal quarrel—Hipparchus had insulted Harmodius's sister after being rejected by Harmodius. Hippias survived the attempt and thereafter, according to Thucydides and the Aristotelian Athenaion Politeia, ruled with increasing suspicion and severity, executing and exiling opponents and fortifying the Munychia hill in Piraeus. The exiled Alcmaeonid family, having rebuilt the temple at Delphi, persuaded the Spartans through the oracle to "free Athens", and after an initial failed attempt by sea, a Spartan army under King Cleomenes I trapped Hippias on the Acropolis in 510 BC. Hippias surrendered under terms allowing him and his family safe departure, and left Attica.

Exile and death

Hippias settled at Sigeum and then at the court of Lampsacus, and eventually with the Persian king. Athens's offers and intrigues concerning his possible restoration became a recurring theme in relations with Sparta and Persia. In 490 BC the Persian expedition of Datis and Artaphernes landed at Marathon, reportedly guided by the aged Hippias, who believed a dream promised his restoration. The Athenian victory ended any prospect of his return; tradition says he died soon afterwards, at Lemnos or on the return voyage.

Assessment

Later Athenian democratic tradition, which celebrated Harmodius and Aristogeiton as liberators, cast Hippias as a cruel tyrant; Thucydides, correcting the popular memory, stressed that the tyranny became oppressive only in its last years and that Hippias had been the ruling brother all along. Modern historians similarly treat the Peisistratid regime as comparatively moderate until 514 BC.
