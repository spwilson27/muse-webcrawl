Protective tariff

Customs duties have historically been used both to raise revenue and to shield domestic industry.

A protective tariff is a duty imposed on imported goods with the deliberate purpose of making them more expensive than comparable domestic products, thereby protecting home industries from foreign competition. Unlike a revenue tariff, whose primary aim is to raise money for the government, a protective tariff is set high enough to reduce or eliminate imports of the protected commodity altogether.

The doctrine is closely associated with infant industry reasoning: a young domestic industry, unable at first to match the economies of scale of established foreign producers, is sheltered until it matures. Alexander Hamilton's Report on Manufactures (1791) made the case for the United States, and Friedrich List developed the argument in Germany in the nineteenth century.

History

Protective tariffs were a central instrument of economic policy in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The United States maintained high protective duties from the Civil War era through the 1920s, culminating in the Smoot–Hawley Tariff Act of 1930, which many economists blame for deepening the Great Depression by provoking retaliatory tariffs abroad. Britain, by contrast, repealed its Corn Laws in 1846 and embraced free trade for much of the Victorian era.

After the Second World War, successive rounds of negotiation under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT) and later the World Trade Organization sharply reduced average tariff levels among industrialised countries, though protective duties remain common in agriculture and in sensitive manufacturing sectors.

Arguments for and against

Supporters argue that protective tariffs preserve domestic jobs, safeguard industries vital to national security, and give developing economies room to industrialise. Critics respond that protection raises prices for consumers, invites retaliation, shelters inefficiency, and transfers income from consumers and exporters to the protected producers. Most mainstream economists hold that the aggregate welfare cost of a tariff exceeds its benefits except in narrow circumstances, such as the "optimal tariff" available to a large economy able to influence world prices.
