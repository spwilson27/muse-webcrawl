Contour ploughing

Contour ploughing (or contour farming) is the practice of ploughing and planting across a slope following its lines of consistent elevation, known as contour lines, rather than up and down the slope. The furrows, which act as a series of small dams, slow water runoff and give rain time to infiltrate the soil, thereby reducing soil erosion and the loss of nutrients. It is one of the simplest and most widely used soil-conservation techniques.

Contour lines traced by crop rows on sloping land.

History

The technique is ancient: the Phoenicians are credited with developing contour cultivation on steep Mediterranean hillsides, and terraced farming across Asia applied the same principle on a grander scale. In the modern era it was promoted vigorously in the United States during the 1930s, when the Dust Bowl crisis led the Soil Conservation Service to teach contour ploughing, strip cropping and terracing to farmers across the Great Plains and the Midwest.

How it works

On gentle slopes of roughly 2–10 percent, contour ridges are most effective. Each furrow runs level around the hillside, so rainwater collects in the ridge-and-furrow pattern instead of running downhill and carrying topsoil with it. Studies commonly report erosion reductions of around half compared with up-and-down cultivation. On steeper or highly irregular land, contouring is combined with strip cropping — alternating contour bands of close-growing and clean-tilled crops — grass waterways, or permanent terraces.

Limitations

Contour lines must be laid out accurately, historically with an A-frame level and today often with GPS-guided machinery. In very heavy storms, water ponded behind a ridge can breach it and cascade down the slope, causing worse gullies than gradual runoff. Contouring is therefore usually one element of a broader conservation plan that may include conservation tillage, cover crops and buffer strips.
