Port wine (Portuguese: vinho do Porto) is a Portuguese fortified wine produced in the Douro Valley of northern Portugal. Fermentation is arrested by adding grape spirit (aguardente), leaving residual sugar and raising the alcohol to about 19–22%. True port is shipped from the city of Porto, from which it takes its name, and the name is protected within the European Union.

History

The Douro was demarcated in 1756 under the Marquis of Pombal, one of the world's oldest regulated wine regions. British merchants, favoured by the Methuen Treaty of 1703 and based in lodges at Vila Nova de Gaia across the river from Porto, dominated the trade; famous houses such as Taylor's, Graham's, Sandeman and Dow's remain British-founded.

Styles

  • Ruby: young, fruity, aged in bulk; Vintage port, from a single declared year, is the finest ruby style and ages in bottle.
  • Tawny: aged in wooden casks, oxidising to a nutty, amber-brown wine; sold with age indications of 10–40 years.
  • Late Bottled Vintage (LBV), white and rosé ports complete the range.
