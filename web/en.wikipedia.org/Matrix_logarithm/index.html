Logarithm of a matrix

Inverse operation of the matrix exponential

In mathematics, a logarithm of a matrix is another matrix whose exponential equals the given matrix. It is the inverse operation of taking the matrix exponential: a matrix B is a logarithm of a matrix A if eB = A. As with the complex logarithm of a number, matrix logarithms are not unique — a matrix may have many logarithms, or none at all over the reals.

Definition and existence

Every invertible complex matrix has a (complex) logarithm, but an invertible real matrix has a real logarithm if and only if each Jordan block corresponding to a negative eigenvalue occurs an even number of times. For a matrix with no eigenvalues on the closed negative real axis, a unique principal logarithm can be defined, generalising the principal branch of the scalar logarithm.

Computation and properties

For diagonalisable matrices the logarithm acts on the eigenvalues: if A = PDP−1, then log A = P (log D) P−1. More generally, logarithms can be computed by power series, by the inverse scaling-and-squaring method (the counterpart of the standard algorithm for the exponential), or via Schur decomposition. Unlike scalars, log(AB) is not generally equal to log A + log B unless A and B commute. Matrix logarithms are fundamental to the theory of Lie groups, whose Lie algebras consist of logarithms of group elements near the identity, and they appear in statistics, continuum mechanics and the analysis of Markov chains.
