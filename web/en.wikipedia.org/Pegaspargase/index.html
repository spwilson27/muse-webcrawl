Pegaspargase


Pegaspargase is used against acute lymphoblastic leukaemia

Pegaspargase, sold under the brand name Oncaspar among others, is a medication used in the treatment of acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (ALL), most often in children. It is a pegylated form of the enzyme L-asparaginase derived from Escherichia coli: attaching polyethylene glycol (PEG) chains extends the enzyme's half-life and reduces its immunogenicity compared with native asparaginase, allowing less frequent dosing.

Mechanism

Leukaemic lymphoblasts cannot synthesise enough of the amino acid asparagine, which they require for protein synthesis, whereas healthy cells can make their own. Pegaspargase circulates in the blood and hydrolyses asparagine into aspartic acid and ammonia, starving the malignant cells and inhibiting their growth while largely sparing normal cells.

Use and side effects

It is given by intramuscular injection or intravenous infusion, typically every two weeks as part of multi-agent chemotherapy protocols. Common and serious side effects include allergic reactions (including anaphylaxis — the main reason for choosing pegaspargase over the native enzyme is its lower, though not absent, rate of hypersensitivity), pancreatitis, liver dysfunction and abnormal blood tests, blood clots and bleeding from disturbed clotting-factor synthesis, and high blood sugar. Silent inactivation of the drug by antibodies can occur, so asparaginase activity levels may be monitored. Pegaspargase was approved for medical use in the United States in 1994 and in the European Union in 2016.
