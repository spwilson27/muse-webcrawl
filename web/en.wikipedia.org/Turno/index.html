Turno

In Argentine politics, the turno refers to the practice, established after the National Organization period, by which the presidency alternated between leaders of the two dominant factions or parties — notably between the Partido Autonomista Nacional (PAN) and its rivals, and later between the leading figures Justo José de Urquiza and Bartolomé Mitre's successors in the late 19th century.

Meaning and usage

The term, borrowed from the Spanish Restoration system of the turno pacífico, describes a managed rotation of power among elites, often maintained through electoral fraud (fraude patriótico) rather than genuine competition. In Argentina the practice underpinned the conservative republic of 1880–1916, in which the PAN dominated national politics and provincial governors took turns aligning with presidential succession plans.

End of the system

The Sáenz Peña Law of 1912, introducing secret and compulsory male suffrage, weakened the turno system and led to the election of Hipólito Yrigoyen in 1916, though informal rotation among dominant parties remained a recurring theme in Argentine political commentary.
