The Titan II was an American intercontinental ballistic missile (ICBM), designated LGM-25C, developed by the Glenn L. Martin Company from the earlier Titan I. It carried the largest warhead of any U.S. ICBM — the 9-megaton W53 — and, in the Titan II GLV configuration, launched all of NASA's crewed Gemini spacecraft. It remained in service longer than any other first-generation ICBM, from 1963 to 1987, and converted Titan II missiles flew space missions until 2003.

Development

Titan II was designed to remedy Titan I's shortcomings: it used storable hypergolic propellants (Aerozine 50 fuel and nitrogen tetroxide oxidiser) that ignited on contact, allowing the missile to be kept fuelled in its silo and launched in about a minute, rather than requiring lengthy cryogenic fuelling. Inertial guidance replaced Titan I's radio guidance, increasing accuracy and autonomy. Fifty-four missiles were deployed in hardened silos around Davis–Monthan, McConnell and Little Rock Air Force Bases.

Gemini and space launches

Because of its power and smooth ride, Titan II was chosen as the booster for the two-man Gemini capsule: twelve Gemini-Titan launches (two uncrewed, ten crewed) took place from Cape Canaveral in 1964–1966, carrying astronauts including the first American spacewalk on Gemini 4. Refurbished Titan II 23G vehicles later launched weather satellites, scientific payloads such as the Clementine lunar probe, and Landsat and NOAA spacecraft from Vandenberg Air Force Base between 1988 and 2003.

Accidents and retirement

The storable propellants that made Titan II quick to launch were also toxic and corrosive. A 1978 oxidiser leak at a Kansas silo killed an airman, and in September 1980 at Damascus, Arkansas, a dropped socket punctured a missile's fuel tank; the ensuing explosion blew off the silo door and ejected the warhead, killing one airman and injuring others (the warhead's safety devices prevented any nuclear detonation). The accidents hastened retirement; the last Titan II ICBM was deactivated in 1987 under the Reagan administration's modernisation programme. One silo, near Green Valley, Arizona, is preserved as the Titan Missile Museum.
