Bacterial cell wall

The bacterial cell wall is a layer surrounding the cell membrane of most bacteria that provides structural strength, maintains cell shape, and protects against osmotic lysis. Its main structural component is peptidoglycan (murein), a mesh of sugar chains cross-linked by short peptides, which is unique to bacteria and a principal target of antibiotics such as penicillin.

Bacterial cell wall
Main component: Peptidoglycan
Gram-positive: Thick peptidoglycan, teichoic acids
Gram-negative: Thin peptidoglycan plus outer membrane
Absent in: Mycoplasma species

Gram-positive and Gram-negative walls

In Gram-positive bacteria the wall is a thick peptidoglycan layer (roughly 20–80 nm) threaded with teichoic acids. In Gram-negative bacteria the peptidoglycan layer is thin (about 2–7 nm) but is covered by an outer membrane containing lipopolysaccharide (LPS), which acts as an endotoxin and as a barrier to many antibiotics. The structural difference between the two wall types underlies the Gram stain, the classic first step in bacterial identification.

Medical significance

Enzymes and drugs that interfere with peptidoglycan synthesis — beta-lactam antibiotics — or that degrade it, such as the lysozyme in tears and saliva, kill or weaken bacteria. Cell-wall-deficient forms (L-forms and mycoplasmas) are intrinsically resistant to such agents.
