Other namesDeoxythymidine

Thymidine (also termed deoxypyrimidine, deoxythymidine, or thymine deoxyriboside) is a pyrimidine deoxynucleoside. Deoxythymidine is the nucleoside T, which pairs with deoxyadenosine (A) in double-stranded DNA. In cell biology it is used to synchronize the cells in G1/early S phase.

Thymidine consists of the pyrimidine base thymine attached to the sugar deoxyribose. It can be phosphorylated with one, two or three phosphoric acid groups, creating dTMP, dTDP or dTTP (deoxythymidine mono-, di- or triphosphate) respectively.

Structure and properties

Thymidine is obtained by hydrolysis of DNA. It crystallizes as fine white needles. Under standard conditions, thymidine exists in solid form as small white crystals or white crystalline powder. It is weakly acidic and soluble in water.

Modified analogues used in medicine

Several antiviral and anticancer drugs are thymidine analogues in which the molecule has been modified to interfere with DNA replication. These include zidovudine (AZT), a cornerstone of early HIV therapy; stavudine (d4T); idoxuridine; trifluridine; and edoxudine. The compound telbivudine is the L-enantiomer of thymidine used against hepatitis B.

Radiolabeling in research

Tritiated thymidine (3H-TdR) is classically used to measure DNA synthesis and cell proliferation, since dividing cells incorporate it into newly synthesized DNA. The BrdU (bromodeoxyuridine) assay is a modern alternative that serves a similar purpose without radioactivity.
