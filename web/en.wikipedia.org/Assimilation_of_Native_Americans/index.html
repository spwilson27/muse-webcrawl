Assimilation of Native Americans

The assimilation of Native Americans refers to the series of policies pursued by the United States government (and, in parallel forms, by other settler states) from the late 18th century through the mid-20th century, intended to absorb Indigenous peoples into Euro-American culture and society. These policies included forced removal, the allotment of communal land, the establishment of off-reservation boarding schools, and the suppression of Native languages and religions. The era's motto was often summarised by boarding-school founder Richard Henry Pratt's phrase "kill the Indian in him, and save the man".

Early "civilization" policy

From the presidency of George Washington, federal policy under Henry Knox promoted the "civilization" of Native nations through agriculture, Christianity, and private property, even as land cessions were demanded. Some Native nations, including the Cherokee, adopted aspects of this programme, developing a written language (Sequoyah's syllabary, 1821), a constitution, and a newspaper. Nevertheless, the Indian Removal Act of 1830 led to the forced expulsion of the southeastern nations on the Trail of Tears.

Allotment

The Dawes Act (General Allotment Act) of 1887 divided communal reservation land into individual parcels, declaring the remainder "surplus" and open to non-Native purchase. By 1934 Native landholdings had fallen from about 138 million acres to about 48 million. Allotment was halted by the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934.

Boarding schools

Beginning with the Carlisle Indian Industrial School (1879), the government and churches operated boarding schools that removed children from their families, forbade Native languages and dress, and trained students for manual labour. Investigations, including a 2022 US Department of the Interior report, documented widespread neglect and abuse, and identified burial sites at former schools. Canada operated a parallel residential school system.

Termination and relocation

In the 1950s federal policy shifted to "termination" of tribal governments and the relocation of Native people to cities. These policies were repudiated in the era of Native activism; the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975 marked a turn toward tribal self-governance. The legacies of assimilation — language loss, land fractionation, and intergenerational trauma — remain central issues in Native communities today.
