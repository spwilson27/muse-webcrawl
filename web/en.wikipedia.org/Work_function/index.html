Work function

In solid-state physics, the work function (sometimes spelled workfunction) is the minimum thermodynamic work (i.e., energy) needed to remove an electron from a solid to a point in the vacuum immediately outside the solid surface. Here "immediately" means that the final electron position is far from the surface on the atomic scale, but still close to the solid.

Values and measurement

The work function is a property of a material's surface, depending on the material and the crystallographic orientation of the face; typical values for metals range from about 2 to 6 electron volts. Caesium has one of the lowest work functions of the elements (about 2.14 eV), while platinum has one of the highest (about 5.6 eV). The work function is commonly measured by photoelectric methods, based on the photoelectric effect, or by thermionic emission methods based on the Richardson–Dushman equation.

Applications

The work function governs electron emission in devices such as vacuum tube cathodes, photomultipliers and electron microscopes. In electronics, differences in work function between materials produce contact potentials, and the work function of a gate electrode affects the threshold voltage of MOSFETs. Surface treatments and coatings are used to lower the work function of emitters.
