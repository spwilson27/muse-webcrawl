Tropical rainforest

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Tropical rainforests are dense, warm, wet forests located within 28 degrees north or south of the equator, characterised by high rainfall (typically at least 1,750 mm annually), high humidity, and a closed, multi-layered canopy. The largest expanses are the Amazon rainforest in South America, the Congo Basin in Africa, and the forests of Southeast Asia, Indonesia, and New Guinea.

Structure and ecology

Rainforest vegetation is structured in layers: emergent trees rising above 45 metres, a continuous canopy, an understorey of shade-tolerant plants, and a dark forest floor where rapid decomposition recycles nutrients. Despite covering only about 6–7 percent of Earth's land surface, tropical rainforests harbour roughly half of all plant and animal species. Soils are typically nutrient-poor, with most nutrients locked in living biomass.

Importance and threats

Rainforests regulate regional rainfall, store vast amounts of carbon, and supply products from timber and rubber to medicines derived from rainforest plants. They are threatened by deforestation for cattle ranching, soy and oil-palm plantations, logging, and mining; the Amazon alone lost large areas in recent decades, raising concern about a tipping point toward savanna. Conservation efforts include protected areas, indigenous reserves, and reduced-deforestation agreements.
