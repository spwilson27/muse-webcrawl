Investment (military)

In military terminology, an investment is the surrounding of an enemy fortress, city, or force by troops in order to cut it off from supplies, reinforcements, and escape, as a prelude to a siege or to compel surrender by starvation. The investing force establishes lines of circumvallation facing the objective and, historically, lines of contravallation facing outward against any relieving army.

Method

A complete investment prevents all movement into or out of the invested place. Commanders achieved it by encircling positions with entrenched infantry, cavalry screens, or naval blockade where the objective lay on water. Once invested, the besieger could proceed to formal siege operations — sapping, bombardment, and assault — or simply wait for supplies within to run out.

Historical examples

Julius Caesar's investment of Alesia in 52 BC, famous for its double lines of fortification, defeated both the garrison under Vercingetorix and a Gallic relief army. The investment of Paris in 1870–71 by Prussian forces ended with the city's capitulation by famine. In modern warfare the term survived in operations such as the investment of fortified localities in the Second World War, though air supply — attempted at Stalingrad and achieved in Berlin in 1948–49 — could frustrate a purely land-based investment.

Related concepts

Investment differs from a blockade, which may be purely naval or economic, and from encirclement in open warfare, which traps a field force rather than a fixed position. An incompletely invested fortress is said to be merely "masked" or observed.
