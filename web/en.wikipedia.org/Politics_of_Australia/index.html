Politics of Australia

Parliament House, Canberra.

The politics of Australia operate within a federal parliamentary constitutional monarchy. Australia is a federation of six states and two self-governing territories, established by the Constitution of 1901. The monarch of Australia is head of state, represented federally by the governor-general; executive power is exercised in practice by the prime minister and cabinet, who must command the confidence of the House of Representatives.

Parliament and elections

The federal Parliament consists of the monarch, the Senate (76 members, 12 per state and 2 per territory, elected by proportional representation), and the House of Representatives (151 members elected from single-member districts by preferential, or instant-runoff, voting). Voting is compulsory for enrolled citizens aged 18 and over, a distinctive feature dating to 1924. Federal elections are held at least every three years.

Parties

Two political groupings dominate: the centre-left Australian Labor Party and the centre-right Coalition of the Liberal Party and the National Party. Third parties — notably the Australian Greens and One Nation — exert influence mainly through the Senate, where proportional representation gives them seats. The system combines the Westminster tradition of responsible government with a strong, elected upper house on the American model, sometimes called the "Washminster" system.

States and judiciary

Each state has its own constitution, parliament, premier, and courts; the High Court of Australia is the final court of appeal and arbiter of the federal balance. Australia has no bill of rights in its federal constitution, though a small number of rights are expressly protected.
