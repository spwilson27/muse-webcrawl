Christian cross variants

The Christian cross, seen as a representation of the instrument of the crucifixion of Jesus, is the best-known symbol of Christianity. Over the centuries, many variants of the cross have developed, each carrying denominational, regional, or heraldic associations.

Basic forms

The Latin cross (†) has a longer vertical arm below the crossbar and is the most common form in Western Christianity. The Greek cross (✚) has arms of equal length and is associated with Eastern Christianity and with ancient art. The crucifix bears the figure of Christ on the cross and is characteristic of Catholic and some Lutheran and Anglican practice.

Named variants

Other well-known forms include the patriarchal cross, with two horizontal bars, used by archbishops and in Eastern European heraldry; the papal cross, with three bars; the cross crosslet, whose arms each end in small crosses; the St. Peter's cross, inverted in reference to the apostle Peter's martyrdom; the St. Andrew's cross or saltire, a diagonal X shape; the tau cross, shaped like the letter T and associated with St. Francis; the Celtic cross, combining a cross with a ring; the Russian Orthodox cross, with a slanted lower bar; the Jerusalem cross, a large cross surrounded by four smaller ones; and the ankh-like Coptic cross forms used in Egypt.

Heraldic and national use

Cross variants appear extensively in heraldry and on national flags. The Scandinavian cross appears on the flags of the Nordic countries; St. George's Cross on the flag of England; and the Southern Cross constellation — not a Christian symbol in origin — on the flags of Australia and New Zealand. Crosses of military orders, such as the Maltese cross of the Knights Hospitaller, remain in use by orders of chivalry and emergency services.
