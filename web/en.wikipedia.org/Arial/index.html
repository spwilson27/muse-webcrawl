Arial

Category: Sans-serif
Classification: Neo-grotesque
Designers: Robin Nicholas, Patricia Saunders
Foundry: Monotype
Date created: 1982

Arial is a sans-serif typeface designed in 1982 by Robin Nicholas and Patricia Saunders for Monotype Typography. It was commissioned by IBM for its laser printers and later became famous as a core font of Microsoft Windows, where it has shipped with every version since Windows 3.1 in 1992.

Design

Arial is a neo-grotesque design whose proportions and stroke weights closely follow those of Helvetica, the Linotype typeface created in 1957. This metric compatibility means Arial can substitute for Helvetica without changing line breaks or page layout. Nevertheless, differences distinguish the two: Arial has more rounded, "softer" curves, diagonal terminals on letters such as t, f, and r where Helvetica uses horizontal cuts, and distinctive shapes in the R, G, and numerals.

History

Monotype developed Arial to provide IBM with a metrically compatible alternative to Helvetica that avoided licensing costs. After IBM's printer business moved on, Microsoft adopted Arial as one of its core TrueType fonts, and it spread worldwide through Windows and Microsoft Office. Variants include Arial Black, Arial Narrow, Arial Rounded, and Arial Unicode MS, a version supporting a wide range of scripts.

Usage and criticism

Arial's ubiquity has made it a default choice for corporate documents and web pages (the CSS generic stack "Helvetica, Arial, sans-serif"). Typographers have criticized it as an imitation of Helvetica and for its uneven letter spacing in large display sizes, while others defend it as a workmanlike design optimized for low-resolution screens.
