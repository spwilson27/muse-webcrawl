An mRNA vaccine is a type of vaccine that uses a copy of a molecule called messenger RNA (mRNA) to produce an immune response. The vaccine delivers molecules of antigen-encoding mRNA into immune cells, which use the mRNA as a blueprint to build a foreign protein (an antigen) that would normally be produced by a pathogen (such as a virus) or by a cancer cell. The immune system learns to recognise that protein, generating antibodies and T-cell immunity without exposure to the pathogen itself.

mRNA vaccines came to prominence during the COVID-19 pandemic: the Pfizer–BioNTech vaccine tozinameran (Comirnaty) and Moderna's elasomeran (Spikevax) were the first mRNA vaccines authorised for general use, receiving emergency authorisations in December 2020, less than a year after the SARS-CoV-2 genome was published. Billions of doses were administered worldwide. In 2023, Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the nucleoside-modification discoveries that made mRNA vaccines practical.

Mechanism

The vaccine mRNA is manufactured by in-vitro transcription and packaged in lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) that protect it from degradation and carry it into cells, mainly at the injection site and draining lymph nodes. Inside the cell, ribosomes translate the mRNA into the antigen protein — for the COVID-19 vaccines, the coronavirus spike protein, stabilised in its prefusion conformation. The mRNA is broken down by normal cellular processes within days and never enters the nucleus or alters DNA. Two chemical tricks made the platform work: replacing uridine with N1-methylpseudouridine (the Karikó–Weissman modification), which both increases translation and reduces inflammatory recognition of the RNA, and LNP delivery, developed over decades by researchers including Pieter Cullis.

Advantages and limitations

mRNA vaccines can be designed within days of a pathogen's genome being sequenced, manufactured cell-free at scale, and rapidly re-targeted to variants. Because no live pathogen is handled, production is comparatively safe, and the platform is being applied to influenza, respiratory syncytial virus (an mRNA RSV vaccine was approved in 2024), cytomegalovirus, Zika, rabies, HIV and personalised cancer vaccines. Limitations include cold-chain requirements for early products (the Pfizer–BioNTech vaccine initially required −70 °C storage), short shelf life, reactogenicity (injection-site pain, fatigue, fever), rare cases of myocarditis and pericarditis after COVID-19 mRNA vaccination, particularly in young males, and higher cost than some conventional vaccines.

History

Key steps include the demonstration in 1990 that injected mRNA could be expressed in mouse muscle (Wolff et al.), Karikó and Weissman's 2005 finding that modified nucleosides suppress immune recognition of RNA, the founding of BioNTech (2008) and Moderna (2010), and the first human trials of mRNA vaccines (rabies, influenza) in the 2010s. Before 2020 no mRNA drug or vaccine had been licensed; the COVID-19 pandemic compressed a decade of development into months, aided by large public funding programmes such as Operation Warp Speed and by prior coronavirus research following SARS and MERS.
