Structure-mapping theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Diagram of an analogy as a mapping between two relational structures.

Structure-mapping theory is a theory of analogy and analogical reasoning developed by the cognitive scientist Dedre Gentner, first set out in 1983. It proposes that analogy works by aligning the relational structure of two situations while discarding their surface features.

Core claims

An analogy maps knowledge from a familiar base domain onto a less familiar target. What carries over is not object attributes (that the sun is yellow) but relations (that the sun attracts the planets), and especially systems of higher-order relations such as causal chains. This preference for relational, systematic matches is called the systematicity principle. Mappings are structurally consistent: one-to-one correspondences and parallel connectivity between the domains.

Development and evidence

Experiments show a "relational shift" in development: young children tend to match on surface similarity, while older children and adults prefer relational matches. The theory has been implemented computationally in the Structure-Mapping Engine (SME) built with Ken Forbus and colleagues, and extended to similarity, metaphor, and learning by comparison.

Applications

Structure-mapping underpins accounts of scientific discovery by analogy (such as Rutherford's solar-system model of the atom), analogy in education, and retrieval of analogues from memory, which the theory predicts is driven more by surface similarity than by the structural alignment that governs mapping.
