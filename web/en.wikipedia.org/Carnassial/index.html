Carnassial

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Carnassials are a pair of enlarged, blade-like cheek teeth found in many placental mammals of the order Carnivora, adapted for shearing flesh and tendon in a scissor-like action. In most carnivorans the carnassial pair consists of the fourth upper premolar (P4) and the first lower molar (m1). These teeth have sharp, self-sharpening cutting edges that slide past one another as the jaw closes, allowing the animal to slice meat efficiently rather than crush it.

Structure and function

The upper carnassial (P4) typically bears three roots and a prominent blade formed by the paracone and metacone, while the lower carnassial (m1) has a two-cusped trigonid that forms the shearing blade and a reduced talonid basin behind it. As the jaw closes, the blades pass each other like the blades of scissors, cutting soft tissue into pieces small enough to swallow. The temporomandibular joint in carnivorans restricts sideways jaw movement, maximising the shearing action.

Evolution

Carnassial-like shearing teeth evolved convergently in several extinct groups, including the creodonts, in which different teeth (the upper molars and lower molars further back in the jaw) formed the shear. This difference in carnassial position helps palaeontologists distinguish creodonts from true carnivorans. In some lineages, such as the extinct borhyaenid marsupials of South America, analogous blade-like teeth evolved independently.

Modifications and loss

In carnivorans that shifted toward omnivory or herbivory, the carnassials were reduced or repurposed: in bears and the giant panda the shearing blade is diminished and the teeth are adapted for crushing and grinding vegetation. In pinnipeds (seals and sea lions) the carnassials are lost entirely, replaced by simple, homodont postcanine teeth suited to gripping fish. Hyenas retain powerful carnassials alongside massive bone-cracking premolars.

Dental health

In domestic dogs, the upper carnassial is the largest tooth in the mouth and is commonly fractured by chewing hard objects; such "slab fractures" expose the pulp and often require extraction or root canal treatment. Carnassial abscesses, presenting as swelling below the eye, are a familiar condition in veterinary dentistry.
