Al-Aqsa Mosque

Al-Aqsa Mosque
Location: Old City of Jerusalem
Also known as: Qibli Mosque (the prayer hall)
Tradition: Islam
Significance: third holiest site in Islam

Al-Aqsa Mosque (Arabic: المسجد الأقصى‎, "the farthest mosque"), also known as the Qibli Mosque, is a congregational prayer hall in the Old City of Jerusalem. It stands on the southern side of the raised enclosure known to Muslims as al-Haram al-Sharif (the Noble Sanctuary) and to Jews as the Temple Mount. The name "Al-Aqsa" is also widely used for the entire compound, including the Dome of the Rock.

Religious significance

The mosque is considered the third holiest site in Islam, after Mecca and Medina. Muslims believe it was the destination of Muhammad's Night Journey (Isra and Mi'raj) and the first qibla — the direction of prayer — before the Kaaba. The present building, with its lead-covered dome and seven-aisled prayer hall, preserves work of many periods.

History

A first prayer house was built under the early caliphs; the Umayyad caliphs Abd al-Malik and al-Walid I erected the mosque in monumental form in the late 7th and early 8th centuries, alongside the Dome of the Rock. Earthquakes repeatedly damaged it; it was rebuilt under the Abbasids and Fatimids, modified under the Ayyubids, Mamluks and Ottomans, restored under the British Mandate and Jordan, and its dome was re-covered in lead. The mosque was set alight by an arsonist in 1969, an attack condemned internationally. Given the religious and political sensitivity of the compound, the site is administered by the Jerusalem Islamic Waqf under a long-standing "status quo" arrangement.
