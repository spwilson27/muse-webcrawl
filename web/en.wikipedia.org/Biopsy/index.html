Biopsy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A biopsy is a medical test commonly performed by a surgeon, interventional radiologist or interventional cardiologist. The process involves the extraction of sample cells or tissues for examination to determine the presence or extent of a disease. The tissue is then examined under a microscope by a pathologist and may also be chemically analysed. When an entire lump or suspicious area is removed, the procedure is called an excisional biopsy; when only a sample is taken, it is an incisional or core biopsy.

Types

Common methods include fine-needle aspiration, in which a thin needle extracts cells; core-needle biopsy, which removes a small cylinder of tissue; endoscopic biopsy taken through an endoscope; punch and shave biopsies of the skin; and bone-marrow biopsy. Increasingly, image guidance by ultrasound, CT or MRI is used to target lesions precisely. A newer approach, liquid biopsy, analyses circulating tumour DNA or cells in a blood sample.

Purpose and analysis

Biopsy is central to the diagnosis of cancer, distinguishing benign from malignant growths and determining tumour type and grade, which guides treatment. It is also used to diagnose infections, inflammatory and autoimmune conditions, and unexplained organ dysfunction — for example liver or kidney disease. Pathological examination may involve histology, immunohistochemistry and molecular testing.

Risks

Biopsies are generally safe but carry small risks of bleeding, infection, pain and, rarely, injury to nearby structures or seeding of tumour along the needle track. Results are usually integrated with imaging and clinical findings, since sampling error can occur if the needle misses the most abnormal tissue.
