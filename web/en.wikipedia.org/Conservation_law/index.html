Conservation law

A pendulum exchanges kinetic and potential energy while total energy is conserved.

In physics, a conservation law states that a particular measurable property of an isolated physical system does not change as the system evolves over time. Exact conservation laws include conservation of mass–energy, conservation of linear momentum, conservation of angular momentum, and conservation of electric charge. There are also many approximate conservation laws, which apply to specific classes of systems, such as the conservation of baryon number (approximate) and conservation of mass in non-relativistic physics.

Noether's theorem

Conservation laws are fundamental to our understanding of the physical world, in that they describe which processes can or cannot occur in nature. In 1915, Emmy Noether proved that every conservation law corresponds to a differentiable symmetry of the action of the physical system: conservation of energy follows from time-translation symmetry, conservation of momentum from spatial-translation symmetry, and conservation of angular momentum from rotational symmetry.

List of conservation laws

Exact laws include conservation of energy, linear momentum, angular momentum, electric charge, colour charge, and probability. Approximate or partial laws include conservation of mass, baryon number, lepton number, flavour, parity (violated by the weak interaction), and CP symmetry (violated in some weak decays).
