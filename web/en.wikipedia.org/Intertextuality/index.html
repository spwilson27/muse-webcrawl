Intertextuality

Intertextuality is the shaping of a text's meaning by another text, including an author's borrowing and transformation of a prior text, and a reader's referencing of one text in reading another. The term was coined by post-structuralist Julia Kristeva in 1966, drawing on the dialogism of Mikhail Bakhtin and the semiotics of Ferdinand de Saussure.

Kristeva argued that any text is "a mosaic of quotations" and the absorption and transformation of other texts, so that meaning is never wholly original but always mediated by prior discourse.

Concepts

Related concepts include allusion, quotation, plagiarism, pastiche, parody, and translation. Gérard Genette developed a systematic taxonomy in his theory of transtextuality, distinguishing intertextuality (the literal presence of one text in another), paratextuality (titles, prefaces, notes), metatextuality (commentary), hypertextuality (transformation of an earlier hypotext), and architextuality (genre classification).

Applications

Intertextual analysis is used in literary criticism, film studies, biblical studies, and media studies. Examples include James Joyce's Ulysses reworking Homer's Odyssey, T. S. Eliot's The Waste Land weaving quotations from many sources, and the dense web of references in contemporary popular culture and internet memes.

Criticism

Some critics argue the concept is so broad as to be trivial, since all language use references prior usage. Others distinguish deliberate, marked intertextuality from the general intertextual condition of texts.
