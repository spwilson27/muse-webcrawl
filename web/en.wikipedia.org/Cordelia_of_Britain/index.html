Cordelia, the youngest daughter of King Lear in the legendary history of Britain.

Cordelia (Welsh: Creiddylad) is a legendary queen of the Britons, known chiefly as the youngest of the three daughters of King Leir (Lear) in the pseudo-historical accounts popularised by Geoffrey of Monmouth's Historia Regum Britanniae (c. 1136). She is best known to modern readers as a central figure in William Shakespeare's tragedy King Lear.

In Geoffrey of Monmouth

According to Geoffrey, the ageing King Leir divided his kingdom according to his daughters' professions of love. Cordelia, refusing to flatter him, was disinherited and married Aganippus, king of the Franks. When Leir was later deposed and mistreated by his elder daughters Goneril and Regan, Cordelia received him in Gaul and led an army to Britain that restored him to the throne. After Leir's death, Cordelia succeeded him and ruled peacefully for five years, until her nephews Marganus and Cunedagius rebelled, imprisoned her, and drove her to suicide.

In Shakespeare

In King Lear (c. 1605–1606), Cordelia's story is given a tragic ending that departs from the sources: she returns with French forces to rescue her father but is defeated, and both she and the reconciled Lear die. Her honesty and devotion in the opening scene — her answer "Nothing" when asked to declare her love — set the play's catastrophe in motion, and she has become a byword for dutiful, truthful love.

Other versions

The tale appears in Holinshed's Chronicles, Spenser's The Faerie Queene, and the anonymous play King Leir, in which Cordelia and her father survive happily — the ending Shakespeare rejected. Her Welsh counterpart, Creiddylad, appears in Welsh tradition associated with Lludd Llaw Eraint.
