Anomalous monism

Anomalous monism is a theory in the philosophy of mind proposed by Donald Davidson in his 1970 essay "Mental Events." It holds that individual mental events are identical with individual physical events (token identity, hence monism), but that there are no strict laws connecting mental and physical descriptions of those events (anomalous) — mental types cannot be reduced to physical types.

The three principles

Davidson argued from three seemingly conflicting claims: (1) the principle of causal interaction — mental events causally interact with physical events (my desire causes me to reach for a glass); (2) the principle of the nomological character of causality — events related as cause and effect fall under strict deterministic laws; and (3) the principle of the anomalism of the mental — there are no strict laws on the basis of which mental events can be predicted and explained, because mental ascription is governed by norms of rationality that have no counterpart in physical theory. The reconciliation: the laws under which mental events cause physical ones describe those events in physical, not mental, vocabulary; each mental event just is a physical event, described two ways.

Reception and criticism

Anomalous monism became one of the most influential forms of non-reductive physicalism. Critics, notably Jaegwon Kim, pressed the problem of mental causation: if an event causes something only in virtue of its physical properties, its mental properties may seem causally irrelevant — a charge of epiphenomenalism that Davidson and others have disputed. The view is closely connected with Davidson's wider philosophy, including radical interpretation and the denial of a sharp scheme–content distinction.
