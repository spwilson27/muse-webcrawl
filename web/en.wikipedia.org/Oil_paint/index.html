Oil paint

Oil paint is a type of slow-drying paint that consists of particles of pigment suspended in a drying oil, commonly linseed oil. The viscosity of the paint may be modified by the addition of a solvent such as turpentine or white spirit, and varnish may be added to increase the glossiness of the dried film.

History

Oil-based paints were used in Europe from the 12th century for simple decoration, but were not widely adopted as an artistic medium until the early 15th century in Northern Europe. Flemish painter Jan van Eyck is often credited with popularising — though not inventing — oil technique, whose flexibility, rich colour, and slow drying enabled unprecedented detail and blending. The medium spread to Italy later in the 15th century and became the dominant medium for easel painting, displacing tempera.

Properties and technique

Oil paint dries by oxidation rather than evaporation, over days to months depending on pigment and film thickness. Artists exploit its slow drying to blend colours on the canvas and revise compositions; pentimenti may appear as underlayers become translucent with age. Techniques include glazing (thin transparent layers), impasto (thick textured application), scumbling, and alla prima (wet-on-wet). Traditional practice follows the "fat over lean" principle to prevent cracking.

Pigments and vehicles

Common drying oils include linseed (most typical), walnut, poppy, and safflower oils, each differing in drying time and yellowing. Pigments range from earth colours and lead white historically, to modern cadmiums, cobalt, and synthetic organic pigments. Modern oil paints are sold in tubes — an innovation of 1841 that facilitated outdoor (plein air) painting and Impressionism.
