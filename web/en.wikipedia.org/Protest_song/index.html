Protest song

Protest songs have accompanied labour, civil rights and anti-war movements.

A protest song is a song that is associated with a movement for protest and social change, and which is often written or performed to raise awareness, express dissent or rally supporters. Protest songs may be topical — responding to particular events — or general expressions of opposition to war, poverty, racism or other injustices. They range from folk ballads and spirituals to rock, hip hop and punk.

History

Political song is ancient, but the modern protest song is often traced to the labour movements of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, when the Industrial Workers of the World published its Little Red Songbook and songwriters such as Joe Hill wrote union anthems. In the United States, the folk revival of the 1940s–1960s produced artists including Woody Guthrie ("This Land Is Your Land"), Pete Seeger and Bob Dylan, whose "Blowin' in the Wind" and "The Times They Are a-Changin'" became anthems of the civil rights and anti-Vietnam War movements. "We Shall Overcome", adapted from gospel and labour traditions, became the emblematic song of the civil rights movement.

Later decades saw protest expressed through soul and funk (Marvin Gaye's "What's Going On"), reggae (Bob Marley), punk, and hip hop (Public Enemy's "Fight the Power"). Protest songs have played roles in movements worldwide, from anti-apartheid South Africa to the Arab Spring and pro-democracy demonstrations in Hong Kong.

Characteristics and debate

Protest songs often favour simple, singable melodies and direct lyrics suited to collective singing at marches and meetings. Scholars debate their actual political efficacy: songs rarely change policy directly, but they can build solidarity, transmit memory between movements and articulate grievances in memorable form. Commercial success can also blunt a song's edge, a tension performers and audiences have negotiated for a century.
