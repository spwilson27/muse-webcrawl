First Amendment

The Bill of Rights, of which the First Amendment is the first article.

The First Amendment (Amendment I) to the United States Constitution prevents the government from making laws that regulate an establishment of religion, prohibit the free exercise of religion, or abridge the freedom of speech, the freedom of the press, the freedom of assembly, or the right to petition the government for redress of grievances. It was adopted on 15 December 1791, as one of the ten amendments that constitute the Bill of Rights.

Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof; or abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press; or the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the Government for a redress of grievances.

Rights protected

The amendment's clauses protect several distinct rights. The Establishment Clause and Free Exercise Clause together govern religion and government. The Free Speech Clause and Free Press Clause protect expression; the Supreme Court has interpreted them broadly to cover political speech, symbolic speech, and much commercial speech, while recognising limited categories such as incitement, obscenity, and defamation as less protected. The Assembly Clause protects peaceful gatherings, and the Petition Clause protects appeals to government.

Interpretation

Originally applicable only to the federal government, the First Amendment was applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment during the twentieth century (beginning with Gitlow v. New York in 1925). Landmark cases include New York Times Co. v. Sullivan (1964) on defamation of public officials, Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969) on incitement, and Citizens United v. FEC (2010) on campaign finance. Debates continue over religious exemptions, school prayer, compelled speech, and the application of free-speech principles to new media.
