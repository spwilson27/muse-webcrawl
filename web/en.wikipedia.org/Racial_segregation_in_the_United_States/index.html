Racial segregation in the United States
Legal and de facto separation of racial groups
Jim Crow era c. 1877–1965

Racial segregation in the United States is the systematic separation of facilities and services — housing, healthcare, education, employment and transportation — into racially separate groups. While mainly directed against African Americans, segregation also affected Latino, Asian and Native Americans in different regions.

Jim Crow era

After Reconstruction, Southern states enacted "Jim Crow" laws mandating segregation in schools, transport and public accommodations. The Supreme Court upheld the doctrine of "separate but equal" in Plessy v. Ferguson (1896). Segregation was enforced by law in the South and by custom, redlining and restrictive covenants elsewhere.

Civil rights era

The Supreme Court overturned school segregation in Brown v. Board of Education (1954), declaring separate educational facilities inherently unequal. Mass protest — the Montgomery bus boycott, sit-ins and Freedom Rides — pressured Congress to pass the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965, which dismantled legal segregation. The Fair Housing Act of 1968 addressed discrimination in housing.

Legacy

Although de jure segregation was abolished, de facto patterns persist in residential patterns, school demographics and wealth distribution, and remain subjects of research and policy debate. The broader system of classification is discussed at Race (human categorization).
