Assignment (law)

Assignment is a legal term used in the context of the law of contracts and of property. In contract law, assignment is the transfer of rights or benefits under a contract from one party (the assignor) to another (the assignee). In property law, assignment typically refers to the transfer of an interest in property, such as a lease, to another person.

Assignment of contract rights

Generally, rights under a contract may be assigned freely unless the contract prohibits assignment, the rights involve personal services or materially change the obligor's duty, or assignment is barred by statute or public policy. The assignor transfers the benefit of the contract; the assignee steps into the assignor's shoes and may enforce the rights against the obligor, subject to any defences the obligor could have raised against the assignor. Notice to the obligor is not strictly required for the assignment to be effective between assignor and assignee, but it protects the assignee against the obligor paying the assignor instead.

Assignment versus novation and delegation

Assignment transfers benefits but not burdens: contractual obligations generally cannot be assigned, though they may be delegated, with the delegating party usually remaining liable. A novation replaces the original contract with a new one and requires the consent of all parties, extinguishing the original obligation.

Property assignments

In real property law, assignment commonly refers to the transfer of a leasehold interest from one tenant to another, transferring the entire remaining term. This is distinguished from a sublease, in which the original tenant retains some interest. Many leases restrict assignment without the landlord's consent. In intellectual property law, patents, copyrights and trademarks may be assigned by written instrument, transferring ownership to the assignee.

Anti-assignment clauses

Parties frequently include clauses prohibiting assignment. Courts construe such clauses narrowly, and statutes in many jurisdictions limit their effect in areas such as insurance claims, factoring of receivables, and consumer contracts.
