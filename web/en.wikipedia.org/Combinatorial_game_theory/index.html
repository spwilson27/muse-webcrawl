Combinatorial game theory

Combinatorial game theory is a branch of mathematics that studies sequential games with perfect information, typically two-player games in which the players move alternately, there is no element of chance, and the game ends after finitely many moves. It is distinct from the game theory of economics, which concerns strategies, payoffs, and hidden information.

History

The subject grew from the analysis of the game of Nim, solved by Charles Bouton in 1901, and from the Sprague–Grundy theorem of the 1930s, which showed that every impartial game is equivalent to a Nim heap of a certain size. In the 1970s John Horton Conway, Elwyn Berlekamp, and Richard Guy systematised the field; Conway's On Numbers and Games showed that game positions themselves form a number system, the surreal numbers.

Concepts

A game is defined by the set of moves available to the Left and Right players. Positions are classified as wins for the first or second player, or for Left or Right regardless of who starts. Temperature measures how urgent a move is, and games can be added together as disjunctive sums. Combinatorial game theory has been applied to chess and Go endgames, Dots and Boxes, Treblecross, and many other puzzles.
