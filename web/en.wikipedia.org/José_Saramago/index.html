José de Sousa Saramago (16 November 1922 – 18 June 2010) was a Portuguese writer and recipient of the 1998 Nobel Prize in Literature. The Swedish Academy praised his "parables sustained by imagination, compassion and irony" with which he made "a tangled reality apprehensible". His works, some of which can be seen as allegories, commonly present subversive perspectives on historic events, emphasising the theopoetic human factor.

Life

Saramago was born in Azinhaga, in the Ribatejo, to landless peasants; the surname Saramago (a plant name) was a family nickname accidentally added to his birth registration. The family moved to Lisbon, where he trained as a mechanic and worked in jobs including translator, editor and journalist. A member of the then-clandestine Portuguese Communist Party from 1969, he lost his newspaper post after the Carnation Revolution of 1974–75 and turned to fiction full-time. His breakthrough came with Levantado do Chão (1980) and the historical fantasia Baltasar and Blimunda (1982). After the Portuguese government blocked the European candidacy of The Gospel According to Jesus Christ (1991) over objections from the Catholic Church, Saramago moved in protest to Lanzarote in the Canary Islands, where he lived with his wife, the journalist Pilar del Río.

Works and style

Saramago's novels are marked by long, winding sentences using commas in place of conventional punctuation, dialogue run into narrative, and a narrator who comments ironically on the action. Blindness (1995), in which an epidemic of white blindness overwhelms an unnamed city, is his most famous work; its sequel Seeing (2004) imagines an election sabotaged by blank ballots. Other major novels include The Stone Raft (1986), The History of the Siege of Lisbon (1989), All the Names (1997), The Double (2002), Death with Interruptions (2005) and Cain (2009). He died of leukaemia at his home in Tías, Lanzarote, in 2010.
