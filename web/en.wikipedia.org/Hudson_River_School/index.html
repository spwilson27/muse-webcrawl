Hudson River School

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Hudson River School was a mid-19th-century American art movement embodied by a group of landscape painters whose aesthetic vision was influenced by Romanticism. Their paintings depict the Hudson River valley and the surrounding Catskill, Adirondack and White Mountains, and later the American West and South America, celebrating the American landscape as a manifestation of God.

First generation

The movement’s founder, Thomas Cole (1801–1848), an English-born artist who settled in Catskill, New York, began painting the region in 1825. His grand, sometimes allegorical scenes (such as The Course of Empire) were joined by the more serene naturalism of Asher Brown Durand, whose Kindred Spirits (1849) memorialised Cole with the poet William Cullen Bryant in a Catskill glen.

Second generation

From the 1850s, artists such as Frederic Edwin Church, Albert Bierstadt, Sanford Robinson Gifford and John Frederick Kensett developed the style sometimes called Luminism, with dramatic light effects and vast, detailed canvases. Church’s The Heart of the Andes (1859) and Bierstadt’s Rocky Mountain scenes made the painters celebrities. The movement faded after the Civil War as tastes shifted toward the Barbizon style and Impressionism, and the name “Hudson River School” — originally a derisive label coined in the 1870s — was later embraced by art historians.
