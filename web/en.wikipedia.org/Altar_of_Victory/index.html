Altar of Victory

Location: Curia Julia, Rome
Erected: 29 BC by Augustus
Removed: 382 AD by Gratian; controversy to 394
Figures: Symmachus, Ambrose of Milan

The Altar of Victory was an altar in the Curia Julia, the meeting house of the Roman Senate in Rome, set up by Augustus in 29 BC and surmounted by a gilded statue of the goddess Victory brought from Tarentum. Senators traditionally burned incense and made offerings at the altar before sessions, and swore oaths there. Its removal and restoration became a defining controversy in the religious conflicts of the late Roman Empire.

History of the dispute

Constantius II first removed the altar during a visit to Rome in 357; Julian restored it, and Valentinian I retained it. In 382 the Christian emperor Gratian removed it again and abolished public funding for traditional Roman cults. The pagan senator Symmachus famously petitioned Valentinian II in 384 to restore it, arguing for religious tolerance in his Relatio; Bishop Ambrose of Milan countered the plea, and the altar was never permanently reinstated. Brief restorations under Eugenius and Arbogast ended with Theodosius I's victory at the Frigidus in 394.

Significance

The affair is a classic case study of the Christianisation of the Roman Empire and of the arguments — toleration versus truth — that framed late antique religious debate. Symmachus's plea, "we contemplate the same stars", remains a famous statement of pagan traditionalism.
