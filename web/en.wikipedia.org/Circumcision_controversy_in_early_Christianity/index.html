Circumcision controversy in early Christianity

The circumcision controversy in early Christianity was a dispute in the 1st-century Church over whether Gentile converts to Christianity were required to be circumcised and to observe the whole of the Mosaic Law. It was one of the first major internal conflicts of the Christian movement and central to the separation of early Christianity from Judaism.

Background

Circumcision was the sign of God's covenant with Abraham and a defining mark of Jewish identity. Jesus and the first apostles were Jews, and the earliest Jesus movement was located within Judaism. As the movement gained Gentile adherents, particularly through the mission of Paul of Tarsus, the question arose whether male converts first had to become Jewish proselytes through circumcision.

Council of Jerusalem

Around the year 49, the apostles and elders met at Jerusalem (Acts 15) to decide the question. According to Acts, James the Just pronounced that Gentile converts should not be burdened with circumcision, requiring instead abstinence from idol pollution, sexual immorality and certain foods. Paul, in his letters — above all Galatians — argued more radically that justification comes through faith in Christ rather than works of the Law, and that requiring circumcision would nullify the Gospel. The related "incident at Antioch", in which Paul rebuked Peter for withdrawing from table fellowship with Gentiles, shows the controversy continued after the council. The Jerusalem decision opened Christianity to the Gentile world and set it on the path to becoming a separate religion.
