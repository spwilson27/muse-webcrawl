The 2009 satellite collision was the first accidental hypervelocity collision between two intact artificial satellites in Earth orbit. On 10 February 2009, the operational US communications satellite Iridium 33 and the defunct Russian military communications satellite Kosmos 2251 collided at an altitude of 776 kilometres above Siberia, destroying both spacecraft.

Debris

The impact, at a relative velocity of about 11.7 km/s (42,000 km/h), produced more than 2,300 pieces of trackable debris, many of which will remain in orbit for decades. Debris from the collision has since forced avoidance manoeuvres by other satellites and has been cited as a practical illustration of the Kessler syndrome risk. In 2012 debris from the event passed close to the International Space Station, requiring its crew to take shelter precautions.

Aftermath

The collision highlighted gaps in international space traffic management: conjunction warnings at the time relied on incomplete public data, and predictions for that day had not flagged the pair as the closest expected approach. It spurred improvements in conjunction screening by the US military's space surveillance network and commercial operators, and Iridium replaced the lost satellite with a spare.
