Grand Duke

Grand duke (female: grand duchess) is a European hereditary title for either a sovereign ruler of a grand duchy or a member of a ruling or formerly ruling dynasty. In the hierarchy of nobility, a grand duke traditionally ranks below an emperor or king and above a sovereign prince or duke. The style of address for a reigning grand duke is usually "Royal Highness".

Sovereign grand dukes

The first notable use of the title was by the Medici rulers of Florence, made Grand Dukes of Tuscany in 1569. During the Napoleonic era and the Congress of Vienna, several German states — including Baden, Hesse-Darmstadt, Mecklenburg-Schwerin, Mecklenburg-Strelitz, Oldenburg, and Saxe-Weimar-Eisenach — became grand duchies, as did Luxembourg in 1815. Most German grand dukes lost their thrones in the German Revolution of 1918–1919. Luxembourg is today the world's only sovereign grand duchy; its head of state, the Grand Duke of Luxembourg, is a reigning monarch.

Dynastic use

In Imperial Russia, children and male-line grandchildren of the emperor bore the title Grand Duke (Veliky Knyaz) or Grand Duchess with the style "Imperial Highness". The title was also used in the Byzantine-influenced world and by the rulers of Lithuania (where "Grand Duke of Lithuania" was equivalent to a king) and of Muscovy before the adoption of the title Tsar. In Finland, "Grand Duke of Finland" was a title of the Swedish king and later of the Russian emperor, who ruled Finland as an autonomous grand duchy from 1809 to 1917.

Order of precedence

In the protocol of European courts, a sovereign grand duke was placed after kings but before reigning dukes and princes, though details varied. Non-sovereign grand dukes, such as Russian imperial princes, ranked according to their dynastic position rather than the literal title.
