Birthday problem

In probability theory, the birthday problem asks for the probability that, in a set of n randomly chosen people, at least two share a birthday. The surprising result is that in a group of just 23 people the probability reaches about 50.7%, and with 70 people it exceeds 99.9% — a counter-intuitive finding sometimes called the birthday paradox.

Explanation

The result is driven not by comparisons with one fixed birthday but by the number of pairs: with 23 people there are 253 pairs, each with roughly a 1/365 chance of matching. The probability of no shared birthday is 365/365 × 364/365 × … falling quickly as n grows. The calculation assumes birthdays are uniformly distributed over 365 days and independent.

Applications

The same mathematics underlies the birthday attack in cryptography, where the effort needed to find a collision in a hash function grows with the square root of the number of possible outputs, motivating longer hash lengths. Analogous reasoning appears in polling, database matching and the analysis of coincidences.
