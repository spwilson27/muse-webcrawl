Permanent income hypothesis

The permanent income hypothesis (PIH) is an economic theory of consumption behaviour proposed by the American economist Milton Friedman in his 1957 book A Theory of the Consumption Function. It holds that consumers base their spending not on their current (measured) income but on their long-term expected, or "permanent", income.

Content

Friedman divided measured income into a permanent component — the steady income a household expects to receive over time, reflecting education, occupation and wealth — and a transitory component, such as a one-off bonus or a temporary loss. Consumption, he argued, depends essentially on permanent income: people smooth consumption over time by saving and borrowing, so a temporary windfall is mostly saved rather than spent, while a permanent rise in income raises spending by nearly the full amount.

Implications and evidence

The hypothesis reconciles two previously conflicting findings: that richer households save a larger fraction of income (cross-section data) while the aggregate saving rate stays roughly constant as incomes grow over time (time-series data). It also implies that temporary tax cuts or stimulus payments should have only small effects on consumer spending — a result that sparked extensive empirical debate. The PIH is closely related to Franco Modigliani's life-cycle hypothesis, and together they form the foundation of modern consumption theory, later formalised with rational expectations by Robert Hall (1978).
