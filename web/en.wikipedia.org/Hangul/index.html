Hangul (Korean: 한글; in North Korea called Chosŏn'gŭl, 조선글) is the modern official writing system for the Korean language. It was created in 1443 by King Sejong the Great of Joseon and scholars of the Hall of Worthies, and promulgated in 1446 with the document Hunminjeongeum. Hangul is a featural alphabet: the shapes of its letters (called jamo) are designed to reflect the articulatory features of the sounds they represent.

History

Before Hangul, Korean was written using Chinese characters (hanja) in systems such as idu and hyangchal, which were difficult for commoners to learn. Sejong's preface to the Hunminjeongeum states that because the speech of Korea differs from that of China, ordinary people could not express themselves in writing, and that he therefore devised 28 new letters "so that every man may learn them easily and use them conveniently in daily life". The new script met opposition from Confucian officials such as Choe Malli, who feared it would undermine the classical Chinese tradition, and it was at one point banned by King Yeonsangun in 1504. It survived in private and women's correspondence, Buddhist texts and vernacular fiction, and was revived and standardised in the late 19th and 20th centuries; after 1945 it became the sole official script in North Korea and the dominant script in South Korea, where hanja is now used only sparingly. The original Hunminjeongeum manuscript is a National Treasure of South Korea and is inscribed in UNESCO's Memory of the World register.

Letters and syllable blocks

Modern Hangul has 14 basic consonant letters and 10 basic vowel letters, which combine into compound letters (including five tense consonants and eleven complex vowels), giving 24 letters in common counting. Consonant shapes depict the position of the tongue, lips and throat: for example ㄱ shows the root of the tongue blocking the throat, and strokes are added systematically for aspiration. Jamo are not written in a simple line but grouped into square syllable blocks, each block consisting of an initial consonant, a vowel, and optionally a final consonant (batchim). The total number of theoretically possible blocks is 11,172.

Use and recognition

Hangul is praised by linguists for its scientific design and learnability, and 9 October is celebrated as Hangul Day in South Korea (15 January in North Korea). The script has also been adapted experimentally for a few unwritten languages, most famously in an attempt to write the Cia-Cia language of Indonesia.
