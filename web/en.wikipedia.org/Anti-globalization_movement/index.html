Anti-globalization movement

The movement mobilised mass protests against summits of the WTO, IMF, World Bank and G8.

The anti-globalization movement, also known as the global justice movement or alter-globalization movement, is a social movement critical of economic globalization and of the institutions that promote it, including the World Trade Organization (WTO), the International Monetary Fund, and the World Bank. Participants—a loose coalition of labour unions, environmentalists, farmers, students, anarchists, and NGOs—generally argue that neoliberal trade and finance policies harm workers, communities, democracy, and the environment, while benefiting multinational corporations.

History

The movement drew on earlier resistance, including the Zapatista uprising in Chiapas, which began on 1 January 1994—the day NAFTA took effect. It erupted onto the world stage with the 1999 Seattle WTO protests, when roughly 50,000 demonstrators shut down the WTO ministerial conference. Major mobilisations followed at summits in Prague (2000), Quebec City (2001), Gothenburg (2001), and Genoa (2001), where the G8 protests saw the death of protester Carlo Giuliani. The World Social Forum, first held in Porto Alegre, Brazil in 2001 under the slogan "another world is possible", became the movement's principal gathering place.

Ideas

Shared themes include opposition to corporate-led trade liberalisation, structural adjustment, and privatisation; support for labour rights, environmental protection, debt cancellation for developing countries, food sovereignty, and participatory democracy. Writers such as Naomi Klein (No Logo, 1999) and Joseph Stiglitz gave the movement widely read critiques. After 2001 the movement's energy partly dispersed into anti-war protest and later into movements such as Occupy Wall Street (2011), which echoed many of its themes.
