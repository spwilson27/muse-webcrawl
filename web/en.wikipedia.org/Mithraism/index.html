Mithraism

Mithraism
Central deity: Mithras
Region: Roman Empire
Period: 1st–4th centuries CE
Worship site: Mithraeum (underground temple)
Membership: initiatory, predominantly male soldiers and merchants

Mithraism, also known as the Mithraic mysteries, was a Roman mystery religion centered on the god Mithras. It was practiced across the Roman Empire from roughly the 1st to the 4th century CE, especially among soldiers. The Roman cult is related in name to the Iranian god Mithra but appears to have been a largely new creation of the Roman world; its beliefs must be reconstructed mainly from archaeology, since almost no Mithraic scripture survives.


The tauroctony, the cult's central image

Iconography and temples

The cult's central image is the tauroctony: Mithras, wearing a Phrygian cap and Persian dress, kneels on a bull and slays it in a cave, accompanied by a dog, snake, scorpion, raven, and two torchbearers, Cautes and Cautopates. A second scene shows Mithras born from a rock (petra genetrix). Worship took place in a mithraeum, a windowless, cave-like hall—often underground—with benches along the walls for communal meals, imitating the cave of the bull-slaying.

Initiation and grades

Initiates progressed through seven grades, each associated with a planet: Corax (raven, Mercury), Nymphus (bridegroom, Venus), Miles (soldier, Mars), Leo (lion, Jupiter), Perses (Persian, Moon), Heliodromus (sun-runner, Sun), and Pater (father, Saturn). Initiation involved ordeals and oaths of secrecy; no women appear to have been admitted. Communal banquets reenacted Mithras's feast with Sol after the bull-slaying. More than 400 mithraea sites have been identified, from Rome and Ostia to Hadrian's Wall and the Danube frontier.

Decline and scholarship

Mithraism flourished alongside early Christianity and shared features—initiation, communal meals, moral striving, and a December 25 festival of Sol Invictus—that made it a notable rival. It declined in the 4th century under Christian emperors and disappeared by the early 5th century. Scholarly debate has long concerned how much the Roman cult owed to Iranian religion; Franz Cumont's theory of direct Persian origins has been largely revised, with many scholars now treating Roman Mithraism as a distinct creation with an astrological character, the tauroctony encoding a star map.
