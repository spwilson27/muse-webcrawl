Bible translations

The Bible has been translated into many languages from the biblical languages of Hebrew, Aramaic, and Greek. As of September 2023, the complete Bible has been translated into 756 languages, the New Testament into more than 1,700 additional languages, and portions of scripture into more than 3,700 languages in total — more than any other book in history.

Ancient translations

The first major translation was the Septuagint, a Greek rendering of the Hebrew Bible produced in Alexandria beginning in the 3rd century BCE. Other ancient versions include the Aramaic Targums, the Syriac Peshitta, and Jerome's Latin Vulgate (late 4th century CE), which became the standard Bible of the Western Church for over a thousand years. Early translations also appeared in Coptic, Gothic, Armenian, Georgian, Ethiopic, and Old Church Slavonic, the last by the missionaries Cyril and Methodius in the 9th century.

Reformation and printing

The printing press and the Protestant Reformation transformed Bible translation. Martin Luther's German New Testament (1522) and full Bible (1534) set a model for vernacular translation. In English, milestones include William Tyndale's New Testament (1526), the Geneva Bible (1560), and the King James Version (1611), whose language profoundly shaped English literature.

Modern translations and mission

The 19th and 20th centuries saw an explosion of missionary translation work coordinated by Bible societies, alongside scholarly versions such as the Revised Standard Version, the New International Version (1978), and dynamic-equivalence renderings like the Good News Bible. Debates continue over translation philosophy — formal equivalence versus dynamic equivalence — and over textual basis and inclusivity of language.
