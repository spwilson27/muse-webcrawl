General of the Army (abbreviated as GA) is a five-star general officer rank in the United States Army. It is generally equivalent to the rank of field marshal in other countries. The rank stands above the four-star rank of general and below the special grade of General of the Armies, which has been held only by John J. Pershing and, posthumously, George Washington and Ulysses S. Grant.

History

The rank was first created in a different form in 1866 for Ulysses S. Grant, and later held by William Tecumseh Sherman and Philip Sheridan. In its five-star form, the rank of General of the Army was established by Congress in December 1944, during the Second World War, to give senior American commanders rank parity with allied field marshals.

Holders

Five officers held the five-star rank: George C. Marshall, Douglas MacArthur, Dwight D. Eisenhower, Henry H. "Hap" Arnold (later also the only General of the Air Force), and Omar Bradley. Bradley, promoted in 1950, was the last; no officer has held the rank since his death in 1981.

Insignia

The insignia consists of five silver stars arranged in a circle, worn on the shoulder. Officers holding the rank never retire in the formal sense but remain on the active-duty rolls for life, drawing full pay and allowances.
