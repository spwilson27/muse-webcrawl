Optical rotation

Optical rotation, also known as polarisation rotation, is the turning of the plane of linearly polarised light about the direction of motion as the light travels through certain materials. It is the defining measurable property of an optically active substance. The angle of rotation, measured with a polarimeter, is proportional to the thickness of the sample and, for solutions, to concentration; dividing by these gives the specific rotation, a characteristic constant of a chiral compound at a given wavelength and temperature.

Sign and nomenclature

If the plane rotates clockwise as seen by an observer facing the incoming light, the substance is dextrorotatory, designated (+); anticlockwise rotation is levorotatory, (−). These signs are empirical and do not map directly onto the absolute configuration labels R/S, nor onto the older D/L system, even though (+) was historically written as d- and (−) as l-.

Explanation and applications

The effect arises because chiral media have different refractive indices for left- and right-circularly polarised light (circular birefringence); the phase difference accumulated over the path rotates the recombined linear polarisation. Optical rotation is used to assay the purity and identity of pharmaceuticals and fine chemicals, to monitor the inversion of sucrose in the sugar industry, and to infer enantiomeric excess. Its wavelength dependence (optical rotatory dispersion) and differential absorption analogue (circular dichroism) provide further structural information about molecules.
