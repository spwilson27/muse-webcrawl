Chaff

Chaff is the dry, scaly protective casings of the seeds of cereal grain, or similar fine plant material. It is separated from the grain during threshing and winnowing. In early agriculture, harvested grain was threshed to loosen the husks and then winnowed — tossed into the wind so that the light chaff blew away while the heavier grain fell back — a process referenced in many ancient texts and idioms, such as "separating the wheat from the chaff."

Agricultural use

Chaff is inedible to humans but is used as livestock fodder, as a component of compost and mulch, and for thatching and other rural crafts. Mixed with grain it can reduce storage quality, so cleaning grain of chaff remains a standard step in milling and seed production, now performed mechanically by combine harvesters that thresh and winnow in a single pass.

Radar countermeasure

The word is also used for a radar countermeasure: clouds of small, thin pieces of aluminium, metallized glass fibre or plastic released by aircraft and ships to create false radar echoes and confuse radar-guided missiles and tracking systems. Known in the Second World War by the British code name "Window" and the German "Düppel", chaff was first used operationally during the Hamburg raids of July 1943, when it blinded German air-defence radars. Modern chaff is cut to lengths tuned to act as half-wave dipoles for expected threat radar frequencies and is dispensed from aircraft in cartridges, often together with infrared flares.
