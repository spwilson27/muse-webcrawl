Object (grammar)

Object
FieldLinguistics, grammar
TypesDirect object, indirect object, object of a preposition
Typical formNoun phrase or pronoun
Related notionsSubject, transitive verb, complement

In grammar, an object is a noun, pronoun, or noun phrase that is governed by a verb or a preposition. In a typical transitive clause, the object is the participant affected by the action of the verb, standing in contrast to the subject, which typically performs or controls the action.

Types of object

A direct object receives the action of a transitive verb directly: in "She read the book", the book is the direct object. An indirect object indicates the recipient or beneficiary of the action, as in "She gave him the book", where "him" is the indirect object and "the book" remains the direct object. Verbs that take two objects are called ditransitive. An object of a preposition is the noun phrase following a preposition, as in "the keys are on the table".

Objects across languages

In languages with grammatical case, objects are often marked in the accusative case (and indirect objects in the dative), while in English word order and pronouns (me, him, her, us, them) signal object status. Some languages distinguish objects by agreement on the verb or by particles. In linguistic typology, the basic ordering of subject, verb and object — SVO in English, SOV in Japanese and Turkish, VSO in Welsh and Classical Arabic — is a fundamental classification of clause structure.

Objects and complements

Traditional grammar distinguishes objects from complements, though usage varies. An object complement describes or renames the object, as in "They elected her president". Modern syntactic theories analyse objects within the verb phrase and debate whether some apparent objects are better treated as complements.
