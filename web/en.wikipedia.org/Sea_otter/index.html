The sea otter (Enhydra lutris) is a marine mammal native to the coasts of the northern and eastern North Pacific Ocean. Adult sea otters typically weigh between 14 and 45 kg (31 and 99 lb), making them the heaviest members of the weasel family but among the smallest marine mammals in the world.

Characteristics

Unlike most marine mammals, sea otters lack blubber and rely instead on an exceptionally thick coat of fur — the densest in the animal kingdom, with up to a million hairs per square inch — to keep warm. They are famed for their use of tools: a sea otter will repeatedly strike a hard-shelled mollusc against a rock balanced on its chest to break the shell open. Otters carry a favourite rock in a loose pouch of skin under the forearm.

Diet and behaviour

Sea otters eat sea urchins, abalone, mussels, clams, crabs and fish, consuming roughly a quarter of their body weight in food each day to sustain their high metabolism. They spend much of the day foraging, grooming and resting in groups called rafts, often wrapping themselves in kelp to avoid drifting while asleep.

Ecology

The sea otter is a classic keystone species. By preying on sea urchins, otters prevent urchins from overgrazing kelp forests, which in turn provide habitat for many other species. Where otters have been removed, "urchin barrens" often result.

Conservation

Hunted to near extinction for their fur during the maritime fur trade of the 18th and 19th centuries, sea otter numbers fell from perhaps 300,000 to fewer than 2,000. Protected since 1911, populations have partially recovered through reintroduction, but the species remains listed as endangered, threatened by oil spills, predation and disease.
