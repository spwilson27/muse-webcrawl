Modus ponens

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Modus ponens is a rule of inference in propositional logic and a basic valid form of deductive argument. It states that if a conditional statement ("if P then Q") is accepted, and its antecedent P holds, then the consequent Q may be concluded:

  • Premise 1: P → Q
  • Premise 2: P
  • Conclusion: Q

For example: "If it is raining, the ground is wet. It is raining. Therefore the ground is wet." The name is short for the Latin modus ponendo ponens, "the mode that by affirming affirms", referring to the affirmation of the antecedent.

History and role

The rule was understood by the Stoic logicians of antiquity as the first of their basic argument forms ("indemonstrables") and appears throughout medieval and modern logic. In formal systems it is frequently the only rule of inference in Hilbert-style axiomatics, and it underlies implication elimination in natural deduction and the forward-chaining inference of expert systems and logic programming.

Related forms

Modus ponens contrasts with its valid counterpart modus tollens (denying the consequent) and with the invalid fallacies of affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent, which resemble it superficially. Lewis Carroll's dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" (1895) famously probes what justifies applying the rule itself, showing that a rule of inference cannot simply be added as another premise without regress.
