Missionaries of Charity

The Missionaries of Charity is a Catholic religious congregation established on October 7, 1950, in Calcutta (now Kolkata), India, by Mother Teresa. Its members, recognizable by their white saris with blue borders, dedicate themselves to serving "the poorest of the poor" — the destitute, the dying, abandoned children, leprosy patients, and people with HIV/AIDS — without charge.

Origins

Mother Teresa, then a Loreto sister teaching in Calcutta, reported receiving a "call within a call" in 1946 to serve the poorest directly. After receiving permission, she began work in the slums in 1948 and was joined by former students. The Vatican formally approved the new congregation in 1950. In 1952 she opened Nirmal Hriday ("Place for the Pure Heart"), a home for the dying, in a donated former temple near the Kalighat Kali temple, and later Shishu Bhavan, a home for children.

Growth and branches

The order expanded across India in the 1960s and internationally from 1965, beginning with Venezuela. It now works in well over a hundred countries. Branches include the Missionaries of Charity Brothers (founded 1963), the contemplative sisters and brothers, the Missionaries of Charity Fathers, and lay Co-Workers. Members take the traditional vows of poverty, chastity, and obedience, plus a fourth vow of wholehearted free service to the poorest of the poor.

Work and criticism

The sisters run hospices, orphanages, leprosy clinics, soup kitchens, and mobile dispensaries. The congregation has also faced criticism over standards of medical care in its homes, its opposition to abortion and contraception, and questions about its finances and the handling of donations — criticism the order and its supporters have disputed. Mother Teresa received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1979 and was canonized in 2016.
