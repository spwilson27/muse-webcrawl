Lobbying

Lobbying is the act of attempting to influence the actions, policies, or decisions of government officials — most often legislators or members of regulatory agencies — on behalf of a particular interest. The term derives from the lobbies of legislative chambers where advocates would wait to speak with lawmakers. Lobbying is carried out by professional lobbyists, trade associations, corporations, non-profit organisations, advocacy groups and ordinary citizens, and is a protected activity in many democracies — in the United States under the First Amendment right to petition the government.

Methods

Lobbying ranges from direct contact — meetings, testimony at hearings, drafting model legislation — to indirect "grassroots" campaigns that mobilise public pressure, and "astroturfing," the simulation of grassroots support. Lobbyists provide information, research and political intelligence to officials, and frequently move between government and lobbying careers through the so-called "revolving door," a practice many jurisdictions restrict with cooling-off periods.

Regulation

In the United States, the Lobbying Disclosure Act of 1995 requires registered lobbyists to report their clients, issues and expenditures; the Foreign Agents Registration Act (1938) covers those acting for foreign principals. Federal lobbying spending exceeds $4 billion per year, led by the pharmaceutical, insurance, technology and energy sectors. The European Union maintains a voluntary Transparency Register for lobbyists active with its institutions, and countries including Canada, Germany and Australia maintain statutory registers.

Criticism and debate

Critics argue that well-funded lobbying amplifies the voices of wealthy interests over ordinary voters and can shade into corruption, as in the Jack Abramoff scandal of the 2000s. Defenders respond that lobbying supplies expertise to lawmakers, represents legitimate stakeholder interests, and is constitutionally protected political participation. Political scientists distinguish lobbying's influence on access and agenda-setting, which is well documented, from direct vote-buying, which is rarer and illegal in most systems.
