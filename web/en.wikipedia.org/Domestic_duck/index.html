The domestic duck or domestic mallard (Anas platyrhynchos domesticus) is a subspecies of the mallard that has been domesticated by humans and raised for its meat, eggs, and feathers. Nearly all breeds of domestic duck descend from the mallard; the exception is the domestic Muscovy duck, which descends from a different wild species. Ducks were domesticated in China more than two thousand years ago and are now farmed worldwide.

Breeds

Recognised breeds include the white Pekin (the dominant commercial meat duck), the Khaki Campbell and Indian Runner (prolific egg layers, capable of around 300 eggs per year), the Rouen (resembling a large mallard), the Aylesbury, the Cayuga, and ornamental Calls. Selective breeding has produced birds much heavier than wild mallards, and most domestic breeds have lost the ability to fly more than short distances.

Farming

China accounts for the large majority of world duck meat production. Ducks are hardier than chickens in wet conditions, forage on paddy fields — where they eat pests and fertilise the rice — and are traditionally integrated with rice cultivation in Asia. Their eggs, including salted eggs and the Filipino delicacy balut, are widely eaten in East and Southeast Asia. Duck down is a premium filling for bedding and clothing.

Behaviour

Domestic ducks retain the mallard's social, water-loving character. Ducklings imprint on the first moving object they see, a phenomenon made famous by Konrad Lorenz's experiments. Unlike wild mallards, domestic drakes are largely silent; the familiar loud quack is produced by the female.
