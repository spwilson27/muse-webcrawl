Hue and cry

In common law, a hue and cry is a process by which bystanders are summoned to assist in the apprehension of a criminal who has been witnessed in the act of committing a crime. The phrase derives from Anglo-Norman French hu e cri — an outcry and clamour — and reflects the medieval obligation of all able-bodied men to join the pursuit of felons.

History

Under English law from the time of the Anglo-Saxon and Norman kings, anyone who witnessed a felony was required to raise the hue and cry; the men of the village or hundred were then obliged to turn out and pursue the offender to the boundary of their jurisdiction, where the neighbouring community took up the chase. The Statute of Winchester (1285) reinforced the system, making communities answerable for robberies committed within their bounds if the offenders were not captured. Failure to join the hue and cry, or raising it falsely, could itself be punished.

Survival of the practice

The institution declined as professional policing developed, but traces remained in law for centuries; a requirement relating to hue and cry after robbery influenced claims for compensation against the hundred under the Riot (Damages) Act 1886 and its predecessors. The term survives in general usage to mean any public outcry or clamour, and in the title of publications such as police gazettes — The Hue and Cry was an early name for the Police Gazette in Britain.
