Solnhofen Limestone

The Solnhofen Limestone or Solnhofen Plattenkalk is a geological formation in Bavaria, Germany, famous as a Konservat-Lagerstätte that preserves a rare assemblage of fossilized organisms, including highly detailed imprints of soft tissues. It is a fine-grained limestone from the Upper Jurassic (early Tithonian, about 150 million years ago), deposited in hypersaline lagoons. Its most celebrated fossil is Archaeopteryx, the early bird-like dinosaur.

Geology and deposition

During the Late Jurassic, the area lay on the northern margin of the Tethys Ocean, in an archipelago of islands separated by shallow lagoons protected by sponge and coral reefs. The lagoon bottoms were hypersaline and low in oxygen — hostile to scavengers and burrowing organisms — so dead animals that sank were preserved undisturbed in fine carbonate mud that lithified into thinly bedded limestone.

Fossils

More than 600 species have been identified from Solnhofen, including jellyfish, crustaceans, horseshoe crabs (some with their trackways), fishes such as Leedsichthys-relatives and coelacanths, ichthyosaurs, turtles, lizards, crocodilians, pterosaurs such as Rhamphorhynchus and Pterodactylus, and dinosaurs such as the small theropod Compsognathus. Delicate structures — feathers, wings membranes, and jellyfish bells — are preserved because of the absence of decay and disturbance.

Twelve or so specimens of Archaeopteryx have been found in the quarries around Solnhofen and Eichstätt since 1861, providing key evidence for the evolutionary link between dinosaurs and birds.

Quarrying and uses

The limestone has been quarried for centuries for roofing and floor tiles, and in the 19th century its extremely fine, uniform grain made it the preferred stone for lithographic printing — the process from which lithography takes its name. Quarries near Solnhofen, Langenaltheim, and Eichstätt still operate, and fossil preparation from their slabs is a local craft; the Bürgermeister-Müller Museum in Solnhofen and the Jura-Museum in Eichstätt display major finds.
