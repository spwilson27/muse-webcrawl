Quebec City (French: Ville de Québec) is the capital city of the Canadian province of Quebec. Founded in 1608 by the French explorer Samuel de Champlain, it is one of the oldest European settlements in North America. The city is famous for Old Quebec, the only walled city in North America north of Mexico, whose fortifications and historic district are a UNESCO World Heritage site.

History

The site, where the St. Lawrence River narrows (the Algonquin name Kébec means "where the river narrows"), had been the Iroquoian village of Stadacona visited by Jacques Cartier in 1535. Champlain's habitation of 1608 became the heart of New France. The city was captured by the British in 1759 after the Battle of the Plains of Abraham, in which both commanders, Wolfe and Montcalm, were killed, and was ceded to Britain in 1763. American forces failed to take it in the winter of 1775–76. It served as capital of the Province of Canada at intervals before becoming Quebec's provincial capital at Confederation in 1867; the Quebec Conferences of 1943 and 1944 were held here during the Second World War.

Geography and sights

The city stands on Cap Diamant above the confluence of the St. Lawrence and Saint-Charles rivers. Landmarks include the Château Frontenac hotel (1893), the Citadelle, the National Assembly building, the Plains of Abraham (now a national historic park), and Montmorency Falls just outside the city. The Winter Carnival (Carnaval de Québec) is one of the world's largest winter festivals.
