Rice Krispies

Rice Krispies (known as Rice Bubbles in Australia and New Zealand) is a breakfast cereal produced by WK Kellogg Co in North America and by Kellanova internationally. It is made of crisped rice: rice and sugar paste formed into rice shapes and cooked, dried, and toasted, which causes them to expand into thin, hollowed-out walls that are crunchy when they collapse. When milk is added to the cereal, the walls tend to collapse, creating the characteristic "Snap, Crackle and Pop" sounds after which the cereal's mascots are named.

Rice Krispies were introduced in 1928. In the United Kingdom the cereal was first marketed in 1929, and it has been sold in many countries under variants such as Cocoa Krispies (chocolate-flavoured) and Frosted Rice Krispies. The cereal is also the key ingredient in Rice Krispies Treats, marshmallow-bound bars whose recipe Kellogg's promoted from 1939–40 onward.

History

Rice Krispies were developed in the late 1920s at Kellogg's Battle Creek, Michigan laboratories; employee Eugene McKay is often credited with a central role in perfecting the process by which cooked rice grains are dried and then puffed by heat. The brand launched in 1928 in the United States and quickly spread internationally, becoming one of Kellogg's core cereals. Wartime rationing affected production in the 1940s; afterward, marketing emphasized the audible fun of the cereal and its use in home-made marshmallow treats. Following the 2023 split of Kellogg Company, the North American cereal business, including Rice Krispies in the US and Canada, passed to WK Kellogg Co, while Kellanova retained the brand in most other markets.

Snap, Crackle and Pop

The cereal's mascots are a trio of gnome-like elves named Snap, Crackle, and Pop, introduced in the early 1930s: Snap first appeared in 1933, with Crackle and Pop following later in the decade. They are among the most durable advertising characters in grocery history, and their names capture the sounds made when the crisped rice meets milk — the noises result from the thin baked walls of the kernels fracturing and collapsing as they absorb liquid. A fourth, short-lived mascot named Pow appeared in the 1950s. The trio's appearance has been redesigned several times; in 2020 they were depicted for a time in animated form.

Production

To make the cereal, rice is cooked with sugar, salt, and malt flavouring, partially dried, allowed to temper so moisture equalizes, then rolled and toasted in hot ovens, where the remaining moisture flashes to steam and puffs each grain into a thin-walled crisp. Vitamins and minerals are added for fortification in most markets. Because the grains are fragile and generate dust, the product is screened and then packaged promptly to preserve crispness.

Variants and related products

Spin-offs include Cocoa Krispies (called Coco Pops in the UK, Australia, and other markets), Frosted Rice Krispies, Rice Krispies Treats bars (sold ready-made since the 1990s), and numerous seasonal and licensed versions. The home recipe for Rice Krispies Treats — cereal bound with melted butter and marshmallows — was devised by Kellogg's home economics staff, commonly credited to Mildred Day and Malitta Jensen, and printed on boxes from 1940, making the snack a fixture of American bake sales.
