Free jazz

Free jazz is a style of jazz music developed in the late 1950s and 1960s, characterised by a liberation from conventional harmony, chord changes, fixed rhythm, and chorus structures in favour of collective improvisation, expressive timbres, and new forms of musical organisation. The term comes from Ornette Coleman's 1960 album Free Jazz: A Collective Improvisation, recorded by a double quartet.

Characteristics

Free jazz loosens or abandons the functional harmony of bebop and modal jazz. Performers may use extended techniques — multiphonics, overblowing, growls, and unconventional articulation — draw on folk, African, Middle Eastern, and Indian musics, and organise pieces around motifs, energy levels, or conduction cues rather than written chord progressions. Rhythm may be fluid, with drummers acting as equal improvising partners rather than timekeepers.

Key figures

Pioneers include Ornette Coleman, Cecil Taylor, John Coltrane (whose late works such as Ascension, 1965, embraced collective improvisation), Albert Ayler, Sun Ra, Eric Dolphy, Don Cherry, Archie Shepp, and Pharoah Sanders. In Europe, musicians such as Peter Brötzmann, Derek Bailey, and Evan Parker developed related but distinct free improvisation scenes.

Reception and influence

Free jazz was controversial on its emergence, praised as a liberation and condemned as chaos; it became linked in the United States with the Black Arts Movement and assertions of cultural self-determination. Its approaches have influenced avant-garde rock, contemporary classical music, and improvised music worldwide.
