Sacred tree

Sacred trees are venerated in religious traditions around the world.

A sacred tree or holy tree is a tree which is considered to be sacred or worthy of spiritual respect or reverence. Such trees appear throughout world history in many religions and mythologies, where they may serve as dwelling places of deities, as links between heaven and earth, or as objects of pilgrimage and offering.

Examples in world religions

In Buddhism, the Bodhi Tree at Bodh Gaya is the descendant of the fig tree under which Siddhartha Gautama attained enlightenment. In Hinduism, the banyan, peepal, and tulsi are sacred, and trees are protected in sacred groves across India. Norse mythology centers on Yggdrasil, the immense world tree connecting the realms of gods and mortals. The Tree of Life appears in Mesopotamian, Egyptian, and Kabbalistic traditions, and the oak was sacred to the Celts and to Zeus in Greek religion; the Druids conducted rites in oak groves.

In Shinto, sacred trees called shinboku are marked with a straw rope (shimenawa) and serve as yorishiro, vessels able to attract spirits. In East Asian folk religion, village guardian trees in China and Korea shelter tutelary shrines. Ancient individual trees, such as the Jaya Sri Maha Bodhi in Sri Lanka — planted in 288 BCE and among the oldest documented planted trees in the world — remain living objects of worship.

Sacred groves

Trees are often protected collectively in sacred groves, which function as refuges for biodiversity. India's thousands of sacred groves, the chinju no mori shrine forests of Japan, and church forests of Ethiopia preserve old-growth ecosystems that have vanished from surrounding landscapes.
