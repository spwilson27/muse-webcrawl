Simone Lucie Ernestine Marie Bertrand de Beauvoir (9 January 1908 – 14 April 1986) was a French existentialist philosopher, writer, social theorist, and feminist activist. Though she did not consider herself a philosopher early in her career, she had a significant influence on both feminist existentialism and feminist theory.

Early life

De Beauvoir was born in Paris to Georges Bertrand de Beauvoir, a legal secretary, and Françoise (née Brasseur). She was educated at Catholic girls' schools and later studied mathematics and philosophy at the Institut Sainte-Marie and the Sorbonne. She passed the agrégation in philosophy in 1929, placing second behind Jean-Paul Sartre, whom she met while preparing for the examination.

Career

She taught philosophy in lycées in Marseille, Rouen, and Paris until 1943, when she left teaching—officially dismissed after a complaint—to write full-time. Her first novel, She Came to Stay (1943), explored existentialist themes through a fictionalised account of her relationship with Sartre and Olga Kosakiewicz.

The Second Sex

Her most influential work, The Second Sex (Le Deuxième Sexe, 1949), is a foundational text of modern feminism. In it she argued that women have historically been regarded as the deviant "Other" of a male norm, famously writing: "One is not born, but rather becomes, a woman." The book examined women's oppression across history, biology, psychoanalysis, and literature.

Later life and death

De Beauvoir won the Prix Goncourt in 1954 for The Mandarins. She published several volumes of autobiography, as well as works on ageing (The Coming of Age, 1970) and, after Sartre's death in 1980, Adieux: A Farewell to Sartre (1981). She died of pneumonia in Paris in 1986 and was buried beside Sartre in Montparnasse Cemetery.
