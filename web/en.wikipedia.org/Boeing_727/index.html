Boeing 727

The Boeing 727 is an American narrow-body airliner that was developed and produced by Boeing Commercial Airplanes. After the heavier 707 quad-jet was introduced in 1958, Boeing addressed the demand for shorter flight lengths from smaller airports. The 727 was designed with three rear-mounted Pratt & Whitney JT8D turbofans, a T-tail, and a distinctive built-in rear airstair. It first flew in February 1963 and entered service with Eastern Air Lines in February 1964.

Design and development

The 727 was Boeing's only trijet airliner. Its wing incorporated sophisticated high-lift devices — triple-slotted flaps, leading-edge slats, and Krueger flaps — that let it operate from short runways of about 1,500 m (5,000 ft) at smaller airports while cruising at Mach 0.84. The ventral airstair in the rear fuselage allowed passenger boarding at airports without jet bridges; it became famous in 1971 when hijacker D. B. Cooper parachuted from one in flight.

Variants and production

The original 727-100 was followed by the stretched 727-200, which carried more passengers and became the most popular version. Freighter and convertible variants served cargo roles for decades. Production ended in 1984 after 1,832 aircraft were built, and the type was for a time the best-selling jet airliner in history. Passenger service largely ended in the 2000s as noise and fuel-efficiency rules tightened, though cargo and charter examples flew into the 2010s.

Operators

Major operators included United Airlines, American Airlines, Eastern Air Lines, Pan Am, Lufthansa, and FedEx Express, which flew a large freighter fleet.
