Indirect object

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In linguistics, an indirect object is a grammatical element that indicates the recipient or beneficiary of the action expressed by a verb — the one to or for whom something is done. It contrasts with the direct object, which is directly acted upon.

Maria gave her brother a book.

Here a book is the direct object (what was given), and her brother is the indirect object (the recipient of the giving).

In English

Modern English marks the indirect object chiefly by word order: in the dative construction, the indirect object precedes the direct object without a preposition ("She baked him a cake"). Most such sentences have an alternative prepositional construction using to or for ("She baked a cake for him"). Only certain verbs — typically verbs of giving, sending, telling, and making — licence the double-object pattern. Pronouns preserve a vestige of case: me, him, her, us, and them serve as both direct and indirect objects.

In other languages

Many languages mark the indirect object with the dative case: in German, Ich gab dem Mann das Buch ("I gave the man the book") uses the dative article dem. Latin, Russian, Greek, and Sanskrit similarly distinguish dative recipients from accusative direct objects. Some languages instead employ applicative verb morphology to promote a beneficiary to object status.

Theoretical issues

Linguists debate how indirect objects are represented syntactically: whether the double-object construction contains two distinct object positions, and why some verbs resist the alternation ("She donated money to charity" but not "*She donated charity money"). Related notions include the distinction between primary and secondary objects used in typology.
