Magic (illusion)

Magic, also known as illusion, conjuring or prestidigitation, is a performing art in which audiences are entertained by tricks or illusions of seemingly impossible feats using natural means. It is distinct from paranormal or supernatural magic, which historical and religious traditions hold to be genuine occult or supernatural powers. The performer of stage magic is called a magician, illusionist or conjuror.

History

Magic tricks were performed in ancient Egypt — the cups and balls routine appears to be depicted in a tomb painting at Beni Hasan around 2000 BCE, though this interpretation is debated — and in ancient Greece and Rome, where feats were attributed to the gods or sold as secret knowledge. The first book in English explaining magic tricks, The Discoverie of Witchcraft (1584) by Reginald Scot, exposed sleight of hand partly to combat witchcraft persecutions. The 19th century is regarded as magic's golden age: Jean-Eugène Robert-Houdin, often called the father of modern conjuring, brought magic from fairs into theatres, dressed as a gentleman and using elegant apparatus. Later stars included Harry Houdini, famous for escapes; Howard Thurston and Harry Blackstone Sr. with large-scale stage illusions; and Dai Vernon, whose close-up card work revolutionised sleight of hand.

Effects and methods

Magicians classify effects into broad categories:

  • Production — producing something from nothing (a rabbit from an empty hat)
  • Vanish — making something disappear
  • Transformation — changing one object into another
  • Restoration — restoring a destroyed object (torn-and-restored paper)
  • Teleportation / transposition — moving an object from place to place
  • Escape — escaping restraints or confinement
  • Levitation — defying gravity
  • Penetration — one solid passing through another
  • Prediction and mentalism — apparently reading minds or foretelling choices

Underlying methods include sleight of hand, misdirection, mechanical apparatus, mirrors, trapdoors, mathematics, psychology of attention and perception, and pre-arranged confederates ("stooges"). The profession guards its secrets: most magic societies require members to swear never to reveal methods to non-magicians, although exposure programmes and online tutorials have made many secrets widely accessible.

Categories of performance

Stage illusions are large-scale spectacles for big audiences, popularised in modern times by David Copperfield and Siegfried & Roy. Close-up or micromagic is performed at arm's length, often with cards and coins; parlour magic occupies the middle ground. Street magic, mentalism, escapology, children's magic and online or television magic are recognised specialities.
