Polyester

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Polyester fibres magnified

Polyester is a category of polymers that contain ester linkages in their main chain. Although many polyesters exist, the term most commonly refers to polyethylene terephthalate (PET), a thermoplastic widely used in fibres for clothing, containers for liquids and foods, films, and engineering resins. Polyester fibres are the most produced synthetic fibres in the world.

History

PET was patented in 1941 by John Rex Whinfield and James Tennant Dickson at the Calico Printers' Association in Manchester, building on earlier work by Wallace Carothers at DuPont. It was commercialised as Terylene in the United Kingdom and Dacron in the United States in the early 1950s, and became a staple of the textile industry.

Properties and uses

Polyester fibres are strong, resistant to stretching, shrinking, abrasion and most chemicals, and they dry quickly and resist wrinkling. They are used in clothing — often blended with cotton or wool — as well as in upholstery, carpets, tyre reinforcement, conveyor belts and safety belts. PET film (such as Mylar) is used in packaging, electrical insulation and magnetic tape. Because it melts rather than burning readily, and is inexpensive to produce, polyester accounts for roughly half of the global fibre market.

Production

PET is synthesised from ethylene glycol and terephthalic acid (or its dimethyl ester) by polycondensation, releasing water or methanol. The polymer is extruded and spun into fibres, or moulded and stretch-blow-moulded into bottles. Most virgin polyester is derived from petroleum feedstocks, though bio-based and recycled routes are growing.

Environmental issues

Polyester is not biodegradable, and washing synthetic garments releases microplastic fibres into waterways. PET is, however, one of the most widely recycled plastics: bottles are commonly recycled into new containers or into textile fibre (rPET). Research continues into chemical recycling and enzymatic depolymerisation.
