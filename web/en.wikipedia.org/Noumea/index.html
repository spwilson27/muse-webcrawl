Nouméa (French pronunciation: [numea]) is the capital and largest city of the French special collectivity of New Caledonia, in the south-west Pacific Ocean. Situated on a hilly peninsula at the southern end of the main island of Grande Terre, it is protected by one of the largest lagoon harbours in the Pacific and is home to a large share of the territory's population.

History

The town was founded in 1854 by Tardy de Montravel as Port-de-France, a year after France annexed New Caledonia. Renamed Nouméa in 1866, it served as the centre of a penal colony until 1897 and grew on the strength of the territory's nickel mining industry. During the Second World War it was a major United States naval and military base in the South Pacific, headquarters of the US fleet in the region.

Geography and economy

Greater Nouméa, which includes the communes of Dumbéa, Mont-Dore and Païta, had a population of roughly 180,000 at recent censuses. The city contains the territory's government, its main port, the seat of the University of New Caledonia and the Tjibaou Cultural Centre, designed by Renzo Piano and opened in 1998 to celebrate Kanak culture. Nickel processing — notably the Doniambo smelter — tourism and services dominate the economy, and the bays of Anse Vata and Baie des Citrons are the city's principal beach districts.

Politics

Nouméa gave its name to the Nouméa Accord of 1998, which set out a process of progressive transfer of powers from France and a series of referendums on independence, held in 2018, 2020 and 2021, in which voters chose to remain part of France.
