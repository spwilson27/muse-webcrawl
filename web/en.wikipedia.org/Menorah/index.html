Menorah

The menorah (Hebrew: מְנוֹרָה, mənōrā) is a seven-branched candelabrum described in the Hebrew Bible as one of the furnishings of the Tabernacle and later of the Temple in Jerusalem. Made of pure gold according to the account in Exodus, it was lit daily by the priests with olive oil. The menorah is one of the oldest symbols of Judaism and appears in the emblem of the modern State of Israel.

Biblical account

Exodus 25:31–40 gives detailed instructions for the menorah: hammered from a single piece of gold, with a central shaft and six branches curving out from it, decorated with cups, knobs and flowers in the form of almond blossoms. It stood in the Holy Place of the Tabernacle opposite the table of showbread, and its seven lamps were tended morning and evening by Aaron and his sons.

Temple and fate

The menorah was among the vessels of Solomon's Temple and of the Second Temple. A famous relief on the Arch of Titus in Rome, erected about 81 AD, shows Roman soldiers carrying the captured menorah in triumph after the sack of Jerusalem in 70 AD. Its subsequent fate is unknown; it was reportedly kept in Rome's Temple of Peace and may have been taken to Constantinople or lost during the Vandal sack of Rome in 455.

Symbolism

Interpretations of the seven branches have included the seven days of creation, the planets of ancient astronomy, and the light of divine presence. The distinct nine-branched Hanukkah menorah (hanukkiah) commemorates the rededication of the Temple and is a separate object; synagogue art traditionally avoided exact replicas of the Temple menorah. A monumental bronze menorah, a gift of the British Parliament, stands outside the Knesset in Jerusalem.
