Pierre Alphonse Laurent (18 July 1813 – 2 September 1854) was a French mathematician and military engineer, best known for discovering the Laurent series, an expansion of a complex function that generalises the Taylor series by permitting terms of negative degree. He is not to be confused with the French composer Pierre Boulez, nor with the mathematician Hermann Laurent.

Life

Laurent entered the École Polytechnique in 1830 and trained as an engineer officer. He served in Algeria in the 1830s and later worked for several years on the enlargement of the port of Le Havre. His duties left limited time for research, and his mathematical work was done largely in isolation from the Parisian academic establishment. He died in Paris in 1854 at the age of 41, leaving manuscripts that his widow later sought to have published.

The Laurent series

In 1843 Laurent submitted a memoir to the Paris Academy of Sciences for its Grand Prize competition on functions of a complex variable. The memoir contained his theorem: a function holomorphic in an annular region can be expanded in a series of both positive and negative powers of (z − z₀). Karl Weierstrass had arrived at the same result in an unpublished 1841 manuscript, but Laurent's was the first published account, and the expansion bears his name. The Laurent series is fundamental to complex analysis: its negative-power ("principal") part classifies isolated singularities — removable, pole, or essential — and underlies the residue theorem used to evaluate integrals.

Other work

Laurent also worked on the theory of waves and optics, contributing to Augustin-Jean Fresnel's and François Arago's studies of polarisation, and on a posthumously published treatise concerning the calculus of variations and partial differential equations. Because much of his output was neglected or published late, his reputation rests almost entirely on the series that bears his name.
