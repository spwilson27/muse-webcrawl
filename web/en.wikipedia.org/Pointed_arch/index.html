Pointed arch

A pointed arch, also called a Gothic arch or ogival arch, is an arch with a pointed crown, typically formed by two centres and radii larger than the arch's span. It is the defining structural and visual element of Gothic architecture, replacing the round Romanesque arch in European building from the 12th century onward.

Structural advantages

Compared with a semicircular arch, a pointed arch directs more of the load vertically downward and less outward, reducing lateral thrust. It also allows arches of the same height to span different widths, which made it possible to vault rectangular bays with level crowns — a key enabling technology of Gothic rib vaults and flying buttresses.

Origins

Pointed arches appeared earlier in Islamic architecture, including in Umayyad and Abbasid buildings, and in some Romanesque contexts such as Burgundy and Sicily. The form was adopted systematically in the Île-de-France in the 1140s, notably at the Basilica of Saint-Denis under Abbot Suger.

Types

Varieties include the lancet arch (tall and narrow), the equilateral arch, the drop arch, the four-centred or Tudor arch, and the ogee arch, in which the curves reverse near the apex.
