Scientific name

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A scientific name is a formal name given to a species (or other taxon) of living organism under the international codes of biological nomenclature. For most organisms it is a binomial name: a two-part Latinized name consisting of the genus name followed by a specific epithet—for example, Homo sapiens for humans and Panthera leo for the lion. Scientific names are unique, stable, and universal, unlike common names, which vary between languages and regions.

History

The system was formalized by the Swedish naturalist Carl Linnaeus in the eighteenth century, in works such as Species Plantarum (1753) for plants and the tenth edition of Systema Naturae (1758) for animals. Before Linnaeus, species were described by long polynomial Latin phrases; his consistent two-word scheme was rapidly adopted worldwide.

Rules and form

Scientific names are governed by codes including the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature (animals), the International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants, and separate codes for bacteria and viruses. By convention, the genus name is capitalized and both parts are printed in italics; the name of the describing author may follow (in parentheses if the species has been moved to a different genus). Names below the rank of species add further elements—subspecies names are trinomial, e.g. Panthera tigris sumatrae. Names are formed from Latin or Latinized Greek, or from any words treated as Latin.

Purpose

Because a single valid scientific name applies to a taxon everywhere, the system allows unambiguous communication among scientists regardless of language. Names also encode classification: the genus part places the species within a hierarchy of related organisms. Synonyms—older or superseded names—accumulate as classification changes, and the codes provide rules of priority and typification (linking each name to a type specimen) to keep usage stable.
