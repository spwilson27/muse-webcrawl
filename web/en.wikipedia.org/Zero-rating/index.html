Zero-rating

Zero-rating is the practice of providing Internet access without financial cost under certain conditions, such as by permitting access to only certain websites by a mobile telecommunications company, or exempting certain websites or services from data-usage caps. A mobile network operator that zero-rates a service does not count data used by that service against the customer's monthly data allowance.

Programmes and examples

Notable zero-rating programmes have included Facebook's Free Basics (originally Internet.org), Wikipedia Zero, which offered free access to Wikipedia in developing countries until its discontinuation in 2018, and Google Free Zone. Mobile carriers in many countries also zero-rate music or video streaming services, or their own content offerings, as a competitive feature of particular plans.

Debate

Supporters argue that zero-rating expands Internet access for low-income users and can promote digital inclusion. Critics, including many net neutrality advocates, argue that it distorts competition by favouring selected services, entrenches dominant platforms, and gives users a curated "walled garden" rather than the open Internet. Regulators have responded differently by jurisdiction: India prohibited discriminatory data tariffs in 2016, a rule widely seen as banning services such as Free Basics, while other countries have assessed zero-rating offers case by case under net-neutrality rules.
