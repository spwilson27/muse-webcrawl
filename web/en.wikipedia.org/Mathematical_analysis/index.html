Mathematical analysis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Analysis is built on the rigorous theory of limits and the real numbers.

Mathematical analysis is the branch of mathematics dealing with continuous functions, limits, and related theories, such as differentiation, integration, measure, infinite sequences and series, and analytic functions. It grew out of calculus, which was developed independently by Isaac Newton and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz in the 17th century, and was placed on rigorous foundations in the 19th century by Cauchy, Weierstrass, Riemann, and Dedekind.

History

Ancient roots include Zeno's paradoxes and Archimedes' method of exhaustion. The calculus of Newton and Leibniz enabled rapid progress in physics and geometry but rested on intuitive infinitesimals. Augustin-Louis Cauchy introduced precise limit definitions in the 1820s, Karl Weierstrass formalised the epsilon–delta definition of limit, and Richard Dedekind and Georg Cantor constructed the real numbers rigorously. In the 20th century, Henri Lebesgue's measure theory transformed integration, and functional analysis extended the methods to infinite-dimensional spaces.

Main branches

  • Real analysis — properties of real numbers, sequences, series, continuity, differentiation and Riemann integration
  • Complex analysis — functions of a complex variable, analyticity, contour integration
  • Functional analysis — vector spaces of functions, normed and Hilbert spaces, operators
  • Measure theory — measures, measurable functions, Lebesgue integration
  • Harmonic analysis — representation of functions as superpositions of waves, Fourier analysis
  • Differential equations — ordinary and partial differential equations
  • Numerical analysis — algorithms for approximating solutions

Key concepts

The fundamental theorem of calculus links differentiation and integration. A metric space formalises distance; completeness guarantees limits of Cauchy sequences exist. Series such as the Taylor series represent functions as infinite polynomials, and uniform convergence controls when limits can be interchanged with sums, derivatives, and integrals.

Applications

Analysis underpins physics and engineering — from fluid dynamics and electromagnetism (partial differential equations) to signal processing (Fourier analysis) and probability theory, which is built on measure theory.
