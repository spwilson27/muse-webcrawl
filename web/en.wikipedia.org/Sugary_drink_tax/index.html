Sugary drink tax

A sugary drink tax, soda tax, or sweetened beverage tax (SBT) is a tax or surcharge designed to reduce the consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages, which are associated with obesity, type 2 diabetes, and tooth decay. Taxes may be levied as a flat excise per litre, per gram of sugar, or in tiers by sugar content, and are usually applied to manufacturers or distributors rather than at the till.

Notable examples

Mexico introduced a tax of one peso per litre in 2014; studies found purchases of taxed beverages fell by roughly 5–10% over the following two years. The United Kingdom's Soft Drinks Industry Levy (2018) is tiered by sugar concentration and induced widespread reformulation, sharply reducing the sugar content of drinks sold. In the United States, Berkeley, California adopted the first major city soda tax in 2014, followed by Philadelphia, San Francisco, Seattle, and others; several states later pre-empted local taxes.

Debate

Supporters, including the World Health Organization (which recommends taxes raising retail prices by at least 20%), cite measurable falls in consumption and reformulation. Critics argue soda taxes are regressive, burden small retailers, and push shoppers to neighbouring jurisdictions, and beverage-industry groups have funded campaigns against them.
