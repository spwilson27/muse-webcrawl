Brandenburg-Prussia

Brandenburg-Prussia (German: Brandenburg-Preußen) is the historiographic term for the early modern realm established by the personal union, from 1618, of the Margraviate of Brandenburg — a principality of the Holy Roman Empire — with the Duchy of Prussia, a Polish fief lying outside the Empire. Ruled by the electors of the House of Hohenzollern, the composite monarchy survived the devastation of the Thirty Years' War, won great-power status under Frederick William, the "Great Elector", and was transformed in 1701, when Elector Frederick III crowned himself King in Prussia, founding the Kingdom of Prussia.

Formation

The union came about through inheritance diplomacy. Elector John Sigismund married Anna of Prussia, daughter of Duke Albert Frederick; when the duke died without male heirs in 1618, John Sigismund inherited the Duchy of Prussia, which the Hohenzollerns held as a fief of the Polish crown. In 1614, the Treaty of Xanten had already given Brandenburg the Duchy of Cleves and the counties of Mark and Ravensberg in western Germany, making the Hohenzollern lands a scattered arc from the Rhine to the Niemen — territories with different laws, estates, religions and administrations, held together only by the person of the ruler.

The Thirty Years' War and the Great Elector

Brandenburg was ravaged during the Thirty Years' War (1618–1648): under the indecisive George William, armies of the Empire, Sweden and others marched across the electorate, and Brandenburg lost perhaps half its population. Frederick William (r. 1640–1688), the Great Elector, rebuilt the state: the Peace of Westphalia (1648) added Eastern Pomerania and several secularised bishoprics; he created a standing army financed by permanent taxation negotiated with the estates, curtailed noble obstruction at home, and in the Treaties of Wehlau and Oliva (1657–1660) secured the end of Polish suzerainty over Ducal Prussia. His victory over Sweden at the Battle of Fehrbellin (1675) made his reputation. In 1685, the Edict of Potsdam opened Brandenburg to Huguenot refugees fleeing France, bringing some 20,000 skilled immigrants.

Toward kingship

Frederick William's son Frederick III pursued the royal dignity that would raise the dynasty's status and underline Prussia's sovereignty. Emperor Leopold I consented by the Crown Treaty of 1700, in exchange for troops for the impending War of the Spanish Succession: on 18 January 1701 Frederick crowned himself in Königsberg as Frederick I, "King in Prussia" — in, because part of Prussia remained Polish and the imperial title of king could not be taken within the Empire. Thereafter the whole Hohenzollern monarchy was increasingly known simply as Prussia.
