The Koch snowflake (also known as the Koch curve, Koch star, or Koch island) is a fractal curve and one of the earliest fractals to be described. It is built by starting with an equilateral triangle and recursively altering each line segment as follows: divide the segment into three equal parts, draw an equilateral triangle pointing outward on the middle third, and remove the base of that triangle. The curve was introduced in a 1904 paper by the Swedish mathematician Helge von Koch.

Construction

Beginning with an equilateral triangle, each iteration replaces every straight segment with four segments one-third as long. After infinitely many steps, the resulting curve is continuous everywhere but differentiable nowhere — a property that made it a striking early example in the study of pathological curves.

Properties

At each iteration the perimeter grows by a factor of 4/3, so the limiting curve has infinite length:

Pn = (4/3)n P0 → ∞

The area, by contrast, converges. Each iteration adds a diminishing amount of area, and the total area of the snowflake is exactly 8/5 of the area of the original triangle:

A = (8/5) A0

The snowflake is self-similar: each of its parts is a scaled copy of the whole. Its Hausdorff dimension is log 4 / log 3 ≈ 1.2619, strictly between that of a line (1) and a plane (2). The curve can also be generated by an L-system or an iterated function system.

Variations

Related constructions include the Koch anti-snowflake (triangles cut inward), quadratic Koch curves using squares instead of triangles, and three-dimensional analogues such as the Koch tetrahedron. The complementary region and the curve's generalizations have been used as models for coastlines and other rough natural boundaries.
