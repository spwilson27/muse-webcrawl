Malice aforethought

Malice aforethought is a legal term in common-law jurisdictions describing the mental state (mens rea) traditionally required for the crime of murder. Despite its name, it requires neither spite nor premeditation: it can be formed in an instant before the act.

Meaning

In modern formulations, malice aforethought generally includes: an intent to kill; an intent to inflict grievous bodily harm; knowledge that an act is virtually certain to cause death or grievous harm ("depraved heart" recklessness in some jurisdictions); and, in many U.S. jurisdictions, intent to commit a felony during which a death occurs (felony murder).

History

The phrase developed in English common law to distinguish murder from manslaughter. In England and Wales the Homicide Act 1957 and later case law clarified the intention element, while in the United States definitions vary by state and in federal law.
