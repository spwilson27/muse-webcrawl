Buffalo jump

A buffalo jump, or bison jump, is a cliff formation used by Indigenous peoples of the North American Great Plains to hunt bison in large numbers. Hunters would stampede herds of American bison over the cliff edge; the animals fell to their deaths or were crippled below, where they could be dispatched and butchered. The practice allowed whole communities to harvest far more meat, hides and bone in a single event than could be taken by hunting on foot.

Method

Successful jumps depended on detailed knowledge of bison behaviour and landscape. Hunters built long drive lanes marked by lines of stone cairns ("deadmen") that funnelled a grazing herd toward the cliff. Specialised runners, sometimes wearing bison or calf skins to lure or guide the herd, provoked a stampede while others concealed along the lanes waved robes to keep the animals moving and prevent escape. Below the cliff lay a processing camp where the carcasses were skinned, cut up, and the meat dried or made into pemmican.

Notable sites

The best-known site is Head-Smashed-In Buffalo Jump in Alberta, Canada, a UNESCO World Heritage Site used for nearly 6,000 years, where bone deposits reach depths of about 10 metres. Other important sites include the Madison Buffalo Jump in Montana, the Olsen–Chubbuck site in Colorado, and Bonfire Shelter in Texas. Buffalo jumps were used for millennia and declined only when horses and firearms changed hunting practices in the 18th and 19th centuries.
