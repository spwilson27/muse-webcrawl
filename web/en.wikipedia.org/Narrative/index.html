Narrative

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A narrative, story, or tale is any account of a series of related events or experiences, whether non-fictional (such as memoirs, biographies, and news reports) or fictional (such as fairy tales, novels, and films). Narratives can be presented through written or spoken words, images, or performance, and are found in all human cultures as a fundamental way of making sense of experience.

Structure

Narratives typically organise events into a sequence with a beginning, middle, and end. The German writer Gustav Freytag analysed classical drama into a five-part structure — exposition, rising action, climax, falling action, and dénouement — often drawn as "Freytag's pyramid". The causal connection of events, rather than mere chronology, is often taken to distinguish a narrative plot from a simple chronicle: in E. M. Forster's famous formulation, "the king died and then the queen died" is a story, while "the king died and then the queen died of grief" is a plot.

Elements

Common elements of narrative include characters, setting, plot, conflict, theme, and narration — the voice and perspective through which the story is conveyed. Joseph Campbell's "hero's journey", or monomyth, proposed a common template underlying myths from many cultures.

Narrative in human life

Beyond literature, narrative plays a central role in history, law, journalism, politics, and psychology. "Narrative identity" theory holds that people construct their sense of self by weaving life events into an internalised story. Persuasive narratives can shape public opinion, a fact studied in rhetoric and media studies.
