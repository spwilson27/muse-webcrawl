Antipope

An antipope (Latin: antipapa) is a person who claims to be Bishop of Rome and leader of the Roman Catholic Church in opposition to the legitimately elected pope. Between the 3rd and mid-15th centuries, antipopes were supported by factions within the Church itself and by external rulers.

History

The first antipope was Hippolytus of Rome (217–235), who opposed Pope Callixtus I. Notable antipopes include Novatian in the 3rd century, and the rival claimants of the Western Schism (1378–1417), when popes at Rome and Avignon — and later a third line at Pisa — each claimed legitimacy. The schism was resolved by the Council of Constance, which deposed or received the resignations of the claimants and elected Martin V in 1417.

The last historically significant antipope was Felix V (Amadeus VIII of Savoy), elected by the Council of Basel in 1439 and abdicating in 1449. In the modern era, small sedevacantist groups have occasionally elected their own "popes", none recognized by the wider Church.

Assessment

Which claimants count as antipopes is sometimes disputed, since later judgments on legitimacy have varied; the official list in the Annuario Pontificio is not considered infallible.
