Modern synthesis (20th century)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The architects of the modern synthesis unified genetics with natural selection.

The modern synthesis was the early 20th-century synthesis of Charles Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection with Gregor Mendel's theory of genetics. Between roughly 1918 and 1947, mathematical population genetics — developed by Ronald Fisher, J. B. S. Haldane, and Sewall Wright — showed that Mendelian inheritance is fully consistent with gradual, selection-driven evolution, resolving the apparent conflict between Mendelians and biometricians and refuting Lamarckism and mutationism as primary mechanisms.

Development

Fisher's 1918 paper reconciled continuous variation with particulate inheritance, and his The Genetical Theory of Natural Selection (1930), Haldane's mathematical series (1924–34), and Wright's work on genetic drift and shifting balance built the theoretical framework. Field and laboratory researchers then integrated it: Theodosius Dobzhansky's Genetics and the Origin of Species (1937) connected population genetics to speciation; Ernst Mayr's Systematics and the Origin of Species (1942) defined the biological species concept and allopatric speciation; George Gaylord Simpson's Tempo and Mode in Evolution (1944) showed the fossil record was compatible; and G. Ledyard Stebbins extended the synthesis to plants (1950). Julian Huxley popularised the term in Evolution: The Modern Synthesis (1942).

Core tenets

  • Evolution is change in the genetic composition of populations
  • Variation arises from mutation and recombination; genes are particulate and are not blended away
  • Natural selection is the main directional mechanism, alongside genetic drift, gene flow, and mutation
  • Speciation usually occurs through geographic isolation and the gradual accumulation of differences
  • Macroevolution (large-scale patterns) is explained by the same microevolutionary processes extended over long timescales

Aftermath

The synthesis became the dominant paradigm of evolutionary biology, consolidated in the 1947 Princeton conference and in new journals and societies. Later developments — molecular evolution and the neutral theory (Kimura), punctuated equilibrium (Eldredge and Gould), evo-devo, and epigenetics — extended or challenged aspects of it, prompting debate over an "extended evolutionary synthesis", but its core framework remains the basis of modern evolutionary theory.
