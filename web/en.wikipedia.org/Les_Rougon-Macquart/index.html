Les Rougon-Macquart is a cycle of twenty novels by the French writer Émile Zola, published between 1871 and 1893. Subtitled "Natural and social history of a family under the Second Empire," the cycle follows the fortunes of the two branches of a single family — the legitimate Rougons and the illegitimate Macquarts — through the reign of Napoleon III, and stands as the supreme monument of literary naturalism.

Conception

Zola planned the cycle in 1868–69, modelling it loosely on Balzac's Comédie humaine but grounding it in the scientific language of heredity and environment he drew from Claude Bernard and contemporary medicine. The family's founding figure, Adélaïde Fouque, passes a hereditary nervous "lesion" to her descendants, whose lives display its forms — ambition, alcoholism, violence, genius — under the pressures of the Second Empire's money, cities, mines, railways, and shops.

The novels

The best known volumes include L'Assommoir (1877), a study of working-class alcoholism in Paris; Nana (1880); Germinal (1885), set in a coal-mining strike and often called Zola's masterpiece; La Bête humaine (1890), about a railway engine driver and murder; and Le Docteur Pascal (1893), which closes the cycle. Others — La Fortune des Rougon, La Curée, Le Ventre de Paris, Au Bonheur des Dames, L'Œuvre, La Terre, and La Débâcle — explore politics, speculation, food markets, department stores, art, peasant life, and the Franco-Prussian War.

Legacy

The cycle established naturalism internationally and remains central to the history of the French novel; many volumes are in print in modern translations, and Germinal in particular is a fixture of French national education and identity.
