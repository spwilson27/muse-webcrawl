The Willamette Valley is a 150-mile (240 km) long valley in northwestern Oregon, in the United States, flanked by mountain ranges on the east (Cascade Range), west (Oregon Coast Range) and south (Calapooya Mountains). It forms the cultural and political heart of Oregon and is home to roughly 70% of its population, including the cities of Portland, Salem — the state capital — and Eugene. The valley was the destination of most emigrants on the Oregon Trail in the 1840s.

Geography and geology

The valley floor is a broad alluvial plain drained by the Willamette River, running south to north before joining the Columbia River near Portland. Its fertile soils owe much to the Missoula Floods of the last ice age, which repeatedly scoured topsoil from eastern Washington and deposited it in the valley. Summers are warm and dry, winters mild and wet, under a Mediterranean-influenced climate ideal for agriculture.

Agriculture and wine

The valley produces grass seed, hazelnuts, berries, hops, Christmas trees and nursery stock, and is internationally known for its wine industry — particularly Pinot noir — centred in areas such as the Dundee Hills and the Yamhill-Carlton and Eola-Amity districts of the Willamette Valley AVA.

History

Before European settlement the valley was inhabited by Kalapuya and other Indigenous peoples, whose population was devastated by disease in the 1830s. Fur traders and missionaries arrived in the 1820s–30s, and mass overland migration followed the opening of the Oregon Trail; the provisional government of Oregon Country was organised at Champoeg in 1843.
