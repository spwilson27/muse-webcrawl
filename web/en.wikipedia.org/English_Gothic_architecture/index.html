English Gothic architecture

English Gothic architecture is the term for the forms of Gothic architecture that flourished in England from about 1180 until about 1520, divided by historians into three main phases: Early English, Decorated, and Perpendicular. Its defining features are the pointed arch, the ribbed vault, and the flying buttress, evolving toward ever-larger windows filled with stained glass and increasingly elaborate stone tracery.

Early English (c. 1180–1275)

Also called the Lancet style, Early English Gothic followed Romanesque (Norman) building after the rebuilding of Canterbury Cathedral's choir from 1174 under William of Sens. Salisbury Cathedral, built almost entirely in this style between 1220 and 1258, is its textbook example. Characteristics include tall narrow lancet windows, often grouped, simple rib vaults, and restrained ornament with dog-tooth moulding.

Decorated (c. 1275–1380)

The Decorated style is marked by elaborate window tracery — first geometric, then flowing curvilinear forms — as at Exeter Cathedral and the Eleanor crosses. Ballflower ornament, ogee arches, and lierne vaults with intermediate ribs appear, and buildings such as the Angel Choir at Lincoln show growing richness of carving.

Perpendicular (c. 1380–1520)

Unique to England, Perpendicular Gothic emphasises strong vertical lines and huge windows divided by mullions and transoms into grid-like panels. Gloucester Cathedral's choir was an early essay in the style. Its supreme achievements are fan vaults — as in King's College Chapel, Cambridge, and Henry VII's Chapel at Westminster Abbey — where conoid vault surfaces are carved with radiating ribs of equal length.

Later influence

After the Reformation, Gothic building for churches largely ceased until the nineteenth-century Gothic Revival, when architects such as Augustus Pugin championed English Gothic as the national Christian style, producing works like the Palace of Westminster.
