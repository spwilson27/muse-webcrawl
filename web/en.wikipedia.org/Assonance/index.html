Assonance

Assonance is a resemblance in the sounds of words or syllables either between their vowels (e.g., meat and bean) or between their consonants (e.g., keep and cape). However, assonance between consonants is generally called consonance in American usage. The two types are often combined, as between the words six and switch, in which the vowels are carried on medial consonants.

Use in poetry

Assonance is a figure of speech and a stylistic device used in poetry and prose to enhance rhythm, mood and musicality. It was a structural feature of early Romance-language verse: in the Chanson de Roland and the Spanish Cantar de mio Cid, stanzas were bound by assonant rather than fully rhyming line endings.

Examples

English poets have used assonance extensively, as in the repeated long vowels of Dylan Thomas's "Do not go gentle into that good night". Assonance also appears in proverbs and slogans, and forms the basis of vowel rhyme in many song lyrics and hip-hop verses.

Related concepts

Assonance differs from rhyme, which requires matching vowel and following consonant sounds, and from alliteration, the repetition of initial consonant sounds. Together these devices form the sound patterning of verse.
