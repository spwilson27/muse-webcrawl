Phonon

In physics, a phonon is a collective excitation in a periodic, elastic arrangement of atoms or molecules in condensed matter, specifically in solids and some liquids. A type of quasiparticle, a phonon is an excited state in the quantum mechanical quantization of the modes of vibrations for elastic structures of interacting particles. Phonons can be thought of as quantized sound waves, similar to photons as quantized light waves.

Definition

A phonon is a quantum of vibrational mechanical energy in a crystal lattice. The study of phonons is an important part of condensed matter physics. They play an important role in many physical properties of condensed matter systems, such as thermal conductivity and electrical conductivity, as well as in models of neutron scattering and related effects.

Acoustic and optical phonons

Lattices with more than one atom in the unit cell exhibit two kinds of modes. In acoustic modes, neighboring atoms move in phase, and these correspond to sound waves in the long-wavelength limit. In optical modes, adjacent atoms move out of phase; they are so named because in ionic crystals they can be excited by infrared light. Each branch has a characteristic dispersion relation relating its frequency to its wavevector.

Properties and effects

Phonons have energy E = ħω and crystal momentum ħk. As bosons, their equilibrium populations follow Bose–Einstein statistics. Heat in insulating solids is carried almost entirely by phonons; their scattering — by other phonons, impurities, and boundaries — limits thermal conductivity. Electron–phonon interactions underlie electrical resistance in metals and the pairing mechanism in conventional superconductors (BCS theory).

Measurement

Phonon spectra are measured by inelastic neutron scattering and inelastic X-ray scattering, and the concept was introduced in 1932 by the Soviet physicist Igor Tamm; the name "phonon" was coined by Yakov Frenkel, from the Greek phōnē, "sound/voice".
