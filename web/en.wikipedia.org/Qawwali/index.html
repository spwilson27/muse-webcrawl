Qawwali

Qawwali (Urdu: قوّالی) is a form of Sufi Islamic devotional singing, originating from the Indian subcontinent, performed at Sufi shrines (dargahs) and mosques, particularly in Pakistan, India, and Bangladesh. It is the musical component of sama, the Sufi practice of listening to music and poetry as a means of spiritual contemplation and ecstasy.

Origins

The form is traditionally credited to the Indo-Persian saint and musician Amir Khusrau (1253–1325), who is said to have fused Persian, Arabic, Turkic, and Indian musical traditions into qawwali and other genres. Qawwali songs are poems, often in Urdu, Persian, Hindi, or Punjabi, praising God, the Prophet Muhammad, Ali, and Sufi saints, and expressing themes of divine love and longing in the language of earthly romance.

Performance

A qawwali party typically consists of a lead singer, one or two side singers, and a chorus that repeats key lines, accompanied by harmonium, tabla, and dholak, with rhythmic handclapping. A performance may last from fifteen minutes to over an hour; it builds gradually from a slow, contemplative opening through an intense, improvisatory climax designed to induce a state of spiritual rapture (wajd) in performers and listeners. Verses are repeated and interwoven to emphasize meaning, and audience members may offer money to the singers in appreciation.

Notable performers

The most internationally famous qawwal was Nusrat Fateh Ali Khan (1948–1997) of Pakistan, whose recordings and collaborations with Western musicians brought qawwali to global audiences. Other celebrated performers include the Sabri Brothers, Aziz Mian, Abida Parveen, and the Rizwan–Muazzam duo, nephews of Nusrat. Qawwali has also entered popular culture through Bollywood films and fusion genres.
