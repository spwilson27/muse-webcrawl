Health insurance in the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Health insurance in the United States is provided through a mixture of private insurance — mostly obtained through employers — and public programmes. In the US, health insurance pays for medical expenses through premiums, deductibles, copayments, and coinsurance, and the system is noted for high costs: the United States spends roughly 17–18% of GDP on healthcare, far more per capita than other wealthy nations. In recent years, about 92% of the population has had coverage, leaving roughly 26–30 million people uninsured.

Private coverage

The largest source of coverage is employer-sponsored insurance, a system that grew out of wage controls during the Second World War and is subsidised by the exclusion of employer premium contributions from taxable income. Individuals also buy plans on the Affordable Care Act (ACA) marketplaces, with income-based premium subsidies. Plans are organised as HMOs, PPOs, EPOs, and high-deductible plans paired with health savings accounts.

Public programmes

Medicare (1965) covers people 65 and older and some younger people with disabilities, financed by payroll taxes and premiums; many beneficiaries add private Medicare Advantage or Medigap plans. Medicaid, jointly funded by federal and state governments, covers low-income people, and the ACA's Medicaid expansion was adopted by most states; the Children's Health Insurance Program (CHIP) covers children in moderate-income families. Other public systems include the Veterans Health Administration and the military's TRICARE.

Reform and issues

Major reforms include the creation of Medicare and Medicaid (1965), COBRA continuation coverage, HIPAA (1996), and the Affordable Care Act (2010), which banned pre-existing-condition exclusions, created insurance marketplaces, mandated essential health benefits, and cut the uninsured rate roughly in half. Persistent issues include medical debt and bankruptcy, surprise billing (addressed by the No Surprises Act of 2020), narrow provider networks, and administrative complexity.
