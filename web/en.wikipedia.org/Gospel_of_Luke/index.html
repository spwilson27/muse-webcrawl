The Gospel of Luke (Greek: Εὐαγγέλιον κατὰ Λουκᾶν, romanized: Euaggélion katà Loukân) is the third of the four canonical gospels and, together with the Acts of the Apostles by the same author, forms the two-volume work known as Luke–Acts. It tells of the origins, birth, ministry, death and resurrection of Jesus, framed as researched history addressed to a certain Theophilus: the longest of the four gospels, it contains a detailed infancy narrative, a genealogy tracing Jesus back to Adam, and a large central "travel narrative" of the journey to Jerusalem.

Luke is one of the three Synoptic Gospels. Scholars hold that its author used Mark's gospel and a hypothetical sayings source ("Q"), together with material unique to Luke, including some of the best-known parables: the Good Samaritan, the Prodigal Son, the Rich Man and Lazarus, and the Pharisee and the Tax Collector. The gospel is noted for its emphasis on the poor, women, Samaritans and other marginal figures, its interest in prayer and the Holy Spirit, and its portrayal of Jesus's compassion.

Authorship and dating

The text is anonymous, but tradition since the late 2nd century attributes it to Luke, a companion of Paul described in Colossians as "the beloved physician". The majority of scholars date it to about 80–90 AD, after the destruction of the Jerusalem Temple in 70 AD, which the narrative appears to presuppose. Whether the author was the historical Luke remains disputed, but the polished Greek and formal literary prologue mark the writer as an educated Hellenistic author writing for a largely Gentile audience.

Structure

After the prologue (1:1–4) come parallel infancy accounts of John the Baptist and Jesus (chapters 1–2), the ministry in Galilee (3–9), the long journey to Jerusalem (9:51–19:27), and the passion, death and resurrection in Jerusalem (chapters 19–24), ending with the ascension and a promise fulfilled in Acts.
