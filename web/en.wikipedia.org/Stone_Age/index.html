Stone Age

The Stone Age was a broad prehistoric period during which stone was widely used to make implements with an edge, a point, or a percussion surface. The period lasted for roughly 3.4 million years and ended between 4,000 BC and 2,000 BC, with the advent of metalworking. In some regions, such as Sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia, less technologically developed societies were still characterised as living in a Stone Age culture as late as the 19th century AD.

Stone Age artefacts include tools used by modern humans, by their predecessor species in the genus Homo, and possibly by the earlier partly contemporaneous genera Australopithecus and Paranthropus. Bone tools were also used during this period, but are less frequently preserved in the archaeological record. The Stone Age is further divided by the types of stone tools in use.

Subdivisions

Paleolithic

The Paleolithic ("Old Stone Age") covers the greatest portion of the Stone Age, from the earliest known use of stone tools by hominins c. 3.3 million years ago to the end of the Pleistocene c. 11,650 BP. It is divided into the Lower, Middle and Upper Paleolithic. Characteristic developments include the Oldowan and Acheulean tool industries, the control of fire, and, in the Upper Paleolithic, cave art, ornaments and increasingly sophisticated blade technologies.

Mesolithic

The Mesolithic ("Middle Stone Age") is the period between the end of the last glacial period and the adoption of agriculture, whose dates vary by region. Mesolithic hunter-gatherers produced microliths — small stone blades hafted into composite tools — and adapted to post-glacial forests and coastlines.

Neolithic

The Neolithic ("New Stone Age") began with the Neolithic Revolution, the independent emergence of agriculture in several regions beginning around 10,000 BC in the Fertile Crescent. Neolithic societies domesticated plants and animals, settled in permanent villages, ground and polished stone tools, and produced pottery. Monumental architecture of the period includes sites such as Stonehenge and Göbekli Tepe.

End of the Stone Age

The Stone Age ended at different times in different places, as copper and then bronze metallurgy spread. The three-age system (Stone, Bronze, Iron), proposed by the Danish antiquarian Christian Jürgensen Thomsen in the 1830s, remains a convenient framework, although the transition was gradual everywhere and some societies adopted ironworking without a substantial bronze phase.
