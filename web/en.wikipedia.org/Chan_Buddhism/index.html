Chan Buddhism

Chan (traditional Chinese: 禪; simplified Chinese: 禅; pinyin: Chán), from Sanskrit dhyāna ("meditation"), is a Chinese school of Mahāyāna Buddhism that developed in China from the 6th century CE onwards, becoming the dominant monastic form of Buddhism in China and influencing surrounding regions. Chan is known in Japan as Zen, in Korea as Seon, and in Vietnam as Thiền. The tradition emphasises direct insight into one's true nature (seeing one's nature, jianxing) through meditation (zazen/zuochan), the guidance of a master, and — in the Linji (Rinzai) school — the use of koans (gōng'àn), paradoxical questions or anecdotes intended to exhaust conceptual thought.

History

Tradition traces Chan to the Buddha's "flower sermon", in which he silently held up a flower and Mahākāśyapa smiled — a transmission "outside the scriptures" passed mind-to-mind. The Indian monk Bodhidharma legendarily brought the teaching to China around the 5th–6th century, meditating nine years facing a wall at Shaolin Monastery. The line of patriarchs culminated in Huineng (638–713), the Sixth Patriarch, whose Platform Sutra championed "sudden enlightenment" against the "gradual" teaching of his rival Shenxiu — a polemic shaping Chinese Chan identity.

In the Tang and Song dynasties Chan flourished: iconoclastic masters such as Mazu Daoyi and Linji Yixuan developed encounter dialogue, shouts and blows; monastic codes (attributed to Baizhang) organised communal work — "a day without work, a day without food". Recorded sayings (yulu) and koan collections such as the Blue Cliff Record (1125) and the Gateless Barrier (1228) became its classic literature. Of the "five houses" of Chan, only Linji and Caodong survived as major lineages, transmitting to Japan (as Rinzai and Sōtō Zen, notably by Eisai and Dōgen), Korea and Vietnam.

Teachings and practice

Chan teaches that Buddhahood is inherent: one's own mind is Buddha, obscured by delusion. Practices include seated meditation; in Caodong/Sōtō, "silent illumination" (just sitting, shikantaza); in Linji/Rinzai, intensive koan introspection (kanhua Chan, "Chan of contemplating the phrase"), often in retreats (sesshin) with interviews (sanzen/dokusan) with the master. Monastic life regulates labour, liturgy and meditation together. Chan profoundly influenced Chinese and Japanese arts — ink painting, calligraphy, poetry, tea ceremony and garden design.

Chan in the modern world

Twentieth-century reformers such as Taixu and Xuyun revived Chan in China; masters including Hsuan Hua, Sheng Yen and Thich Nhat Hanh (in the Vietnamese Thiền tradition) carried it West, where Zen became widely known through writers such as D. T. Suzuki and Alan Watts, and mindfulness movements adapted its meditation practices.
