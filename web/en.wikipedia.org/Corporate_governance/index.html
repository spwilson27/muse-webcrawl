Corporate governance

Corporate governance is the set of processes, customs, policies, laws, and institutions affecting the way a corporation is directed, administered, or controlled. It defines the relationships among the many stakeholders involved — principally shareholders, the board of directors, and management — and the goals for which the corporation is governed.

Principles

Widely cited principles, such as those of the OECD, include the rights of shareholders, equitable treatment of shareholders, the role of stakeholders, disclosure and transparency, and the responsibilities of the board. A central concern is the principal–agent problem: managers, as agents of the shareholders, may pursue their own interests unless monitored and incentivised appropriately.

Models

Anglo-American systems typically have a single board with a mix of executive and independent non-executive directors and dispersed share ownership. Continental European systems often feature two-tier boards, with a supervisory board overseeing a management board, and greater roles for banks and employees; Germany's co-determination laws give workers seats on supervisory boards. Japanese keiretsu structures historically emphasised cross-shareholdings and main banks.

Regulation and reform

Major scandals have repeatedly driven reform: the Cadbury Report (1992) in the UK, the Sarbanes–Oxley Act (2002) in the United States after Enron and WorldCom, and successive UK Corporate Governance Codes. Topics of continuing debate include executive pay, board diversity, shareholder activism, and the purpose of the corporation beyond shareholder value.
