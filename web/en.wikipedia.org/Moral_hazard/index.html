Moral hazard

Moral hazard
FieldEconomics, insurance, finance
Related conceptsAdverse selection, principal–agent problem, information asymmetry

Moral hazard is a situation in which one party to a transaction has an incentive to take unusual risks because the costs that could result will not be borne by the party taking the risk. In other words, it is a tendency for a party, insulated from risk, to behave differently from how it would behave if it were fully exposed to the risk. Moral hazard arises when a party does not bear the full consequences of its actions, and therefore has a tendency to act less carefully, leaving another party to bear some responsibility for the consequences of those actions.

The concept originated in the insurance industry, where insurers observed that protecting policyholders against loss encouraged riskier behaviour. It has since been applied widely in economics, finance, and political science.

History

The term was used by insurers in England in the 17th and 18th centuries, initially with a strongly moralising connotation implying fraud or dishonesty on the part of the insured. By the mid-20th century, economists such as Kenneth Arrow formalised the concept as a consequence of asymmetric information: after a contract is signed, the insured party may change its behaviour in ways the insurer cannot easily observe or control.

In insurance

In insurance markets, moral hazard occurs when the behaviour of the insured changes in a way that raises the cost to the insurer after the insurance has been purchased. For example, a person with comprehensive car insurance may drive less carefully, or a homeowner with fire insurance may invest less in fire prevention. Insurers counter moral hazard with deductibles, co-payments, and coinsurance, which leave part of the loss with the policyholder and thus preserve an incentive for care.

In finance

In financial markets, moral hazard is often invoked in discussions of bailouts and institutions considered "too big to fail." If banks expect to be rescued by governments in a crisis, they may take greater risks in lending and trading, since profits remain private while extreme losses are socialised. The concept was widely discussed after the 2007–2008 financial crisis in debates over deposit insurance, lender-of-last-resort facilities, and executive compensation.

Principal–agent problem

Moral hazard is closely related to the principal–agent problem, in which an agent (such as an employee, manager, or contractor) acts on behalf of a principal but has interests that diverge from the principal's, and the principal cannot perfectly monitor the agent's effort. Economists distinguish moral hazard (hidden action after contracting) from adverse selection (hidden information before contracting).
