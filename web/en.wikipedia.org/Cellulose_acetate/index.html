Cellulose acetate

In biochemistry, cellulose acetate refers to any acetate ester of cellulose, usually cellulose diacetate. It was first prepared in 1865. A bioplastic, cellulose acetate is used as a film base in photography, as a component in some coatings, and as a frame material for eyeglasses; it is also used as a synthetic fiber in the manufacture of cigarette filters and playing cards. In photographic film, cellulose acetate replaced nitrate film in the 1950s, being far less flammable and cheaper to produce.

History

Paul Schützenberger first prepared cellulose acetate in 1865 by reacting cotton with acetic anhydride. Commercial production began in the early twentieth century; the Swiss brothers Camille and Henri Dreyfus developed industrial processes and produced acetate fibres (marketed as Celanese) and film. During the First World War acetate "dope" was used to coat aircraft fabric.

Production

Cellulose from wood pulp or cotton linters is treated with acetic acid and acetic anhydride in the presence of a sulfuric acid catalyst, giving cellulose triacetate, which is partially hydrolysed to the desired degree of substitution. Diacetate has a degree of substitution around 2.4–2.5.

Uses

Cellulose acetate is used for photographic film ("safety film"), cigarette filters (the largest single use), textile fibres, spectacle frames, tool handles, and as a membrane material. It is biodegradable, though degradation of archival film by "vinegar syndrome" (release of acetic acid) is a conservation problem.
