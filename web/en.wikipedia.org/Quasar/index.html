A quasar (from quasi-stellar radio source) is an extremely luminous active galactic nucleus (AGN), in which a supermassive black hole with mass ranging from millions to tens of billions of solar masses is surrounded by a gaseous accretion disk. Gas in the disk falling toward the black hole heats up because of friction and releases enormous amounts of energy in the form of electromagnetic radiation, making quasars among the most luminous, powerful and energetic objects known in the universe. Many emit relativistic jets, and the brightest outshine their entire host galaxies.

Discovery and identification

Quasars were first identified in the 1950s as radio sources resembling stars. In 1963 Maarten Schmidt recognised that the spectrum of 3C 273 showed hydrogen lines redshifted by 15.8 per cent, proving it lay at cosmological distance and was extraordinarily luminous. Subsequent surveys found quasars most numerous when the universe was about 2–3 billion years old; the most distant known lie at redshifts above 7, seen as they were less than a billion years after the Big Bang.

Properties

Quasar spectra show broad emission lines from fast-moving gas near the black hole, and their light varies on timescales of days, implying emitting regions only light-days across. Under the unified model of AGN, quasars, blazars and radio galaxies are the same phenomenon viewed at different angles and accretion rates. Energy from quasar activity ("feedback") is thought to regulate star formation and the growth of galaxies.
