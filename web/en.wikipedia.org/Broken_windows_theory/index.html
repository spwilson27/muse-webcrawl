Broken windows theory

Visible signs of disorder are said to invite further crime

The broken windows theory is a criminological theory that states that visible signs of crime, antisocial behaviour, and civil disorder — graffiti, vandalism, litter, unrepaired broken windows — create an urban environment that encourages further and more serious crime. It was introduced in a 1982 Atlantic Monthly article by social scientists James Q. Wilson and George L. Kelling.

Theory

The name comes from the article's example: if a window in a building is broken and left unrepaired, passers-by conclude that no one cares, and more windows will be broken. Disorder, the theory holds, signals the absence of informal social control, frightening law-abiding residents away and lowering a neighbourhood's capacity to police itself. Philip Zimbardo's 1969 abandoned-car experiments were cited as early evidence: a car left in the Bronx was stripped within hours, while one in Palo Alto remained untouched until Zimbardo himself smashed it, after which others joined in.

Policing influence

The theory underpinned "order-maintenance" or "quality-of-life" policing, most famously in 1990s New York City under Mayor Rudolph Giuliani and police commissioner William Bratton, whose crackdowns on fare evasion, graffiti, and aggressive panhandling coincided with dramatic crime declines. Similar strategies spread internationally.

Criticism

Criminologists debate causality: crime fell across the United States, including in cities that did not adopt such policing, and explanations such as demographics, the waning crack epidemic, and economic change compete with the disorder thesis. Critics also charge that aggressive misdemeanour enforcement damages police-community relations and disproportionately targets minorities and the homeless, while supporters argue that residents of high-crime neighbourhoods most demand order.
