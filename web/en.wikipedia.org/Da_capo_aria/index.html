Da capo aria

The da capo aria follows an ABA ternary plan: an opening section, a contrasting middle, and a repeat of the opening.

The da capo aria (Italian pronunciation: [da ˈkaːpo]) is an aria in ternary form used in Baroque opera and cantata. Da capo means "from the head" (i.e., from the beginning): after a contrasting middle section (B), the opening section (A) is repeated from the start, conventionally marked "D.C." in the score. The form is usually written out as A only, with the instruction that the singer return to the top after the middle section, giving an ABA structure overall.

Characteristics

The two A sections typically set the same text and express a single dominant affect, while the B section provides contrast in text, key, mood, or scoring. Because the repeat of A was rarely sung exactly as written, it became the singer's principal opportunity for improvised ornamentation and virtuoso display — a convention that made the da capo aria the centrepiece of opera seria and a showcase for star singers such as the castrati Senesino and Farinelli.

History

The form emerged in Italian opera of the late 17th century and dominated serious opera throughout the Baroque era, in works by Alessandro Scarlatti, Handel, Hasse, and Vivaldi. George Frideric Handel's operas and oratorios contain celebrated examples, such as "Lascia ch'io pianga" from Rinaldo. With the reforms of Gluck and the rise of through-composed forms in the Classical era, the da capo aria gradually fell out of use, though its ABA principle survived in countless later songs and instrumental movements.
