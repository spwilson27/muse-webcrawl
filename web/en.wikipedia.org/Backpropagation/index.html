Backpropagation

Backpropagation, short for "backward propagation of errors," is an algorithm used to train artificial neural networks. It computes the gradient of a loss function with respect to the network's weights by applying the chain rule of calculus, propagating the error from the output layer backward through the hidden layers. Combined with an optimization method such as gradient descent, backpropagation efficiently adjusts the weights to reduce prediction error.

History

The underlying mathematics derives from earlier work on automatic differentiation and control theory. The method was popularised for neural networks by David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton, and Ronald Williams in a widely cited 1986 paper, "Learning representations by back-propagating errors," which demonstrated that networks trained this way could learn useful internal representations. Backpropagation became the workhorse algorithm of the deep learning revolution that followed in the 2010s.

Method

Training proceeds in two phases. In the forward pass, inputs are propagated through the network to produce outputs, and the loss is computed. In the backward pass, partial derivatives of the loss with respect to each weight are computed layer by layer, reusing intermediate results through the chain rule. Weights are then updated in the direction that decreases the loss, scaled by a learning rate. The algorithm requires the activation functions to be differentiable (or approximately so, as with ReLU).
