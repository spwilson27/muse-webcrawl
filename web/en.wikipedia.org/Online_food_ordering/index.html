Online food ordering

Online food ordering is the process of ordering food from a local restaurant or food cooperative through a web page or app. Much like ordering consumer goods online, many of these services allow customers to keep accounts with them in order to make frequent ordering convenient. A customer will search for a favourite restaurant, usually filtered via type of cuisine, and choose from available items; the order is then delivered to the customer's home or office, or made ready for takeaway pickup.

History

The first online food order is often credited to Pizza Hut's "PizzaNet" service in 1994, which allowed customers in Santa Cruz, California to order via the early web. Dedicated platforms followed in the late 1990s and 2000s: Waiter.com, Grubhub (founded 2004), Just Eat (founded in Denmark in 2001), and Delivery Hero in Germany. The smartphone era brought app-based, venture-funded platforms with their own courier fleets — including DoorDash, Uber Eats, Deliveroo, Swiggy, Zomato, and Meituan — transforming food delivery from a niche service offered mainly by pizza and Chinese restaurants into a general logistics industry. The COVID-19 pandemic greatly accelerated adoption.

Types of services

  • Restaurant-controlled ordering, where the customer orders directly from the restaurant's own website or app and the restaurant handles delivery or pickup.
  • Aggregator/marketplace platforms, which list many restaurants; some merely forward orders, while others also provide the delivery couriers.
  • Ghost kitchens (cloud kitchens), delivery-only cooking facilities that serve app-based brands without a dining room.
  • Grocery and convenience rapid-delivery services that use similar courier networks.

Business model and controversies

Third-party platforms typically charge restaurants commissions that can range from roughly 15% to 30% of the order value, in addition to delivery and service fees paid by customers. This model has been controversial: critics argue commissions squeeze thin restaurant margins, while platforms point to the customers and logistics they provide. The classifications and pay of gig-economy couriers, the effect of "dark" delivery-only kitchens on neighbourhoods, and municipal fee caps have all been subjects of public debate and regulation.
