Semantic theory of truth


Alfred Tarski developed the semantic conception of truth in the 1930s.

A semantic theory of truth is a theory of truth in the philosophy of language which holds that truth is a property of sentences. The semantic conception of truth, most famously formulated by the Polish logician Alfred Tarski, seeks to give a formally precise definition of the truth predicate for a given formal language while avoiding paradoxes such as the liar paradox.

Tarski's theory

Tarski presented his theory in the 1933 paper "The Concept of Truth in Formalized Languages" (published in Polish, with an English translation appearing in 1956). He required that any adequate definition of truth satisfy Convention T: the definition must entail, for every sentence s of the object language, a biconditional of the form:

T(s) if and only if p,

where T is the truth predicate, s is a structural-descriptive name of a sentence, and p is a translation of that sentence into the metalanguage. The classic example is: "Snow is white" is true if and only if snow is white.

Object language and metalanguage

A central feature of Tarski's approach is the distinction between the object language, the language for which truth is being defined, and the metalanguage, the language in which the definition is given. Because a sufficiently rich language that contains its own truth predicate gives rise to semantic paradoxes, Tarski held that the truth predicate for a language must be defined in an essentially richer metalanguage. For this reason, he considered the theory inapplicable to natural languages, which he regarded as "semantically closed".

Influence

Tarski's work became a foundation for model theory and influenced philosophers such as Donald Davidson, who adapted the semantic conception into a theory of meaning for natural languages, and Karl Popper, who saw it as rehabilitating the correspondence theory of truth. Critics, including later deflationists, have debated whether Tarski's definition constitutes a substantive correspondence theory or merely a formal device.
