In mathematics, two quantities are in the golden ratio if their ratio is the same as the ratio of their sum to the larger of the two quantities. Expressed algebraically, for quantities a > b > 0, (a + b)/a = a/b = φ, where the Greek letter phi (φ) represents the golden ratio. It is an irrational number with value φ = (1 + √5)/2 ≈ 1.6180339887.

The golden ratio has been called the "golden number", "divine proportion", "golden section", and "extreme and mean ratio". It appears in the geometry of pentagons and pentagrams, in the Fibonacci sequence (whose consecutive ratios converge to φ), and in the phyllotaxis of plants. Since the Renaissance, many artists and architects have been said to proportion their works by it — claims that are debated by scholars.

Mathematics

The golden ratio is the positive solution of the quadratic equation x² = x + 1. Its continued fraction is all ones, making it in a precise sense the most difficult irrational number to approximate by rationals. Powers of φ satisfy φⁿ = F₉φ + F₉₋₁, linking it to the Fibonacci numbers. A golden rectangle, whose sides are in the ratio φ, can be dissected into a square and a smaller rectangle similar to itself, yielding the familiar logarithmic spiral construction.

History

The ratio was first defined and studied by Euclid in the Elements (c. 300 BC) as the "extreme and mean ratio". Luca Pacioli's De divina proportione (1509), illustrated by Leonardo da Vinci, celebrated its theological significance, and Johannes Kepler described it as one of geometry's two great treasures. The letter φ was assigned in the 20th century, in honour of the sculptor Phidias.
