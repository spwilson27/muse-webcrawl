Hubble's law, also known as the Hubble–Lemaître law, is the observation in physical cosmology that galaxies are moving away from Earth at speeds proportional to their distance. In other words, the farther a galaxy is, the faster it recedes, as expressed by v = H0D, where H0 is the Hubble constant.

History

Georges Lemaître derived the relationship theoretically in 1927 and estimated its constant from available data. Edwin Hubble published confirming observational evidence in 1929, based on redshift measurements largely made by Vesto Slipher and distances estimated with the help of Henrietta Swan Leavitt's period–luminosity relation. In 2018 the International Astronomical Union voted to recommend the name Hubble–Lemaître law in recognition of both contributions.

Interpretation

The law is interpreted as evidence that space itself is expanding, carrying galaxies apart — the foundation of Big Bang cosmology. The inverse of the Hubble constant gives an estimate of the age of the universe. Precise measurement of H0 remains an active problem: values derived from the cosmic microwave background and from local distance ladders disagree at a level known as the "Hubble tension".
