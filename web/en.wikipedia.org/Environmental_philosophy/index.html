Environmental philosophy

Environmental philosophy is a branch of philosophy that is concerned with the natural environment and humanity's place within it. It asks crucial questions about human environmental relations, such as "What do we mean when we talk about nature?", "What is the value of the natural, non-human environment to us, or in itself?", and "How should we respond to environmental challenges such as environmental degradation, pollution, and climate change?"

Wilderness areas figure prominently in debates about the intrinsic value of nature

Scope

Environmental philosophy includes environmental ethics, environmental aesthetics, ecofeminism, environmental hermeneutics, and environmental theology. A central debate concerns whether nature has only instrumental value — value as a means to human ends — or also intrinsic value independent of human use. Thinkers such as Aldo Leopold, whose "land ethic" appeared in A Sand County Almanac (1949), argued that humans should see themselves as plain members of a biotic community.

Key movements

The Norwegian philosopher Arne Næss coined the distinction between "shallow" and "deep" ecology in 1973; deep ecology advocates a fundamental reorientation of human values toward the flourishing of all life. Other influential approaches include social ecology, associated with Murray Bookchin, and ecofeminism, which links the domination of nature with the domination of women.

Contemporary issues

Recent environmental philosophy grapples with the Anthropocene, climate ethics, the moral status of animals and ecosystems, geoengineering, and obligations to future generations.
