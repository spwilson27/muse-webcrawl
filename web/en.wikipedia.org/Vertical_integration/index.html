Vertical integration

Vertical integration is a business strategy in which a company owns or controls multiple stages of the production and distribution process for its products — for example, a manufacturer acquiring its suppliers of raw materials, or a retailer acquiring its wholesalers. It contrasts with horizontal integration, in which a company expands within the same stage of production.

Types

Backward integration occurs when a firm takes control of earlier stages of its supply chain, such as a carmaker buying a steel plant. Forward integration occurs when a firm controls later stages, such as a manufacturer opening its own retail stores. A firm practising both is said to have balanced integration. Integration may be achieved through merger and acquisition, or by building new capacity internally.

Rationale and history

Firms integrate vertically to secure supplies, reduce transaction costs, capture margins otherwise lost to intermediaries, control quality, and raise barriers to competitors. The great exemplars of the strategy were the American industrial firms of the late 19th and early 20th centuries: Andrew Carnegie's steel company controlled ore mines, railroads, and ships as well as mills, and Henry Ford's River Rouge complex famously turned raw materials into finished cars under one organisation. Economist Ronald Coase's theory of the firm (1937) explained integration as a response to the costs of using markets.

Criticism and regulation

Vertical integration can reduce competition by foreclosing rivals from supplies or outlets, and it has accordingly drawn antitrust scrutiny — for example, the 1948 United States v. Paramount Pictures decision that separated Hollywood studios from their theatre chains. Critics also note that integrated firms may become bureaucratic and lose the flexibility of market contracting, a concern that drove widespread outsourcing from the 1980s onward. Modern technology companies such as Apple, which designs its own chips, and Tesla, which manufactures its own batteries, are frequently cited contemporary examples.
