Theraphosidae is a family of large, hairy spiders commonly known as tarantulas. The family comprises roughly 1,000 described species in more than 150 genera, found principally in tropical and subtropical regions of the Americas, Africa, Asia and Australia, with a few species in southern Europe. Like most spiders they are predators, feeding largely on insects and other arthropods, though the largest species may take small vertebrates.

Characteristics

Tarantulas range in size from a few centimetres to the goliath birdeater (Theraphosa blondi), whose leg span can approach 30 cm. Their bodies are densely covered in hairs; many New World species bear urticating hairs on the abdomen that can be flicked at predators as a defence. Their venom is adapted for subduing invertebrate prey and, while bites may be painful, none is considered medically dangerous to humans.

Behaviour and life history

Most theraphosids live in burrows or silk-lined retreats and hunt by ambush. Females are long-lived, sometimes surviving 20 to 30 years in captivity, whereas mature males typically live only a few more months after their final moult. Many species are popular in the exotic pet trade, which has raised conservation concerns for some heavily collected populations.

Taxonomy

The family name derives from the Greek thera (wild beast) and phos (light). Theraphosids belong to the infraorder Mygalomorphae, the group of spiders with downward-striking, parallel fangs. Notable genera include Grammostola, Brachypelma, Avicularia and Poecilotheria.
