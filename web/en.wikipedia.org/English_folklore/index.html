English folklore

English folklore consists of the myths and legends of England, including the region's characteristic folkloric figures and traditions, such as Robin Hood and King Arthur. It encompasses the traditional beliefs, customs and stories of the English people, passed through the generations by word of mouth, and now partly recorded in writing.

Figures and creatures

Recurring figures include Robin Hood, the outlaw of Sherwood Forest; King Arthur and his knights; Herne the Hunter of Windsor Forest; and the giants Gog and Magog, guardians of London. Supernatural creatures include fairies, brownies, boggarts, black dogs such as Black Shuck of East Anglia, dragons (the Lambton Worm, the Sockburn Worm), and ghosts attached to countless houses and battlefields.

Customs and calendar

The ritual year features Morris dancing, maypole dancing on May Day, mummers' plays at Christmas, well dressing in Derbyshire, cheese-rolling at Cooper's Hill in Gloucestershire, the Abbots Bromley Horn Dance, and Guy Fawkes Night on 5 November. Many customs blend pre-Christian seasonal festivals with the Christian calendar of saints' days and church ales.

Roy Plomer and regional traditions

Folklore varies strongly by county: Cornish piskies and knockers, Lancashire boggarts, and the Norse-tinged customs of the Danelaw regions. London traditions include the Pearly Kings and Queens and the ceremony of Beating the Bounds.

Study and preservation

The systematic study of folklore began with William John Thoms, who coined the word "folk-lore" in 1846. The Folklore Society was founded in 1878. Collectors such as Sabine Baring-Gould and, for folk song, Cecil Sharp — whose work sparked the English folk-song revival — recorded customs and tales that industrialisation threatened to erase.
