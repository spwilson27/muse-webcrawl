Accessibility

Accessibility
FocusDesign of products, devices, services and environments for people with disabilities
Key conceptsUniversal design, assistive technology, reasonable accommodation
Major lawsAmericans with Disabilities Act (1990), Section 508, Equality Act 2010, EN 301 549
Web standardWeb Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG)

Accessibility is the design of products, devices, services, vehicles, or environments so as to be usable by people with disabilities. The concept focuses on enabling access for people with a disability, or special needs, or enabling access through the use of assistive technology; however, accessible design research activities do not necessarily address suitable requirements for specific disorders.

Accessibility is not to be confused with usability, which is the extent to which a product (such as a device, service, or environment) can be used by specified users to achieve specified goals with effectiveness, efficiency, comfort and satisfaction.

History

The modern accessibility movement grew out of the broader disability rights movement of the mid-20th century. In the United States, returning veterans of the Second World War and the Korean War pressed for barrier-free environments; the first American national standard for barrier-free design, ANSI A117.1, was published in 1961. The Rehabilitation Act of 1973, the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 (ADA), and the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006) established legal frameworks requiring accessible public spaces, employment, transport, and digital services.

Physical accessibility

Physical accessibility concerns the built environment: step-free entrances, ramps, lifts, accessible toilets, tactile paving, audible signals at crossings, and adequate door widths and turning circles for wheelchair users. Standards such as ISO 21542 and national building codes specify dimensions and requirements.

Digital accessibility

Digital accessibility refers to the ability of websites, mobile applications, and electronic documents to be navigated and understood by all users, including those who use screen readers, screen magnifiers, voice recognition, or alternative input devices. The World Wide Web Consortium's Web Accessibility Initiative publishes the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG), organised around four principles: content must be perceivable, operable, understandable, and robust (POUR).

Transport

Accessible transport includes low-floor buses, level boarding at railway stations, audio-visual next-stop announcements, and paratransit services. Many metro systems built before accessibility legislation have been retrofitted with lifts, though coverage remains incomplete in many cities.
