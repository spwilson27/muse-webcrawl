Continuity of Operations

Continuity of Operations (COOP) is a United States federal government initiative, required by Presidential directive, to ensure that agencies are able to continue performance of essential functions under a broad range of circumstances, including natural disasters, accidents, technological emergencies and military or terrorist attacks. Each federal agency must maintain a COOP plan identifying its essential functions, orders of succession, delegations of authority, alternate facilities, interoperable communications and vital records.

Origins

Continuity planning grew out of Cold War-era continuity of government programs designed to preserve constitutional government in the event of nuclear war. Modern COOP planning, formalized through a series of Federal Preparedness Circulars and Presidential directives (including Federal Continuity Directive 1), broadened the focus to all hazards. COOP plans are required to be capable of activation within 12 hours and to sustain essential operations for up to 30 days.

Elements

Standard COOP elements include: identification of mission-essential functions; lines of succession for key leadership positions; pre-delegated authorities; primary and alternate operating facilities, sometimes including devolution sites where operations transfer to a different region; redundant communications; protection and accessibility of vital records and databases; and regular testing, training and exercises. State and local governments and private-sector organizations maintain analogous business continuity plans.
