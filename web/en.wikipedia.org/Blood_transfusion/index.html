Blood transfusion

Blood transfusion is the process of transferring blood products into a person's circulation intravenously. Transfusions are used for various medical conditions to replace lost components of the blood. Early transfusions used whole blood, but modern medical practice commonly uses only components of the blood, such as red blood cells, plasma, platelets, and cryoprecipitate.

History

Early attempts included animal-to-human transfusions by Richard Lower and Jean-Baptiste Denys in the 1660s, which were banned after fatal reactions. James Blundell performed the first successful human-to-human transfusion in 1818 for postpartum haemorrhage. Karl Landsteiner's discovery of the ABO blood groups in 1901, and the development of citrate anticoagulants and blood banking in the 1930s–40s, made transfusion routine and comparatively safe.

Compatibility

Red cell transfusions require ABO and RhD compatibility between donor and recipient; group O negative blood is the universal red-cell donor and AB positive the universal recipient. Before transfusion, the recipient's blood is typed and crossmatched against the donor unit to detect antibodies.

Risks

Adverse events include allergic and febrile reactions, acute haemolytic reactions from ABO incompatibility, transfusion-associated circulatory overload (TACO), transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI), and a small residual risk of viral infection. Screening of donated blood has made infection extremely rare in developed countries.
