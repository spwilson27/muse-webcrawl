Antifreeze protein

Antifreeze proteins (AFPs), also called ice-binding proteins, are a class of polypeptides produced by certain animals, plants, fungi, and bacteria that permit their survival in subzero environments. AFPs bind to small ice crystals to inhibit the growth and recrystallization of ice that would otherwise be fatal.

Mechanism

Unlike conventional antifreezes such as ethylene glycol, AFPs do not lower the freezing point of water in proportion to their concentration. Instead, they act by an adsorption–inhibition mechanism: the proteins bind to specific faces of ice crystals, forcing the crystal to grow in curved fronts between the bound proteins, which lowers the local freezing point in a non-colligative effect known as thermal hysteresis.

Distribution and evolution

AFPs were first discovered in Antarctic notothenioid fishes in the late 1960s by Arthur DeVries. Structurally unrelated AFPs have since been found in winter flounder, insects such as the spruce budworm and mealworm beetle, plants such as winter rye, and various fungi and bacteria — a striking case of convergent evolution. Some fish AFP genes appear to have evolved from a pancreatic trypsinogen gene, and lateral gene transfer has been proposed for their appearance in herring.

Applications

Potential uses include improving freeze tolerance in crops and aquaculture, cryopreservation of tissues and organs, and controlling texture and recrystallization in frozen foods such as ice cream.
