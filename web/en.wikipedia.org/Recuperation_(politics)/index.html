Recuperation (politics)

Recuperation turns the symbols of rebellion into commodities, stripping them of subversive force.

Recuperation, in the Situationist vocabulary taken up by later radical and cultural theory, is the process by which the dominant order absorbs, defuses and commodifies subversive ideas, symbols and movements, turning them into harmless or profitable products of the system they opposed. The concept was developed by Guy Debord and the Situationist International in the 1960s, in works such as The Society of the Spectacle (1967). Its opposite is détournement, the hijacking of mainstream material for subversive ends.

Concept

For Debord, the "spectacle" — the social organisation of appearances under capitalism — maintains itself partly by incorporating its own critique. Revolutionary slogans become advertising copy; the imagery of revolt sells clothing and music; former radicals become media commentators and managers. Once recuperated, an idea survives only as a commodity or a style, separated from the practice that made it dangerous. The Situationists regarded the recuperation of their own slogans after the May 1968 events in France as evidence for the theory.

Examples

Commonly cited examples include the mass marketing of Che Guevara's image on merchandise; the absorption of punk style into high fashion; the use of countercultural themes of rebellion and authenticity in automobile and technology advertising; and the re-branding of corporate products with the language of environmental or social protest ("greenwashing"). Later theorists have applied the term to the institutionalisation of avant-garde art, the commodification of formerly oppositional music scenes, and corporate diversity and wellness programmes derived from earlier critiques of work.

Criticism

Critics argue that recuperation theory can be unfalsifiable — any mainstream success of a radical idea can be dismissed as co-optation — and that it underestimates genuine change, since movements also win real concessions. Others respond that the concept does not deny such gains but draws attention to the difference between a movement's symbols being celebrated and its aims being realised.
