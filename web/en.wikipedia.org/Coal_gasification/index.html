Coal gasification

Coal gasification is the process of converting coal into a combustible gas mixture, principally carbon monoxide and hydrogen, known as synthesis gas or syngas. The coal is reacted at high temperature with a controlled amount of oxygen and steam rather than being burned completely, so the product retains most of the coal's chemical energy in gaseous form.

Process

In a gasifier, coal undergoes a sequence of reactions: drying and pyrolysis release volatile matter, combustion of part of the coal supplies heat, and reactions of the remaining char with steam and carbon dioxide produce carbon monoxide and hydrogen. Modern gasifiers operate at temperatures high enough to melt the mineral ash into slag. The raw syngas is cleaned of particulates, sulfur compounds, and other impurities before use.

Applications

Syngas can be burned in gas turbines in integrated gasification combined cycle (IGCC) power plants, shifted to produce additional hydrogen, or converted into liquid fuels and chemicals through processes such as Fischer–Tropsch synthesis and methanol production. Historically, gasification of coal in town gas works supplied lighting and heating gas to cities in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and South Africa's Sasol plants long produced liquid fuels from coal at large scale.

Environmental considerations

Gasification allows pollutants such as sulfur and mercury to be removed from a concentrated gas stream before combustion, and it produces a concentrated carbon dioxide stream that is in principle easier to capture and store than flue gas from conventional plants. Nonetheless, coal-derived fuels remain carbon-intensive, and the cost of gasification plants has limited their deployment.
