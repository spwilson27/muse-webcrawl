Neurokinin 1 receptor

The NK1 receptor is a seven-transmembrane G protein-coupled receptor.

The neurokinin 1 receptor (NK1 receptor, substance P receptor, or TACR1) is a G protein-coupled receptor found in the central and peripheral nervous systems. It is the principal receptor for the tachykinin neuropeptide substance P, for which it has the highest affinity among the tachykinin receptors NK1, NK2, and NK3. The receptor consists of 407 amino acid residues and couples primarily to Gq/11 proteins, stimulating phospholipase C and calcium mobilization.

Function

Substance P and the NK1 receptor are widely distributed in the brain, regulating emotional behaviour, stress responses, pain transmission, and the vomiting reflex. In the periphery, NK1 activation mediates neurogenic inflammation, vasodilation, plasma extravasation, and smooth muscle contraction. The receptor is expressed in regions including the striatum, amygdala, periaqueductal gray, and the area postrema and nucleus tractus solitarius of the brainstem.

Clinical significance

NK1 receptor antagonists — the "-pitant" drugs — are established antiemetics. Aprepitant, approved in 2003, and its intravenous prodrug fosaprepitant are used for the prevention of chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting, often in combination with 5-HT3 antagonists and dexamethasone; related drugs include netupitant and rolapitant. Although NK1 antagonists were investigated intensively as antidepressants, analgesics, and anxiolytics, these indications largely failed in clinical trials.
