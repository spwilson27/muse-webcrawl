Tribal art

Tribal art is visual art and material culture produced by the indigenous peoples of Africa, Oceania, Asia, and the Americas. The term, also historically rendered as "primitive art", is traditionally applied to the sculpture, masks, textiles, and ritual objects of non-Western, often pre-literate societies, and the objects were usually made for functional, religious, or ceremonial purposes within their cultures of origin rather than as art for art's sake.

Character and scope

Tribal art encompasses the famous mask traditions of West and Central Africa, the power figures (nkisi) of the Kongo, Benin bronzes, Māori carving, Aboriginal Australian art, Native American pottery and weaving, Dayak shields, and the moai of Easter Island, among many others. Objects range from royal regalia to everyday items elevated by decoration.

Influence on modern art

From the early twentieth century, tribal objects collected in ethnographic museums and curio shops profoundly influenced Western modernism. Artists including Picasso, Matisse, Modigliani, and the German Expressionists drew on the abstracted forms of African and Oceanic sculpture; the impact of an African mask on Picasso's Les Demoiselles d'Avignon (1907) is a canonical example. The 1984 Museum of Modern Art exhibition "'Primitivism' in 20th Century Art" provoked lasting debate about this relationship.

Controversy

The category "tribal art" has been criticised for implying timelessness and anonymity, for separating objects from their makers and contexts, and for its entanglement with colonial collecting. Questions of provenance, looting, and restitution — prominent in the case of the Benin Bronzes — now dominate museum discussion, and many institutions have moved toward displaying such works as art of specific peoples and named artists rather than under a generic tribal label.
