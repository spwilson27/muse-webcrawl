Sindoor

Sindoor, the red-orange powder worn by married Hindu women.

Sindoor (Sanskrit: सिन्दूर, sindūra) is a traditional vermilion red or orange-red coloured cosmetic powder originating from the Indian subcontinent, usually worn by married women along the parting of their hair. In Hindu communities, it is one of the most visible signs of a woman's married status, and its discontinuation is traditionally associated with widowhood.

Composition

Traditional sindoor was made from turmeric and alum or lime, or from cinnabar (mercuric sulfide). Modern commercial preparations may use synthetic pigments and fillers; health concerns have been raised about products adulterated with red lead or containing mercury compounds, and regulators in several countries have tested imported sindoor for lead content.

Use and significance

During a Hindu wedding ceremony, the groom applies sindoor to the bride's hair parting, a rite that forms a central moment in many regional wedding traditions. Thereafter the wife traditionally applies it daily. The practice is associated with prayers for the husband's long life and with the goddess Parvati and other married goddesses who are depicted wearing it. In some customs, unmarried women apply sindoor during particular festivals or rituals.

Cultural references

Sindoor appears widely in Indian literature, film and television as a symbol of marriage, and the phrase "Operation Sindoor" was adopted by India as the codename for its May 2025 military strikes, invoking the symbolism of the widows of the Pahalgam attack.
