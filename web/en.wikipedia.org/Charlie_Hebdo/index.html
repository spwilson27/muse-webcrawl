Charlie Hebdo (French for "Charlie Weekly") is a French satirical weekly newspaper featuring cartoons, reports, polemics, and jokes. Strongly secular, left-wing, and anti-authoritarian, it lampoons the far right, religion — Catholicism, Islam, Judaism and others — and political leaders of all stripes. The paper came to worldwide attention after the terrorist attack on its Paris offices in January 2015.

History

The paper's lineage begins with the monthly Hara-Kiri, founded in 1960 by Georges Bernier ("Professeur Choron") and François Cavanna. After its weekly spin-off L'Hebdo Hara-Kiri was banned in November 1970 for mocking the death of Charles de Gaulle ("Bal tragique à Colombey: 1 mort"), the staff relaunched it within a week as Charlie Hebdo — the "Charlie" referencing both Charlie Brown (a Peanuts reprint ran in the paper) and, dually, de Gaulle. Its cartoonists, including Cabu, Wolinski, Gébé, and Reiser, defined French satirical drawing of the 1970s. Struggling commercially, the paper ceased publication in 1981 (with one 1982 special issue) and was relaunched in 1992 by Philippe Val, Cabu, and others, adopting an uncompromisingly secularist line.

Muhammad cartoons controversy

In February 2006 Charlie Hebdo republished the Danish Jyllands-Posten cartoons of the Prophet Muhammad with new covers of its own; French Muslim organisations sued for "public injury" toward Islam, and in 2007 a Paris court acquitted the paper, affirming the legality of blasphemy in France. Its office was firebombed in November 2011 after a "Charia Hebdo" special issue. Editor Stéphane Charbonnier ("Charb"), who took over in 2009, lived under police protection.

2015 attack

On 7 January 2015, brothers Saïd and Chérif Kouachi, claiming allegiance to al-Qaeda in the Arabian Peninsula, forced entry to the paper's offices and murdered twelve people: editor Charb, cartoonists Cabu, Wolinski, Tignous, and Honoré, columnist Elsa Cayat, economist Bernard Maris, staff and guests, and two police officers. The killings, followed by related attacks in Paris over the next two days, triggered mass demonstrations under the slogan Je suis Charlie and a solidarity march of world leaders in Paris on 11 January. The surviving staff produced a "survivors' issue" (14 January 2015) with a cover showing Muhammad weeping beneath "Tout est pardonné" ("All is forgiven"); nearly 8 million copies were sold (normal circulation was about 60,000). The paper continues publication from secure, undisclosed premises. Suspects tried in 2020 were convicted in connection with the January 2015 attacks.
