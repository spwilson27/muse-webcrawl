Coase theorem

The Coase theorem, named after economist Ronald Coase, holds that if property rights are clearly defined and transaction costs are zero or negligible, private parties can bargain among themselves to resolve problems of externalities efficiently, regardless of which party is initially assigned the rights. The allocation of resources will be the same (and efficient) either way; only the distribution of wealth between the parties will differ.

Origin

The idea was developed in Coase's 1960 paper "The Problem of Social Cost," which examined cases such as a rancher's cattle straying onto a farmer's crops. Coase argued that the harm is reciprocal in nature—restricting the rancher also imposes a cost—and that with costless bargaining the parties would arrive at the arrangement that maximizes the joint value of production, whether the law initially favored the rancher or the farmer. The label "Coase theorem" was coined by George Stigler.

Interpretation and criticism

The theorem is often read less as a description of the real world—where transaction costs are rarely zero—than as a demonstration of why transaction costs and the precise definition of property rights matter, and as a benchmark against which legal rules and institutions can be judged. Critics point to wealth effects, strategic bargaining, holdout problems, and the difficulty of organizing many affected parties, all of which can prevent the efficient outcome. Coase himself emphasized that the zero-transaction-cost world was a stepping stone to analyzing the real world of positive transaction costs, an insight central to his later work on the nature of the firm and institutions.

Influence

The theorem became a foundation of the field of law and economics and influenced environmental policy debates, supporting market-based instruments such as tradable pollution permits when transaction costs are low.
