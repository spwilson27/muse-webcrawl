Sitcom

A traditional multi-camera sitcom set, filmed before a studio audience.

A sitcom (a clipping of situation comedy) is a genre of comedy that centres on a fixed set of characters who (mostly) carry over from episode to episode. Sitcoms can be contrasted with stand-up comedy, where a comedian tells jokes to an audience, and with sketch comedy. The format arose in radio before transferring to television, where it became one of the most dominant and enduring forms of programming.

Characteristics

Episodes are typically 20–30 minutes long and revolve around a self-contained comic situation in a home, workplace or shared social setting, with the status quo generally restored by the end. Traditional sitcoms are shot with multiple cameras on standing sets before a live studio audience, with a laugh track or recorded audience reaction; since the 2000s many acclaimed sitcoms have adopted single-camera filming without a live audience, such as The Office, 30 Rock and Modern Family.

History

Early television landmarks include I Love Lucy (1951–57), which pioneered the three-camera filmed format and the rerun syndication model, and in Britain Hancock's Half Hour. In later decades, series such as Cheers, Seinfeld, Friends and The Simpsons (an animated sitcom) became international cultural phenomena, while British series including Fawlty Towers, Only Fools and Horses and Peep Show developed shorter-series variants of the form. Animated sitcoms and, more recently, streaming-native series have continued to renew the genre.
