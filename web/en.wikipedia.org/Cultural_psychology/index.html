Cultural psychology

Cultural psychology is the study of how cultures reflect and shape the psychological processes of their members. Its central premise is that mind and culture are inseparable and mutually constitutive — psychological processes are shaped by cultural meanings, practices, and institutions, which are in turn sustained by those processes.

The field is associated with scholars such as Richard Shweder, who argued that "culture and psyche make each other up," and Hazel Markus and Shinobu Kitayama, whose 1991 work on independent and interdependent self-construals contrasted the individualistic orientation common in North America and Western Europe with the collectivistic orientation common in East Asia. Their research demonstrated differences in cognition, emotion, and motivation across cultures — for instance, analytic versus holistic styles of thinking documented by Richard Nisbett and colleagues.

Distinction from cross-cultural psychology

Cultural psychology differs from cross-cultural psychology in that cross-cultural psychology typically treats culture as an independent variable and compares psychological functioning across groups using standardized measures, whereas cultural psychology holds that psychological phenomena cannot be separated from their cultural contexts and questions whether measures developed in one culture are valid in another. Critics of mainstream psychology have pointed out that most published research draws on WEIRD (Western, Educated, Industrialized, Rich, Democratic) samples, which may not generalize to the rest of humanity.

History

The roots of cultural psychology reach back to Wilhelm Wundt's Völkerpsychologie (folk psychology) and to Lev Vygotsky's cultural-historical psychology, which emphasized the mediation of thought by language and cultural tools. The field coalesced as a named discipline in the 1980s and 1990s.
