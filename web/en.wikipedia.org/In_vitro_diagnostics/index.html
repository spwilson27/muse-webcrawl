In vitro diagnostics

IVD tests examine specimens such as blood and urine outside the body to detect disease and guide treatment.
AbbreviationIVD
TypeMedical devices and tests
SpecimensBlood, urine, tissue, saliva, other body fluids
Major areasClinical chemistry, immunoassay, molecular diagnostics, haematology, microbiology, point-of-care testing
RegulationEU IVDR (2017/746); FDA device rules in the US

In vitro diagnostics (IVDs) are tests performed on samples taken from the human body — such as blood, urine or tissue — in a laboratory or other setting outside the body (in vitro, "in glass"). They are used to detect diseases and infections, monitor health and disease progression, screen donors and patients, and guide therapy, and they underpin a large share of clinical decision-making.

Categories

The IVD field spans several major disciplines:

  • Clinical chemistry — measurement of electrolytes, enzymes, metabolites and drugs in blood and urine, largely on automated analysers.
  • Immunoassays — antibody-based tests for hormones, cardiac markers, infectious diseases and tumour markers, including rapid lateral-flow tests.
  • Molecular diagnostics — nucleic-acid methods such as PCR and sequencing, used for pathogens and inherited or cancer-related genetic variants.
  • Haematology and coagulation — blood counts and clotting tests.
  • Microbiology — culture, identification and antimicrobial susceptibility testing.
  • Point-of-care testing — devices used at the bedside, in clinics or at home, from glucose meters to home pregnancy and antigen tests.
  • Companion diagnostics — tests linked to a specific therapy, identifying patients likely to benefit from a drug.

Regulation

Because faulty results can cause serious harm, IVDs are regulated as medical devices. In the European Union the In Vitro Diagnostic Regulation (EU) 2017/746 (IVDR), fully applicable from 2022, classifies devices into risk classes A–D and tightened evidence and notified-body requirements. In the United States, IVDs are regulated by the Food and Drug Administration, with most tests classed as Class I–III devices; the oversight of laboratory-developed tests has been a recurring policy debate.

Industry and importance

Although IVDs account for only a small fraction of healthcare spending, their results influence a disproportionate share of clinical decisions — diagnosis, admission, treatment choice and discharge. The COVID-19 pandemic dramatically expanded public awareness of IVDs, from central-lab PCR to self-administered antigen tests.
