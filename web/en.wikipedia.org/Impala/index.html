The impala (Aepyceros melampus) is a medium-sized antelope found in eastern and southern Africa. The sole member of the genus Aepyceros, it was first described to European audiences by German zoologist Martin Lichtenstein in 1812. Two subspecies are recognised: the common impala and the larger, darker black-faced impala. The name "impala" comes from the Zulu language.

A slender, agile antelope, the impala stands 70–92 cm at the shoulder and weighs 40–65 kg. Its coat is reddish brown, paler on the flanks and white on the belly, with black markings on the ears, tail and hindquarters — including characteristic vertical stripes on the rump. Only males bear the distinctively lyre-shaped, ridged horns, which can reach 45–92 cm.

Behaviour and ecology

Impala are highly social: females and young form herds of 15–100, while males hold territories during the rut and maintain bachelor herds otherwise. They are mixed feeders, grazing in the wet season and browsing in the dry season, favouring ecotones between woodland and grassland near water. Famous for their leaping — clearing over 2 m in height and 10 m in length — impala use bounding "pronking" flights and scent glands on their hind feet that release alarm pheromones as they kick up their heels.

Breeding peaks in the wet season; the gestation period is about six to seven months, and a single calf is born, often synchronised with thousands of others to swamp predators such as leopards, cheetahs, lions, wild dogs and hyenas. Impala engage in obligate allogrooming, using comb-like lower incisors to remove ticks from herd-mates — a behaviour unusual among antelopes.

Distribution and status

The species ranges from Kenya and Uganda south to South Africa, and west into Namibia and Angola. With a population estimated around two million, the impala is listed as Least Concern by the IUCN, though the black-faced subspecies of Namibia and Angola is considered Vulnerable. Impala are farmed for meat and trophy hunting across southern Africa.
