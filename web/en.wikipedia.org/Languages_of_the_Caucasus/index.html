Languages of the Caucasus

The Caucasus region, at the border of Europe and Asia between the Black Sea and the Caspian Sea, is one of the most linguistically diverse areas on Earth. The languages spoken there belong to several distinct families, three of which are indigenous and found nowhere else. Arab geographers called the Caucasus Jabal al-Alsun, the "Mountain of Languages."

Indigenous families

Kartvelian (South Caucasian)

The Kartvelian family includes Georgian — the official language of Georgia, with its own unique alphabet and a literary tradition going back to the 5th century AD — as well as Mingrelian, Laz, and Svan, spoken in western Georgia and northeastern Turkey.

Northeast Caucasian (Nakh-Daghestanian)

This family includes the Nakh languages (Chechen, Ingush, and Bats) and the many languages of Dagestan, such as Avar, Lezgian, Dargwa, Lak, and Tabasaran. Dagestan alone has dozens of languages, many with extraordinarily complex morphology and large consonant inventories.

Northwest Caucasian

The Northwest Caucasian family includes Abkhaz, Abaza, Circassian (Adyghe and Kabardian), and the extinct Ubykh. These languages are famous for their huge consonant systems — Ubykh had over 80 consonants — and correspondingly tiny vowel systems, as well as polysynthetic, ergative grammar.

Other families

Indo-European languages in the region include Armenian, an independent branch with its own alphabet, and Ossetian, an Iranian language descended from that of the Alans, as well as Russian, Greek, and Kurdish varieties. Turkic languages include Azerbaijani, Karachay-Balkar, Kumyk, and Nogai. Arabic was historically the language of scholarship in Dagestan, and Persian long served as a literary language in the eastern Caucasus.

Features and study

Caucasian languages are noted for ergative alignment, ejective consonants, and complex verb morphology with polypersonal agreement. The three indigenous families have not been demonstrably shown to be related to each other, though proposals linking them (such as "Ibero-Caucasian") have been made. Many smaller Caucasian languages are endangered as speakers shift to Russian, Georgian, Azerbaijani, or Armenian.
