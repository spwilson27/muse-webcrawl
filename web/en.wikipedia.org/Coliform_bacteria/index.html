Coliform bacteria

CharacteristicsRod-shaped, Gram-negative, non-spore-forming, facultative anaerobes

SignificanceIndicator organisms for water quality

Coliform bacteria are defined as either motile or non-motile, Gram-negative, non-spore-forming bacilli that can grow in the presence of bile salts and ferment lactose to produce acid and gas within 48 hours at 35–37 °C. They are a commonly used indicator of sanitary quality of foods and water.

Coliforms include genera originating in the faeces of warm-blooded animals (thermotolerant coliforms) and non-faecal, environmental genera. The best-known coliform is Escherichia coli, whose presence in drinking water strongly indicates recent faecal contamination and the possible presence of pathogens.

Use as indicators

Because pathogens are diverse, sparse, and difficult to detect directly, water-quality standards instead test for coliforms, which are abundant in sewage and comparatively easy to culture. Regulatory standards for drinking water commonly require zero total coliforms and zero E. coli per 100 mL sample. "Faecal coliform" tests incubate samples at 44–45 °C to select thermotolerant organisms associated with the gut.

Detection

Standard methods include the multiple-tube fermentation (most probable number) technique, membrane filtration on selective agar, and defined-substrate enzyme tests such as Colilert, in which β-galactosidase activity (ONPG) indicates total coliforms and β-glucuronidase activity (MUG fluorescence) indicates E. coli.

Health significance

Most coliforms do not cause disease, but their presence signals that water may be contaminated with disease-causing organisms such as Salmonella, Shigella, hepatitis A virus, and pathogenic E. coli strains. Coliform testing is also used in the dairy and food industries to detect inadequate sanitation or pasteurisation failures.
