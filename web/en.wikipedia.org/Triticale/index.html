Triticale

Triticale (/trɪtɪˈkeɪliː/; × Triticosecale) is a hybrid of wheat (Triticum) and rye (Secale) first bred in laboratories during the late 19th century in Scotland and Germany, and commercially available since the 1960s. The name is a portmanteau of the Latin names of the two parent genera. The goal of the hybrid was to combine the yield potential and grain quality of wheat with the disease resistance, hardiness, and tolerance of poor soils of rye.

Biology and breeding

Most modern triticale is an amphiploid — a fertile hybrid produced by crossing wheat and rye and doubling the chromosome number, often using colchicine. Hexaploid triticales derive from durum wheat × rye; octaploid types derive from bread wheat × rye. Early hybrids were largely sterile, and it took decades of breeding to achieve stable, high-yielding varieties.

Cultivation and uses

Triticale is grown on several million hectares worldwide, with Poland, Germany, France, Belarus, and Australia among the largest producers. It is used mainly as animal feed — grain, forage, and silage — and as a cover crop, but it is also milled for human food, appearing in multigrain breads, breakfast cereals, and pasta, and is increasingly used for bioethanol production. It generally out-yields wheat on marginal, acidic, or drought-prone soils.

In popular culture

Triticale achieved a measure of fame through the Star Trek episode "The Trouble with Tribbles" (1967), which mentioned a fictional derivative grain, "quadrotriticale", essential to the plot — an invention of scriptwriter David Gerrold inspired by the real hybrid.
