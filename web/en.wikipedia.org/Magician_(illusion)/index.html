Magician (illusion)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia — this article is about the stage performer. For the practitioners of ritual magic, see Magi.

A magician, also called an illusionist or conjurer, is an entertainer who performs tricks that appear impossible or supernatural, using sleight of hand, mechanical devices, misdirection and showmanship. Stage magic is one of the oldest performing arts, with professional magicians documented from the eighteenth century and informal conjuring — such as the cups and balls — depicted as far back as ancient Egypt.

Types of performance

Magicians specialise in genres including stage illusions (large-scale effects such as sawing a person in half or levitation), parlour magic, close-up and micromagic performed at arm's length, card magic, coin magic, mentalism (simulated mind-reading), escapology, and children's magic. Some combine magic with comedy or storytelling. The performer's persona — mysterious mystic, elegant gentleman, or avant-garde provocateur — is a central part of the art.

History

Jean-Eugène Robert-Houdin, a French clockmaker who opened a theatre of magic in Paris in 1845, is regarded as the father of modern magic for moving conjuring from fairgrounds to elegant stages. His successors included John Nevil Maskelyne in London, Harry Kellar and Howard Thurston in the United States, and the escapologist Harry Houdini, whose name became synonymous with escape artistry. In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries, magicians such as David Copperfield, Penn & Teller, and Derren Brown brought large illusions, comedy and psychological illusion to television audiences worldwide.

Secrecy and ethics

Magicians traditionally swear not to reveal the methods behind their effects, and professional bodies such as the Magic Circle (founded in London in 1905) and the Society of American Magicians enforce codes of conduct. The intellectual property of tricks is protected mainly by professional custom rather than law.
