Quest of the Historical Jesus

The quest of the historical Jesus is the scholarly effort to reconstruct the life and teachings of Jesus of Nazareth using historical methods, distinguishing the historical figure from the Christ of faith presented in Christian theology. The term derives from Albert Schweitzer's 1906 book Von Reimarus zu Wrede, translated into English as The Quest of the Historical Jesus.

First quest

The first quest is generally dated from Hermann Samuel Reimarus (1694–1768), whose unpublished fragments, published posthumously by Gotthold Lessing, argued that Jesus's disciples had invented the resurrection. David Friedrich Strauss's Life of Jesus (1835) treated the miraculous elements of the Gospels as myth. The quest wound down after William Wrede (1901) showed that Mark's Gospel reflects theology rather than biography, and Schweitzer concluded that scholars had projected their own values onto Jesus.

No quest and new quest

Influenced by Rudolf Bultmann, who argued that little could be known about the historical Jesus and that faith did not require it, scholarship entered a period of scepticism. A "new quest" began after Ernst Käsemann's 1953 lecture argued that the Gospels do preserve historical information and that continuity exists between the Jesus of history and the Christ of faith.

Third quest

From the 1980s, a "third quest" emphasised Jesus's Jewish context, used archaeology and social-scientific methods, and applied criteria of authenticity such as multiple attestation, embarrassment, and dissimilarity. Scholars such as E. P. Sanders, John P. Meier, and N. T. Wright produced major reconstructions, though no consensus portrait has emerged. The Jesus Seminar, founded by Robert Funk in 1985, attracted attention for voting on the authenticity of sayings attributed to Jesus.
