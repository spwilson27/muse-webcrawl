Dissipative system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A dissipative system is a thermodynamically open system operating out of, and often far from, thermodynamic equilibrium in an environment with which it exchanges energy and matter. It is characterised by the spontaneous appearance of order — a breaking of symmetry and the formation of complex, sometimes chaotic, structures. The concept was developed by the Belgian physicist Ilya Prigogine, who received the 1977 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for his work on dissipative structures.

Dissipative structures

Classic examples of dissipative structures include Bénard convection cells, which form when a liquid is heated from below; the oscillating Belousov–Zhabotinsky chemical reaction; hurricanes, which are sustained by the flux of heat and moisture from the ocean; and living organisms themselves, which maintain internal order by exporting entropy to their surroundings. Such structures persist only while energy flows through them; when the flow stops, they decay to equilibrium.

Mathematical description

In dynamical systems theory, a dissipative system is one whose phase-space volume contracts under the flow, so that trajectories eventually settle onto an attractor of lower dimension, such as a limit cycle or a strange attractor. This contrasts with conservative systems, in which phase volume is preserved (Liouville's theorem). The mathematics of dissipative systems underpins modern treatments of turbulence, pattern formation and self-organization.
