Country music

The Grand Ole Opry in Nashville, Tennessee, broadcasting country music since 1925.

Country (also called country and western) is a music genre originating in the southern and southwestern United States. First produced in the 1920s, country music primarily draws from folk music and blues, and ballads and dance tunes with simple forms, folk lyrics, and harmonies generally accompanied by instruments such as banjos, fiddles, harmonicas, and many types of guitar (acoustic, electric, steel and resonator).

History

The genre's commercial birth is usually dated to the 1927 Bristol sessions in Tennessee, where producer Ralph Peer recorded the Carter Family and Jimmie Rodgers — often called the "big bang" of country music. Radio barn dances (the Grand Ole Opry began in 1925 on WSM Nashville) spread the music nationally. Hank Williams defined the honky-tonk style in the late 1940s; the smoother "Nashville sound" of the 1950s–60s (Patsy Cline, Jim Reeves) sought pop crossover; outlaw country (Waylon Jennings, Willie Nelson), bluegrass (Bill Monroe), country rock and later the stadium-scale pop-country of Garth Brooks, Shania Twain and Taylor Swift successively reshaped the market. Modern stars include Luke Combs and Chris Stapleton, and streaming data analysed with deep learning recommendation systems has further globalised the audience.

Characteristics and themes

Country songs favour direct storytelling and vernacular language; recurring themes include love and heartbreak, rural and working-class life, faith, drinking, trucks and home towns. Regional food culture — such as the cuisine of the Southwestern United States — frequently appears in lyrics celebrating place and identity. The genre's institutions include Nashville's Music Row, the Country Music Hall of Fame, and the CMA Awards.
