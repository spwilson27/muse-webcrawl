Infrared astronomy

Astronomy using infrared radiation (roughly 0.75–300 μm), revealing cold and dust-obscured objects.

Infrared astronomy is a sub-discipline of astronomy which specializes in the observation and analysis of astronomical objects using infrared (IR) radiation. The wavelength of infrared light ranges from 0.75 to 300 micrometers, between visible light and submillimeter radiation. Infrared light was discovered in 1800 by William Herschel, who detected radiant heat beyond the red end of sunlight.

Because most infrared radiation is absorbed by water vapour in the Earth's atmosphere, infrared observatories are placed at high, dry sites such as Mauna Kea and the Atacama Desert, flown on aircraft (NASA's SOFIA), or sent into space. Space telescopes must be cryogenically cooled so that their own thermal emission does not swamp faint cosmic signals.

Science

Infrared observations penetrate the dust clouds that block visible light, revealing star-forming regions, the structure of the Milky Way's centre, and protostars still embedded in their natal clouds. Cool objects—brown dwarfs, protoplanetary discs and the most distant, highly redshifted galaxies—emit most of their energy in the infrared, so the field is central to the study of the early universe and of planet formation. Much of the energy radiated by dusty, starbursting galaxies also emerges in the far infrared.

Observatories

Major infrared missions include IRAS (1983), the first all-sky infrared survey; the Infrared Space Observatory (1995–98); NASA's Spitzer Space Telescope (2003–2020); the Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer (WISE, 2009–); ESA's Herschel Space Observatory (2009–13) for the far infrared; and the James Webb Space Telescope (launched 2021), whose instruments observe from 0.6 to 28.5 μm behind a tennis-court-sized sunshield.
