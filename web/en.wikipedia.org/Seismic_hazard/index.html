Seismic hazard

Probabilistic seismic hazard maps show ground motion with a given chance of being exceeded in a given period.

A seismic hazard is the probability that an earthquake will occur in a given geographic area, within a given window of time, and with ground motion exceeding a given threshold. Seismic hazard analysis underpins earthquake-resistant design in building codes, insurance pricing, and land-use planning. Hazard differs from seismic risk, which combines hazard with the vulnerability and value of the structures and population exposed.

Methods

Two main approaches are used. Deterministic seismic hazard analysis (DSHA) considers specific earthquake scenarios — typically the largest earthquake considered plausible on identified faults near the site — and estimates the resulting ground motion. Probabilistic seismic hazard analysis (PSHA), developed from the 1960s work of C. Allin Cornell, integrates over all possible earthquake sources, magnitudes and distances, weighting each by its rate of occurrence (often using the Gutenberg–Richter relation) and by ground-motion models (attenuation relations), to produce hazard curves and maps, such as ground motion with a 10% probability of exceedance in 50 years.

Ground motion and site effects

Hazard is usually expressed in terms of peak ground acceleration or spectral acceleration at the periods relevant to buildings. Local soil conditions can amplify shaking substantially — soft sediments amplified motion dramatically in Mexico City in 1985 — so analyses incorporate site classification and, for critical facilities, site-specific studies. Secondary hazards such as landslides, liquefaction and tsunami may be evaluated separately.

Applications

National hazard maps, such as those published by the U.S. Geological Survey, feed directly into model building codes. Special studies are required for nuclear power plants, dams, hospitals and other critical infrastructure, where very low exceedance probabilities are targeted.
