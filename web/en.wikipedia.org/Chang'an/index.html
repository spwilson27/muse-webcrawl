Chang'an


Chang'an (長安, "perpetual peace"), ancient capital near modern Xi'an.

Chang'an (simplified Chinese: 长安; traditional Chinese: 長安; pinyin: Cháng'ān, "perpetual peace") was the capital city of several Chinese dynasties, located on the site of modern Xi'an in Shaanxi province. It served as the capital of the Western Han, Sui and Tang dynasties, among others, and as the eastern terminus of the Silk Road. At its height under the Tang, Chang'an was one of the largest and most cosmopolitan cities in the world, with a population approaching one million within its walls.

History

The area around Xi'an had been inhabited since Neolithic times, and the Zhou dynasty founded capitals nearby. Liu Bang, founder of the Han dynasty, established Chang'an as his capital in 202 BCE, building the Weiyang Palace, one of the largest palace complexes of the ancient world. After the Han collapse the city declined, but the Sui dynasty rebuilt it on a grand grid plan in 582 as Daxing; the Tang renamed it Chang'an and kept it as their capital.

The Tang capital

Tang Chang'an was a vast rectangle enclosed by walls some 36 kilometres in circumference, laid out on a strict north–south grid of avenues. Two great markets, the East and West Markets, served merchants from Persia, Central Asia, Korea and Japan. Foreign religions — Buddhism, Nestorian Christianity, Zoroastrianism, Manichaeism and Islam — all had communities in the city. Its layout became the model for later East Asian capitals such as Heian-kyō (Kyoto) and Nara in Japan.

Decline

The An Lushan Rebellion (755–763) and later warlord conflicts devastated Chang'an. In 904 the warlord Zhu Wen forced the court to move to Luoyang and systematically demolished the city, ending its career as an imperial capital.
