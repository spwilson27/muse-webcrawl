Laron syndrome

Profound short stature is the hallmark of Laron syndrome
Laron syndrome
Other namesGrowth hormone insensitivity syndrome; Laron dwarfism
CauseAutosomal recessive mutations in the growth hormone receptor gene (GHR, chromosome 5p13–p12)
SymptomsSevere short stature, obesity, characteristic facies, delayed puberty
BiochemistryNormal/high growth hormone, very low IGF-1
TreatmentRecombinant IGF-1 (mecasermin)
Described byZvi Laron (1966)

Laron syndrome is a rare autosomal recessive disorder characterised by an insensitivity to growth hormone (GH), caused by mutations in the growth hormone receptor. Because the receptor cannot respond, the liver and other tissues fail to produce insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), producing severe growth failure despite normal or elevated GH secretion. It was described in 1966 by the Israeli endocrinologist Zvi Laron, initially in a cohort of Jewish patients from Yemen and other Middle Eastern populations.

Clinical features

Affected children have markedly short stature (adult heights typically 4–10 standard deviations below the mean), a characteristic face with a protruding forehead and saddle nose, small hands and feet, truncal obesity, a high-pitched voice and delayed puberty. Intelligence is usually normal. The largest known cohort lives in southern Ecuador, where several hundred people with the condition descend from converso settlers — a population studied intensively since the 1980s by Jaime Guevara-Aguirre and Valter Longo.

Cancer and diabetes resistance

Remarkably, the Ecuadorian cohort and other studies report that people with Laron syndrome have a near-absence of type 2 diabetes and a strikingly low incidence of cancer, despite high rates of obesity. This has made the condition a natural experiment in the biology of ageing: reduced GH/IGF-1 signalling parallels the lifespan extension produced by the same pathway in laboratory animals, and Laron cohorts have informed research into IGF-1–lowering strategies for cancer prevention. The protection is not complete — cardiovascular disease and accidents still occur, and overall lifespan advantage remains unproven.

Treatment

Because GH itself is ineffective, treatment uses recombinant IGF-1 (mecasermin), which improves growth velocity though final height usually remains well below average. Genetic counselling is offered in affected families, particularly in populations with founder effects.
