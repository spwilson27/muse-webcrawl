Complementarity (physics)

In physics, complementarity is a conceptual aspect of quantum mechanics that Niels Bohr regarded as an essential feature of the theory. The complementarity principle holds that objects have certain pairs of complementary properties which cannot all be observed or measured simultaneously, such as position and momentum, or wave and particle aspects. Examples include the wave–particle duality of light and matter.

Niels Bohr (1885–1962)

Overview

Bohr first presented the principle at the International Physics Congress in Como, Italy, in September 1927. According to complementarity, the behaviour of quantum objects depends on the experimental apparatus used to observe them: an experiment designed to reveal wave-like behaviour cannot simultaneously reveal particle-like behaviour. The two descriptions are mutually exclusive yet jointly necessary for a complete account of the phenomena.

Relation to the uncertainty principle

Complementarity is closely related to Werner Heisenberg's uncertainty principle, though Bohr regarded complementarity as the broader concept. The quantum eraser and delayed-choice experiments are often discussed as illustrations: information about which path a particle took and interference patterns are complementary observables.

Philosophical reception

The principle became a cornerstone of the Copenhagen interpretation of quantum mechanics. It was debated extensively in the Bohr–Einstein debates, in which Albert Einstein sought counterexamples showing quantum mechanics to be incomplete. Bohr extended the idea beyond physics, suggesting analogies in biology and psychology.
