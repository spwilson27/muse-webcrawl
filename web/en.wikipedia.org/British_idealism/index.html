British idealism

British idealism was a philosophical movement that dominated British philosophy from the mid-19th century to the early 20th century. Influenced by the German idealism of Kant and especially Hegel, its leading figures held that reality is fundamentally mental or spiritual in character, that the finite self and the world of experience are parts of a single unified whole (the Absolute), and that truth and reality come in degrees.

Principal figures

The movement's founders included James Hutchison Stirling, whose The Secret of Hegel (1865) introduced Hegel to British readers, and Edward Caird of Glasgow and Oxford. Its most influential exponents were T. H. Green of Balliol College, Oxford, whose Prolegomena to Ethics (1883) grounded ethics and politics in the self-realisation of a divine consciousness in human beings; F. H. Bradley, whose Appearance and Reality (1893) argued that ordinary experience is riddled with contradiction and only the harmonious Absolute is fully real; and Bernard Bosanquet, who extended idealism into aesthetics, logic and social philosophy. Other figures include J. M. E. McTaggart at Cambridge — famous for arguing for the unreality of time — and the political theorist and educationalist tradition influenced by Green's liberalism and doctrine of the common good.

Influence and decline

British idealism shaped British social liberalism (Green's ideas informed the "New Liberalism" and the early welfare state), adult education and the settlement movement. Its dominance was broken in the early 1900s by the revolt of G. E. Moore and Bertrand Russell at Cambridge, whose defence of common sense and development of analytic philosophy rejected idealist metaphysics. After the First World War the movement faded, though elements survived in the work of R. G. Collingwood and Michael Oakeshott.
