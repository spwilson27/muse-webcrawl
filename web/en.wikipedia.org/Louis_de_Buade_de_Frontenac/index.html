Louis de Buade, Comte de Frontenac et de Palluau (22 May 1622 – 28 November 1698) was a French soldier, courtier, and Governor General of New France. He is best known for defending Quebec against the English invasion of 1690 during King William's War, for expanding the French fur trade and network of western posts, and for his turbulent relations with colonial officials, merchants and clergy.

Early life and military career

Frontenac was born at Saint-Germain-en-Laye, the son of Henri de Buade, colonel of the regiment of Navarre, and Anne Phélypeaux; King Louis XIII was his godfather. He joined the army young and fought in the Thirty Years' War in the Netherlands and Italy, rising to the rank of maréchal de camp. In 1648 he married Anne de La Grange-Trianon against her family's wishes, a union that brought him wealth but also lawsuits and chronic debt. His extravagance at court and his campaigning in Crete against the Ottomans left him heavily indebted when Louis XIV appointed him Governor General of New France in 1672.

First administration (1672–1682)

Arriving at Quebec in September 1672, Frontenac set about asserting royal authority and his own precedence. He summoned an assembly of the three estates — clergy, nobility and commoners — to swear allegiance, an innovation the minister Colbert rebuked as inappropriate for a colony. In 1673 he ascended the St. Lawrence and founded Fort Frontenac (at present-day Kingston, Ontario) at the outlet of Lake Ontario, securing the fur trade route and impressing the Iroquois Confederacy. He protected and encouraged the explorer René-Robert Cavelier, Sieur de La Salle, whose expeditions extended French claims into the Great Lakes and Mississippi valley.

Frontenac quarrelled persistently with the Sovereign Council, the intendant Jacques Duchesneau, Bishop François de Laval and the Jesuits — disputes over precedence, the brandy trade with Indigenous peoples, and the expansion of the fur trade versus agricultural settlement. Recalled to France in 1682 along with Duchesneau, he languished at court in debt for seven years.

Second administration (1689–1698)

War with England and Iroquois raids — notably the Lachine massacre of August 1689 — brought Frontenac back to New France in October 1689 with instructions to attack New England and capture New York, plans frustrated by circumstance. In October 1690 he repelled Sir William Phips's New England fleet at the Battle of Quebec. Asked to surrender, Frontenac famously replied through an aide: "Je vais lui répondre par la bouche de mes canons" — "I will answer him only by the mouth of my cannons."

During the 1690s Frontenac waged a gruelling frontier war — la petite guerre — of raids against English settlements, rebuilt alliances with western nations, and in 1696 personally led a large expedition against the Onondaga and Oneida, burning villages and crops. The Treaty of Ryswick (1697) ended the war with England, though conflict with the Iroquois continued until the Great Peace of Montreal in 1701, after his death.

Death and legacy

Frontenac died at the Château St-Louis in Quebec on 28 November 1698 and was buried in the cathedral of Notre-Dame; his heart, by tradition, was sent to France in a lead casket. The Château Frontenac hotel in Quebec City, opened in 1893, bears his name, as do Fort Frontenac, the Frontenac Axis geological feature, and streets and schools across Canada. Historians judge him a brilliant, imperious and theatrical figure whose energy preserved New France in its most dangerous decade.
