Systems thinking

Systems thinking is a way of making sense of the complexity of the world by looking at it in terms of wholes and relationships rather than by splitting it down into its parts. It has been used to explore and develop effective action in complex contexts, enabling systems change. Systems thinking draws on and contributes to systems theory and other systems sciences.

Rather than analysing a problem by breaking it into isolated components, systems thinking emphasises feedback loops, stocks and flows, delays, emergence and the boundaries chosen for the system under study. Behaviour over time is seen as arising from the structure of the system rather than from external events alone.

History

The approach grew from Ludwig von Bertalanffy's general systems theory, Norbert Wiener's cybernetics, and the system dynamics developed by Jay Forrester at MIT in the 1950s–60s. Peter Senge's The Fifth Discipline (1990) popularised systems thinking in management, and Donella Meadows' Thinking in Systems became a widely read primer.

Tools

Common tools include causal loop diagrams, stock-and-flow models, behaviour-over-time graphs, and soft systems methodology, developed by Peter Checkland, which treats problem situations as socially constructed.
