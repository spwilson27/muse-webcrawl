Lucius Cornelius Sulla Felix (c. 138 – 78 BC), commonly known as Sulla, was a Roman general and statesman who twice marched his legions on Rome, won a civil war, and as dictator carried out a sweeping — and bloody — program of constitutional reform intended to restore the power of the Senate. His example of seizing power by military force cast a long shadow over the last decades of the Roman Republic.

Rise

Born into an impoverished patrician family, Sulla rose serving under Gaius Marius in Numidia, where in 105 BC he negotiated the capture of King Jugurtha — an achievement commemorated on his signet ring and a seed of his later rivalry with Marius. He distinguished himself in the Social War (91–87 BC) and was elected consul for 88 BC, receiving the command against Mithridates VI of Pontus. When the tribune Sulpicius transferred that command to Marius, Sulla took the unprecedented step of marching his army on Rome.

Civil war and dictatorship

After campaigning successfully in Greece and making peace with Mithridates, Sulla returned to Italy in 83 BC and defeated the Marian forces in a civil war, sealing victory at the Battle of the Colline Gate (82 BC). Appointed dictator "for the making of laws and the settling of the constitution" — without the traditional six-month limit — he published proscription lists offering rewards for the killing of his enemies; thousands, including some 40 senators and 1,600 equestrians, were murdered and their property confiscated. Plutarch and Appian preserve the terror of this period.

Reforms and retirement

Sulla's reforms enlarged the Senate, curtailed the tribunes, reformed the courts and the governorship of provinces, and regularized the cursus honorum. In a move that astonished contemporaries, he resigned the dictatorship in 79 BC, served as consul in 80 BC, and retired to his estate at Puteoli, where he died in 78 BC while writing his memoirs. Many of his reforms were undone within a decade, but his precedent — that a Roman army would follow its commander against the state — was followed by Pompey and, fatefully, by Julius Caesar.
