Eratosthenes of Cyrene (c. 276 BC – c. 194 BC) was a Greek polymath: a mathematician, geographer, poet, astronomer, and music theorist. He was a man of learning, becoming the chief librarian at the Library of Alexandria. He invented the discipline of geography, including the terminology used today, and is best known for being the first person known to calculate the circumference of the Earth, which he did with remarkable accuracy.

Measurement of the Earth

Eratosthenes learned that at noon on the summer solstice at Syene (modern Aswan) the Sun stood directly overhead, casting no shadow, while at the same moment in Alexandria a vertical staff cast a shadow corresponding to an angle of about 7.2° — one fiftieth of a circle. Knowing the distance between the cities (about 5,000 stadia), he computed Earth's circumference as 250,000 stadia, within a few percent of the true value depending on the stadium's length used. See also Earth's rotation.

Other work

In mathematics he devised the sieve of Eratosthenes, a simple algorithm for finding prime numbers still taught today. He created one of the earliest maps of the known world, incorporating information from Alexander the Great's campaigns, and introduced a system of latitude and longitude. He also wrote poetry, literary criticism, and a chronology of Greek history, and calculated the tilt of Earth's axis.

Later life

Nicknamed "Beta" by colleagues — supposedly because he was second-best in many fields — Eratosthenes worked at Alexandria until his death around 194 BC, reportedly starving himself after going blind.
