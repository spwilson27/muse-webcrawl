Declination

In astronomy, declination (abbreviated dec; symbol δ) is one of the two angles that locate a point on the celestial sphere in the equatorial coordinate system, the other being right ascension. Declination is the angular distance of a point north or south of the celestial equator, analogous to latitude on Earth.

Explanation

Declination is measured in degrees, arcminutes and arcseconds, positive north of the celestial equator and negative south of it. The celestial equator has declination 0°, the north celestial pole +90°, and the south celestial pole −90°. An object on the celestial equator passes overhead at the Earth's equator; objects with declination near +90° are circumpolar for northern observers and never set.

Variation

Because of the precession of the equinoxes, the declinations of fixed stars change slowly over time, which is why star catalogues specify an epoch such as J2000.0. The Sun's declination varies through the year between about +23.44° at the June solstice and −23.44° at the December solstice, a range set by the axial tilt of the Earth. The Moon's declination varies over a month, and the planets remain near the ecliptic, so their declinations stay within a band around the Sun's path.

Circumpolar stars

For an observer at latitude φ, stars with declination greater than 90° − φ never set, while stars with declination less than −(90° − φ) never rise. At the poles, half the sky is circumpolar and the other half is never visible.
