Chocolate agar

Chocolate agar (CHOC) or chocolate blood agar (CBA) is a nonselective, enriched culture medium used in microbiology to grow fastidious respiratory bacteria, such as Haemophilus influenzae and Neisseria meningitidis. Despite its name, it contains no actual chocolate; its brown color comes from heated blood.

A chocolate agar plate; the medium's brown color comes from lysed red blood cells

Composition and preparation

Chocolate agar is essentially blood agar that has been heated to about 80 °C, which lyses the red blood cells. This process releases intracellular nutrients — including hemin (factor X) and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD, factor V) — that fastidious organisms require but cannot obtain from intact red cells, in which NAD is destroyed by enzymes. Heating also inactivates those enzymes, making factor V available in the medium.

Uses

The medium supports the growth of a wide range of pathogens and is routinely used for culturing specimens from normally sterile sites and respiratory samples. It is the standard medium for isolating Neisseria gonorrhoeae in modified selective forms such as Thayer-Martin agar, which adds antibiotics to suppress normal flora. Colonies on chocolate agar are typically grayish and may have a characteristic appearance depending on the organism.
