Great American Interchange

The Great American Interchange, also known as the Great American Biotic Interchange, was an important late Cenozoic paleozoogeographic event in which land and freshwater fauna migrated from North America via Central America to South America and vice versa, as the volcanic Isthmus of Panama rose up out of the sea and bridged the formerly separated continents.

Background

South America had been isolated from the other continents since the breakup of Gondwana, developing a unique fauna of marsupials, xenarthrans (sloths, armadillos, anteaters), and endemic ungulates. North America, by contrast, periodically exchanged species with Asia via Beringia, so its fauna was cosmopolitan. The closure of the Central American Seaway and emergence of the Panamanian land bridge — generally dated to around 2.8 million years ago, near the end of the Pliocene — ended South America's long isolation.

The exchange

Migration peaked around 2.6–2.4 million years ago. Northern animals that moved south included cats (including Smilodon), dogs, bears, horses, tapirs, deer, peccaries, rabbits, and rodents. South American emigrants that successfully established themselves in the north were fewer but included opossums, armadillos, porcupines, the giant ground sloth, and the flightless terror bird Titanis.

Asymmetry and extinctions

The interchange was notably asymmetric: many more North American lineages prospered in South America than the reverse, and a large fraction of South America's native mammal fauna went extinct, including the sparassodont marsupial predators and many endemic ungulate groups. Explanations include superior competition and predation from northern carnivores, climatic change, and the fact that northern lineages had already survived intense competition in a larger connected landmass. However, some northern groups that entered South America later succumbed during the end-Pleistocene extinctions as well.

Although the great pulse occurred about 2.6 million years ago, some "herald" species crossed earlier by island-hopping or swimming — for example, procyonids (raccoons and relatives) reached South America by about 7 million years ago, and ground sloths reached North America by about 9 million years ago.

Significance

The event fundamentally reshaped the ecosystems of both continents and remains a classic case study in biogeography. The modern fauna of South America still reflects the mixture: roughly half of its mammal species descend from North American immigrants.
