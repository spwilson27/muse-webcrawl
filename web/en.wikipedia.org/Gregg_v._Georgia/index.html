Gregg v. Georgia

Gregg v. Georgia, 428 U.S. 153 (1976), was a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States that reaffirmed the constitutionality of the death penalty and ended the de facto moratorium on executions that had followed Furman v. Georgia (1972). Troy Leon Gregg had been sentenced to death in Georgia for the murders of two men. In a 7–2 decision, the Court held that Georgia's new capital sentencing procedures — providing a bifurcated trial, specific aggravating circumstances to guide the jury's discretion, and automatic appellate review — satisfied the Eighth Amendment's prohibition of cruel and unusual punishment.

Significance

The plurality opinion by Justice Potter Stewart concluded that the death penalty is not inherently cruel and unusual for the crime of murder, and that carefully drafted statutes can prevent the arbitrary and capricious sentencing condemned in Furman. Companion cases decided the same day upheld statutes from Florida and Texas while striking down mandatory death penalty laws from North Carolina and Louisiana. Executions resumed in the United States in 1977, when Gary Gilmore was executed by firing squad in Utah. Gregg remains the foundational precedent of modern American capital punishment law.
