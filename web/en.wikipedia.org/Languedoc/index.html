Languedoc (Occitan: Lengadòc) is a former province of France, now continued in the region of Occitania in the south of the country. Its territory roughly covered the area between the Rhône and the Garonne, from the Mediterranean coast to the Cévennes. The name derives from the langue d'oc, the Romance language in which "yes" is òc — Occitan — as opposed to the northern French langue d'oïl.

History

The coastal plain was Romanised early as part of Gallia Narbonensis, and Narbonne was Rome's first colony in Gaul (118 BCE). In the Middle Ages the region was ruled largely by the Counts of Toulouse and the Trencavel viscounts, and developed a distinctive courtly culture — the troubadours wrote lyric poetry in Occitan. The region became a stronghold of the Cathar religious movement, against which the papacy and the French crown launched the Albigensian Crusade (1209–1229), beginning with the massacre at Béziers; Toulouse was absorbed into the royal domain by 1271. Montpellier, with its famous medieval medical school, remained a major learned centre.

From the later seventeenth century the Canal du Midi (opened 1681) linked the region's trade to the Atlantic. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries the Languedoc plain became the world's largest wine-producing area by area, dominated by high-yield table wine — a specialisation that produced crises of overproduction and famous winegrowers' revolts (1907, and the violent protests of the 1970s). Since the 1980s the region has shifted towards quality appellations (Languedoc AOC) and a technology economy centred on Montpellier.

Culture

Languedoc preserves strong Occitan cultural traditions, a Mediterranean cuisine based on olive oil, garlic and seafood, the bull games of the Camargue fringe, and a distinctive stock of medieval monuments: the fortified city of Carcassonne, the cathedral of Albi, Roman Nîmes with its arena and the Pont du Gard aqueduct nearby.
