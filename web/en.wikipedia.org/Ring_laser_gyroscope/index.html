A ring laser gyroscope (RLG) is a rotation sensor consisting of a ring-shaped laser cavity in which two beams of light travel in opposite directions around the same closed path. Rotation of the device changes the effective path length for the two beams, producing a measurable difference in their frequencies. RLGs have no moving parts and are widely used in inertial navigation.

Principle of operation

The device exploits the Sagnac effect: when the cavity rotates, the beam travelling with the rotation must cover a slightly longer path than the counter-rotating beam. Because both beams resonate in the same cavity, this path difference appears as a beat frequency between them, proportional to the rotation rate. Counting the interference fringes between the two output beams gives an incremental measure of rotation angle with very high precision.

Construction

Commercial RLGs are usually triangular or square blocks of low-expansion glass-ceramic (such as Zerodur) with precisely machined tunnels forming the ring path, mirrors at the corners, and a helium–neon gas discharge providing the gain medium. One mirror is partially transmitting so the two beams can be combined on a photodetector.

Lock-in and dither

At very low rotation rates, backscattering from the mirrors tends to couple the two beams so their frequencies lock together — the "lock-in" problem, which creates a dead band around zero rate. Most RLGs overcome this by mechanical dither: the whole cavity is oscillated at a few hundred hertz so the sensor spends little time in the lock-in region. Multi-oscillator designs achieve the same result optically.

Applications

Ring laser gyroscopes first entered service in commercial airliners in the 1980s (for example in the Boeing 757/767 inertial reference systems) and are now standard in military aircraft, naval vessels, missiles and spacecraft attitude reference units. They compete with fibre-optic gyroscopes, which use the same Sagnac principle in a coil of optical fibre.
