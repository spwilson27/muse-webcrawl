Imperial cult (ancient Rome)

The imperial cult of ancient Rome identified emperors and some members of their families with the divinely sanctioned authority of the Roman state. The cult spread gradually through the Roman Empire, becoming a unifying religious practice that expressed loyalty to the emperor and to Rome itself.

Origins

The cult drew on precedents in ruler worship in the Hellenistic kingdoms and in Rome's own cult of the genius of the paterfamilias. Julius Caesar was formally deified after his assassination (as Divus Iulius), and his adopted heir Augustus carefully encouraged cult of his own genius and of Roma in the provinces while avoiding direct worship of himself at Rome during his lifetime.

Practice

Deceased emperors judged worthy were formally deified (apotheosis) by the Senate and received temples, priests (flamines), and festivals. Provincial and municipal assemblies maintained shrines and games in honour of the emperor. Refusal to perform a token sacrifice before the emperor's image became a test of loyalty and brought Jews and later Christians into conflict with the state, contributing to waves of persecution.

Decline

The cult faded with the Christianization of the empire in the fourth century AD, though its forms influenced later court ceremonial in Byzantium.
