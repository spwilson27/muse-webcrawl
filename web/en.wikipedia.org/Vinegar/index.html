Vinegar

Vinegar is an acidic liquid produced by the fermentation of ethanol by acetic acid bacteria (Acetobacter). It typically contains 5–8% acetic acid by volume and is used as a condiment, flavouring, and preservative in cooking, as well as for pickling, cleaning, and traditional medicinal purposes.

History

Vinegar-making is ancient: traces of vinegar have been found in Egyptian urns dating to about 3000 BC, and it was used in Babylon, China, Greece, and Rome. Hippocrates recommended it for treating wounds, and medieval producers in Orléans, France, developed the slow "Orléans process" of barrel fermentation. Louis Pasteur demonstrated in 1864 that vinegar results from a biological process; industrial submerged fermentation followed in the 20th century.

Varieties

Vinegars take their character from the source material: wine vinegar (red, white, sherry), apple cider vinegar, malt vinegar, rice vinegar, coconut and cane vinegars, and balsamic vinegar, traditionally aged in Modena, Italy. Distilled white vinegar is made by fermenting distilled alcohol. Commercial production uses either slow surface methods or fast submerged fermentation with aeration.

Culinary and other uses

Vinegar is essential in salad dressings (such as vinaigrette), marinades, sauces, and pickling of vegetables and other foods. Outside the kitchen, dilute vinegar is a traditional household cleaner and descaler, and has folk-medicine uses; it also features in horticulture as a herbicide.
