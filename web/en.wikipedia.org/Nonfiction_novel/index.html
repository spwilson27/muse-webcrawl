Nonfiction novel

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The nonfiction novel is a literary genre that, broadly speaking, depicts real historical events and persons using the narrative techniques of fiction — scene-setting, dialogue, interior monologue and an omniscient or shifting point of view — while claiming fidelity to the factual record. The term is most closely associated with Truman Capote, who described his In Cold Blood (1966), about the 1959 murders of the Clutter family in Kansas, as a "nonfiction novel" and claimed to have invented the form.

Precursors and development

Earlier works anticipate the genre: Rodolfo Walsh's Operación Masacre (1957) reconstructed state killings in Argentina through testimony and narrative, and John Hersey's Hiroshima (1946) applied novelistic techniques to survivors' accounts. Capote's book, serialized in The New Yorker in 1965, became a landmark of the New Journalism movement, alongside works such as Norman Mailer's The Armies of the Night (1968) and The Executioner's Song (1979).

Debates

The genre raises persistent questions about where documentation ends and invention begins: dialogue is reconstructed, thoughts attributed, and scenes compressed or composited, and Capote's account was later shown to include fabricated or altered details. Defenders argue that the techniques convey truths inaccessible to conventional reporting; critics counter that presenting invented material as fact undermines both journalism and literature. The nonfiction novel overlaps with creative nonfiction, narrative nonfiction and the documentary novel.
