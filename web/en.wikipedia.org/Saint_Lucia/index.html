Saint Lucia is an island country in the eastern Caribbean Sea, part of the Windward Islands of the Lesser Antilles. It lies northeast of Saint Vincent and south of Martinique. Named after Saint Lucy of Syracuse, it is the world's only sovereign state named after a historical woman.

Geography

The volcanic island is dominated by forested mountains. Its most famous landmarks are the twin peaks of the Pitons — Gros Piton and Petit Piton — near the town of Soufrière, within a UNESCO World Heritage Site that also includes the Sulphur Springs drive-in volcano. Mount Gimie, at 950 m, is the highest point. The climate is tropical with a dry season from December to May.

History

First settled by Arawak and later Kalinago peoples, the island was contested by France and Britain, changing hands 14 times before the Treaty of Paris (1814) finally ceded it to Britain — earning it the nickname "Helen of the West Indies". Slavery was abolished in 1834 (with full freedom in 1838). Saint Lucia joined the West Indies Federation (1958–1962), became an associated state in 1967, and achieved independence in 1979, remaining a Commonwealth realm.

Economy and culture

Tourism is the largest industry, followed by banana exports and offshore services. The island hosts the renowned Saint Lucia Jazz Festival. Saint Lucia has produced two Nobel laureates: economist Sir W. Arthur Lewis and poet Derek Walcott. English is official, but most Saint Lucians also speak Kwéyòl, a French-based creole.
