Morphogenesis

[Image: Morphogen gradients patterning a developing embryo]

Morphogenesis (from Greek morphē, "shape," and genesis, "creation") is the biological process that causes a cell, tissue or organism to develop its shape. Together with cell growth and cellular differentiation, it is one of the fundamental aspects of developmental biology. The process controls the organized spatial distribution of cells during embryonic development and can also occur in mature organisms, cell culture and tumours.

Mechanisms

Morphogenesis arises from the interplay of gene expression patterns with physical and chemical processes in cells and tissues. Signaling molecules called morphogens diffuse from localized sources to form concentration gradients that give cells positional information; cells respond according to threshold levels, a mechanism described by Lewis Wolpert's "French flag model." Alan Turing proposed in 1952 that interacting chemicals undergoing reaction-diffusion could spontaneously generate patterns such as stripes and spots. Mechanical forces also shape tissues: cell adhesion, cortical tension, cell migration, programmed cell death (which sculpts structures such as fingers), and cell proliferation all contribute.

Molecular basis

Key molecular players include transcription factors such as the Hox genes that pattern body axes, cell adhesion molecules (cadherins, integrins), the extracellular matrix, and cytoskeletal dynamics driven by actin and myosin. Signaling pathways central to morphogenesis include Wnt, Hedgehog, BMP/TGF-beta and Notch. Errors in morphogenesis lead to congenital malformations; the study of organoids seeks to harness self-organizing morphogenetic processes in vitro.
