Antireligion

Antireligion denotes opposition to religion in general or to particular religions

Antireligion is opposition to religion. It involves opposition to organized religion, religious practices, or religious institutions, and may be directed against religion in general or against specific faiths. Antireligion differs from atheism (lack of belief in deities) and from antitheism (opposition to belief in gods), though the positions often overlap.

Historical forms

Enlightenment thinkers such as Voltaire criticized ecclesiastical power and dogma, and in the nineteenth century writers including Ludwig Feuerbach and Karl Marx treated religion as a human projection or an instrument of social control—Marx famously calling it the "opium of the people." Antireligious sentiment also featured in revolutionary movements and in anticlericalism directed at the political power of churches.

State antireligion

Several twentieth-century states adopted antireligious policies, most notably the Soviet Union, where the League of Militant Atheists promoted atheism and many churches, mosques, and synagogues were closed or demolished. Similar campaigns of varying intensity occurred in revolutionary Mexico, Albania (declared the world's first atheist state in 1967), and China during the Cultural Revolution.
