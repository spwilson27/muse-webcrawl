Uniform Crime Reports

The Uniform Crime Reports (UCR) are official data on crime in the United States, published by the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI). UCR data are compiled from reports submitted voluntarily by roughly 18,000 city, university and college, county, state, tribal, and federal law enforcement agencies across the country, and have been collected since 1930.

Scope

The UCR traditionally divided offenses into Part I and Part II. Part I offenses — criminal homicide, rape, robbery, aggravated assault, burglary, larceny-theft, motor vehicle theft, and (added in 1979) arson — are serious crimes reported in detail. Part II offenses, including simple assault, fraud, vandalism, and drug offenses, are counted primarily through arrest data. The annual publication Crime in the United States also covers officers killed and assaulted, hate crime statistics (collected since the 1990s under the Hate Crime Statistics Act), and cargo theft.

Transition to NIBRS

For decades the UCR used the Summary Reporting System, which applied a "hierarchy rule" counting only the most serious offense in an incident. The National Incident-Based Reporting System (NIBRS), developed in the 1980s, records far richer detail on each incident, including all offenses, victims, offenders, and circumstances. On January 1, 2021, the FBI retired the summary system and made NIBRS the national standard, though participation gaps in the first transition years — including the absence of some large city departments — temporarily reduced population coverage.

Criticism

Because participation is voluntary and definitions vary, UCR figures are widely used but treated with caution. The "dark figure" of unreported crime is addressed by the separate National Crime Victimization Survey, conducted by the Bureau of Justice Statistics, which surveys households directly and typically finds substantially higher levels of victimization than police reports show.
