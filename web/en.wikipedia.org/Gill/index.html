Gill


The feathery, blood-rich gill filaments of a fish, exposed by lifting the operculum.

A gill ( /ɡɪl/ ) is a respiratory organ that many aquatic organisms use to extract dissolved oxygen from water and to excrete carbon dioxide. The gills of some species, such as hermit crabs, have adapted to allow respiration on land provided they are kept moist. The microscopic structure of a gill presents a high surface area to the external environment. Branchia (pl.: branchiae) is the zoologists' name for gills (from Ancient Greek βράγχια).

With the exception of some aquatic insects, the filaments and lamellae (folds) contain blood or coelomic fluid, from which gases are exchanged through thin walls. The blood carries oxygen to other parts of the body. Carbon dioxide passes from the blood through the thin gill tissue into the water. Gills or gill-like organs, located in different parts of the body, are found in various groups of aquatic animals, including molluscs, crustaceans, insects, fish, and amphibians.

History

Galen observed that fish had many openings (foramina), big enough to admit gases, but that no water passed through them. In 1554, Guillaume Rondelet distinguished the gill structures of fish from their lids. Later, the work of many scientists clarified the role of gills in gas exchange and the mechanics of ventilation.

Invertebrates

Crustaceans, molluscs and some aquatic insects have tufted or plate-like gills. In molluscs the gills (ctenidia) are comb-like structures used both for respiration and, in bivalves, for filter feeding; currents of water are driven by beating cilia. Crustacean gills are typically feathery appendages attached to limbs or the body wall within a branchial chamber, ventilated by movements of specialised appendages. Echinoderms such as starfish have papulae (skin gills), while sea spiders absorb oxygen directly across the body surface.

Fish

The gills of fish form a number of slits connecting the pharynx to the outside of the animal on either side of the fish behind the head. In bony fish there are typically four pairs of gill arches supporting rows of filaments; each filament bears many thin lamellae. Water taken in through the mouth passes over the gills and out, while blood in the lamellae flows in the opposite direction to the water — a countercurrent exchange system that maintains a diffusion gradient along the whole exchange surface and allows extraction of a large fraction of the water's dissolved oxygen. In most bony fish the gills are covered by a protective bony flap, the operculum, which also pumps water; sharks and rays lack an operculum over each slit, having separate gill openings, and many sharks must keep swimming (ram ventilation) or pump water actively to breathe.

Amphibians and other vertebrates

Many amphibians have external gills in the larval stage, such as the feathery tufts of tadpole salamanders; some, like the axolotl, retain external gills throughout life (neoteny). Tadpole frogs have internal gills covered by an operculum-like flap. Lungless salamanders lack both gills and lungs as adults, relying on cutaneous respiration.
