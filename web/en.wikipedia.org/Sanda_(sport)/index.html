Sanda (Chinese: 散打; pinyin: Sǎndǎ), formerly romanised as Sanshou, is a Chinese full-contact combat sport developed from traditional kung fu techniques under the modern wushu system. It combines punches, kicks, knees (in professional rules), clinch fighting, takedowns and throws, and is often described as Chinese kickboxing with wrestling-style throws.

History

Sanda was systematised in the 1970s and 1980s when the People's Liberation Army and state sports bodies sought a practical fighting method drawn from traditional martial arts, boxing and wrestling. It became the sparring half of modern wushu alongside the forms discipline of taolu. The first national championships were held in the 1980s, and world championships under the International Wushu Federation began in the 1990s.

Rules

Amateur bouts are fought on a raised platform called a leitai, without ropes; a fighter can win by pushing or throwing the opponent off the platform, and two falls from the platform in a round end the contest. Bouts are typically three rounds. Points are scored for clean punches and kicks, leg catches converted to throws, and takedowns; knockouts win immediately. Amateur competitors wear headgear, gloves, a chest protector and shin guards. Professional sanda, contested in events such as the Sanda World Cup and the former King of Sanda tournaments, permits knee strikes and is sometimes fought in a ring.

Influence

Notable sanda practitioners who crossed into mixed martial arts include Cung Le, Liu Hailong, Zhang Tiequan and Zhang Weili. Sanda techniques — particularly side kicks, spinning kicks and scissor takedowns — have influenced striking in modern MMA.
