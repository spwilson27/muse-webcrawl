Jalal ad-Din Muhammad Rumi (30 September 1207 – 17 December 1273), known simply as Rumi, was a 13th-century Persian poet, Islamic scholar, theologian, and Sufi mystic originally from Greater Khorasan. His poems, written chiefly in Persian, have been translated into many languages, and he has been among the best-selling poets in the English-speaking world in recent decades.

Life

Rumi was born in Balkh (in present-day Afghanistan) or Wakhsh (in present-day Tajikistan), the son of the theologian Baha al-Din Walad. The family migrated westward, travelling through Nishapur — where tradition says the young Rumi met the poet Attar — Baghdad, Damascus, and finally settling in Konya in Anatolia, then ruled by the Seljuk Sultanate of Rum (from which his name "Rumi", "the Roman/Anatolian", derives). On his father's death Rumi succeeded him as a religious teacher in Konya.

Shams and the poetic turn

In 1244 Rumi formed an intense spiritual friendship with the wandering dervish Shams-i Tabrizi, which transformed his life and turned him toward poetry and the whirling sama ceremony. Shams disappeared twice; after the second disappearance in 1248 he was never seen again. Rumi's grief fuelled his greatest works: the lyrical Divan dedicated to Shams, and the six-volume Masnavi ("Spiritual Couplets"), a vast didactic poem of stories and commentary often called the "Qur'an in Persian".

Legacy

Rumi's tomb in Konya, beneath the green dome of the Mevlana Museum, remains a major pilgrimage site. His followers founded the Mevlevi Sufi order, famous for the whirling dervishes. His poetry, centred on divine love and the soul's longing for union with God, has transcended national and religious boundaries and is celebrated from Turkey and Iran to the wider world.
