Sanctions, in law and international relations, are restrictive measures imposed by one or more countries, or by international organizations, against a target state, group or individual to coerce a change in behavior, constrain capabilities or signal disapproval. They occupy a middle ground between diplomacy and war. The most common form is economic sanctions, which include trade embargoes, financial restrictions and asset freezes.

Types of sanctions

Economic sanctions range from comprehensive embargoes — such as the long-standing United States embargo against Cuba — to targeted or "smart" sanctions aimed at named individuals and entities, freezing their assets and banning travel. Diplomatic sanctions include expelling diplomats or severing relations, while military sanctions include arms embargoes. The United Nations Security Council may impose binding sanctions under Chapter VII of the UN Charter, as it has against North Korea for its nuclear weapons program.

Effectiveness and criticism

Scholars debate how often sanctions achieve their stated political goals; estimates commonly suggest success in roughly a third of cases, with better results for modest aims and multilateral support. Sanctions contributed to the end of apartheid in South Africa and to the negotiations leading to the 2015 Iran nuclear deal, but comprehensive regimes against Iraq in the 1990s were criticized for severe humanitarian effects on civilians. Targeted sanctions were developed partly in response to such criticism, though they can be evaded through illicit networks and third countries.

Legal frameworks

In the United States, sanctions are administered by the Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC) of the Treasury Department under statutes such as the International Emergency Economic Powers Act. The European Union adopts restrictive measures through its Common Foreign and Security Policy. Secondary sanctions extend pressure by threatening third parties that continue to deal with the target.
