Pope Gregory I

Pope Gregory I (Latin: Gregorius I; c. 540 – 12 March 604), commonly known as Saint Gregory the Great, was the bishop of Rome from 3 September 590 until his death. He is known for instigating the first recorded large-scale mission from Rome, the Gregorian mission, to convert the pagan Anglo-Saxons to Christianity, and for his writings, which were more prolific than those of any of his predecessors. He is one of the four great Latin Doctors of the Church and is a saint in the Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Anglican, and some Lutheran traditions.

Early life

Gregory was born to a wealthy Roman senatorial family with close ties to the Church; his ancestor Felix III had been pope. He served as prefect of Rome, the city's highest civil office, but resigned to become a monk, founding the monastery of St Andrew on his family's Caelian Hill estate and several monasteries in Sicily. Pope Pelagius II made him a deacon and sent him as apocrisiarius (ambassador) to Constantinople, where he served for several years before returning to Rome as the pope's secretary.

Pontificate

Elected pope after Pelagius II died of plague, Gregory administered Rome amid famine, plague, and Lombard invasion: he organised food distribution from Church estates, ransomed captives, and negotiated with the Lombards when imperial authority failed. He reformed the administration of the papal patrimony, wrote the Pastoral Care (Regula Pastoralis), a hugely influential guide for bishops, and the Dialogues, whose life of St Benedict shaped medieval spirituality. In 596 he sent Augustine of Canterbury to evangelise the Anglo-Saxons of Kent — the mission that founded the English Church. Tradition credits him with the codification of Gregorian chant, though the extent of his personal role is debated; he styled himself servus servorum Dei ("servant of the servants of God"), a title popes still use.

Legacy

Gregory is regarded as a founder of the medieval papacy: a monk-pope who combined contemplative ideals with energetic administration and diplomacy. His letters — over 850 survive — are a major source for the history of the late 6th century. The Gregorian calendar of 1582 is named not for him but for Gregory XIII.
