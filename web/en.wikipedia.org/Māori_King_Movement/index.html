The Māori King Movement, called the Kīngitanga in Māori, is a movement arising among some iwi (tribes) of New Zealand's North Island that established a hereditary Māori monarchy in 1858. It was created to unite Māori under a single sovereign in response to colonisation, land sales and the growing power of the British Crown's government, and to halt the alienation of Māori land.

History

After several years of meetings and debate, the Waikato chief Pōtatau Te Wherowhero accepted the kingship and was crowned in 1858 at Ngāruawāhia. His successor, Tāwhiao, led the movement through the Waikato War of 1863–64, in which Crown forces invaded the Waikato and confiscated some 1.2 million acres of land; Tāwhiao withdrew to the area later called the King Country, where he lived in exile until a settlement allowed his return in 1881. In the twentieth century, Princess Te Puea Herangi became the movement's energetic leader, rebuilding Tūrangawaewae Marae as its headquarters.

The monarchy today

The monarch — king or queen — is chosen by consensus among the leaders of the supporting tribes on the death of the incumbent. Notable monarchs include Te Atairangikaahu, who reigned for forty years from 1966 until her death in 2006 and was widely respected across New Zealand; her son Tūheitia Paki, who reigned from 2006 until his death in 2024; and his daughter Ngā Wai Hono i te Pō, who succeeded him as the eighth Māori monarch. The Kīngitanga has no constitutional status under New Zealand law but remains a significant political and ceremonial force, and its annual Koroneihana celebrations draw thousands to Ngāruawāhia.
