Pasteur Institute

The Pasteur Institute (French: Institut Pasteur) is a French non-profit private foundation dedicated to the study of biology, micro-organisms, diseases, and vaccines. It is named after Louis Pasteur, who invented pasteurization and vaccines for anthrax and rabies. The institute was founded on 4 June 1887 and inaugurated on 14 November 1888. It is located in Paris.

History

Following the success of Pasteur's rabies treatment in 1885, donations poured in from around the world to found an institute for rabies treatment, microbiology research and teaching. Pasteur himself directed the institute until his death in 1895; he is buried in a crypt beneath it.

Research

For over a century the institute has been at the forefront of the battle against infectious diseases. Researchers there discovered, among other things, the bacterium that causes plague, developed the BCG vaccine for tuberculosis, and in 1983 isolated HIV, the virus that causes AIDS — work that earned Françoise Barré-Sinoussi and Luc Montagnier the 2008 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Ten scientists associated with the institute have received Nobel Prizes.

International network

The Institut Pasteur International Network comprises over 30 institutes worldwide on five continents, working on public health, teaching and research.
