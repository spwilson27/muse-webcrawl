Error theory

In moral philosophy, an error theory is a position which holds that ethical statements do express genuine propositions about objective moral facts, but that all such positive statements are false, because there are no moral facts of the kind they presuppose. The theory combines cognitivism about moral language with nihilism about morality.

Mackie's argument

The best-known error theory is that of J. L. Mackie, set out in Ethics: Inventing Right and Wrong (1977). Mackie argued from "queerness": objective values, if they existed, would be entities of a very strange sort, utterly different from anything else in the universe, and knowable only by a peculiar faculty of moral intuition. He also argued from relativity: the variation of moral codes between cultures is best explained by ways of life rather than by perception of objective truths.

Responses and variants

Critics object that error theory carries implausible implications for everyday reasoning and deliberation, and that it struggles to explain the apparent necessity of moral truths. Variants include moral fictionalism, which proposes that we continue using moral discourse as a useful fiction while denying its literal truth; error theories have also been defended for other domains, such as colour or mathematics.
