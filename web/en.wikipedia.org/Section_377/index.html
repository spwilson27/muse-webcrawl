Section 377

Section 377 is a section of the Indian Penal Code introduced in 1861 during British colonial rule. Modelled on the English offence of buggery, it criminalised "carnal intercourse against the order of nature," a formulation long used to prosecute consensual homosexual sex as well as non-consensual acts. Equivalent provisions were exported across the British Empire and survive in the penal codes of many former colonies.

Text and scope

The section punished whoever "voluntarily has carnal intercourse against the order of nature with any man, woman or animal," with imprisonment for life, or for up to ten years, and liability to a fine. Courts interpreted the phrase expansively to cover oral and anal sex, including between consenting adults in private.

Legal challenges in India

The Naz Foundation, an HIV/AIDS organisation, first challenged the provision in the Delhi High Court in 2001, arguing it impeded public-health work and violated constitutional rights to equality, privacy and dignity. In 2009, in Naz Foundation v. Government of NCT of Delhi, the High Court read down the section to decriminalise consensual adult sex. The Supreme Court reversed that ruling in Suresh Kumar Koushal v. Naz Foundation (2013), holding that Parliament should decide the matter.

On 6 September 2018, a five-judge bench of the Supreme Court, in Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India, unanimously read down Section 377 once more, decriminalising consensual sexual conduct between adults while leaving the provision in force for non-consensual acts, acts with minors, and bestiality. The judgment relied on the right to privacy recognised in Justice K. S. Puttaswamy v. Union of India (2017). Following the enactment of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita in 2024, which replaced the Indian Penal Code, no equivalent general provision was carried over.

In other jurisdictions

Sections numbered 377, 377A and similar appear in the penal codes of Singapore (repealed in 2022), Malaysia, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Myanmar and a number of African and Caribbean states, a legacy of the colonial codes drafted under Lord Macaulay's influence. Reform of these laws has proceeded unevenly, jurisdiction by jurisdiction.
