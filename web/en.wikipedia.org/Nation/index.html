Nation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A nation is a type of social organisation where a collective identity, a national identity, has emerged from a combination of shared features across a given population, such as language, history, ethnicity, culture, territory, or society. Some nations are constructed around ethnic groups (see ethnic nationalism), while others are bound by political constitutions (see civic nationalism).

Definitions and debate

A nation has been defined as a community of people formed on the basis of a combination of shared features such as language, history, ethnicity, culture and/or territory, and it is distinguished from a state, which is a political and legal entity. A nation may or may not have a state of its own; groups such as the Kurds and Palestinians are often described as stateless nations. Conversely, a single state may contain several nations.

Scholars debate the origins of nations. "Primordialists" hold that nations have ancient roots, while "modernists" such as Ernest Gellner and Benedict Anderson argue that nations are largely modern constructions, produced by industrialisation, print capitalism, and state education. Anderson famously described the nation as an "imagined community" — imagined because most members will never meet one another, yet hold in their minds a shared image of communion.

Nationalism

The political ideology asserting that the nation and the state should be congruent is called nationalism. The doctrine that every nation should govern itself, free from outside interference, is known as self-determination, and it shaped the redrawing of borders after the First World War and the decolonisation of Africa and Asia after the Second.
