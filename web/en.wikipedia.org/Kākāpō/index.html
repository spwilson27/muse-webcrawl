The kākāpō (Strigops habroptilus), also known as the owl parrot, is a species of large, flightless, nocturnal, ground-dwelling parrot endemic to New Zealand. It is the world's only flightless parrot, the heaviest parrot (males weigh up to 4 kg), and possibly one of the world's longest-living birds, with a reported lifespan of up to 100 years. It has finely blotched yellow-green plumage, a distinct facial disc, whisker-like vibrissae, and a strongly musky odour.

Behaviour and breeding

Kākāpō are solitary, nocturnal herbivores that climb trees and "parachute" down using their short wings for balance. Males gather at lek arenas in summer, excavating bowl-shaped depressions from which they emit deep booming calls and low chings to attract females — the only parrot known to use a lek breeding system. Breeding occurs only every two to four years, coinciding with the mass fruiting (masting) of rimu trees.

Conservation

The arrival of humans and introduced predators such as rats, stoats and cats devastated the species. By 1995 only 51 birds survived. The Department of Conservation's Kākāpō Recovery Programme relocated all remaining birds to predator-free islands, including Codfish Island / Whenua Hou and Anchor Island, and manages every individual by name. Intensive management — supplementary feeding, nest monitoring and artificial insemination — has slowly raised the population to roughly 240 birds.
