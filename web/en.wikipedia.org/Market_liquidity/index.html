In business, economics and finance, market liquidity is a market's feature whereby an individual or firm can quickly purchase or sell an asset without causing a drastic change in the asset's price. Liquidity involves the trade-off between the price at which an asset can be sold and how quickly it can be sold. In a liquid market, the trade-off is mild: selling quickly will not reduce the price much. In a relatively illiquid market, selling an asset quickly forces it to be sold at a lower price.

Dimensions of liquidity

Liquidity is commonly described along several dimensions: tightness (the cost of turning around a position, typically measured by the bid–ask spread), depth (the volume that can be traded without moving the price), breadth (the number of participants and orders), and resiliency (how quickly prices recover after a large trade). Cash is the most liquid asset, while tangible assets such as real estate are comparatively illiquid because they take time to sell and have high transaction costs.

Liquidity risk and crises

Liquidity can evaporate rapidly in stressed markets, a phenomenon central to financial crises. During the 2007–2008 financial crisis, markets for mortgage-backed securities seized up, forcing holders to sell at fire-sale prices or not at all. Central banks act as lenders of last resort partly to supply liquidity in such episodes. Funding liquidity (the ability to raise cash) and market liquidity are linked in destabilizing spirals: falling prices erode collateral values, forcing further sales.

Measurement

Common proxies include the bid–ask spread, daily trading volume and turnover, the Amihud illiquidity ratio, and the price impact per unit of order flow. Market makers improve liquidity by continuously quoting buy and sell prices, earning the spread as compensation for inventory and adverse-selection risk.
