Junk bond

Also called: High-yield bond; non-investment-grade bond
Credit rating: Below BBB− (S&P/Fitch) or Baa3 (Moody's)
Key figure: Michael Milken
Primary market maker (1980s): Drexel Burnham Lambert

In finance, a junk bond — formally a high-yield bond — is a bond rated below investment grade by credit rating agencies. These bonds carry a higher risk of default than investment-grade issues, so they must offer higher yields to attract investors. Issuers include young or highly leveraged companies, and "fallen angels" — once-solid firms whose ratings have been downgraded.

History

Low-rated bonds existed long before the term "junk" — many were fallen angels such as 19th-century railroad bonds — but before the 1970s the market was small and dominated by these downgraded issues. In the 1970s and 1980s, Michael Milken of Drexel Burnham Lambert argued, citing research by W. Braddock Hickman, that a diversified portfolio of low-rated bonds historically earned returns that more than compensated for their defaults. Drexel built a powerful market for newly issued high-yield debt, raising capital for companies such as MCI and McCaw Cellular that banks and investment-grade markets would not serve.

The 1980s boom and collapse

High-yield financing fuelled the leveraged buyout wave of the 1980s, culminating in the $25 billion RJR Nabisco buyout of 1989. The market then collapsed: Drexel failed in 1990 amid the insider-trading scandal that sent Milken to prison, defaults spiked, and the savings and loan crisis was worsened by S&Ls' junk holdings. The market nonetheless survived and matured; renamed "high yield," it grew into a multi-trillion-dollar asset class routinely used by major corporations.

Risks and returns

Junk bonds behave partly like equities: their prices respond to the issuer's business prospects and the economic cycle as much as to interest rates. Yields are quoted as a spread over government bonds, widening sharply in crises — as in 2008 and 2020 — when default fears rise. Supporters credit the asset class with democratizing access to capital; critics blame it for encouraging excessive leverage.
