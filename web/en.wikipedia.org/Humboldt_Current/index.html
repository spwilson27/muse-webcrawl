Humboldt Current

The Humboldt Current, also called the Peru Current, is a cold, low-salinity ocean current that flows northward along the western coast of South America. It is an eastern boundary current similar to the California Current, but it is much larger. It is named after the German naturalist Alexander von Humboldt, even though it was discovered by Spanish navigator Juan Fernández de Eliza. In 1846, Humboldt reported measurements of the cold current in his book Cosmos.

Physical characteristics

The current carries cold Antarctic-influenced water northward, and offshore Ekman transport drives coastal upwelling that brings nutrient-rich deep water to the sunlit surface. This makes the Humboldt Current system one of the most productive marine ecosystems in the world, supporting the great Peruvian anchoveta fishery, as well as sardines, hake, sea birds and marine mammals.

Climate effects and El Niño

The cold current cools the coastal climate of Peru and northern Chile, contributing to the extreme aridity of the Atacama Desert and to coastal fog (garúa). During El Niño events, warm equatorial water replaces the cold upwelled water, collapsing fisheries, increasing rainfall and flooding, and causing mass mortality among seabirds and marine life — as occurred dramatically in 1982–83 and 1997–98.
