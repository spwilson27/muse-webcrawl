Heart failure (HF), also known as congestive heart failure (CHF), is a clinical syndrome in which the heart is unable to pump enough blood to satisfy the body's metabolic requirements, or can do so only at elevated filling pressures. Common symptoms include shortness of breath (especially on exertion or when lying flat), fatigue and swollen legs. Heart failure is a major and growing public-health problem, affecting more than 64 million people worldwide.

Classification

Heart failure is commonly classified by the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF): HFrEF (reduced ejection fraction, LVEF ≤40%), HFmrEF (mildly reduced, 41–49%) and HFpEF (preserved ejection fraction, ≥50%), the last reflecting impaired relaxation and filling rather than weak contraction. It may also be described by the side predominantly affected (left, right, or biventricular) and by functional severity using the New York Heart Association (NYHA) classes I–IV.

Causes

Any condition that damages or overworks the heart muscle can cause heart failure. The most common causes are coronary artery disease (including prior myocardial infarction), hypertension, cardiomyopathies (including alcoholic and genetic forms), valvular heart disease, arrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation, congenital heart disease, diabetes, and pulmonary disease leading to right heart failure (cor pulmonale).

Signs, symptoms and diagnosis

Typical features are breathlessness (dyspnoea), orthopnoea, paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, reduced exercise tolerance, ankle swelling and fatigue; examination may reveal raised jugular venous pressure, lung crackles, a third heart sound and displaced apex beat. Diagnosis combines clinical assessment with blood tests (notably natriuretic peptides BNP/NT-proBNP), electrocardiography, chest imaging and echocardiography, which measures ejection fraction and identifies structural causes.

Management

Treatment addresses the underlying cause, relieves congestion and improves survival. Diuretics control fluid overload. For HFrEF, four drug classes form the foundation of therapy: angiotensin receptor–neprilysin inhibitors (or ACE inhibitors/ARBs), beta blockers, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, and SGLT2 inhibitors. Selected patients benefit from implantable cardioverter-defibrillators, cardiac resynchronisation therapy, revascularisation, valve intervention, and — in advanced disease — mechanical circulatory support or heart transplantation. Lifestyle measures include salt restriction, fluid management, exercise rehabilitation and vaccination.

Prognosis and epidemiology

Heart failure carries a substantial mortality: roughly half of patients die within five years of diagnosis, though outcomes have improved with modern therapy. Prevalence rises steeply with age, affecting around 10% of people over 70 in developed countries. It is a leading cause of hospitalisation in older adults and imposes large economic costs.
