Cliff

A cliff is a vertical, or nearly vertical, rock exposure. Cliffs are common on coasts, in mountainous areas, along escarpments and river valleys. Most cliffs are formed by processes of weathering and erosion, which create a steep face where a resistant rock layer overlies weaker material, or where water, ice or tectonic forces cut into the land.

Formation

Coastal cliffs are carved by wave action undercutting a rock face until the overhang collapses, leaving a wave-cut platform at the base and a retreating cliff line. Inland, cliffs result from differential erosion — hard caprock such as sandstone or limestone protecting softer strata below — from glacial plucking and overdeepening in U-shaped valleys, and from faulting, where uplifted blocks form fault scarps and escarpments.

Notable cliffs

Among the tallest land cliffs are the north face of Mount Thor in Canada, with a vertical drop of about 1,250 metres, and the cliffs of Kalaupapa on Molokai, Hawaii, among the highest sea cliffs at roughly 1,010 metres. Northern Europe's Hornelen in Norway (860 metres) rises directly from a fjord. Famous coastal cliffs include the White Cliffs of Dover in England and the Cliffs of Moher in Ireland.

Ecology and hazards

Cliff faces support specialised plants and provide undisturbed nesting sites for seabirds and raptors. They are hazardous environments subject to rockfalls and landslides, and are popular for climbing, BASE jumping and cliff diving.
