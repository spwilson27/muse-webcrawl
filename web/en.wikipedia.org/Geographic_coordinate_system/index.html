Geographic coordinate system

A geographic coordinate system (GCS) is a spherical or geodetic coordinate system for measuring and communicating positions directly on Earth as latitude and longitude. It is the simplest, oldest, and most widely used of the various spatial reference systems and forms the basis for most others.

Latitude and longitude

Latitude measures the angle north or south of the equator, from 0° at the equator to 90° at the poles. Longitude measures the angle east or west of a prime meridian — since 1884, the meridian through Greenwich, London — from 0° to 180°. Together with elevation above a reference surface, the two angles specify any location on Earth. Coordinates are written in degrees, minutes, and seconds or in decimal degrees.

Geodetic datum

Because the Earth is an oblate spheroid rather than a perfect sphere, positions depend on the reference ellipsoid and datum adopted. The World Geodetic System 1984 (WGS 84), used by GPS, is the global standard; older regional datums such as NAD27 in North America or OSGB36 in Britain can differ from it by hundreds of metres. Latitude defined against a spheroid is called geodetic latitude, as distinct from geocentric latitude measured from Earth's centre.

History

The division of the circle into 360 degrees and the concept of latitude and longitude descend from Babylonian astronomy through Greek geographers such as Hipparchus and Ptolemy. Accurate longitude at sea remained unsolved until the 18th century, when lunar-distance methods and John Harrison's marine chronometers made it practical.
