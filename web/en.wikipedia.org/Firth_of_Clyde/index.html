Firth of Clyde

The Firth of Clyde is the estuary of the River Clyde on the west coast of Scotland. It extends from the tidal limit of the Clyde at Glasgow seaward past Greenock and Dumbarton to the open waters of the North Channel and the Irish Sea, and contains several islands including Arran, Bute and Great Cumbrae. It is one of the largest enclosed coastal waters in the British Isles and has been central to Scottish shipbuilding, trade and naval activity for centuries.

Geography

The firth begins where the River Clyde becomes tidal at Glasgow and widens as it passes the towns of the lower Clyde. Sea lochs such as Loch Long, the Gare Loch, Loch Fyne and Loch Striven open off its shores. The Isle of Arran, often described as "Scotland in miniature", lies at the mouth of the firth between Ayrshire and Kintyre. The Cumbrae islands and the Isle of Bute sit further up the firth, enclosing the waters around Millport and Rothesay.

History

The Clyde estuary was a major centre of shipbuilding from the nineteenth century, when yards at Govan, Partick, Clydebank and Port Glasgow built a large share of the world's shipping, including famous liners. In wartime the firth served as a principal convoy assembly anchorage, and the Gare Loch remains the site of HMNB Clyde at Faslane, home of the United Kingdom's submarine-based nuclear deterrent.

Wildlife and environment

The firth supports important populations of harbour porpoise, seals and seabirds, and its sea lochs include areas designated for nature conservation. The recovering Clyde estuary has seen the return of migratory fish such as Atlantic salmon after decades of industrial pollution.
