Los Caprichos ("The Caprices") is a series of 80 prints in aquatint and etching created by the Spanish artist Francisco Goya in 1797 and 1798, and published as an album in 1799. The prints were an artistic experiment: a medium for Goya's condemnation of the universal follies and foolishness in the Spanish society in which he lived.

The criticisms are far-ranging and acidic; the images expose the period's prevailing superstitious beliefs, ignorant and incompetent government, and the consequences of ignorance and intellectual stagnation. Goya described the series as depicting "the innumerable foibles and follies to be found in any civilized society, and from the common prejudices and deceitful practices which custom, ignorance and self-interest have made usual."

Publication and withdrawal

The prints were first announced for sale in the Diario de Madrid on February 6, 1799. However, the series was withdrawn from sale within days — some 27 sets had reportedly been sold — apparently out of fear of the reaction of the Spanish Inquisition and the authorities. In 1803, Goya offered the copper plates and the remaining unsold sets to King Charles IV in exchange for a pension for his son; the plates are now held by the Calcografía Nacional in Madrid.

Notable plates

The most famous plate is No. 43, El sueño de la razón produce monstruos ("The Sleep of Reason Produces Monsters"), a self-portrait of the artist slumped at his desk while owls and bats swarm around him. Originally intended as the frontispiece, it appears instead as plate 43. Other celebrated images include "And Still They Don't Go!" (No. 59), "You Who Cannot" (No. 42), and "Witches' Flight" (No. 68).

Technique

Goya combined etching with aquatint, a technique that allowed broad tonal areas resembling wash, to give the prints their dramatic chiaroscuro effects. Many plates also employ drypoint and burin work, and the images are accompanied by laconic, enigmatic captions that sharpen their satire.

Influence

Los Caprichos is regarded as one of the most influential series of prints in the history of Western art, marking Goya's emergence as an independent and critical artistic voice and anticipating modern art's preoccupation with the irrational and the subjective.
