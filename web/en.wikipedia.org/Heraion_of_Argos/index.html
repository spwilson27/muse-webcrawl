The Heraion of Argos (Ancient Greek: Ἡραῖον) was an ancient sanctuary dedicated to the goddess Hera, situated on a hillside in the Argive plain of the northeastern Peloponnese, between the cities of Argos and Mycenae. It was the principal sanctuary of Hera in the Argolid, where the goddess bore the epithet Argeia, "Hera of Argos", and one of the most important religious centres of the Archaic and Classical Greek world.

History

The site was occupied from the Bronze Age, with a Mycenaean settlement and tombs nearby. Cult activity at the sanctuary is attested from the Geometric period, and by the seventh century BC an early temple stood on the upper terrace; fragments of its roof and votive offerings show the sanctuary already drew worshippers from across the region. The Heraia festival, central to Argive civic identity, included a procession from Argos and athletic contests; its victors received a bronze shield, since Argos was famed for shield-making. Argive priestesses of Hera were so prominent that the historian Hellanicus used their succession as a chronological framework for Greek history. Around 423 BC the old temple burned down — tradition blamed the priestess Chrysis, who fell asleep while lamps set the garlands alight — and a new temple, in the Doric order, was built a little lower on the slope in about 420–410 BC, associated with the architect Eupolemos.

The site

The sanctuary is arranged on three artificial terraces cut into the hillside. The highest holds the remains of the early temple; the middle terrace carries the classical temple of Hera, a peripteral Doric building of roughly 6 by 12 columns standing on a stepped platform, with stoas and subsidiary buildings around it; the lowest terrace includes a large stoa and other structures for visitors and festivals. From the site there are commanding views over the Argive plain toward the Gulf of Argos.

Excavation

The Heraion was the first major excavation of the American School of Classical Studies at Athens, directed by Charles Waldstein in 1892–1895, and among its notable early workers was the young archaeologist who later systematic work would continue in the twentieth century. Finds include bronze and terracotta votives, Geometric bronzes, and inscriptions, now largely in the National Archaeological Museum of Athens.
