Quadratic residue

In number theory, an integer q is called a quadratic residue modulo n if it is congruent to a perfect square modulo n; that is, if there exists an integer x such that x2 ≡ q (mod n). Otherwise, q is called a quadratic nonresidue modulo n. Originally an abstract mathematical concept from the theory of modular arithmetic, quadratic residues are now used in applications ranging from acoustical engineering to cryptography and the factorisation of large numbers.

Properties

For an odd prime p not dividing a, exactly half of the nonzero residue classes modulo p are quadratic residues. Euler's criterion states that a is a residue modulo p if and only if a(p−1)/2 ≡ 1 (mod p), a fact encoded in the Legendre symbol. The law of quadratic reciprocity, proved by Gauss, relates the solvability of x2 ≡ p (mod q) to that of x2 ≡ q (mod p) for distinct odd primes, and together with its supplements allows efficient computation of Legendre and Jacobi symbols.

Applications

Quadratic residues underlie pseudorandom number generators such as the Blum Blum Shub generator, zero-knowledge identification schemes such as the Feige–Fiat–Shamir protocol, and primality tests including the Solovay–Strassen test. Deciding whether a number is a quadratic residue modulo a composite integer is believed to be computationally hard, and this assumption is used in the Goldwasser–Micali cryptosystem.
