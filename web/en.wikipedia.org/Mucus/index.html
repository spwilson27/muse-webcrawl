Mucus

Mucus is a slippery aqueous secretion produced by, and covering, mucous membranes in the animal kingdom. It is a viscous colloid containing inorganic salts, water, desquamated cells, leukocytes and, most importantly, mucins — large, heavily glycosylated proteins that give mucus its gel-like properties. Mucus serves to protect epithelial cells and to trap pathogens and particles.

Production and composition

Mucus is produced by specialized goblet cells in the epithelium and by submucosal glands. Mucins are its chief structural components; when secreted and hydrated they expand enormously, forming a protective gel layer. The composition and thickness of mucus vary by location: respiratory mucus forms a two-layer system swept by cilia (the "mucociliary escalator"), while gastric mucus forms a thick barrier shielding the stomach wall from acid and pepsin.

Functions

Mucus lubricates the passage of food through the gut, moistens inhaled air, protects the eyes and reproductive tract, and serves as a first line of immune defence, containing secretory IgA antibodies, lysozyme and other antimicrobial factors. Abnormal mucus production or consistency is a feature of diseases such as cystic fibrosis, chronic bronchitis and the common cold, in which increased, coloured mucus (phlegm or sputum when expectorated) is a familiar symptom.
