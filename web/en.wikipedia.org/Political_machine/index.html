A political machine is a party organization that recruits its members by the use of tangible incentives — money, political jobs, and the opportunity to get favors from politicians — and is characterized by a high degree of leadership control over member activity. In the politics of representative democracies, a political machine is a party organization headed by a single boss or a small autocratic group whose members are rewarded with patronage: jobs, contracts and services distributed in exchange for votes and political work.

Structure and operation

Machines are organized hierarchically, from the boss through ward leaders down to precinct captains, each responsible for delivering the vote of a small territory. The machine rewards supporters with public-sector jobs, municipal contracts, licenses, and help with problems — naturalization papers, coal in winter, intervention with the police or courts. This exchange, known as patronage or the spoils system, made machines especially effective among poor and immigrant voters, for whom the machine functioned as an informal welfare system.

History in the United States

The most famous American machine was New York's Tammany Hall, which dominated Democratic politics in the city from the 1850s, reaching a peak of corruption under William M. "Boss" Tweed in the 1860s–70s, and retained influence into the twentieth century. Other major machines included the Pendergast machine in Kansas City, the Hague machine in Jersey City, and the Chicago machine of Richard J. Daley, often considered the last of the great urban machines.

Decline

Machines declined in the twentieth century because of civil service reform (beginning with the Pendleton Act of 1883), the direct primary, the secret ballot, welfare-state programs that replaced machine charity, suburbanization, and court decisions restricting patronage hiring. Elements of machine politics persist wherever personal favors and organizational loyalty outweigh programmatic appeals.

Criticism and defence

Critics charged machines with corruption, electoral fraud and inefficiency; defenders, including some political scientists, argued they integrated immigrants into politics, humanized bureaucracy, and delivered services that formal institutions failed to provide.
