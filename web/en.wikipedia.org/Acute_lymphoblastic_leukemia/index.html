Acute lymphoblastic leukemia

Medical information
Specialty:Haematology, oncology
Symptoms:Fatigue, pallor, fever, easy bruising, bone pain, lymphadenopathy
Usual onset:Most common in children aged 2–5
Treatment:Chemotherapy, targeted therapy, stem cell transplantation, CAR T-cell therapy

Acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL) is a cancer of the lymphoid line of blood cells characterised by the development of large numbers of immature lymphocytes. Symptoms may include feeling tired, pale skin colour, fever, easy bleeding or bruising, enlarged lymph nodes, or bone pain. As an acute leukemia, ALL progresses rapidly and is typically fatal within weeks or months if left untreated.

Causes and classification

ALL arises from genetic damage to the DNA of a developing lymphocyte in the bone marrow, leading to uncontrolled growth and accumulation of lymphoblasts that crowd out normal blood cell production. Most cases have no clear inherited cause, though certain genetic syndromes (such as Down syndrome) and prior exposures increase risk. The disease is classified by immunophenotype into B-cell ALL (the majority) and T-cell ALL, and by cytogenetic abnormalities — for example, hyperdiploidy and the ETV6::RUNX1 fusion are favourable features in childhood B-ALL, while hypodiploidy and, historically, the Philadelphia chromosome (BCR::ABL1) confer higher risk.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by blood counts, bone marrow examination showing ≥20–25% lymphoblasts, flow cytometry, and cytogenetic/molecular testing. Treatment uses multi-phase chemotherapy (induction, consolidation, maintenance), central nervous system prophylaxis, and risk stratification based on age, white cell count, genetics, and measurable residual disease. Tyrosine kinase inhibitors are added for BCR::ABL1-positive disease; immunotherapies including blinatumomab and CAR T-cell therapy (e.g. tisagenlecleucel) are used for relapsed/refractory disease, and allogeneic stem cell transplantation in selected cases.

Prognosis and epidemiology

ALL is the most common cancer in children, with a peak at ages 2–5. Childhood cure rates exceed 85–90% in high-income countries with modern protocols; outcomes in adults are poorer, though improving. The disease also occurs under the related designation lymphoblastic lymphoma when disease is mainly in lymph nodes rather than blood and marrow.
