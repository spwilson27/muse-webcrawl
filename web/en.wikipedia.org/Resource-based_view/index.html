Resource-based view

The resource-based view (RBV) is a theory in strategic management that explains differences in firm performance primarily through the resources and capabilities controlled by each firm. Rather than treating firms in an industry as nearly identical, it assumes that resources are heterogeneously distributed and imperfectly mobile.

Development

Birger Wernerfelt's 1984 article gave the approach its name, drawing on earlier work by Edith Penrose. Jay Barney's 1991 framework became especially influential by specifying when resources can generate sustained competitive advantage.

VRIN and VRIO

Under the VRIN formulation, a resource must be Valuable, Rare, Imperfectly imitable, and Non-substitutable. The later VRIO framework replaces non-substitutability with a question about whether the firm is Organised to capture value. Relevant resources may include technology, brands, locations, data, relationships, tacit knowledge, organisational routines, and culture.

Capabilities

Resources are stocks that a firm owns or controls; capabilities are capacities to deploy resources through coordinated processes. Dynamic capabilities extend RBV by examining how firms integrate, build, and reconfigure competences as technologies and markets change. This extension responds to the criticism that a static resource position cannot explain adaptation in rapidly changing environments.

Criticism

Critics have questioned whether “valuable” resources can be identified without circular reasoning from successful outcomes, how managers can reliably measure intangible assets, and whether the theory underplays market structure, institutions, luck, and managerial action. Nevertheless, RBV remains a central framework for analysing why superficially similar firms develop different products, costs, and reputations.
