Design–build

In design–build delivery, one entity — the design–builder — contracts with the owner to provide both design and construction services.

Design–build (or design/build, and abbreviated D–B or D/B accordingly) is a project delivery system used in the construction industry. It is a method to deliver a project in which the design and construction services are contracted by a single entity known as the design–builder or design–build contractor. It can be subdivided into architect-led design–build and contractor-led design–build.

Comparison with design–bid–build

Design–build stands in contrast to the traditional design–bid–build (D/B/B) approach, in which the owner contracts separately with a designer and a contractor, design is completed before construction is bid, and the contractor typically has no input into the design. In design–build there is a single point of responsibility for both design quality and construction cost and schedule; design and construction phases overlap ("fast-tracking"), which can reduce delivery time. Studies by industry bodies such as the Design-Build Institute of America (founded 1993) report that design–build projects are delivered faster and with fewer cost overruns on average than design–bid–build projects.

Criticisms

Critics argue that design–build diminishes the architect's role as the owner's independent advocate and checker of the contractor's work, and may reduce design quality or owner control if construction cost pressures dominate. Variants such as design–build–operate, bridging, and progressive design–build seek to balance these concerns.

Usage

Once rare in public procurement, design–build has been progressively authorized for U.S. federal and state infrastructure, including highways under successive transportation acts, and accounts for a large and growing share — by some estimates over 40 percent — of non-residential construction spending in the United States.
