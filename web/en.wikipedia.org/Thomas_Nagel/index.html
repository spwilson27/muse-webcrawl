Thomas Nagel (born 4 July 1937) is an American philosopher. He is University Professor Emeritus at New York University, where he taught from 1980 to 2016. His work spans the philosophy of mind, political philosophy and ethics, and he is known especially for his critique of materialist reductions of consciousness and for his development of a form of liberal egalitarian political theory.

Life and career

Nagel was born in Belgrade, Yugoslavia, to a Jewish family that emigrated to the United States in 1939. He studied at Cornell University under the influence of Wittgensteinian philosophers, and received his doctorate from Harvard University in 1963. He taught at the University of California, Berkeley, and at Princeton University before moving to NYU, where, with colleagues, he helped build one of the leading philosophy departments in the English-speaking world. With John Rawls and others he founded the journal Philosophy & Public Affairs.

Philosophy of mind

In his celebrated 1974 article "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?", Nagel argued that consciousness has an irreducibly subjective character — there is "something it is like" to be a conscious organism — that cannot be captured by purely physical, objective descriptions. The paper became one of the most widely cited in modern philosophy and a touchstone in debates about qualia and the "hard problem" of consciousness. In The View from Nowhere (1986) he explored the tension between subjective and objective standpoints across philosophy. His later book Mind and Cosmos (2012) argued that the materialist neo-Darwinian conception of nature is "almost certainly false", provoking wide and often heated discussion.

Ethics and political philosophy

In The Possibility of Altruism (1970) Nagel defended a rationalist account of moral motivation. His essay "War and Massacre" (1972) examined the morality of warfare, and in "What Makes Political Philosophy Utopian?" and Equality and Partiality (1991) he addressed the demands of equality and the limits of impartiality. With Liam Murphy he wrote The Myth of Ownership (2002), an influential critique of libertarian views on taxation.
