Organophosphate

In organic chemistry, an organophosphate (sometimes abbreviated OP) is a class of organophosphorus compounds having the general structure O=P(OR)₃, a central phosphate molecule with alkyl or aromatic substituents. They are esters of phosphoric acid. Organophosphates occur in biology—DNA, RNA, and ATP are organophosphates—and in industry as plasticisers, flame retardants, lubricants, pesticides, and chemical warfare agents.

Pesticides and nerve agents

Many organophosphates are potent insecticides, developed in Germany in the 1930s; examples include parathion and malathion. The same research programme produced the nerve agents tabun and sarin. Organophosphate pesticides and nerve agents act by inhibiting the enzyme acetylcholinesterase, causing acetylcholine to accumulate at synapses and producing overstimulation of muscles and glands, convulsions, and potentially death by respiratory failure. Antidotes include atropine and oximes such as pralidoxime.

Health and environmental concerns

Organophosphate poisoning is a major clinical problem worldwide, particularly from occupational exposure and intentional ingestion of pesticides in agricultural regions. Chronic low-level exposure has been associated with neuropsychiatric symptoms. Because of toxicity concerns, many organophosphate insecticides have been restricted or replaced by alternatives such as neonicotinoids and pyrethroids, which bring their own trade-offs.
