Compensating variation

In economics, compensating variation (CV) is a measure of the change in a consumer's welfare caused by a change in prices, taxes, or the introduction of a new product. It is the amount of additional money a consumer would need after the change in order to be restored to their original level of utility—or, for a beneficial change, the maximum amount they could pay and still be as well off as before.

Definition

Formally, if prices change from p⁰ to p¹ and the consumer's income is m, compensating variation compares the expenditure needed to reach the original utility u⁰ at the new prices with the original income: CV = e(p¹, u⁰) − m, where e(·) is the expenditure function. A positive CV for a price increase represents the compensation required; it is related to the consumer's willingness to pay to avoid the change.

Relation to other measures

Compensating variation is one of several money-metric welfare measures developed from the work of John Hicks. It differs from equivalent variation, which measures the income change at original prices that would be equivalent in welfare to the change, and from consumer surplus, the Marshallian area under the demand curve, which lies between the two Hicksian measures when income effects are present. For changes with no income effects (quasilinear utility), all three coincide.

Applications

CV is used in cost–benefit analysis and tax policy to quantify welfare losses and gains, and underlies the Kaldor–Hicks compensation criterion, under which a policy is judged an improvement if gainers could in principle compensate losers.
