Einstein manifold

In differential geometry and mathematical physics, an Einstein manifold is a Riemannian or pseudo-Riemannian manifold whose Ricci tensor is proportional to the metric. They are named after Albert Einstein because this condition can be interpreted as saying that the metric solves the vacuum Einstein field equations (with cosmological constant), although neither dimension nor signature need match anything resembling realistic spacetime; a four-dimensional Lorentzian Einstein manifold is exactly a vacuum solution of general relativity with cosmological constant.

Definition

A Riemannian manifold (M, g) of dimension n > 2 is called an Einstein manifold if the Ricci curvature Ric and the metric g satisfy

Ric = λ g

for some constant λ, the Einstein constant. By the contracted Bianchi identity, λ is automatically constant when n ≥ 3 (a result sometimes called Schur's lemma). Einstein manifolds with λ > 0, λ = 0, and λ < 0 are called positive, Ricci-flat, and negative respectively. In dimension two the condition is automatic for metrics of constant Gaussian curvature; in dimension three it forces constant sectional curvature.

Examples

  • Constant sectional curvature: spheres, Euclidean space, and hyperbolic space are all Einstein.
  • Complex projective space with the Fubini–Study metric is Einstein with λ > 0.
  • Kähler–Einstein manifolds, including Calabi–Yau manifolds (Ricci-flat Kähler), central to string theory compactifications.
  • The Page metric on a complex projective plane blown up at a point — the first known inhomogeneous Einstein metric on a compact manifold.
  • Sasaki–Einstein manifolds, important in the AdS/CFT correspondence.
  • Kerr and Schwarzschild spacetimes: four-dimensional vacuum (Ricci-flat) solutions of Einstein's equations.

Obstructions and open problems

Not every smooth manifold admits an Einstein metric. Topological obstructions include the Hitchin–Thorpe inequality for compact four-manifolds and obstructions from the Seiberg–Witten invariants. In four dimensions, compact Einstein manifolds are still not classified, and longstanding questions — such as whether every compact Einstein manifold of positive scalar curvature is symmetric — remain active areas of research connecting Riemannian geometry with mathematical physics.
