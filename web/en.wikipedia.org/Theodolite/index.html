Theodolite

UsesMeasuring horizontal and vertical angles
Used inSurveying, construction, meteorology, navigation
Inventor (term)Leonard Digges (word in print, 1571)
RelatedTotal station, transit, dumpy level

A theodolite is a precision optical instrument for measuring angles between designated visible points in the horizontal and vertical planes. The traditional use has been for land surveying, but it has also been used extensively for building and infrastructure construction, and for some specialized applications such as rocket launching and meteorology. A modern theodolite consists of a movable telescope mounted within two perpendicular axes — the horizontal or trunnion axis, and the vertical axis — so that it can be pointed with great accuracy, with angles read from graduated circles or, in electronic instruments, displayed digitally.

History

The word derives from a survey instrument described and named by Leonard Digges in his posthumous Pantometria (1571), though the term was then applied to a simpler angle-measuring device. The recognizable modern form, with the telescope mounted to rotate in the vertical plane on graduated circles, was developed in the eighteenth century by instrument makers such as Jesse Ramsden, whose "Great Theodolite" of 1787 was used in the Principal Triangulation of Great Britain. Vernier scales, micrometers, and later glass-circle optical readouts steadily improved precision to fractions of an arcsecond.

Operation

The instrument is set on a tripod over a survey point, levelled carefully using its plate bubbles and footscrews, and centred over the mark with an optical plummet. The observer sights a target, clamps the motions, and reads the horizontal and vertical angles. Repeating observations with the telescope in its two "faces" cancels instrumental errors. Combined with distance measurement — by stadia hairs, tape, or electronic distance measurement — theodolites fix positions by triangulation and traversing. The digital theodolite and its descendant the total station, which integrates electronic angle and distance measurement with a microprocessor, have largely displaced purely optical models.
