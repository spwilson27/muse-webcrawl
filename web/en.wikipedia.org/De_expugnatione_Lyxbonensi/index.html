De expugnatione Lyxbonensi ("On the Conquest of Lisbon") is a Latin eyewitness account of the Siege of Lisbon in 1147, when a fleet of northern European crusaders en route to the Holy Land helped King Afonso I of Portugal capture the city of Lisbon from its Muslim rulers. It is the principal narrative source for the siege and one of the most vivid surviving accounts of the Second Crusade.

Authorship

The work is cast as a letter by an author who signs himself only "R." The Anglo-Norman priest who wrote it participated in the expedition, serving in the contingent from England. Modern scholarship, following the edition of Charles Wendell David (1936), attributes the letter to Ralph (Raol) of Eye in Suffolk, a priest who may have been attached to the household of Hervey de Glanvill, one of the fleet's leaders.

Content

The narrative begins with the assembling of the crusader fleet — some 164 ships from England, Flanders, Normandy, and the Rhineland — at Dartmouth in May 1147, its oath-bound association, and its stormy voyage to Porto. There the crusaders were persuaded by Afonso I and the bishop of Porto to divert to Lisbon under a treaty promising them the plunder of the city. The account then describes the siege in detail: the encampments, siege engines and mines, negotiations and speeches, and the eventual surrender of the city on 24 October 1147 after a siege of about seventeen weeks. It concludes with the author's frank report of disputes among the crusaders over the division of spoils and the installation of the Englishman Gilbert of Hastings as Lisbon's first bishop after the conquest.

Historical significance

Because it was written by a participant within months of the events, De expugnatione Lyxbonensi is prized for its circumstantial detail about medieval siege warfare, crusader organisation, and relations between the Portuguese crown and its foreign allies. It survives in a single manuscript preserved at Corpus Christi College, Cambridge.

Editions

The standard edition and translation is Charles Wendell David's De expugnatione Lyxbonensi: The Conquest of Lisbon (New York, 1936; reprinted 2001 with a foreword by Jonathan Phillips).
