Smelting

Tapping molten metal from a smelting furnace

Smelting is a process of applying heat and a chemical reducing agent to an ore to extract a desired base metal. It is a form of extractive metallurgy. The reducing agent, commonly a fossil fuel such as coke or, in earlier times, charcoal, undergoes oxidation, removing oxygen atoms from the ore and leaving the metal. The carbon or carbon monoxide derived from it captures the oxygen as CO and CO2.

Process

In a typical smelt, ore concentrate is mixed with a flux, such as limestone, and heated in a furnace. The flux combines with impurities to form a molten slag that floats on the denser molten metal and can be drawn off separately. Gases given off may themselves be valuable — sulphur dioxide from sulphide ores is commonly converted to sulphuric acid. Smelting differs from simple melting because it involves a chemical change: the metal is reduced from its compound in the ore.

History

Smelting is one of the foundational technologies of civilisation. Copper was smelted by the 6th millennium BCE, and cast copper appears at sites such as Çatalhöyük. Tin smelting combined with copper gave rise to the Bronze Age, and iron smelting — requiring higher temperatures achieved with forced draught — ushered in the Iron Age from about 1200 BCE in Anatolia and the Near East. The blast furnace, developed in China and later in medieval Europe, made large-scale iron production possible and underpinned the Industrial Revolution.

Environmental issues

Smelters are significant sources of air pollution, emitting sulphur dioxide, particulates and toxic metals such as lead, arsenic and cadmium. Historic smelting regions have suffered severe environmental damage, and modern plants use extensive gas-cleaning and capture systems.
