The public sphere (German: Öffentlichkeit) is an area of social life in which individuals can come together to discuss societal problems freely, and through that discussion influence political action. The concept was developed by the German philosopher and sociologist Jürgen Habermas in his 1962 book The Structural Transformation of the Public Sphere, which traced the emergence of a "bourgeois public sphere" in 17th- and 18th-century Europe — in coffeehouses, salons, reading societies, and the press — where private persons reasoned publicly about matters of common concern, mediating between civil society and the state.

Theory

In Habermas's account, the public sphere depends on freedom of assembly and expression, a press independent of state control, access for all citizens, and debate governed by argument rather than status. He argued that mass media, advertising, and the welfare state later commercialised and weakened this sphere — a "refeudalisation" of public life.

Criticism and development

Feminist and other scholars, notably Nancy Fraser, criticised Habermas for idealising a sphere that in practice excluded women, workers, and colonised peoples, and proposed instead multiple, overlapping "subaltern counterpublics". The concept has been extended to the internet and social media, whose role in strengthening or fragmenting public debate is a central question in contemporary democratic theory.
