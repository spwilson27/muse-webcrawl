Platonic solid

The five Platonic solids

In geometry, a Platonic solid is a convex, regular polyhedron in three-dimensional Euclidean space. Being a regular polyhedron means that its faces are congruent regular polygons (the same shape and size) and the same number of faces meet at each vertex. There are only five such solids: the tetrahedron (4 triangular faces), cube or hexahedron (6 square faces), octahedron (8 triangular faces), dodecahedron (12 pentagonal faces) and icosahedron (20 triangular faces).

History

The solids are named for the ancient Greek philosopher Plato, who wrote about them in the dialogue Timaeus (c. 360 BC), associating the tetrahedron, cube, octahedron and icosahedron with the classical elements fire, earth, air and water respectively, and the dodecahedron with the heavens or cosmos. Euclid gave a complete mathematical treatment in the final book of the Elements, and the fact that exactly five exist follows from angle constraints at each vertex. Johannes Kepler built his early cosmology on nested Platonic solids in the Mysterium Cosmographicum (1596) and later used the solids in his Harmonices Mundi.

Properties and occurrences

Each Platonic solid has a dual: the cube pairs with the octahedron, the dodecahedron with the icosahedron, and the tetrahedron with itself. Their symmetry groups are the tetrahedral, octahedral and icosahedral groups, the largest being the icosahedral group with 120 symmetries. In nature, the solids appear in crystal habits (fluorite octahedra, pyrite cubes), in the geometry of radiolarian skeletons, in virus capsids with icosahedral symmetry, and in carbon molecules such as dodecahedrane. They are also familiar as the shapes of dice used in tabletop games.
