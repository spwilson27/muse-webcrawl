Niccolò di Bernardo dei Machiavelli (3 May 1469 – 21 June 1527) was an Italian diplomat, author, philosopher, and historian who lived during the Renaissance. He is best known for his political treatise The Prince (Il Principe), written in about 1513 but not published until 1532, five years after his death. He has often been called the father of modern political philosophy and political science.

Career

Machiavelli served as a senior official in the Florentine Republic from 1498 to 1512, undertaking diplomatic missions to France, to the court of Cesare Borgia, and to the Papal States, and organizing a citizen militia. When the Medici returned to power in 1512 he was dismissed, arrested on suspicion of conspiracy, and tortured; after his release he retired to his estate at Sant'Andrea in Percussina and turned to writing.

Works and thought

The Prince offers pragmatic, often ruthless advice on acquiring and holding power, separating political effectiveness from conventional morality; the adjective "Machiavellian" later came to denote cunning and duplicity. The longer Discourses on the First Ten Books of Titus Livius presents his republican thought, praising civic virtue, popular participation, and the productive role of social conflict. He also wrote the comedy The Mandrake, an Art of War, and a history of Florence.
