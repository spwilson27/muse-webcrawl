Gender binary

The gender binary (also known as gender binarism) is the classification of gender into two distinct, opposite forms — masculine and feminine — whether by social system or cultural belief. Under this model, sex, gender and sexual orientation are assumed to align by default: a person assigned male at birth is expected to be masculine in appearance and behaviour and attracted to women, and vice versa.

Description

Most cultures historically recognised two genders, assigning roles, dress and occupations accordingly, and the binary is embedded in many institutions, from legal sex markers to gendered facilities and languages with grammatical gender. Anthropologists nonetheless document many societies with additional recognised categories — such as hijra in South Asia, fa'afafine in Samoa, burrnesha in Albania and various two-spirit roles in Indigenous North American cultures — which are cited as evidence that the binary is not universal.

Reception and critique

Critics, including many feminist and queer theorists, argue that the binary erases intersex people and those whose identities fall outside it, grouped under terms such as non-binary and genderqueer. Legal and social recognition of identities beyond the binary has grown since the early 21st century, with several countries allowing third-gender markers on documents. Supporters of the binary variously ground it in biology, tradition or social function; debates over its status remain a prominent feature of contemporary culture and politics.
