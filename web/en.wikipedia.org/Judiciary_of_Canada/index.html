Judiciary of Canada

The judiciary of Canada is the system of courts that interprets and applies the law in Canada. It comprises federal, provincial, and territorial courts organised in a pyramid, with the Supreme Court of Canada at its apex as the final court of appeal for the whole country.

Structure

Beneath the Supreme Court stand the federal courts — the Federal Court of Appeal, the Federal Court, the Tax Court, and the Court Martial Appeal Court — and the provincial and territorial court systems, each generally consisting of a court of appeal, a superior trial court, and a provincial (lower) court. Provincial superior courts, whose judges are appointed by the federal government under section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867, have inherent jurisdiction over most civil and criminal matters.

Appointment and independence

Federally appointed judges hold office during good behaviour until retirement at 75, with salaries set by Parliament — safeguards central to judicial independence, which the Supreme Court has held to be an unwritten constitutional principle. Complaints against federally appointed judges are handled by the Canadian Judicial Council. Since 1982 the courts have exercised strong judicial review under the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms.
