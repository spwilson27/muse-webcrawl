Public key infrastructure

A simplified PKI: a certificate authority issues certificates binding identities to public keys.

A public key infrastructure (PKI) is the set of roles, policies, hardware, software, and procedures needed to create, manage, distribute, store, and revoke digital certificates and manage public-key encryption. Its central purpose is to bind public keys to identities — people, devices, organisations, or websites — so that strangers can establish trust over insecure networks, for example to authenticate a website in TLS or to verify a digitally signed document.

Components

The core component is the certificate authority (CA), which issues X.509 certificates after a registration authority (RA) verifies the applicant's identity. Certificates, certificate revocation lists (CRLs), and status responders using OCSP are published so relying parties can check validity. Trust is organised hierarchically: devices ship with a store of self-signed root CA certificates; roots delegate to intermediate CAs, which issue end-entity (leaf) certificates. Key ceremonies, hardware security modules, and certificate policies govern the protection of CA private keys.

Applications and problems

The largest PKI is the Web PKI that underlies HTTPS: browsers trust hundreds of root CAs, and any of them can in principle issue a certificate for any domain — a weakness highlighted by breaches such as the 2011 compromise of DigiNotar, which issued fraudulent certificates used in interception attacks and soon went bankrupt. Mitigations include Certificate Transparency logs (public, append-only records of issued certificates), CAA DNS records restricting which CAs may issue for a domain, and shorter certificate lifetimes. Automated issuance via the ACME protocol, popularised by Let's Encrypt (launched 2015), made certificates free and ubiquitous and drove HTTPS adoption. PKI also secures email (S/MIME), code signing, smart cards and e-passports, and enterprise device management. An alternative trust model, the web of trust used by PGP, replaces central authorities with peer endorsements.
