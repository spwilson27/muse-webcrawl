The Whiskey Rebellion (also known as the Whiskey Insurrection) was a violent tax protest in the United States beginning in 1791 and culminating in 1794. It involved farmers and distillers in western Pennsylvania who resisted a federal excise tax on whiskey, the first tax imposed on a domestic product by the newly formed federal government.

Background

The excise, championed by Treasury Secretary Alexander Hamilton to fund the federal assumption of state debts, fell hardest on small frontier distillers, for whom whiskey — made from surplus grain — was a principal cash crop and medium of exchange, while large eastern distillers could pay a cheaper flat fee. Westerners viewed the tax as discriminatory and resented being summoned to distant federal courts in Philadelphia.

The rising

Resistance included intimidation and tarring and feathering of tax collectors. In July 1794, after federal marshal David Lenox and inspector John Neville served writs on delinquent distillers, armed men attacked Neville's home at Bower Hill; Neville's slaves and soldiers from Fort Pitt defended it, and Major James McFarlane, a rebel leader, was killed. Some 7,000 men then mustered at Braddock's Field, and radical leader David Bradford urged an attack on Pittsburgh, though the confrontation ended without a battle.

Suppression and aftermath

President George Washington, determined to uphold federal authority under the new Constitution, called out some 13,000 militiamen from several states and personally led them partway into Pennsylvania — the only time a sitting US president has commanded troops in the field. The rebellion dissolved without fighting. About 20 men were charged with treason; two were convicted and then pardoned by Washington. The tax remained difficult to collect and was repealed in 1802 under President Thomas Jefferson. The episode established that the federal government could enforce its laws and helped define the boundary between legitimate protest and insurrection in the early republic.
