The theory describes superconductors near the critical temperature.

Ginzburg–Landau theory is a phenomenological mathematical theory of superconductivity proposed in 1950 by the Soviet physicists Vitaly Ginzburg and Lev Landau. Based on Landau's general theory of second-order phase transitions, it describes a superconductor in terms of a complex-valued order parameter ψ whose squared magnitude gives the local density of superconducting electrons. Although it does not explain the microscopic mechanism of superconductivity (later supplied by BCS theory), the theory correctly describes the behaviour of superconductors near the critical temperature and remains a standard tool of condensed matter physics.

The free energy

Ginzburg and Landau wrote the free-energy density of a superconductor in a magnetic field as an expansion in the order parameter and its gradients:

f = fn + α|ψ|² + (β/2)|ψ|⁴ + (1/2m*)|(−iħ∇ − e*A)ψ|² + B²/2μ₀

where α changes sign at the critical temperature Tc, A is the magnetic vector potential, and e* and m* are the effective charge and mass of the superconducting carriers. Minimizing the free energy yields the two Ginzburg–Landau equations, which play a role analogous to the Schrödinger equation for ψ coupled to Maxwell's equations. Lev Gor'kov showed in 1959 that the equations can be derived from microscopic BCS theory near Tc, with e* = 2e — the charge of a Cooper pair — as the theory had anticipated.

Length scales and types of superconductor

The theory introduces two characteristic lengths: the coherence length ξ, over which ψ can vary, and the penetration depth λ, over which a magnetic field is screened. Their ratio, the Ginzburg–Landau parameter κ = λ/ξ, divides superconductors into two classes. For κ < 1/√2 the material is type I, expelling magnetic flux completely (the Meissner effect) up to a critical field. For κ > 1/√2 it is type II: above a lower critical field, flux penetrates in quantized vortices — predicted by Alexei Abrikosov in 1957 using the theory — each carrying one flux quantum, arranged in a triangular lattice, until superconductivity is destroyed at an upper critical field.

Applications and generalizations

Ginzburg–Landau equations are used to model vortex dynamics, critical currents and the behaviour of superconducting devices; the time-dependent version (TDGL) describes non-equilibrium phenomena. The same formalism — a free energy expanded in an order parameter — has been generalized across physics, from superfluid helium and liquid crystals to the Higgs mechanism in particle physics and models of the early universe. Ginzburg shared the 2003 Nobel Prize in Physics for pioneering contributions to the theory of superconductors and superfluids.
