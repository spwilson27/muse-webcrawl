Stele (biology)

In a vascular plant, the stele is the central part of the root or stem containing the tissues derived from the procambium. These include vascular tissue (xylem and phloem), sometimes pith, and, when present, a pericycle layer external to the vascular tissue. The concept of the stele was introduced by Philippe Van Tieghem and Henri Douliot in 1886 as a way of classifying plant anatomy by the arrangement of the primary vascular system.

Types

The simplest arrangement is the protostele, a solid central core of xylem surrounded by phloem, found in the earliest land plants and in many roots. A siphonostele has a central pith, and may be ectophloic or amphiphloic depending on whether phloem lies only outside or on both sides of the xylem. In seed plants the vascular system is typically an eustele, in which vascular bundles form a ring around the pith, while monocot stems characteristically have an atactostele of scattered bundles.

Significance

Stelar organisation is a major character in plant morphology and evolution: the transition from protosteles to siphonosteles and eusteles accompanies the evolution of leaves (microphylls and megaphylls) and the increasing size and complexity of land plants recorded in the fossil record.
