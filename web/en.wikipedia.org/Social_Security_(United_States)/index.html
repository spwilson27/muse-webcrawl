Social Security (United States)

A U.S. Social Security card, issued to nearly every American worker

Social Security is the commonly used term for the federal Old-Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance (OASDI) program in the United States, administered by the Social Security Administration. Established by the Social Security Act of 1935, signed by President Franklin D. Roosevelt as part of the New Deal, it provides monthly benefits to retired workers, their dependents, survivors of deceased workers, and people with disabilities.

How it works

Social Security is funded mainly by a payroll tax under the Federal Insurance Contributions Act (FICA), split between employees and employers, and operates largely on a pay-as-you-go basis: today's workers pay for today's beneficiaries. Workers earn credits through covered employment; benefit amounts depend on lifetime earnings and the age at which benefits are claimed (with full retirement age now 67 for those born in 1960 or later). Nearly all Americans receive a Social Security number, which has become a de facto national identifier.

Significance and debates

Social Security is the largest single program in the U.S. federal budget, paying benefits to roughly 66 million people, and it dramatically reduced poverty among the elderly. Long-running debates concern its long-term finances: trustees project that demographic change will deplete the combined trust fund reserves in the mid-2030s, after which payroll taxes would cover roughly 80% of scheduled benefits unless Congress acts.
