Qi (Chinese: 氣, pronounced [tɕʰî]; also romanised ch'i in Wade–Giles) is a fundamental concept in Chinese philosophy and traditional Chinese medicine, usually translated as "life force", "vital energy", or "material energy". The character combines the radicals for steam/vapour and rice, reflecting its earliest sense of air, breath, or vapour rising from cooking grain. In Chinese thought, qi is the circulating force that animates living beings and pervades the cosmos, and its balanced flow through the body is held to be essential to health.

Philosophical concept

Qi developed from an early word for air and weather into a universal medium of cosmology. In Warring States and Han-dynasty thought — systematised by thinkers influenced by Confucianism and Taoism — qi condensed to form the material world and dispersed at death; the interplay of yin and yang and the five phases (wood, fire, earth, metal, water) described its transformations. The philosopher Mencius spoke of cultivating a "vast, flowing qi" (haoran zhi qi) through moral conduct. Neo-Confucians such as Zhang Zai (11th century) taught that everything, even apparent emptiness, is qi in condensed or dispersed states. The concept spread across East and Southeast Asia, becoming ki in Japanese, gi in Korean, and khí in Vietnamese culture.

In traditional Chinese medicine

In Chinese medicine, qi flows through channels called meridians, and illness results from blockage, deficiency, or disordered flow. Different types are distinguished: ancestral qi inherited from one's parents, nutritive qi from food and air, and defensive qi (wei qi) protecting the body's surface. Treatments aim to restore flow and balance: acupuncture needles inserted at points along the meridians, moxibustion, herbal medicine, diet, and movement practices such as qigong and tai chi, which cultivate and direct qi through breathing and slow motion. Concepts similar to qi exist elsewhere — prana in Indian traditions and pneuma in Greek medicine.

Scientific evaluation

Qi is not recognised as a measurable physical quantity by modern science, and no anatomical structures corresponding to the meridians have been demonstrated. Critics classify qi as a pre-scientific, vitalistic notion comparable to discredited concepts such as phlogiston; measured physiological effects of practices like acupuncture and qigong are generally attributed to other mechanisms. Nonetheless, qi remains a living cultural and clinical concept, and the practices organised around it are widely used in China and around the world.
