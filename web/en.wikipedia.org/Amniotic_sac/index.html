The amniotic sac, also called the bag of waters or the membranes, is the fluid-filled sac in which the embryo and later fetus develops in amniotes. It consists of two membranes: the inner amnion and the outer chorion. The sac is filled with amniotic fluid, which surrounds and protects the developing fetus.

Development

The amnion begins to form early in the second week of human development as a small cavity within the epiblast. It expands to surround the embryo entirely, and the cavity fills with amniotic fluid. The chorion develops from trophoblast and extraembryonic mesoderm and forms the outermost fetal membrane, contributing the fetal part of the placenta.

Functions

The amniotic fluid cushions the fetus against mechanical shocks, allows free movement important for musculoskeletal development, prevents adhesions between fetal parts, helps regulate temperature, and provides a medium the fetus "breathes" and swallows, aiding lung and gastrointestinal development. The membranes also form a barrier against infection.

Rupture

During labour the sac normally ruptures ("water breaking"), releasing the fluid. Rupture before labour begins is called prelabour rupture of membranes, and if preterm is a major cause of premature birth. Artificial rupture of membranes (amniotomy) is sometimes performed to induce or augment labour.

Clinical uses

Fluid sampled by amniocentesis can be tested for chromosomal abnormalities and infections. Too little fluid (oligohydramnios) or too much (polyhydramnios) can indicate fetal or maternal problems.
