Motion Picture Association film rating system

Administered byMotion Picture Association (MPA), Classification and Rating Administration (CARA)

PredecessorHays Code (Motion Picture Production Code)

The Motion Picture Association film rating system is used in the United States and its territories to rate a motion picture's suitability for certain audiences based on its content. Introduced in 1968 under MPA president Jack Valenti, it replaced the restrictive Hays Code with a voluntary system intended to inform parents while protecting filmmakers' freedom.

The ratings

G (General audiences) — all ages admitted. PG (Parental guidance suggested) — some material may not be suitable for children. PG-13 (Parents strongly cautioned) — some material may be inappropriate for children under 13; added in 1984 after controversial PG films such as Gremlins and Indiana Jones and the Temple of Doom. R (Restricted) — under 17 requires an accompanying parent or adult guardian. NC-17 (Adults only) — no one 17 and under admitted; introduced in 1990 to replace the X rating, which had become associated with pornography.

Administration

Ratings are assigned by the Classification and Rating Administration, a board of parents based in Los Angeles, after viewing each submitted film. Filmmakers may edit and resubmit, or appeal. Trailers and advertising are also reviewed. The system is voluntary in law, but most theatres enforce the age restrictions and most distributors seek ratings.

Criticism

The system has been criticised for secrecy, for treating violence more leniently than sex or language, and for the commercial stigma attached to NC-17, which many theatres and retailers refuse to carry — effectively pressuring filmmakers to cut films to an R.
