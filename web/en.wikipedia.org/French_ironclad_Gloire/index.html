Gloire (French pronunciation: [ɡlwaʁ], "Glory") was the first ocean-going ironclad warship, launched by the French Navy in 1859. She was developed after the Crimean War, during which French floating batteries with iron armour had proved effective against Russian forts at the Battle of Kinburn in 1855. Designed by Henri Dupuy de Lôme, Gloire was wooden-hulled but sheathed over her full length in wrought-iron armour 120 mm thick.

Her launching rendered every wooden warship in the world obsolete and immediately triggered a naval arms race: Britain replied within a year with the all-iron HMS Warrior, which outclassed Gloire in speed, armour and construction. Nevertheless, Gloire is generally credited with beginning the ironclad era of warship design.

Design

Gloire was essentially an armoured version of a wooden ship of the line. Her hull was built of wood, covered with iron plates from the upper deck to below the waterline. She carried a single gun deck of 36 rifled muzzle-loading guns and combined a steam engine (a single-expansion horizontal engine driving one propeller) with a full sailing rig, like most warships of her transitional period. Two sister ships, Invincible and Normandie, followed.

Career

Commissioned in 1860, Gloire served with the Mediterranean fleet. Her wooden hull deteriorated relatively quickly, as was common with wooden-hulled ironclads whose timbers could not easily be inspected under the armour. She was stricken from the naval register in 1879 and scrapped in 1883.

Significance

Historians regard Gloire as a landmark in naval technology: the first seagoing armoured warship, ancestor of the battleship, and the spark of a Franco-British construction race that transformed the world's navies within a decade.
