Calvin cycle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Calvin cycle, light-independent reactions, bio synthetic phase, dark reactions, or photosynthetic carbon reduction (PCR) cycle of photosynthesis is a series of biochemical reactions that occur in the stroma of chloroplasts in photosynthetic organisms. The reactions use the ATP and NADPH produced by the light-dependent reactions to fix carbon dioxide into organic sugars. The cycle was discovered by Melvin Calvin, James Bassham and Andrew Benson at the University of California, Berkeley, using the radioactive isotope carbon-14; Calvin received the 1961 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the work. It is named for Melvin Calvin and is unrelated to any other "Calvin" in the sciences or arts.

Stages

  1. Carbon fixation – the enzyme RuBisCO catalyses the carboxylation of ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate (RuBP, a 5-carbon sugar) by CO2, producing two molecules of 3-phosphoglycerate (3-PGA).
  2. Reduction – ATP and NADPH from the light reactions convert 3-PGA into glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate (G3P), a three-carbon sugar phosphate; some G3P leaves the cycle to build glucose and other carbohydrates.
  3. Regeneration – the remaining G3P is rearranged, consuming more ATP, to regenerate the RuBP acceptor so the cycle can continue.

For every three turns of the cycle, one net G3P molecule is produced; fixing six CO2 molecules yields the equivalent of one glucose, at a cost of 18 ATP and 12 NADPH.

Significance

The Calvin cycle is the principal route by which inorganic carbon enters the biosphere, and variants of it (C4 and CAM photosynthesis) pre-concentrate CO2 around RuBisCO to improve efficiency in hot, dry environments. RuBisCO is thought to be the most abundant protein on Earth.
