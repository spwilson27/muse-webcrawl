The Magic Mountain

AuthorThomas Mann
Original titleDer Zauberberg
Published1924
LanguageGerman
GenreBildungsroman; novel of ideas

The Magic Mountain (German: Der Zauberberg) is a novel by Thomas Mann, first published in November 1924. It is widely considered one of the most influential works of 20th-century German literature and a central text of European modernism.

Plot

Hans Castorp, a young, unremarkable shipbuilding engineer from Hamburg, travels to Davos in the Swiss Alps to visit his tubercular cousin Joachim at the Berghof sanatorium, intending to stay three weeks. Diagnosed with a "moist spot" on his own lung, he remains for seven years, until the outbreak of the First World War. Immersed in the hermetic world of the sanatorium, where time stretches and distorts, Castorp is exposed to a parade of philosophical mentors: the humanist rationalist Lodovico Settembrini, the reactionary Jesuit-trained Leo Naphta, the sensual Russian Madame Chauchat, and the Dutch coffee magnate Mynheer Peeperkorn. Their debates — on time, illness, death, progress, terror and love — form the novel's intellectual core, culminating in Castorp's visionary "Snow" chapter and a near-duel between Settembrini and Naphta.

Themes

Mann described the book as a novel about time and as a kind of ironic, heightened Bildungsroman. The sanatorium functions as a microcosm of a European bourgeois civilisation suspended above the "flatland" below — decadent, death-haunted and intellectually feverish — in the years before the catastrophe of 1914. Illness is portrayed both as decay and as a perverse route to insight.

Background and reception

Mann began the work in 1912 after visiting his wife Katia during her stay at a Davos sanatorium, originally intending a comic novella in the spirit of Death in Venice. The First World War interrupted and transformed it into a vast novel. On its publication it confirmed Mann's stature in German letters; he received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1929, largely for his earlier Buddenbrooks, but The Magic Mountain is now generally regarded as his masterpiece. Major English translations are by H. T. Lowe-Porter (1927) and John E. Woods (1995).
