English Renaissance

The English Renaissance was a cultural and artistic movement in England from the late 15th century to the early 17th century. It is associated with the pan-European Renaissance that is usually regarded as beginning in Italy in the late 14th century. Like most of northern Europe, England saw little of these developments until more than a century later. Renaissance style and ideas, however, were slow to penetrate England, and the Elizabethan era in the second half of the 16th century is usually regarded as the height of the English Renaissance. Many scholars, however, debate about whether the term "Renaissance" is even appropriate for this time period.

Characteristics

The dominant art forms of the English Renaissance were literature and music. Visual arts in the English Renaissance were much less significant than in the Italian Renaissance. The English period began far later than the Italian, which is usually dated to the mid-to-late 14th century, and the English Renaissance reached its peak in the Elizabethan and early Stuart periods.

Literature

England had a strong tradition of literature in the English vernacular, which gradually increased as English use of the printing press became common by the mid-16th century. This tradition of literature written in English vernacular largely began with the Protestant Reformation's call to let all people read the Bible. Figures such as William Shakespeare, Christopher Marlowe, Edmund Spenser, Ben Jonson and John Donne defined the era's drama and poetry; the King James Bible (1611) fixed much of the language's literary register.

Music

English Renaissance music can be divided into early and late periods. Composers such as Thomas Tallis, William Byrd, John Dowland and Orlando Gibbons flourished at the Elizabethan and Jacobean courts; the madrigal and the lute song were characteristic forms, and Byrd's Latin church music survived the Reformation's liturgical upheavals.

Architecture and visual art

Architecture moved slowly from Perpendicular Gothic to classical forms: prodigy houses such as Hardwick Hall displayed symmetry and vast glass, while Inigo Jones introduced true Palladianism in the early 17th century. Portraiture, particularly the miniature painting of Nicholas Hilliard, was the leading visual art.
