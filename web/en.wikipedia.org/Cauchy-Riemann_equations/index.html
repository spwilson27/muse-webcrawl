Cauchy–Riemann equations

In the mathematical field of complex analysis, the Cauchy–Riemann equations, named after Augustin Cauchy and Bernhard Riemann, consist of a system of two partial differential equations which form a necessary and sufficient condition for a complex function of a complex variable to be complex differentiable (holomorphic).

Statement

Writing a complex function as f(z) = u(x, y) + i·v(x, y), where u and v are real-valued functions of the real variables x and y, the Cauchy–Riemann equations are:

∂u/∂x = ∂v/∂y   and   ∂u/∂y = −∂v/∂x.

If u and v are continuously differentiable and satisfy these equations on an open set, then f is holomorphic there; conversely, every holomorphic function satisfies them.

Interpretation

Geometrically, the equations express that the Jacobian of the map (u, v) is a rotation combined with a scaling — that is, the map is conformal, preserving angles. Physically, u and v can be interpreted as the potential and stream functions of a two-dimensional irrotational, incompressible flow, and both are harmonic functions satisfying Laplace's equation.

History

The equations appeared first in the work of d'Alembert in the 18th century in connection with fluid dynamics. Cauchy used them systematically to build his theory of complex functions, and Riemann made them a foundation of his function theory in his 1851 dissertation.
