Oligomer

In chemistry and biochemistry, an oligomer is a molecule composed of a small number of repeating monomer units — more than one, but far fewer than the hundreds or thousands found in a polymer. The word derives from the Greek oligo-, "a few", and -mer, "part". There is no universally agreed upper limit; molecules of roughly two to twenty units are commonly classed as oligomers.

Terminology

Oligomers are named by the number of their units: a dimer has two, a trimer three, a tetramer four, a pentamer five, and so on. When all the subunits are identical the molecule is a homooligomer; when they differ it is a heterooligomer. The process of forming oligomers is oligomerisation.

Examples

Familiar oligomers include oligosaccharides such as raffinose and stachyose (short chains of sugars), oligopeptides (short amino-acid chains), and oligonucleotides — short DNA or RNA strands, typically 13–25 bases, widely used as PCR primers, probes and antisense agents. In industry, oligomers of ethylene and propylene serve as lubricants, plasticisers and surfactant intermediates, and low-molecular-weight oligomers are the reactive components of many resins, coatings and adhesives.

Oligomers in biology

Many proteins function as oligomeric complexes: haemoglobin is a tetramer of two α and two β subunits, and numerous enzymes, ion channels and receptors assemble from several polypeptide chains. Protein oligomerisation affects stability, regulation and allosteric control. Small soluble oligomers of amyloid proteins are also implicated in the toxicity of neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer's disease.
