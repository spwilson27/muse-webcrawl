Shakespeare's plays

Shakespeare's plays are a body of dramatic works written by the English playwright William Shakespeare (1564–1616) between roughly 1589 and 1613. The canon conventionally comprises 38 or 39 plays, divided by the editors of the First Folio (1623) into comedies, histories and tragedies. Together with his sonnets and narrative poems, the plays form the most influential body of dramatic literature in English, and have been translated into every major living language.

Publication and survival

During Shakespeare's lifetime, about half of the plays were published individually in inexpensive quarto editions, some of them "bad quartos" apparently reconstructed from memory by actors. In 1623, seven years after Shakespeare's death, his fellow actors John Heminges and Henry Condell published the First Folio, Mr. William Shakespeare's Comedies, Histories, & Tragedies, preserving 36 plays — 18 of them printed for the first time, including Macbeth, The Tempest and Twelfth Night, which might otherwise have been lost. Pericles, Prince of Tyre was added in the Third Folio (1664), and The Two Noble Kinsmen, written with John Fletcher, is now widely accepted into the canon.

The genres

Comedies

The comedies range from the farcical The Comedy of Errors to the festive masterpieces of the late 1590s — A Midsummer Night's Dream, Much Ado About Nothing, As You Like It and Twelfth Night. The darker "problem plays", such as Measure for Measure and All's Well That Ends Well, blur generic boundaries, while the late romances — Cymbeline, The Winter's Tale and The Tempest — mix comedy with tragedy and magic.

Histories

The history plays dramatise the reigns of English kings from Richard II to Henry VIII, in two tetralogies covering the Wars of the Roses: from Richard II through Henry IV, Parts 1 and 2 and Henry V, and from the three parts of Henry VI to Richard III. They remain central to English national myth-making.

Tragedies

The tragedies include the great sequence written for the King's Men after 1603: Hamlet, Othello, King Lear and Macbeth, alongside Roman plays such as Julius Caesar, Antony and Cleopatra and Coriolanus, and the early Titus Andronicus and Romeo and Juliet.

Collaboration and attribution

Early modern playwriting was frequently collaborative, and modern attribution studies assign co-authors to several plays: George Peele in Titus Andronicus, Thomas Middleton in Timon of Athens and Macbeth (adaptation), and John Fletcher in Henry VIII and The Two Noble Kinsmen. The lost play Cardenio and the disputed Edward III remain active subjects of scholarship.

Chronology and performance

Shakespeare wrote for the Lord Chamberlain's Men (later the King's Men), performing at the Theatre, the Curtain, the Globe and the Blackfriars. The plays have been in continuous performance ever since, and are the most performed and adapted dramatic works in the world.
