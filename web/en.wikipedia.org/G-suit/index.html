G-suit

An anti-G suit worn by a fighter pilot

A G-suit, more accurately an anti-G suit, is a flight suit worn by aviators and astronauts who are subject to high levels of acceleration force (G). It is designed to prevent a blackout and G-induced loss of consciousness (G-LOC) caused by the blood pooling in the lower part of the body when under acceleration, thus depriving the brain of blood.

Design

The suit fits tightly around the legs and abdomen and contains inflatable bladders. When the aircraft accelerates, compressed air — taken from the engine compressor stages — is fed into the bladders through a G-sensitive valve. The pressure is proportional to the acceleration, typically beginning at about 2 G. As the bladders inflate, they squeeze the legs and abdomen, restricting the downward pooling of blood and helping return blood to the upper body and brain.

History

The first G-suits were developed during the Second World War. In Canada, Wilbur R. Franks and his team at the University of Toronto developed a water-filled suit; a version of it was used operationally by Royal Air Force Hurricane and Spitfire pilots in 1942 during operations over Oran. In the United States, air-filled suits using rubber bladders were developed and became standard equipment for combat pilots by the end of the war.

Effectiveness and use

A modern anti-G suit increases a pilot's G tolerance by roughly 1 to 1.5 G on its own. Combined with pressure breathing for G (PBG) and the anti-G straining manoeuvre (AGSM), trained pilots can sustain up to about 9 G. G-suits remain standard equipment for fighter pilots, aerobatic pilots, and astronaut crews during launch and re-entry.
