John Tyndall

Born2 August 1820, Leighlinbridge, County Carlow, Ireland
Died4 December 1893 (aged 73), Hindhead, Surrey, England

AwardsRoyal Medal (1853), Rumford Medal (1864)

John Tyndall FRS (2 August 1820 – 4 December 1893) was a prominent 19th-century Irish physicist whose initial reputation rested on his studies of diamagnetism and radiant heat. He is also remembered for demonstrating the greenhouse effect of atmospheric gases, the Tyndall effect in colloids, and for popularising science.

Life and career

Tyndall worked as a surveyor and railway engineer before studying in Germany at Marburg, where he took his doctorate in 1850. In 1853 he became professor of natural philosophy at the Royal Institution in London, succeeding his mentor Michael Faraday as superintendent in 1867.

Radiant heat and the atmosphere

Using sensitive thermopiles, Tyndall showed in 1859 that water vapour, carbon dioxide and other gases absorb infrared radiation while oxygen and nitrogen do not — the physical basis of the greenhouse effect. He also measured the scattering of light by particles (the Tyndall effect, which explains the blue of the sky) and studied glacier motion and spontaneous generation, defending Pasteur's germ theory with "Tyndallisation" sterilisation.

Public science and controversy

A charismatic lecturer and mountaineer, Tyndall was a leading scientific naturalist; his 1874 Belfast Address, defending materialism and evolution, provoked religious controversy. He died in 1893 from an accidental overdose of chloral hydrate administered by his wife.
