Pythagoreanism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pythagoreanism originated in the 6th century BC, based on and around the teachings and beliefs held by Pythagoras and his followers, the Pythagoreans. Pythagoras established the first Pythagorean community in the ancient Greek colony of Kroton, in modern Calabria (Italy). Early Pythagorean communities spread throughout Magna Graecia.

Already during Pythagoras's life, the idea that all things are number and the universe is governed by musical ratios animated early Pythagoreans. The community combined religious ritual and a way of life — including dietary rules, secrecy and communal property — with pioneering work in mathematics and astronomy.

Ideas

Central Pythagorean themes include metempsychosis (the transmigration of souls), the belief that number is the principle of all things, the harmony of the spheres — the idea that the movements of the planets produce a cosmic music — and the sacred tetractys, a triangular figure of ten points. The discovery attributed to the school of the theorem about right triangles bearing Pythagoras's name, and the crisis provoked by incommensurable (irrational) magnitudes, became legendary parts of the tradition.

History

Political hostility destroyed the Pythagorean communities in southern Italy around the mid-fifth century BC, though Pythagorean thought continued through figures such as Philolaus and Archytas of Tarentum, a friend of Plato. Plato's philosophy absorbed Pythagorean mathematics and cosmology (as in the Timaeus), and in the first century BC the school was revived as Neopythagoreanism, influencing later Neoplatonism.

Influence

Pythagoreanism shaped the Western ideal of a mathematical order underlying nature, running from Plato through Kepler and into modern mathematical physics, and its vegetarianism, communal ideals and numerical mysticism have recurred throughout Western esotericism.
