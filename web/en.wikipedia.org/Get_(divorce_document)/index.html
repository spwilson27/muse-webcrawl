A get (Hebrew: גט; plural gittin) is a document in Jewish law that effects the dissolution of a marriage. Under halakha (Jewish religious law), a Jewish marriage can only be ended religiously when the husband voluntarily gives his wife a get, written by a scribe and delivered in the presence of a rabbinical court; a civil divorce alone does not end the religious marriage.

Procedure

The get is handwritten in Aramaic by a trained scribe, traditionally in twelve lines, at the husband's instruction and specifically for the named couple. It states that the husband releases his wife and that she is "permitted to any man". The document is folded, signed by witnesses, and dropped by the husband into the wife's hands in a formal ceremony before the beit din; she then walks a short distance to signify acceptance, and the court retains or records the get and issues certificates to both parties.

The agunah problem

Because the husband's consent is required, a woman whose husband refuses to give a get — or who has disappeared without proof of death — becomes an agunah ("chained woman"), unable to remarry within traditional Jewish law and whose children from a new union would face severe status problems. This has generated controversy and various proposed remedies, including prenuptial agreements obliging a recalcitrant spouse, sanctions by rabbinical courts, and, in Israel, imprisonment of refusing husbands. Secular legal systems generally do not recognise the get, so couples may need both a civil divorce and a get.
