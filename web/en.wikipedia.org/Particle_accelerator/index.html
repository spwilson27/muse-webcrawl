Particle accelerator

A particle accelerator is a machine that uses electromagnetic fields to propel charged particles to very high speeds and energies, and to contain them in well-defined beams. Large accelerators are used in particle physics as colliders, while smaller ones serve in particle therapy for oncology, as synchrotron light sources, in materials science, and in other applications.

A large circular accelerator complex, with the ring of a collider visible from the air.

History

Early accelerators included the Cockcroft–Walton generator and the Van de Graaff generator, both electrostatic machines developed in the early 1930s. Ernest Lawrence invented the cyclotron in 1929, in which particles spiral outward in a magnetic field while being accelerated by a radio-frequency electric field. The synchrotron, developed in the 1940s, varies both the magnetic field and the accelerating frequency so that particles travel along a fixed circular path, enabling much higher energies.

Types

Accelerators fall into two broad classes: linear accelerators (linacs), in which particles travel once along a straight line of accelerating structures, and circular accelerators, in which beams circulate many times. In a collider, two beams are accelerated in opposite directions and made to collide head-on, making far more energy available for creating new particles than collisions against a fixed target.

Notable facilities

The largest and most powerful accelerator is the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) at CERN near Geneva, a 27-kilometre ring that collides protons at energies of up to 13 TeV in the centre-of-mass frame. It was used in the discovery of the Higgs boson in 2012. Other major facilities include SLAC in California, Fermilab in Illinois, DESY in Hamburg, and synchrotron light sources such as the ESRF and Diamond Light Source.
