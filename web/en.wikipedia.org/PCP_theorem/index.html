PCP theorem

In computational complexity theory, the PCP theorem (also known as the PCP characterization of NP) states that every decision problem in the complexity class NP has probabilistically checkable proofs that can be verified by a probabilistic algorithm using only a logarithmic number of random bits and reading a constant number of bits from the proof.

Formally, the theorem states that NP = PCP[O(log n), O(1)]: for any NP statement, there exists a proof format (an encoding of an ordinary proof) such that a verifier, using O(log n) random bits, reading only a constant number of bits of the proof, will accept correct proofs with probability 1 and reject purported proofs of false statements with probability at least one-half.

History

The theorem was proved in the early 1990s through the combined work of many researchers, culminating in the 1992 papers by Sanjeev Arora and Shafi Goldwasser and colleagues, and by Arora, Carsten Lund, Rajeev Motwani, Madhu Sudan and Mario Szegedy, who showed PCP[O(log n), O(1)] ⊆ NP. The result revolutionised the theory of approximation algorithms, because it implies that for many NP-hard optimisation problems it is NP-hard to approximate the optimum to within some constant factor. In 2005, Irit Dinur gave a substantially simpler proof of the theorem based on expander graphs and iterative "gap amplification".

Consequences

The PCP theorem is the foundation of the modern theory of hardness of approximation. Among its earliest consequences were optimal inapproximability results for problems such as MAX-3SAT and the maximum clique problem. The closely related Unique Games Conjecture, proposed by Subhash Khot in 2002, seeks even sharper inapproximability thresholds for many problems.
