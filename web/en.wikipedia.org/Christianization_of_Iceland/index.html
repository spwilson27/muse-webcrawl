Christianization of Iceland

The Christianization of Iceland was the process by which Iceland adopted Christianity around the year 1000 CE. Unusually in European history, the conversion was accomplished not by conquest or royal decree alone but by a legal decision of the Althing, Iceland's national assembly, which voted to adopt the new faith while initially permitting certain pagan practices in private. The principal narrative source is Ari Þorgilsson's Íslendingabók, written in the early twelfth century.

Background

Iceland was settled in the late ninth and tenth centuries mainly by Norse pagans, though some settlers from the British Isles were Christians or mixed the faiths. From the 980s, missionaries backed by King Olaf Tryggvason of Norway — who used pressure including hostages and trade embargoes — pressed Iceland to convert. The missionary Thangbrand's violent mission of 997–999 deepened divisions between Christian and pagan chieftains and threatened civil war.

The decision of 1000

At the Althing in the summer of 1000, with armed factions on the verge of splitting the country into two legal communities, the pagan lawspeaker Þorgeir Þorkelsson was asked to decide. According to Ari, he lay under a cloak in silent deliberation for a day and a night, then proclaimed that all Icelanders should be baptized and worship one God under one law — but that the old customs of exposing infants and eating horse meat, and sacrifice in secret, would for the time being remain permitted. Both sides accepted the arbitration.

Aftermath

The church grew steadily; the private concessions were abolished within a few years. Iceland's first native bishop, Ísleifur Gissurarson, was consecrated in 1056 and established a see at Skálholt, followed by a second at Hólar in 1106. Monasteries, tithes (introduced in 1097, earlier than in most of Europe), and church schools made the Church a central institution of medieval Iceland.
