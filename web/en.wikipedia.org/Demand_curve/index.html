Demand curve

A demand curve is a graph of the relationship between the price of a good or service and the quantity demanded over a given period, drawn under the assumption that all other influences on demand are held constant (ceteris paribus). By convention — going back to Alfred Marshall — price is plotted on the vertical axis and quantity on the horizontal axis, even though price is treated as the independent variable. Demand curves typically slope downward from left to right, expressing the law of demand.

Individual and market demand

An individual demand curve shows the quantities one consumer will buy at each price; it can be derived from the consumer's preferences and budget constraint via indifference-curve analysis. The market demand curve is the horizontal sum of all individual demand curves: at each price, the quantities of all buyers are added together. In mathematical form the (inverse) demand function is written P = f(Q); its slope and position encode willingness to pay and hence the demand side of the market.

Movements and shifts

A change in the good's own price produces a movement along the curve (a change in quantity demanded). A change in income, the price of a related good, tastes, expectations, or the number of buyers shifts the curve: an increase in demand shifts it rightward (more demanded at every price), a decrease leftward. Distinguishing these two cases is fundamental in applied analysis — for example, when interpreting how a tax, subsidy, or supply shock affects equilibrium price and quantity.

Elasticity along the curve

The slope of the curve is not the same as the price elasticity of demand, which varies along a straight-line curve: demand is elastic on the upper (high-price) portion, unit-elastic at the midpoint, and inelastic on the lower portion. The elasticity at a point determines whether a price change raises or lowers total revenue.

Exceptions and shapes

Most demand curves slope downward, but economists discuss possible exceptions: Giffen goods (an inferior staple whose income effect outweighs the substitution effect), Veblen goods (valued partly for their conspicuous price), and speculative assets. Industry demand curves faced by firms also vary with market structure — a monopolist faces the whole market demand curve, while a perfectly competitive firm faces a horizontal (perfectly elastic) curve at the market price.
