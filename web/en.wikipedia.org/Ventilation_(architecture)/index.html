Ventilation (architecture)

Ventilation is the intentional introduction of outdoor air into a space, primarily to control indoor air quality by diluting and displacing indoor pollutants. It is also used for thermal comfort and dehumidification. Ventilation is a central concern of building science and of heating, ventilation, and air conditioning (HVAC) design.

Types

Natural ventilation

Natural ventilation uses wind pressure and buoyancy (the stack effect) to move air through openings such as windows, vents, and atria, without mechanical power. Traditional techniques include windcatchers in Persian architecture and operable clerestories. It saves energy but offers less precise control.

Mechanical ventilation

Mechanical systems use fans and ductwork to supply and exhaust air at controlled rates. Configurations include exhaust-only, supply-only, and balanced systems; balanced systems often incorporate heat recovery ventilators or energy recovery ventilators that transfer heat and moisture between airstreams to reduce energy costs.

Mixed-mode ventilation

Mixed-mode (hybrid) buildings combine natural and mechanical strategies, switching according to season, outdoor conditions, and occupancy.

Standards and rates

Ventilation requirements are set by codes and standards such as ASHRAE Standard 62.1, which specifies minimum outdoor air rates per person and per unit floor area for various occupancies. Demand-controlled ventilation adjusts rates using carbon dioxide sensors as a proxy for occupancy. Inadequate ventilation is associated with sick building syndrome, while proper ventilation reduces airborne transmission of respiratory infections.

History

Concern with "vitiated air" grew in the 19th century; Florence Nightingale championed fresh air in hospitals, and early mechanical systems were installed in parliaments, theatres, and mines. Modern practice balances air quality against the energy cost of conditioning outdoor air.
