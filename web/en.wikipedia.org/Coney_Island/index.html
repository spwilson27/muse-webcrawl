Coney Island is a neighbourhood and entertainment area in the southwestern part of the borough of Brooklyn in New York City. Once a true island separated from Brooklyn by Coney Island Creek, landfill partially joined it to the mainland in the early twentieth century. It is famous for its beach, boardwalk, and amusement parks, and was one of the most popular seaside resorts in the United States in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.

History

Early development

Dutch settlers named the area Conyne Eylandt, likely after the rabbits (coneys) found there. After the American Civil War, railways made it easily reachable from Manhattan, and hotels, bathhouses and racetracks proliferated.

The amusement park era

Three great amusement parks defined Coney Island's golden age: Steeplechase Park (1897–1964), Luna Park (1903–1944) and Dreamland (1904–1911), which was destroyed by fire. They pioneered mechanical rides and mass entertainment for a nickel. The Wonder Wheel (1920) and the Cyclone wooden roller coaster (1927) survive as landmarks, and the Parachute Jump, built for the 1939 New York World's Fair, still stands as a symbol of the area.

Decline and revival

Coney Island declined after the Second World War as visitors shifted to car-based destinations, and Steeplechase closed in 1964. The neighbourhood struggled with disinvestment for decades, but the boardwalk and beach remained popular, and a revival began in the 2000s with the opening of a new Luna Park in 2010 and redevelopment around the amusement district. The area was badly flooded by Hurricane Sandy in 2012 but largely recovered.

Culture

Coney Island is home to Nathan's Famous, whose hot dog eating contest is held every Fourth of July, and to the Coney Island Mermaid Parade, founded in 1983. The New York Aquarium, the oldest continually operating aquarium in the United States, is also located there. The Riegelmann Boardwalk stretches some 2.7 miles along the Atlantic beach.
