The canonical gospels are the four gospels accepted as part of the New Testament canon: the Gospel of Matthew, the Gospel of Mark, the Gospel of Luke, and the Gospel of John. They are ancient biographies of the life, teaching, death, and resurrection of Jesus of Nazareth, written in Koine Greek between roughly AD 66 and 110.

The four gospels

Matthew, Mark, and Luke are known as the Synoptic Gospels because they share much of their content, structure, and wording, and can be read in parallel ("with the same eye"). Most scholars hold that Mark was written first and that Matthew and Luke used it, together with a hypothetical sayings source called Q. John's gospel differs substantially in style and content, presenting extended discourses and a high Christology, and includes material — such as the raising of Lazarus — absent from the synoptics.

Canon formation

Many other gospels circulated in early Christianity (such as the gospels of Thomas, Peter, and Judas). By the late 2nd century, Irenaeus of Lyons argued that there could be exactly four authentic gospels. The fourfold canon was widely accepted by the 4th century and was confirmed in lists such as Athanasius's Festal Letter of 367. The non-canonical works are generally classified among the New Testament apocrypha.
