Mitochondrial DNA

Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA or mDNA) is the DNA located in mitochondria, cellular organelles within eukaryotic cells that convert chemical energy from food into a form that cells can use, such as adenosine triphosphate (ATP). Mitochondrial DNA is only a small portion of the DNA in a eukaryotic cell; most of the DNA can be found in the cell nucleus.

The human mitochondrial genome

In humans, mitochondrial DNA spans about 16,500 base pairs and is typically a single circular chromosome — a remnant of the genome of the bacterium from which mitochondria evolved. It contains 37 genes: 13 encoding subunits of the oxidative phosphorylation complexes, 22 transfer RNAs and 2 ribosomal RNAs. A cell contains hundreds to thousands of copies, far more than the two copies of each nuclear chromosome.

Inheritance

In most multicellular organisms, mtDNA is inherited from the mother (egg) only, because the mitochondria of the sperm are generally destroyed after fertilisation. This uniparental inheritance, combined with a relatively rapid mutation rate, makes mtDNA a powerful tool for tracing maternal lineages: population geneticists use it to reconstruct human migrations, and the hypothetical most recent common matrilineal ancestor of all living humans is known as Mitochondrial Eve. It is also widely used in forensic identification and in ancestry testing.

Disease

Mutations in mtDNA cause a range of mitochondrial diseases, including Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy and MELAS, whose severity depends on the proportion of mutant copies (heteroplasmy). Accumulation of mtDNA mutations with age has also been implicated in ageing.
