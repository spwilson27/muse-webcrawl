Extensional tectonics

Extensional tectonics is the branch of structural geology concerned with the structures formed by, and the tectonic processes associated with, the stretching of the Earth's crust or lithosphere. Extension thins the crust and produces normal faults, rift basins and, if continued, new ocean basins.

Structures

The characteristic structures of extensional regimes are planar and listric normal faults, which sole into shallowly dipping detachment faults, and the fault-bounded blocks between them: down-dropped grabens, uplifted horsts and tilted fault blocks. Large amounts of extension can exhume deep crustal rocks in metamorphic core complexes, where a ductile lower crust is drawn out from beneath a brittle, faulted upper plate. Half-grabens, bounded by a single major fault, dominate many continental rifts.

Settings

Extension occurs at divergent plate boundaries such as the East African Rift and the Basin and Range Province of western North America, in back-arc basins behind subduction zones such as the Aegean Sea, at passive margins, and in zones of gravitational collapse of thickened orogenic crust, as in parts of Tibet and the Alps. The degree of stretching is often quantified by a stretching factor, beta, comparing final to initial crustal thickness.

Models

The Wernicke simple-shear model and the McKenzie pure-shear model describe contrasting ways the lithosphere can extend; the McKenzie model underlies the standard account of sedimentary basin formation by thermal subsidence following rifting, of great importance in petroleum geology, since rift basins host many major hydrocarbon provinces.
