Appeal

In law, an appeal is the process in which cases are reviewed by a higher authority, where the losing party in a lawsuit requests a formal change to an official decision. Appeals function both as a process for error correction as well as a process of clarifying and interpreting law.

Nature of appellate review

An appellate court generally does not retry the facts of a case. Instead, it reviews the record of the proceedings below — transcripts, exhibits, and the lower court's decisions — for errors of law, procedural mistakes, or, in some systems, findings of fact that no reasonable fact-finder could have made. Possible outcomes include affirming, reversing, vacating, or modifying the judgment, or remanding the case for further proceedings.

Appeals as of right and discretionary review

Many systems grant an appeal as of right from a final judgment to an intermediate appellate court. Review by the highest court is often discretionary, granted through mechanisms such as certiorari in the United States or leave to appeal in Commonwealth jurisdictions. Parties typically must first object at trial to preserve an issue for appeal, and appeals must usually be filed within strict time limits.

History

Appellate structures developed from the writ of error in English common law and from Roman and canon-law traditions of recourse to higher tribunals. Modern appellate courts emerged as judicial hierarchies were formalized in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.
