Letters from a Stoic is the common English title of the Epistulae Morales ad Lucilium ("Moral Letters to Lucilius"), a collection of 124 letters written by the Roman Stoic philosopher Seneca the Younger near the end of his life, around AD 62–65, after his retirement from the court of Nero. Addressed to Lucilius Junior, then procurator of Sicily, the letters form a course of spiritual direction and are among the most widely read works of ancient philosophy.

Contents and themes

Although framed as private correspondence, the letters were plainly composed for publication. Early letters are short and each ends with a maxim drawn (often critically) from Epicurus; later letters grow into full essays. Recurring themes include the wise use of time — the opening letter urges Lucilius to "hold every hour in your grasp" — the fear of death, the proper attitude to wealth and slavery, friendship, old age, and the discipline of daily self-examination. Famous letters include Letter 7 on avoiding crowds, Letter 12 on old age, Letter 18 on practising poverty, Letter 47 on treating slaves humanely, and Letter 88 on liberal education.

Style and influence

Seneca's pointed, aphoristic Latin style — short sentences, paradoxes, and direct address — made the Letters a model for Montaigne and for the European essay tradition. The work remained a staple of moral reading through the Middle Ages and Renaissance; the title Letters from a Stoic comes from Robin Campbell's popular 1969 Penguin translation, and modern interest in Stoicism has made the letters a bestseller again in recent decades.
