Full-frame digital SLR

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A full-frame digital SLR (or DSLR) is a digital single-lens reflex camera whose image sensor is the same size as a frame of 35 mm film — approximately 36 × 24 mm. The term distinguishes these cameras from the majority of DSLRs and interchangeable-lens cameras, which use smaller sensors such as APS-C (with a crop factor of roughly 1.5×) or Four Thirds.

Advantages

Because the sensor matches the format for which 35 mm lenses were designed, full-frame cameras use the entire image circle of full-frame lenses and preserve their intended angle of view: a 24 mm lens behaves as a true wide-angle. Larger photosites (for a given resolution) gather more light, generally giving better high-ISO noise performance and dynamic range, and the format makes shallow depth of field easier to achieve for portrait and low-light work.

Disadvantages

Full-frame bodies and lenses are larger, heavier, and more expensive than cropped-sensor equivalents, and long telephoto lenses give less effective "reach" than on a crop body at the same focal length. Wide-angle lenses must be of high quality to perform well at the frame edges.

History

Early digital SLRs used small sensors because large chips were prohibitively expensive. Kodak and Contax offered the first full-frame models around 2002; the Canon EOS 5D (2005) brought the format below professional price levels and is credited with popularising full-frame photography, while Nikon's D3 (2007) set new standards for low-light performance. From the 2010s, full-frame mirrorless cameras from Sony, Canon, Nikon, and Panasonic largely succeeded full-frame DSLRs, though the sensor format itself remains the industry benchmark.
