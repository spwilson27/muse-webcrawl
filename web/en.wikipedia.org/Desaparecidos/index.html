Desaparecidos

Photographs of the disappeared carried by the Mothers of the Plaza de Mayo, who demanded to know the fate of their children.

Desaparecidos (Spanish for "the disappeared ones") is the term given to people — overwhelmingly in Latin America — who were secretly arrested, detained, tortured and usually murdered by state security forces, while the authorities denied any knowledge of their fate or whereabouts. The practice became the defining human-rights crime of the military dictatorships of the 1970s and 1980s, and in international law the act is known as enforced disappearance, now a distinct crime under the 2006 International Convention for the Protection of All Persons from Enforced Disappearance.

By country

Argentina. During the "Dirty War" (1976–1983), the military junta's campaign of state terrorism disappeared between 9,000 (the figure documented by the CONADEP commission's 1984 report Nunca Más) and 30,000 people (the figure maintained by human-rights organizations), many of them murdered in clandestine detention centres such as ESMA or thrown from aircraft on "death flights." The Mothers and Grandmothers of the Plaza de Mayo made the fate of the desaparecidos a national cause; the Grandmothers have located and identified more than 130 children born in captivity and stolen by regime families.

Chile. Under Augusto Pinochet (1973–1990), more than 3,000 people were killed or disappeared, documented by the Rettig and Valech commissions. Guatemala's civil war saw some 45,000 disappearances, and the practice was also used extensively in El Salvador, Uruguay, Brazil, Paraguay and Peru, often coordinated across borders through Operation Condor.

Legacy

The demand for truth and justice for the desaparecidos drove landmark prosecutions — Argentina's 1985 Trial of the Juntas, the arrest of Pinochet in London in 1998, and later trials — and the development of forensic anthropology teams dedicated to identifying remains, beginning with the Argentine Forensic Anthropology Team (1984).
