Yangzhou fried rice

Yangzhou fried rice (Chinese: 揚州炒飯; pinyin: Yángzhōu chǎofàn), also known in the West as special fried rice or house fried rice, is a classic dish of Huaiyang cuisine originating from Yangzhou, Jiangsu, China. It is a wok-fried rice dish combining cooked rice with egg and a medley of finely diced ingredients such as char siu pork, shrimp, ham, sea cucumber, bamboo shoots, mushrooms, peas and spring onions.

History

Fried rice dishes have a long history in China; Yangzhou's refined version is often attributed to the Sui dynasty official Yang Su and, in its mature form, to Yi Bingshou, a Qing-dynasty magistrate and gastronome from Yangzhou, whose household recipe is said to have spread widely. As capital of the salt trade and a wealthy Grand Canal city, Qing-era Yangzhou developed the elegant Huaiyang school of cooking, of which the dish is a signature. Cantonese cooks later adapted it — the Cantonese version typically uses char siu and shrimp with lettuce — and it became a staple of Chinese restaurants worldwide.

Preparation

The hallmarks of the dish are separate, distinct grains of rice (often day-old rice to reduce moisture), a golden coating of beaten egg worked through the rice as it fries, and ingredients cut to a uniform small dice and added in sequence over high heat so each retains its character. In 2015, Yangzhou set a Guinness World Record by cooking a 4,192 kg batch of the fried rice — an entry later disqualified when it emerged some of the food had been diverted to pig feed rather than eaten.
