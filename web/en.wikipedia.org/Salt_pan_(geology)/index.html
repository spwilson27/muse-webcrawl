Salt pan (geology)

Polygonal patterns form as salt crusts dry and crack on a salt pan.

A salt pan is a flat expanse of ground covered with salt and other minerals, usually found in deserts. It forms in an enclosed drainage basin where evaporation exceeds precipitation: water collects in a shallow playa lake after rain, dissolves salts from the surrounding rocks, and leaves them behind as a white crust when it evaporates. Natural salt pans are a type of salt flat.

Formation

Salt pans develop in endorheic basins where water has no outlet to the sea. Repeated cycles of flooding and evaporation concentrate dissolved minerals — chiefly sodium chloride (halite), but also gypsum, borates, and carbonates depending on the local geology. As the crust dries it often breaks into striking polygonal patterns of raised salt ridges.

Notable examples

The largest salt pan in the world is the Salar de Uyuni in Bolivia, covering over 10,000 square kilometres. Other major examples include the Bonneville Salt Flats in Utah (famous for land speed records), the Etosha Pan in Namibia, the Makgadikgadi Pan in Botswana, the Rann of Kutch in India and Pakistan, and Badwater Basin in Death Valley, California, the lowest point in North America.

Uses and hazards

Salt pans are mined for table salt, lithium brines (notably in the South American "lithium triangle"), potash, and borax. Their hard, level surfaces have been used for land-speed racing and as emergency runways. However, the crust can conceal soft mud beneath, making crossing dangerous, and sudden flooding can turn a pan into a shallow lake within hours.
