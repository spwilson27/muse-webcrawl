Viviparity

Viviparity is a mode of reproduction in which the embryo develops inside the body of the mother and is born alive, receiving nourishment directly from her during gestation rather than from a yolk in a laid egg. It is characteristic of almost all mammals (except the egg-laying monotremes) and occurs in many other groups, including some sharks, rays, bony fish, amphibians, reptiles and invertebrates. Viviparity has evolved independently more than 150 times among vertebrates.

Forms of viviparity

In placental mammals the developing young are nourished through a placenta, an organ that exchanges gases, nutrients and wastes between mother and fetus. Marsupials give birth to very immature young that complete development in a pouch. In many viviparous sharks and rays, embryos consume unfertilized eggs or siblings in the uterus, or receive a uterine secretion ("uterine milk"). Viviparous lizards and snakes — which have evolved live birth over a hundred times — retain shelled or shell-less eggs internally until hatching.

Evolution and advantages

Retaining embryos internally protects them from predators and environmental fluctuations and lets the mother regulate their temperature and supply, an advantage thought to explain the repeated evolution of viviparity in cold climates and in the sea, where laid eggs would be vulnerable. The costs include greater energetic burden on the mother, reduced litter sizes in many species, and slower reproduction.

In plants

In botany, "vivipary" describes seeds that germinate while still attached to the parent plant, as in some mangroves whose seedlings grow into torpedo-shaped propagules before dropping into the mud.
