Octonion

In mathematics, the octonions are a normed division algebra over the real numbers, a kind of hypercomplex number system. The octonions are eight-dimensional, being twice the size of the quaternions, of which they are an extension. Octonion multiplication is neither commutative nor associative, though it is alternative, and the octonions are, by Hurwitz's theorem, the largest of the four normed division algebras (the others being the real numbers, the complex numbers and the quaternions).

History

The octonions were discovered independently in 1843 by John T. Graves, in a letter to William Rowan Hamilton, and by Arthur Cayley, who published the first paper on them in 1845; they are therefore sometimes called Cayley numbers or the Cayley algebra. They were long a mathematical curiosity but found new prominence through applications in abstract algebra and theoretical physics.

Definition

An octonion is a linear combination of the basis units 1, e1, …, e7 with real coefficients. The multiplication of the imaginary units is conveniently summarised by the Fano plane, a diagram of seven points and seven lines. Conjugation, norm and inverse are defined in analogy with the quaternions, and the norm is multiplicative, giving the eight-square identity.

Properties and applications

The automorphism group of the octonions is the exceptional Lie group G2, and the octonions underlie the construction of the other exceptional Lie groups. They appear in string theory, special relativity, quantum logic and the geometry of the exceptional Jordan algebra.
