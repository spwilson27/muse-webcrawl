Dirichlet's principle

In mathematics, Dirichlet's principle, named after Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet, is the assumption that the minimizer of a certain energy functional is a solution of Poisson's equation. Specifically, among all sufficiently smooth functions u on a domain Ω with prescribed boundary values, the one minimizing the Dirichlet energy integral ∫Ω |∇u|² dx should satisfy Δu = 0 in the interior.

History

Dirichlet used the principle in his lectures as an existence argument for solutions of Laplace's equation with given boundary conditions. Karl Weierstrass later criticized it, pointing out that an infimum of the energy need not be attained by any admissible function, and produced a counterexample for related functionals. Bernhard Riemann coined the name and relied on it in complex analysis; David Hilbert rehabilitated the principle around 1900 by proving, with additional compactness arguments, that a minimizing sequence does converge — an approach called the direct method in the calculus of variations.

Modern formulation

In the modern theory of Sobolev spaces, the principle is rigorous: the Dirichlet energy is weakly lower semicontinuous and coercive on H¹ functions with fixed trace, so a minimizer exists and, by elliptic regularity, is smooth and solves the PDE. The principle underlies finite element methods used to solve boundary value problems numerically.
