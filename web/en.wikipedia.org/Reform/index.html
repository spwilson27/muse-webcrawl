Reform

Reform is the improvement or amendment of what is wrong, corrupt, or unsatisfactory in an institution, law, or practice. Reform is generally distinguished from revolution: a reform seeks to change and improve an existing system from within, whereas a revolution replaces the system entirely.

Political and social reform

Reform movements arise in response to perceived defects in political institutions, social conditions, or economic arrangements. Historical examples include the British Reform Acts of the 19th century, which progressively expanded the parliamentary franchise; the reforms of the Progressive Era in the United States, which targeted corruption, monopolies and working conditions; and social reforms such as the abolition of slavery, the extension of women's suffrage, and the introduction of public education.

Types of reform

  • Electoral reform — changes to voting systems and representation.
  • Economic reform — changes to markets, taxation, and regulation.
  • Legal and judicial reform — changes to laws, courts and policing.
  • Educational and health reform — changes to schools and healthcare systems.
  • Religious reform — changes within religious institutions, such as the Protestant Reformation.

Reform and revolution

Political theorists have long debated whether gradual reform or revolutionary change is the more effective path to social improvement. Reformists argue that incremental change is achievable and stable, while critics contend that entrenched interests limit reform to superficial adjustments.
