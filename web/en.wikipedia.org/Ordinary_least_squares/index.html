Ordinary least squares

In statistics, ordinary least squares (OLS) is a type of linear least squares method for choosing the unknown parameters in a linear regression model. OLS chooses the parameters of a linear function of a set of explanatory variables by minimising the sum of the squares of the differences between the observed dependent variable and the values predicted by the linear function; geometrically, it minimises the sum of squared vertical distances between the observed data points and the fitted line or hyperplane.

Estimation

For the model y = Xβ + ε, the OLS estimator is β̂ = (XᵀX)⁻¹Xᵀy, provided X has full column rank. Under the Gauss–Markov assumptions—linearity, exogeneity of the errors, homoscedasticity, and no perfect multicollinearity—OLS is the best linear unbiased estimator (BLUE), i.e. it has the smallest variance among linear unbiased estimators. If the errors are also normally distributed, OLS coincides with maximum likelihood estimation.

Properties and limitations

OLS estimates are consistent and asymptotically normal under mild conditions. Violations of the assumptions cause problems: heteroscedasticity and autocorrelation make conventional standard errors invalid (though coefficients remain unbiased); omitted variables and measurement error in regressors bias the coefficients; and OLS is sensitive to outliers because squaring magnifies large residuals. Variants such as weighted least squares, generalised least squares, robust regression, and ridge regression address some of these issues.

History

The method of least squares was first published by Adrien-Marie Legendre in 1805 and independently developed by Carl Friedrich Gauss, who claimed to have used it since 1795 and applied it to predict the orbit of the asteroid Ceres.
