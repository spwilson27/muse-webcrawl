Path dependence

Path dependence is a concept in economics and the social sciences describing how the set of decisions people face is limited by decisions made in the past or by events that occurred earlier, even though past circumstances may no longer be relevant. In its strong form, "history matters": small, contingent early events can be amplified by self-reinforcing mechanisms — increasing returns, switching costs, network effects, and institutional inertia — until a system locks in to an outcome that may not be the most efficient available.

Canonical examples

The QWERTY keyboard layout, said to persist despite allegedly faster alternatives such as Dvorak, is the most famous claimed example, popularised by economist Paul David in 1985. W. Brian Arthur formalised the theory of increasing returns and lock-in. Other standard cases include the victory of VHS over Betamax in videocassette formats, the prevalence of particular railway gauges, and divergent institutional development across countries. The empirical strength of the QWERTY example itself has been disputed (notably by Liebowitz and Margolis), making it a case study in how path-dependence claims should be tested.

Mechanisms and criticism

Mechanisms generating path dependence include high set-up costs, learning effects, coordination effects, and adaptive expectations: each makes the chosen option more attractive the more it is used. Critics argue that, loosely used, the term merely restates that history matters trivially, and that genuine lock-in to an inferior, irremediable outcome is rare because markets and political actors can pay switching costs when benefits are large. The concept remains influential in economic history, political science (historical institutionalism), technology studies, and evolutionary economics.
