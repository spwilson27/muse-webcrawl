The Siberian intervention or Siberian expedition of 1918–1922 was the dispatch of troops of the Allied powers to the Russian Maritime Provinces as part of a larger Allied effort to intervene in the Russian Civil War. The principal participants were the Empire of Japan, which deployed by far the largest force (about 70,000 troops at its peak), and the United States, which sent the American Expeditionary Force, Siberia, under Major General William S. Graves.

Background

Following the Bolshevik seizure of power in the October Revolution of 1917 and the Treaty of Brest-Litovsk, the Allies feared that large stockpiles of military supplies at Vladivostok might fall into German or Bolshevik hands, and hoped to assist the Czechoslovak Legion in escaping Russia via the Trans-Siberian Railway. In August 1918 Allied forces landed at Vladivostok.

The intervention

The Japanese, pursuing their own interests in Manchuria and Siberia, supported anti-Bolshevik Cossack leaders such as Grigory Semyonov and remained long after other Allies withdrew. American forces guarded sections of the railway and tried to remain neutral in the fighting between Reds and Whites. Canadian, British, French, Italian, and other contingents also served in smaller numbers. Violence against civilians, including the Nikolaevsk incident of 1920, marred the occupation.

Withdrawal and aftermath

The United States withdrew in April 1920 and the last Allied forces left Vladivostok in October 1922 as the Red Army advanced; Japanese troops held northern Sakhalin until 1925. The intervention failed to dislodge the Bolsheviks and deepened Soviet suspicion of Western intentions for decades.
