Cuisine of the Mid-Atlantic states

Mid-Atlantic specialties range from Maryland crab cakes to the Philly cheesesteak.
Signature foods
Maryland blue crab and crab cakes · Philadelphia cheesesteak · soft pretzels · scrapple · shoofly pie · New York-style pizza and bagels · boardwalk salt water taffy · Smith Island cake
Influences
English, Dutch, German (Pennsylvania Dutch), African-American, Italian, Jewish

The cuisine of the Mid-Atlantic states of the United States — New York, New Jersey, Pennsylvania, Delaware, Maryland and surrounding areas — draws heavily on the region's original immigrant populations: English, Dutch and German settlers, later Italian, Jewish and African-American traditions, and the seafood of the Chesapeake Bay and Atlantic fisheries.

Regional specialties

Maryland is synonymous with blue crab, steamed with Old Bay seasoning, and crab cakes, as well as Smith Island cake, the state's official dessert. Philadelphia contributed the cheesesteak, the hoagie, soft pretzels, water ice and the Pennsylvania Dutch country traditions of scrapple, shoofly pie and chicken pot pie. New York City developed its own styles of pizza and bagels, pastrami on rye from Jewish delicatessens, and the black-and-white cookie. New Jersey is known for pork roll (Taylor ham), diners, tomatoes and boardwalk foods such as salt water taffy from Atlantic City.

Historical development

Colonial ports imported sugar, molasses, coffee and spices while exporting flour and preserved meats; Philadelphia was the young republic's culinary capital, where cooks such as Hercules, enslaved chef to George Washington, shaped early American fine dining. In the 19th and 20th centuries, oyster cellars, automats and diners democratised eating out, and post-war immigration layered Chinese-American, Puerto Rican, Korean and many other cuisines onto the region's foodways. The area is part of the broader tradition of American cuisine.
