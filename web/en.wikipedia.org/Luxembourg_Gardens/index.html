The Luxembourg Gardens (French: Jardin du Luxembourg) are a celebrated public park of about 23 hectares in the 6th arrondissement of Paris, surrounding the Luxembourg Palace, seat of the French Senate. Created beginning in 1612 for Marie de' Medici, widow of Henry IV, as the grounds of her new palace, the gardens blend French formal parterres and tree-lined promenades with lawns, orchards, an apiary, an orangery and more than a hundred statues. They are among the best-loved green spaces of the Left Bank, frequented by students of the Latin Quarter, chess players, joggers, and children sailing model boats on the central basin.

History

Marie de' Medici, nostalgic for Florence's Boboli Gardens, bought the estate of the Duke of Luxembourg in 1611 and commissioned the palace and gardens; some 2,000 elms were planted and an aqueduct built to feed the fountains. The gardens passed through many hands and redesigns: expanded under the Revolution (when the Chartreux nursery grounds were added), reshaped in the 19th century, and trimmed by Haussmann's street works, which cut off several hectares at the south end. The Senate has administered the gardens since 1879.

Features

The octagonal Grand Bassin, where children rent small wooden sailboats to push across with sticks, fronts the palace terrace. The Medici Fountain (c. 1630), a long shaded basin with a grotto facade, was remodelled in the 1860s with Auguste Ottin's sculptural group of Polyphemus surprising Acis and Galatea. Statuary includes the series of twenty queens and illustrious women of France lining the terraces, statues of writers and artists, and a small-scale first model of the Statue of Liberty by Bartholdi. The gardens also contain a famous orchard of historic pear and apple varieties, a school of beekeeping founded in the 19th century, tennis and basketball courts, puppet theatre (the Théâtre du Luxembourg, a guignol stage dating to 1933), and the Musée du Luxembourg at the garden's northwest corner, Paris's first public museum when it opened in 1750.

In culture

The gardens figure in Victor Hugo's Les Misérables — Marius and Cosette meet among its allées — and in works by Hemingway, who recalled walking there hungry in A Moveable Feast. The garden's movable green chairs and its mix of formality and leisure are iconic of Parisian public life.
