Van der Waals equation

Proposed byJohannes Diderik van der Waals
Year1873
TypeEquation of state for real gases
Constantsa (attraction), b (finite molecular volume)

The van der Waals equation is an equation of state that generalises the ideal gas law, incorporating the finite size of molecules and the attractive forces between them. It was proposed by the Dutch physicist Johannes Diderik van der Waals in his 1873 doctoral thesis, work for which he later received the Nobel Prize in Physics (1910).

(p + a/Vm2)(Vm − b) = RT

Here p is pressure, Vm the molar volume, T the temperature, and R the gas constant. The parameter a corrects for intermolecular attraction, which reduces the pressure exerted on the container walls; the parameter b corrects for the volume excluded by the finite size of the molecules. Both constants are characteristic of each gas.

Behaviour

Unlike the ideal gas law, the van der Waals equation predicts a phase transition between liquid and gas, and yields a critical point characterised by critical temperature, pressure, and volume expressible in terms of a and b. Below the critical temperature its isotherms oscillate, and the physically realised behaviour is recovered by Maxwell's equal-area construction. Written in terms of reduced variables, the equation takes a universal form — the law of corresponding states — approximately obeyed by all fluids.

Limitations and successors

The equation is only qualitatively accurate over wide ranges and is rarely used for precise engineering calculations; it has been succeeded by more accurate empirical equations of state such as the Redlich–Kwong and Peng–Robinson equations, which retain its two-parameter cubic structure. Its importance is chiefly conceptual: it was the first theory to explain the continuity of the gaseous and liquid states from molecular assumptions.
