Mishnah

The Mishnah (Hebrew: מִשְׁנָה, "study by repetition", from the verb shanah, "to study and review") is the first major written collection of the Jewish oral traditions known as the Oral Torah. It is also the first major work of rabbinic literature, redacted around 200 CE by Judah ha-Nasi (Rabbi Judah the Prince) in Roman Palestine, probably at Sepphoris or Beth She'arim.

The Mishnah consists of rulings and debates of the rabbis known as the Tannaim, covering Jewish law (halakha) on agriculture, festivals, marriage and damages, as well as Temple worship and ritual purity — much of it concerning institutions, such as the Temple in Jerusalem, that had already been destroyed (in 70 CE). It is written in Mishnaic Hebrew, with some Aramaic and Greek loanwords.

Structure

The work is organised into six orders (shisha sedarim, hence the acronym Shas), each subdivided into tractates (63 in total) and chapters:

Zera'im ("Seeds") — agricultural laws and prayers; Mo'ed ("Festival") — Sabbath and holiday law; Nashim ("Women") — marriage, divorce and vows; Nezikin ("Damages") — civil and criminal law, courts, and the ethical tractate Pirkei Avot ("Ethics of the Fathers"); Kodashim ("Holy Things") — sacrifices and Temple service; Tohorot ("Purities") — laws of ritual purity and impurity.

Relationship to the Talmud

The Mishnah became the foundation text for later rabbinic commentary. The discussions of the following generations of rabbis (the Amoraim) around its text were compiled as the Gemara; Mishnah and Gemara together constitute the Talmud, of which there are two versions — the Jerusalem Talmud (c. 400 CE) and the more authoritative Babylonian Talmud (c. 500 CE). Related tannaitic material not included in the Mishnah survives in the Tosefta and in halakhic midrashim.

Significance

By committing the oral law to writing in an organised code, the Mishnah preserved Jewish legal tradition after the destruction of the Temple and the failure of the Bar Kokhba revolt, and it established the framework within which Jewish law has been studied ever since. It remains a central text of Jewish study and is also an important historical source for Roman Palestine and for the development of Hebrew.
