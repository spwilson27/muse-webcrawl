Gian Lorenzo (or Gianlorenzo) Bernini (UK: /bɛərˈniːni/, US: /bərˈ-/; Italian: [ˈdʒan loˈrɛntso berˈniːni]; 7 December 1598 – 28 November 1680) was an Italian sculptor and architect. While a major figure in the world of architecture, he was more prominently the leading sculptor of his age, credited with creating the Baroque style of sculpture. His prodigious talent, energy, and domination of Roman art under eight popes earned him, in his lifetime, a reputation as a second Michelangelo.

Early works

Born in Naples, the son of the Mannerist sculptor Pietro Bernini, Gian Lorenzo was a child prodigy who was brought to Rome, where he carved for Cardinal Scipione Borghese the mythological groups that made his name: Aeneas, Anchises, and Ascanius (1618–19), The Rape of Proserpina (1621–22), Apollo and Daphne (1622–25), and the twisting, mid-action David (1623–24), whose face is said to be a self-portrait. These works, in the Galleria Borghese, displayed unprecedented virtuosity in rendering flesh, hair, and foliage in marble.

Mature career

Under Pope Urban VIII Barberini, Bernini became Rome's pre-eminent artist: he designed the bronze Baldacchino over the high altar of St Peter's Basilica (1624–33) and the Chair of Saint Peter (Cathedra Petri). His religious sculpture culminated in the Ecstasy of Saint Teresa (1647–52) in the Cornaro Chapel, a theatrical fusion of sculpture, architecture, painting, and light. For Pope Innocent X he created the Fountain of the Four Rivers (1651) in Piazza Navona. Under Alexander VII he gave St Peter's its defining setting: the great elliptical colonnade of St Peter's Square (1656–67), and built the churches of Sant'Andrea al Quirinale and Santa Maria Assunta in Ariccia, and the Scala Regia in the Vatican.

Later life and the Louvre visit

In 1665 Bernini was invited to Paris to design the east front of the Louvre for Louis XIV; his plans were ultimately rejected in favour of a French classical design, but he carved a celebrated bust of the king and kept a diary-chronicled visit recorded by Chantelou. Returning to Rome, he continued working into his eighties and died there in 1680, leaving an unmatched body of sculpture, architecture, fountains, and stage designs across the city.

Legacy

Bernini defined the Roman Baroque: his fusion of the arts (bel composto), emotional intensity, and illusionistic effects shaped European art for a century. After a period of neoclassical disdain, his reputation was restored by 20th-century scholarship, and art historians such as Rudolf Wittkower established him as one of the supreme masters of Western sculpture.
