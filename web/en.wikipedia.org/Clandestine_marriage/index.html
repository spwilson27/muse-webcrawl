Clandestine marriage

A clandestine marriage is a marriage performed secretly, typically without the required formalities, banns, licences or parental consent. In England before 1754, so-called "Fleet marriages" were conducted by imprisoned clergymen in and around London's Fleet Prison, evading the normal requirements of the Church of England.

History

Under medieval canon law, a marriage was valid if the parties exchanged vows in the present tense (per verba de praesenti), even without witnesses or clergy; this created widespread uncertainty about marital status. The Council of Trent (1563) required Catholic marriages to be celebrated before a priest and witnesses.

In England, Lord Hardwicke's Marriage Act 1753 (effective 1754) ended clandestine marriages by requiring banns or a licence, a church ceremony, and registration, with parental consent for minors. Couples evading the Act — which did not apply in Scotland — famously eloped to Gretna Green. The Fleet marriages alone were estimated to number thousands per year in the early 18th century.
