Born: 11 March 1897, Menlo Park, California, US

Died: 10 December 1965 (aged 68), Shady, New York, US

Occupation: Composer, pianist, theorist, publisher

Known for: Tone clusters; New Musical Resources; the Rhythmicon

Cowell demonstrating tone clusters

Henry Dixon Cowell (11 March 1897 – 10 December 1965) was an American composer, pianist, music theorist, publisher, and impresario. His contribution to the world of music was summed up by Virgil Thomson, who wrote that no other composer of the first half of the 20th century had "a larger sample of larger audiences or made a wider impact." Cowell was a leading figure of the American experimental tradition.

Music and ideas

Raised in California with little formal training, Cowell studied with Charles Seeger and became known for tone clusters — chords of adjacent notes played with the fist or forearm — heard in piano pieces such as The Tides of Manaunaun (1917) and The Banshee (1925), the latter played directly on the piano strings. His theoretical book New Musical Resources (written by 1919, published 1930) proposed rhythmic and harmonic systems derived from the overtone series and influenced generations of composers. With Léon Theremin he developed the Rhythmicon, an early drum machine capable of polyrhythms based on harmonic ratios.

Career

Cowell founded the publishing venture New Music Editions, which issued scores by Ives, Ruggles, Varèse and others, and co-founded the Pan American Association of Composers. His later music drew on world traditions and hymn and fiddle tunes, including the Hymn and Fuguing Tune series and 21 symphonies. His students and protégés included John Cage and Lou Harrison. A morals conviction in 1936 led to imprisonment at San Quentin until parole in 1940 and a pardon in 1942, an episode that cast a long shadow over his career.
