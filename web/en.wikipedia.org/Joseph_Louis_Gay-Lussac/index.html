Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac

Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac (6 December 1778 – 9 May 1850) was a French chemist and physicist best known for his laws on the expansion and combining volumes of gases. He is regarded as one of the founders of modern chemistry.

Scientific work

In 1802 Gay-Lussac established that gases expand uniformly with temperature (a result also found by Jacques Charles, and sometimes called Charles's law). In 1804 he made daring balloon ascents to over 7,000 metres to sample the air and measure magnetism and temperature. His 1808 law of combining volumes states that gases react in volume ratios of small whole numbers, a key step toward Avogadro's hypothesis. With Louis Jacques Thénard he isolated boron (1808) and studied iodine; with Alexander von Humboldt he determined the composition of water by volume (1805). He also co-discovered the element boron, developed methods of volumetric analysis, and improved the manufacture of sulfuric acid (the Gay-Lussac tower).

Later life

Gay-Lussac taught at the École Polytechnique and the Sorbonne, served in the Chamber of Peers, and was elected to academies across Europe. His name is inscribed on the Eiffel Tower among the 72 names of French scientists.
