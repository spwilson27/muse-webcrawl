Direct realism

Direct realism holds that we perceive the external world itself, not mental representations of it.

Direct realism, also known as naïve realism, is a theory of perception in philosophy holding that when we perceive, the objects of perception are the ordinary external objects themselves, rather than internal mental representations or "sense data." On this view, the senses provide direct awareness of the world as it is, and the properties presented in experience belong to the objects themselves.

History

Forms of direct realism appear in Aristotle and in medieval scholasticism, and Thomas Reid defended a commonsense version against the "way of ideas" of Descartes and Locke. In the 20th century, the view was revived against sense-datum theories by philosophers influenced by G. E. Moore, and later developed by John Searle, John McDowell, and proponents of disjunctivism.

Arguments and objections

The classic objection is the argument from illusion: when a straight stick looks bent in water, what we see cannot simply be the stick itself, since the stick is not bent; indirect realists conclude that we directly perceive only mental items. Direct realists respond that illusion does not require a special inner object — the stick can appear a way it is not without our perceiving a bent mental entity. Related challenges include hallucination, perceptual relativity, and the time-lag argument (we see distant stars as they were long ago). Disjunctivists answer that veridical perception and hallucination are fundamentally different kinds of mental state, even when subjectively indistinguishable.

Variants

Modern versions include disjunctivism, relationalism (perception as a relation between subject and object), and Searle's intentionalist direct realism. The view is contrasted with indirect (representative) realism, idealism, and phenomenalism.
