A desiccant is a hygroscopic substance that is used to induce or sustain a state of dryness (desiccation) in its vicinity. It is the opposite of a humectant. Commonly encountered pre-packaged desiccants are water-absorbing solids such as silica gel, which are placed inside sealed packaging to control humidity and protect moisture-sensitive goods.

Mechanism

Desiccants work either by physically adsorbing water onto a large internal surface area, as with silica gel and molecular sieves, or by chemically reacting with water to form a new compound, as with calcium oxide and calcium sulfate. The effectiveness of a desiccant is commonly expressed as its moisture capacity—the amount of water it can hold relative to its own mass under given conditions of temperature and relative humidity.

Common desiccants

Silica gel, an amorphous form of silicon dioxide, is the most familiar desiccant and is found in small paper packets inside shoe boxes, electronics packaging, and vitamin bottles. Other widely used materials include activated charcoal, calcium chloride, calcium sulfate (Drierite), and synthetic zeolite molecular sieves, which are used when very low humidity or selective absorption of particular molecules is required.

Applications

Desiccants are used to protect pharmaceuticals, foods, leather goods, electronics, and museum artifacts from moisture damage such as corrosion, mold growth, and degradation. In industry, desiccant dehumidifiers and desiccant wheels dry process air, while desiccants in double-glazed windows absorb moisture trapped between the panes.

Regeneration

Many desiccants can be regenerated by heating, which drives off the absorbed water and restores their drying capacity. Silica gel, for example, can be reactivated by heating it in an oven, a property that makes it reusable in applications such as desiccator cabinets.
