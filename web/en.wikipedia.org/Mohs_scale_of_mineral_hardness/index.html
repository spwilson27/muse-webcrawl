Mohs scale of mineral hardness

The Mohs scale of mineral hardness is a qualitative ordinal scale, from 1 to 10, characterising the scratch resistance of minerals through the ability of harder material to scratch softer material. The scale was introduced in 1812 by the German geologist and mineralogist Friedrich Mohs, in his book Versuch einer Elementar-Methode zur naturhistorischen Bestimmung und Erkennung der Fossilien. It is one of several definitions of hardness in materials science, some of which are more quantitative.

The scale

The method of comparing hardness by observing which minerals can scratch others is of great antiquity, having been mentioned by Theophrastus in his treatise On Stones around 300 BCE, followed by Pliny the Elder in his Naturalis Historia around 77 CE. Mohs based the scale on ten readily available minerals, assigning each a value:

HardnessMineral
1Talc
2Gypsum
3Calcite
4Fluorite
5Apatite
6Orthoclase feldspar
7Quartz
8Topaz
9Corundum
10Diamond

Use and limitations

The scale is widely used by geologists in the field, who test an unknown mineral against reference minerals or common objects: a fingernail has a hardness of about 2.5, a copper coin about 3, a steel nail about 5.5, and a steel file about 6.5. The Mohs scale is purely ordinal and nonlinear: the difference in absolute hardness between corundum (9) and diamond (10) is far greater than the difference between talc (1) and gypsum (2). For quantitative work, indentation tests such as the Vickers and Knoop hardness tests are preferred.
