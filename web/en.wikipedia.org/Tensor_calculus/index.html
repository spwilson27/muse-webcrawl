Tensor calculus

Tensor calculus, or tensor analysis, is an extension of vector calculus to tensor fields — tensors that may vary continuously over a mathematical space. Developed around 1890 by Gregorio Ricci-Curbastro and Tullio Levi-Civita, it was originally called the absolute differential calculus. Tensor calculus became widely known through its use by Albert Einstein in the general theory of relativity, and is fundamental to modern differential geometry, continuum mechanics, and theoretical physics.

History

Ricci-Curbastro developed the calculus in a series of papers beginning in the 1880s, and with Levi-Civita published the landmark exposition Méthodes de calcul différentiel absolu et leurs applications in 1900. Einstein, guided by Marcel Grossmann, adopted the formalism for general relativity (1915), in which the curvature of spacetime is expressed through the Riemann curvature tensor, the Ricci tensor, and the metric tensor. Einstein's popularisation carried the subject far beyond its original specialist audience.

Key concepts

  • Covariant and contravariant components — tensor components transform in definite ways under coordinate changes, indicated by subscript and superscript indices in the Einstein notation, in which repeated indices are summed.
  • Metric tensor — defines distances and angles on a manifold and is used to raise and lower indices.
  • Covariant derivative — a derivative that accounts for the variation of basis vectors in curvilinear coordinates, using Christoffel symbols; the ordinary partial derivative of a tensor is not itself a tensor.
  • Curvature tensors — the Riemann tensor measures the failure of covariant derivatives to commute; its contractions give the Ricci tensor and scalar curvature.
  • Tensor fields and densities — assignments of tensors to each point of a manifold, treated in coordinate-free terms in modern differential geometry.

Applications

Tensor calculus underlies continuum mechanics (stress and strain tensors), electromagnetism in relativistic form (the electromagnetic field tensor), fluid dynamics, and elasticity. In modern mathematics it merges into the theory of differentiable manifolds, connections, and fibre bundles, where computations may be done either in coordinates or in coordinate-free notation.
