Diffusion

Dye molecules spread through water by diffusion.

Diffusion is the net movement of particles — atoms, molecules, or ions — from a region of higher concentration to a region of lower concentration, driven by random thermal motion. It is the principal mechanism of transport over short distances in gases, liquids, and solids, and underlies phenomena from the spreading of a scent across a room to the exchange of oxygen in the lungs.

Fick's laws

Adolf Fick formulated the quantitative theory in 1855. Fick's first law states that the flux J is proportional to the concentration gradient: J = −D ∂φ/∂x, where D is the diffusion coefficient. Combining this with conservation of mass gives Fick's second law, the diffusion equation ∂φ/∂t = D∇²φ, identical in form to the heat equation, since heat conduction is likewise a diffusive process.

Microscopic picture

At the molecular level, diffusion arises from the Brownian motion of particles executing random walks; the mean squared displacement grows linearly in time, ⟨x²⟩ = 2Dt, a relation Einstein derived in 1905 in work that helped establish the reality of atoms. Because displacement grows only as the square root of time, diffusion is fast over micrometres but extremely slow over metres — which is why large organisms need circulatory systems.

Applications and examples

Diffusion is essential in biology (gas exchange across alveoli and cell membranes, neurotransmitter spread), chemistry (mixing, reaction rates), and technology: dopant diffusion in semiconductor manufacture, case hardening of steel, dialysis, drug delivery, and isotope separation. Osmosis is the special case of solvent diffusion across a semipermeable membrane. The concept has also been borrowed metaphorically for the spread of innovations, ideas, and diseases through populations.
