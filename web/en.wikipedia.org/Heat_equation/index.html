Heat equation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics and physics, the heat equation is a parabolic partial differential equation that describes the distribution of heat (or variation in temperature) in a given region over time. For a function u(x, y, z, t) giving temperature at position (x, y, z) and time t, the equation in three spatial dimensions is

∂u/∂t = α ( ∂²u/∂x² + ∂²u/∂y² + ∂²u/∂z² )

where α is the thermal diffusivity of the material. Joseph Fourier introduced the equation in his 1822 treatise Théorie analytique de la chaleur (The Analytical Theory of Heat), developing along the way the method of Fourier series, which transformed mathematical physics.

Interpretation

The equation states that the rate of change of temperature at a point is proportional to the Laplacian of temperature there — roughly, to the difference between the point's temperature and the average temperature of its immediate surroundings. A locally "peaked" temperature (concave down) therefore falls, while a cold spot warms, capturing the smoothing, diffusive character of conduction described physically by Fourier's law of heat conduction.

Solution methods

Classical techniques include separation of variables with Fourier series or transforms, yielding series of decaying modes; the use of the fundamental solution (heat kernel) — for the infinite line, a Gaussian whose variance grows linearly in time; and Green's functions for bounded domains with Dirichlet, Neumann or Robin boundary conditions. The equation enjoys a maximum principle: interior maxima cannot exceed the largest initial or boundary value, and solutions are instantly smoothed (they are analytic in space for positive times). Numerically, it is solved by finite-difference, finite-element and spectral methods, subject to stability constraints (for explicit schemes, a CFL-type condition on the time step).

Related equations and applications

The heat equation is the prototype of parabolic PDEs. Replacing temperature by concentration gives the diffusion equation governing Brownian motion and chemical mixing; the same operator appears in the Black–Scholes equation of finance (after transformation), in image processing (Gaussian blur, scale-space), and in Perelman's work on the Ricci flow, a nonlinear relative used in the proof of the Poincaré conjecture. Its time-independent form is Laplace's equation; adding a source term gives Poisson's equation.
