Intertropical Convergence Zone

The ITCZ appears as a band of clouds encircling the Earth near the equator.

The Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), known by sailors as the doldrums or the calms, is the region near the equator where the northeast and southeast trade winds converge. It encircles Earth near the thermal equator and is marked by a band of heavy cloudiness, frequent thunderstorms, and heavy rainfall.

Formation

The ITCZ forms where intense solar heating causes warm, moist air to rise. As the air ascends it cools, its water vapour condenses, and towering cumulonimbus clouds develop. The rising air flows poleward aloft and descends in the subtropics, completing the Hadley cell circulation.

Seasonal movement

The position of the ITCZ varies seasonally, following the thermal equator and lagging the Sun's declination by roughly a month. It moves farther over continents than over oceans. Its migration drives the wet and dry seasons of the tropics and is intimately connected with the monsoon systems of Africa, Asia, and Australia.

Effects on weather and sailors

In the doldrums, surface winds can be calm and erratic, which historically becalmed sailing ships for weeks. The zone is also a principal breeding ground for tropical cyclones, although storms rarely form within about five degrees of the equator itself because of the weak Coriolis effect.
