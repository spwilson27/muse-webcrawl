Folk etymology

Folk etymology, also known as popular etymology, is a change in a word or phrase resulting from the replacement of an unfamiliar form by a more familiar one through popular usage. The form or meaning of an archaic, foreign, or otherwise unfamiliar word is reanalysed so that it resembles a word speakers already know, even though the resulting interpretation is historically incorrect.

Examples

The English word crayfish derives from Middle English crevis, from Old French escrevisse; the second syllable was remodelled after the familiar word fish. Similarly, bridegroom replaced the older bridegome ("bride-man") when gome fell out of use. The vegetable name asparagus is sometimes rendered "sparrow grass" in dialect, and Jerusalem artichoke arose from the Italian girasole ("sunflower").

Mechanism

Folk etymology is driven by the tendency of speakers to make unfamiliar words meaningful. When a word is borrowed from another language or becomes opaque through sound change, speakers substitute sounds and morphemes that make sense within their own vocabulary. The process can alter pronunciation, spelling, or both, and the new form may eventually displace the original entirely.

Related processes

Folk etymology is related to eggcorns, in which an individual idiosyncratically reshapes a word, and to backronym formation. Unlike deliberate wordplay, folk etymology is typically unconscious and spreads through a speech community over time.
