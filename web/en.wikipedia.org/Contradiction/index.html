Contradiction

In logic, a contradiction is a situation in which inconsistent propositions are both asserted — a statement of the form P and not-P. Aristotle's law of non-contradiction holds that contradictory propositions cannot both be true in the same sense at the same time: “the same attribute cannot at the same time belong and not belong to the same subject.”

Proof by contradiction

In classical logic, a contradiction can be used to prove a proposition: assuming its negation together with accepted premises leads to an absurdity, so the proposition must be true. This is proof by contradiction (reductio ad absurdum), used for example in Euclid's proof of the infinitude of primes and the proof that √2 is irrational.

Explosion and paraconsistency

Classical logic endorses the principle of explosion (ex falso quodlibet): from a contradiction, anything follows, so an inconsistent system is trivial. Paraconsistent logics reject explosion and allow reasoning in the presence of local contradictions, while dialetheists such as Graham Priest argue some contradictions — like the liar paradox — may be true.

Dialectics

In Hegelian and Marxist philosophy, contradiction takes on a broader meaning: the tension between opposing forces within a thing or society, whose struggle drives change and development.
