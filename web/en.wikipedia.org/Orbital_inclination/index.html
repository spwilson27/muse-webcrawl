Orbital inclination

Orbital inclination measures the tilt of an object's orbit around a celestial body. It is expressed as the angle between a reference plane and the orbital plane or axis of direction of the orbiting object. For a satellite orbiting the Earth directly above the equator, the plane of the satellite's orbit is the same as the Earth's equatorial plane, and the satellite's orbital inclination is 0°. In general, an orbit's inclination can be measured relative to the ecliptic, the invariable plane, or the equator of the primary body.

Definition

Inclination is one of the six Keplerian orbital elements that describe an orbit. It is the angle between the orbital plane and a chosen plane of reference, measured at the ascending node. Orbits with inclination between 0° and 90° are prograde, revolving in the same direction as the primary's rotation; inclinations between 90° and 180° are retrograde. An inclination of exactly 90° is a polar orbit.

Examples

The International Space Station orbits at an inclination of about 51.6° to the equator, so that it can be reached from launch sites such as Baikonur. Sun-synchronous satellites use near-polar retrograde orbits of about 98°. The Moon's orbit is inclined about 5.14° to the ecliptic, which is why eclipses do not occur every month. Most planets in the Solar System have low inclinations relative to the ecliptic; Mercury's, at about 7°, is the largest.
