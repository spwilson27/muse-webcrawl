Methanol toxicity

Methanol toxicity (also known as methanol poisoning) is poisoning from methanol, a toxic alcohol found in windshield washer fluid, solvents, fuels and illicitly distilled spirits. Methanol itself is only mildly intoxicating; its danger comes from its metabolites.

Mechanism

In the liver, alcohol dehydrogenase converts methanol to formaldehyde, which is rapidly converted to formic acid (formate). Formate accumulates, inhibits mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase, and causes a severe high anion gap metabolic acidosis. The optic nerve and retina are especially vulnerable, so survivors may suffer permanent blindness; the basal ganglia may also be damaged, producing Parkinson-like symptoms.

Symptoms

Early symptoms resemble ethanol intoxication, followed after a latency of 12–24 hours by visual disturbances ("snowfield vision"), abdominal pain, headache, rapid breathing and, in severe cases, coma and death. As little as 10 mL of pure methanol can cause blindness, and 30 mL or more may be fatal.

Treatment

Treatment aims to block methanol metabolism with an antidote — fomepizole, a competitive inhibitor of alcohol dehydrogenase, or ethanol itself — together with sodium bicarbonate for acidosis, folate supplementation, and haemodialysis to remove methanol and formate.

Epidemiology

Mass poisonings occur when methanol is substituted for ethanol in counterfeit alcoholic drinks; major outbreaks have occurred in many countries, sometimes killing hundreds.
