Skopos theory

Skopos theory (Greek: σκοπός, "purpose, aim") is a theory in the field of translation studies employing the concepts of aim, purpose, intention and function in the analysis and production of translations. It was developed by the German linguist Hans J. Vermeer, with contributions from Katharina Reiss, in the late 1970s and early 1980s, and is a central approach within functionalist translation theory. According to skopos theory, the prime principle determining any translation process is the purpose — the skopos — of the overall translational action.

Basic principles

Skopos theory holds that a translation is determined by the function the target text is meant to fulfil for its recipients in the target culture. The translator works from a commission or "translation brief" that specifies, explicitly or implicitly, the purpose of the translation, its addressees, the time and place of reception, and the intended medium. From this Vermeer derived three rules: the skopos rule, which states that the way a target text is produced is determined by its intended purpose; the coherence rule, which requires the target text to be coherent and understandable for its recipients; and the fidelity rule, which requires intertextual coherence between the target text and the source text, subordinated to the first two rules.

Dethroning the source text

A controversial consequence of the theory is what Vermeer called the "dethronement" (Entthronung) of the source text: the source text is treated as an "offer of information" from which the translator selects what serves the target purpose. Equivalence to the source text is thus one possible translation goal among others, rather than the defining criterion of translation. This functionalist stance freed translation studies from purely linguistic equivalence models and opened it to broader action-theoretic and cultural approaches, later complemented by Christiane Nord's concept of "function plus loyalty."

Criticism

Critics have argued that skopos theory can be stretched to justify almost any deviation from the source text, that many commissions lack an explicit brief from which a skopos can be read, and that the theory applies more readily to pragmatic texts such as manuals and advertisements than to literary translation, where purpose is multiple and unstable. Defenders reply that the theory is descriptive of professional practice and that its rules form a hierarchy that prevents arbitrary translation.
