Flush toilet

A flush toilet (also known as a flushing toilet, water closet or WC) is a toilet that disposes of human waste by using the force of water to carry it through a drainpipe to another location for treatment, thus maintaining a separation between humans and their excreta. Flush toilets are a type of plumbing fixture and usually incorporate an "S", "U", "J", or "P"-shaped bend called a trap that causes water to collect in the toilet bowl, holding the waste and acting as a seal against noxious sewer gases.

Operation

A typical flush toilet consists of a fixed, water-containing vessel (the bowl) connected to a cistern or tank. Flushing releases a rapid flow of water — commonly 6 to 9 litres in modern designs, less in dual-flush models — which rises over the trap weir and siphons the bowl contents into the drainage system. The cistern then refills through a float-controlled inlet valve.

History

Water-flushed latrines existed in the Indus Valley Civilisation and in Roman sanitation systems. The modern flush toilet developed in England: Sir John Harington described a flushing device in 1596, Alexander Cumming patented the S-trap in 1775, and Thomas Crapper's firm popularised improved valves and fittings in the Victorian era, when expanding urban sewer networks made water closets practical for ordinary households.

Designs

Designs include gravity-fed tank toilets, pressure-assist toilets, dual-flush toilets that offer a reduced flush for liquid waste, wall-hung bowls with concealed cisterns, and the squat-style flush toilets common in parts of Asia. Vacuum toilets, used on ships and aircraft, flush with a fraction of a litre of water.

Water use and sanitation

Flush toilets connected to sewers or septic tanks are a cornerstone of modern sanitation, sharply reducing waterborne disease. However, they demand reliable water supplies and wastewater treatment; in much of the world, dry or low-water alternatives are preferred, and billions of people still lack safely managed sanitation.
