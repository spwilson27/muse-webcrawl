War photography

War photography involves photographing armed conflict and its effects on people and places. War photographers work in active combat zones and are among the most exposed of journalists; many have been killed or wounded in the course of their work.

History

The Crimean War (1853–1856) was the first conflict to be photographed extensively, by Roger Fenton, whose posed images of camps and landscapes avoided showing the dead, partly for reasons of decorum and partly due to long exposure times. The American Civil War was documented by Mathew Brady's studio, Alexander Gardner, and Timothy O'Sullivan, whose images of battlefields and corpses brought war's reality to the public. Gardner's and others' photographs were widely exhibited and published as engravings.

Twentieth century

Smaller cameras, faster film, and illustrated magazines transformed war coverage. Robert Capa's images from the Spanish Civil War and the Second World War — including his D-Day landing photographs for Life — defined the modern ideal of the frontline photographer ("If your pictures aren't good enough, you aren't close enough"). The Vietnam War, photographed with relatively uncensored access by Larry Burrows, Don McCullin, Eddie Adams, and Nick Ut, produced images credited with shifting public opinion. In the digital era, embedding with military units and instant transmission reshaped the field, while ethical debates over manipulation and staging continue.

Notable war photographers

  • Robert Capa, Gerda Taro, Margaret Bourke-White
  • Don McCullin, Larry Burrows, James Nachtwey, Lynsey Addario
