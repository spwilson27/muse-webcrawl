Federal judiciary of the United States

The federal judiciary of the United States is one of the three branches of the federal government of the United States organised under the United States Constitution and laws of the federal government. Article III of the Constitution requires the establishment of a Supreme Court and permits Congress to create other federal courts and place limitations on their jurisdiction.

Structure

  • Supreme Court of the United States — the court of last resort, composed of the Chief Justice and eight Associate Justices
  • Courts of Appeals — 13 appellate courts (the twelve geographic circuits and the Federal Circuit)
  • District Courts — 94 federal judicial districts, the general trial courts of the federal system
  • Article I courts — including Bankruptcy Courts, the Court of Federal Claims, and the Tax Court

Judges

Article III judges are nominated by the President and confirmed by the Senate, serving during "good behaviour" (effectively life tenure) with salaries that may not be diminished while in office. The judiciary is administered by the Judicial Conference and the Administrative Office of the U.S. Courts; the Chief Justice presides over both the Supreme Court and the federal judiciary as a whole.

Jurisdiction

Federal courts hear cases arising under the Constitution, federal law and treaties ("federal question" jurisdiction), cases between citizens of different states ("diversity" jurisdiction), and cases involving the United States government itself.
