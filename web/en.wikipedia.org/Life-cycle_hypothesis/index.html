Life-cycle hypothesis

The life-cycle hypothesis (LCH) is a model in economics, proposed by Franco Modigliani and his student Richard Brumberg in the early 1950s, which posits that individuals plan their consumption and savings behaviour over their life-cycle. The key assumption is that people seek to maintain a stable level of consumption over their lifetimes, borrowing when young and income is low, saving during their peak earning years, and dissaving in retirement.

Development

Modigliani developed the hypothesis while working on the aggregate consumption function at the University of Illinois and later at MIT. It was an attempt to reconcile the cross-sectional finding that richer households save a higher fraction of income with the long-run time-series finding that the aggregate saving ratio is roughly constant. The model was presented in the 1954 paper "Utility Analysis and the Aggregate Consumption Function" and developed further in Modigliani's 1986 Nobel Prize lecture.

The model

Assuming an individual expects to live for T more years, has wealth W, and expects to earn income Y until retirement R years from now, lifetime consumption is spread evenly so that annual consumption C = (W + RY)/T. The implied consumption function C = (1/T)W + (R/T)Y shows that the marginal propensity to consume out of income declines with age while the propensity to consume out of wealth is the reciprocal of remaining life expectancy. Aggregation over individuals of different ages yields the standard aggregate consumption function.

Implications

The hypothesis implies that a society's overall saving rate depends on its rate of economic growth: in a growing economy the young savers are wealthier than the old dissavers, so aggregate saving is positive, whereas in a stationary economy saving and dissaving cancel out. It also predicts that demographic structure, retirement age, and social security arrangements strongly affect national saving. The model underlies modern analysis of pension systems and retirement behaviour, and is closely related to Milton Friedman's permanent income hypothesis.

Criticism

Empirical work has questioned several assumptions: retirees often dissave less than predicted (the "retirement-savings puzzle"), bequest motives and precautionary saving matter, and liquidity constraints prevent many young households from borrowing against future income. Later research has embedded the life-cycle framework in models with uncertainty, buffer-stock saving and behavioural frictions.
