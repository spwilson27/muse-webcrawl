Hormone

A hormone is a chemical messenger produced by glands (or individual cells) that travels through the bloodstream — or, in plants, through tissues — to organs and cells where it regulates physiology and behaviour. Hormones coordinate growth, metabolism, reproduction, mood, and homeostasis, acting at very low concentrations via specific receptors.

Types

Animal hormones fall into broad chemical classes: peptide hormones such as insulin and growth hormone, which bind cell-surface receptors; steroid hormones such as oestrogen, testosterone, and cortisol, derived from cholesterol and able to enter cells to bind intracellular receptors; and amino-acid derivatives such as adrenaline and thyroxine. Plants use hormones including auxins, gibberellins, and ethylene.

Regulation

The endocrine system is organised in feedback loops, often governed by the hypothalamus and pituitary gland, the "master gland" that controls the thyroid, adrenals, and gonads. Secretin, identified in 1902, was the first hormone discovered; Ernest Starling coined the word "hormone" (from Greek hormao, "I excite") in 1905.

Disorders and medicine

Hormone excess or deficiency causes diseases such as diabetes mellitus (insulin), hyperthyroidism, and Addison's disease (cortisol). Synthetic hormones are mainstays of medicine, from insulin therapy to hormonal contraception and replacement therapy.
