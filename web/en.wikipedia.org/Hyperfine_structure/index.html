In atomic physics, hyperfine structure is the small splitting of spectral lines and energy levels of atoms, molecules, and ions caused by the interaction between the electrons and the electromagnetic multipole moments of the nucleus—chiefly its magnetic dipole moment (arising from nuclear spin) and its electric quadrupole moment. The splittings are typically about a thousand times smaller than those of fine structure, which arises from electron spin and relativistic effects.

Mechanism

The dominant contribution is usually the magnetic interaction between the nuclear magnetic moment and the magnetic field produced by the electrons at the nucleus. For hydrogen, this splits the ground state into two levels according to whether the proton and electron spins are parallel or antiparallel (total spin F = 1 or F = 0); transitions between them give the famous 21-centimetre hydrogen line at 1420 MHz. Nuclei with spin greater than 1/2 also possess an electric quadrupole moment whose interaction with the electric field gradient at the nucleus adds further splitting.

Applications

Hyperfine transitions provide extremely stable frequency references: the SI second is defined by the hyperfine transition of caesium-133 (9,192,631,770 periods), and hydrogen masers exploit the 21 cm transition. Hyperfine spectroscopy is used to measure nuclear moments, probe molecular structure, and study condensed matter; techniques such as electron paramagnetic resonance and nuclear magnetic resonance analyse hyperfine couplings, and Mössbauer spectroscopy resolves hyperfine fields at nuclei in solids.
