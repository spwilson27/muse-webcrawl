Berlin Circle

The Berlin Circle (German: Berliner Kreis) was a group of philosophers associated with the Society for Empirical/Scientific Philosophy in Berlin during the late 1920s and 1930s. Alongside the Vienna Circle, it was one of the two main centres of the philosophical movement known as logical empiricism (or logical positivism).

Members and activities

The circle formed around Hans Reichenbach, who held a chair in the philosophy of physics at the University of Berlin from 1926. Other prominent members and associates included Carl Gustav Hempel, David Hilbert's collaborator Paul Herz, Kurt Grelling, Richard von Mises, and Walter Dubislav. The group was closely linked organizationally with the Vienna Circle around Moritz Schlick; together they published the journal Erkenntnis ("Knowledge"), edited by Reichenbach and Rudolf Carnap, and organized international congresses for the unity of science.

Philosophy

The Berlin Circle emphasized the application of modern logic and probability theory to philosophical problems, the analysis of causality and space-time in relativity theory, and a strongly naturalistic, anti-metaphysical conception of philosophy. Compared with the Vienna Circle, its members placed greater stress on probability and induction — Reichenbach developed a frequentist theory of probability — and on the philosophy of physics.

Dissolution and legacy

With the Nazi seizure of power in 1933, most members emigrated; Kurt Grelling was murdered in the Holocaust. Reichenbach went first to Istanbul and then to the University of California, Los Angeles, while Hempel and others carried logical empiricism into American philosophy of science, where it shaped the discipline for decades.
