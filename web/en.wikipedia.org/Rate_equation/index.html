Rate equation

In chemical kinetics, the rate equation (or rate law) of a chemical reaction is an empirical mathematical expression linking the reaction rate to the concentrations of the reactants. For a reaction aA + bB → products it commonly takes the form rate = k[A]m[B]n, where k is the rate constant and the exponents m and n are the (partial) orders of reaction, determined experimentally rather than from the stoichiometric coefficients.

Orders of reaction

The overall order is the sum m + n. In a zero-order reaction the rate is independent of concentration; in a first-order reaction it is proportional to one concentration (radioactive decay is the classic example, with a constant half-life); second-order reactions depend on the square of one concentration or the product of two. Rate laws are measured by methods such as initial rates and isolation, and integrated rate laws describe concentration as a function of time.

Interpretation

For elementary steps the rate law reflects molecularity, but an overall rate law reveals the composition of the rate-determining step and any pre-equilibria in the mechanism. The temperature dependence of k is described by the Arrhenius equation.
