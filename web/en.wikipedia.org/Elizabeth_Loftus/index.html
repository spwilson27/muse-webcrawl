Elizabeth F. Loftus (born 16 October 1944) is an American cognitive psychologist known for her research on the malleability of human memory. Her studies demonstrated the misinformation effect — that post-event information can alter a person's recollection of an event — and helped establish that entirely false memories can be implanted through suggestion.

Research

Loftus received her PhD from Stanford University in 1970. In classic experiments with John Palmer in the 1970s, witnesses who were asked how fast cars were going when they "smashed" into each other estimated higher speeds, and later falsely remembered broken glass, compared with witnesses asked with the verb "hit". Her "lost in the mall" study showed that about a quarter of participants could come to remember a fictitious childhood event suggested by a family member.

Impact

Loftus's work has influenced legal practice concerning eyewitness testimony, police interviewing and the assessment of recovered memories in therapy. She has testified as an expert witness in many trials and received numerous honours, including membership of the National Academy of Sciences. Her findings have also attracted criticism and debate, particularly from clinicians and some researchers of trauma, regarding the interpretation of laboratory false-memory studies.

Selected works

Books include Eyewitness Testimony (1979), The Myth of Repressed Memory (1994, with Katherine Ketcham) and The Myth of Memory. She has also served as president of the Association for Psychological Science.
