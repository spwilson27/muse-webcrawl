Camel case

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Camel case (sometimes stylised as camelCase) is the practice of writing phrases without spaces or punctuation, capitalising each word or abbreviation in the phrase. The name comes from the resemblance of the capital letters to the humps on a camel's back. It is widely used in computer programming as a naming convention for variables, functions, and classes.

Variations

Two main variants are distinguished. In lower camel case (also called dromedary case), the first letter of the compound is lowercase and each subsequent word begins with a capital: camelCase, numberOfStudents. In upper camel case, known as PascalCase after the Pascal programming language, the first letter is also capitalised: CamelCase, NumberOfStudents. Languages and style guides differ on which variant to use for which constructs; Java, for example, prescribes lower camel case for methods and variables and upper camel case for classes.

History

The practice of joining words with internal capitals long predates computing, appearing in names such as MacDonald and in chemical formulas. Programmers adopted it early because many programming languages do not allow spaces in identifiers. Some of the earliest documented computing uses date to the 1970s at Xerox PARC, where the Mesa and Smalltalk communities popularised the style; it later became standard in Java, JavaScript, C#, and many other languages and APIs.

Readability studies

Empirical studies on identifier style have produced mixed results: some experiments find camel case read more quickly by experienced programmers, while others find underscore-separated snake case easier for novices. Training and familiarity appear to matter more than the style itself.
