Theory of mind (ToM) is the capacity to attribute mental states — beliefs, desires, intentions, emotions, and knowledge — to oneself and to others, and to understand that others have beliefs and perspectives different from one's own. It is a foundational element of social cognition, enabling prediction and interpretation of behaviour. The term was introduced by David Premack and Guy Woodruff in a 1978 paper asking "Does the chimpanzee have a theory of mind?"

Development

Children typically pass explicit false-belief tests — such as the Sally–Anne task, in which a doll's marble is moved while she is absent and the child must say where Sally will look for it — between the ages of four and five. Precursors include joint attention (from about nine months), pretend play, and understanding of desires, which emerge earlier. Failure of false-belief tasks was influentially linked by Simon Baron-Cohen, Alan Leslie, and Uta Frith (1985) to autism, though later work has complicated the relationship and questioned whether task failure reflects conceptual absence versus performance demands such as language. Deaf children of hearing parents, who have less early conversation about mental states, are often delayed on false-belief tasks.

Mechanisms and debates

Theorists debate whether theory of mind rests on an innate, domain-specific "theory-theory" module, on simulation — using one's own mind as a model of others — or on general executive and linguistic development. Neuroimaging implicates a network including the temporoparietal junction, medial prefrontal cortex, and precuneus. Non-human animal research investigates ToM in chimpanzees, ravens, and other species, with evidence that some animals track what others have seen.

Applications

Theory of mind figures in research on autism spectrum conditions, schizophrenia, dementia, developmental psychology, artificial intelligence evaluation, and philosophy of mind. Ordinal scales of ToM tasks (diverse desires, diverse beliefs, knowledge access, false belief, hidden emotion) chart a typical developmental sequence.
