Witold Lutosławski

Witold Roman Lutosławski (25 January 1913 – 7 February 1994) was a Polish composer and conductor, widely regarded as one of the most important European composers of the 20th century and the foremost Polish composer after Chopin. His major works include four symphonies, a Concerto for Orchestra, the string quartet, and the cello concerto written for Mstislav Rostropovich.

Style

Lutosławski's early music drew on Polish folk material, most famously in the Concerto for Orchestra (1954). From the late 1950s he developed a personal harmonic language based on twelve-note chords and introduced "limited aleatorism" — passages in which performers repeat short figures unsynchronised within controlled limits — beginning with Jeux vénitiens (1961). His later music, including the Third and Fourth Symphonies, combined this technique with large-scale dramatic forms.

Life

Born in Warsaw, he survived the German occupation playing piano in cafés. After the war his First Symphony was banned as "formalist" under Stalinist cultural policy; following the 1956 thaw his international reputation grew steadily, and he conducted his own works around the world until his death in 1994.
