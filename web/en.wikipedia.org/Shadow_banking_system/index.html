Shadow banking system

Entities in the shadow banking system perform bank-like functions outside traditional banking regulation.

The shadow banking system is a term for the collection of non-bank financial intermediaries that provide services similar to those of commercial banks but are not subject to the same level of regulatory oversight, deposit insurance, or access to central bank lending facilities. These entities include money market funds, hedge funds, structured investment vehicles (SIVs), special purpose entities, securities lenders, and finance companies.

The term "shadow banking" is generally attributed to economist Paul McCulley of PIMCO, who used it in a speech at the Federal Reserve's annual Jackson Hole symposium in 2007. The shadow banking system played a major role in the expansion of the housing bubble in the United States and was a central factor in the financial crisis of 2007–2008, when a run on shadow banking entities—similar to a traditional bank run—caused severe liquidity problems throughout the financial system.

Functions and activities

Shadow banking entities perform maturity transformation (borrowing short-term and lending long-term), liquidity transformation, and leverage, much like traditional banks. Common activities include securitization of loans (such as mortgage-backed securities), repurchase agreements (repos), asset-backed commercial paper, and the provision of credit through non-bank channels. Because these activities occur outside the regulated banking perimeter, they historically operated with less capital, less disclosure, and greater interconnectedness with the traditional banking system than was widely appreciated before the crisis.

Size and importance

At its peak before the financial crisis, the shadow banking system in the United States was estimated to rival or exceed the size of the traditional banking sector. The Financial Stability Board (FSB) monitors global shadow banking activity (now often termed "non-bank financial intermediation") and has reported that it accounts for a substantial share of total global financial assets.

Regulation

Following the 2007–2008 crisis, reforms such as the Dodd–Frank Act in the United States and analogous measures elsewhere brought some shadow banking activities under increased oversight, including greater transparency requirements for money market funds and the designation of systemically important financial institutions. Debate continues over the appropriate balance between regulation and the credit provision that shadow banking supports.
