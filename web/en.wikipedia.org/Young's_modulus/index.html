SymbolE, Y

Young's modulus, also called the elastic modulus or tensile modulus, is a mechanical property that measures the stiffness of a solid material. It is defined as the ratio of tensile stress to tensile strain in the linear regime of a material's deformation: E = σ / ε, where σ is the uniaxial stress (force per unit area) and ε is the resulting strain (proportional deformation).

Definition

When a material is loaded in tension or compression within its elastic limit, it deforms in proportion to the applied stress, following Hooke's law. Young's modulus quantifies that proportionality. A large value indicates a stiff material that deforms little; a small value indicates a flexible one.

Typical values

Approximate Young's moduli include: rubber, about 0.01–0.1 GPa; wood along the grain, about 10 GPa; aluminium, about 69 GPa; steel, about 200 GPa; and diamond, about 1,050–1,200 GPa, among the highest known.

History

The concept is named after the British polymath Thomas Young, who described it in 1807, although earlier versions appear in the work of Euler and others. It is one of the fundamental parameters in engineering design, used together with the shear modulus and bulk modulus to describe isotropic elastic behaviour.

Applications

Young's modulus is used to predict deflection of beams, vibration frequencies, buckling loads, and the behaviour of structures and machine parts under load, and it is routinely measured by tensile testing.
