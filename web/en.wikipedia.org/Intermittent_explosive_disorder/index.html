Intermittent explosive disorder

Intermittent explosive disorder (IED) is an impulse-control disorder characterized by sudden episodes of unwarranted anger. The disorder is exemplified by hostility, impulsivity, and recurrent aggressive outbursts. People with IED "explode" into a rage despite a lack of apparent provocation or reason. Individuals suffering from intermittent explosive disorder have reported outbursts as being brief (lasting less than an hour), alongside a variety of bodily symptoms such as sweating, stuttering, a tight chest, twitching and palpitations.

Diagnosis

Under the DSM-5, diagnosis requires recurrent behavioural outbursts representing a failure to control aggressive impulses—either frequent verbal or non-damaging physical aggression over a three-month period, or a smaller number of outbursts involving damage or injury within a year. The magnitude of aggressiveness must be grossly out of proportion to any provocation, the outbursts must not be premeditated, and they must cause marked distress, impairment, or financial or legal consequences. The diagnosis is not made if the behaviour is better explained by another condition.

Epidemiology

Lifetime prevalence estimates in United States community studies have ranged from roughly 3 to 7 percent, depending on how narrowly the disorder is defined. Onset is commonly in adolescence, and the disorder co-occurs frequently with mood, anxiety and substance use disorders.

Treatment

Treatment typically involves psychotherapy, particularly cognitive behavioural approaches and group therapy focusing on impulse control and relaxation training. Medications such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors, mood stabilisers and anticonvulsants have been used, though evidence is limited and no drug is specifically approved for the disorder in many jurisdictions.
