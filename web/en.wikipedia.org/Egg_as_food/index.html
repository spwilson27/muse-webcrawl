Humans and their hominid relatives have consumed eggs for millions of years. The most widely consumed eggs are those of the chicken. However, the eggs of other birds such as duck, quail and goose, as well as fish eggs (roe and caviar) and reptile eggs, are eaten in many cultures.

Nutritional value

A large chicken egg contains roughly 70 kilocalories, 6 grams of complete protein, and significant amounts of vitamin B12, riboflavin, selenium and choline. The yolk holds most of the fat and cholesterol, about 186 mg in a large egg, while the white is mostly water and the protein ovalbumin. Modern dietary guidance generally no longer sets a strict limit on dietary cholesterol for most people.

Culinary uses

Eggs are used in countless dishes: boiled, fried, poached, scrambled and baked, and as an ingredient that binds, leavens and emulsifies in baking, sauces such as mayonnaise and hollandaise, and custards. Cooking methods range from the century egg of China, preserved in alkaline clay, to balut, salted duck eggs, pickled eggs and Scotch eggs.

Production

Global egg production exceeds 80 million tonnes annually, with China the largest producer. Production systems include conventional battery cages, enriched cages, barn, free-range and organic systems, which differ in animal welfare standards and price. Shell colour — white or brown — depends on the breed of hen and does not affect nutrition.

Health and safety

Raw or undercooked eggs can carry Salmonella, and pasteurized eggs are recommended for dishes such as homemade mayonnaise for vulnerable people. Egg allergy is one of the most common food allergies in children, though many outgrow it.
