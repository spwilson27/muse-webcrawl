Diamicton

Diamicton is a non-genetic term in geology for a terrigenous sediment that is very poorly sorted, containing particles ranging in size from clay to boulders embedded in a finer-grained matrix. The consolidated (lithified) equivalent is called diamictite. The terms were introduced to describe such deposits without implying a specific origin, replacing the genetic term tillite where a glacial origin was not demonstrated.

Diamicton: boulders and cobbles suspended in a muddy matrix.

Origin

Diamictons are most commonly deposited by glaciers as till, but they also form by debris flows, landslides, turbidity currents, volcanic lahars, and iceberg rafting in marine and lacustrine settings. Because identical textures arise from many processes, sedimentologists use "diamicton" as a purely descriptive lithofacies term and interpret genesis from context, clast shape, fabric and associated sediments.

Significance

Ancient diamictites are important evidence in reconstructing past glaciations, most famously the widespread Neoproterozoic diamictites associated with the proposed "Snowball Earth" episodes, and the late Palaeozoic glacial deposits of Gondwana. Their interpretation — glacial versus mass-flow — has been a long-running debate in sedimentary geology.
