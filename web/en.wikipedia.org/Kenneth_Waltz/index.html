Kenneth Neal Waltz (8 June 1924 – 12 May 2013) was an American political scientist and a founder of neorealism, or structural realism, in international relations theory. His books Man, the State, and War (1959) and Theory of International Politics (1979) are among the most influential works in the discipline.

Life and career

Waltz served in the United States Army during the Second World War and afterwards studied at Oberlin College and Columbia University, where he earned his doctorate in 1957. He taught at Swarthmore College, Brandeis University, and for many years at the University of California, Berkeley, before returning to Columbia.

Man, the State, and War

In his first book, Waltz organised explanations of war into three "images": human nature, the internal structure of states, and the anarchic structure of the international system. The framework became a standard teaching device in international relations.

Theory of International Politics

Waltz's 1979 book proposed that the structure of the international system — anarchy plus the distribution of capabilities among great powers — constrains state behaviour regardless of leaders or ideologies. States seek security, imitate successful practices, and balance against concentrated power. Bipolar systems, he argued, are more stable than multipolar ones. The theory, termed neorealism, reshaped the field and provoked decades of debate with liberals, constructivists, and classical realists such as Hans Morgenthau.

Nuclear proliferation

Waltz controversially argued that the gradual spread of nuclear weapons could promote stability, since nuclear-armed states face overwhelming incentives for caution — the thesis of his 1981 Adelphi Paper "The Spread of Nuclear Weapons: More May Be Better" and of his later debate with Scott Sagan.
