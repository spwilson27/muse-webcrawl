Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica

Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (English: The Mathematical Principles of Natural Philosophy), often referred to simply as the Principia, is a book by Isaac Newton that expounds Newton's laws of motion and his law of universal gravitation. Published in Latin on 5 July 1687, it is considered one of the most important works in the history of science.

Contents

The Principia consists of three books. Book I, subtitled De motu corporum (On the Motion of Bodies), concerns motion in the absence of any resisting medium. Book II discusses motion in resisting media and hydrostatics. Book III, De mundi systemate (On the System of the World), applies the principles to the solar system, deriving Kepler's laws and explaining tides, comets, and the motion of the Moon.

Laws of motion

Newton set out his three laws of motion: a body remains at rest or in uniform motion unless acted upon by a force; force equals mass times acceleration; and for every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. Combined with universal gravitation, these laws provided the first unified account of terrestrial and celestial motion.

Influence

The work dominated the scientific view of the physical universe for nearly three centuries, until the advent of relativity and quantum mechanics in the early twentieth century. Revised editions appeared in 1713 and 1726.
