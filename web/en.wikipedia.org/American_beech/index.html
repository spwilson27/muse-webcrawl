The American beech (Fagus grandifolia) is a species of beech tree native to eastern North America, from Nova Scotia west to southern Ontario in Canada and south to eastern Texas and northern Florida in the United States. It is a large deciduous tree, growing to 20–35 metres (66–115 ft) tall, with smooth, light grey bark — a surface famously inviting to carved initials — and elliptical leaves with coarse teeth.

Description and ecology

American beech is a shade-tolerant climax species of the beech–maple forest, often found with sugar maple. Its small triangular nuts, borne in a soft-spined husk that opens into four lobes, are an important food for wildlife, including deer, squirrels, black bears, and historically the passenger pigeon. The tree spreads readily by root suckers, forming clonal colonies.

Threats

Beech bark disease, a complex of the introduced beech scale insect (Cryptococcus fagisuga) and Neonectria fungi, has devastated populations since its accidental introduction to Nova Scotia around 1890. More recently, beech leaf disease, associated with a nematode, has spread through the northeastern United States and Canada.

Uses

The dense, hard wood is used for flooring, furniture, tool handles, and railroad ties, though it is difficult to work and does not resist decay outdoors. Beechnuts were historically gathered as food and for oil, and the tree is widely planted as an ornamental shade tree.
