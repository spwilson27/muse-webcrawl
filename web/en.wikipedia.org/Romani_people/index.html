The Romani people (Romani: Romane Manusha), also known as the Roma, are an Indo-Aryan ethnic group who traditionally lived a nomadic, itinerant lifestyle, and are dispersed across Europe and the Americas. Linguistic and genetic evidence indicates that the Romani originated in northern India and migrated westwards, reaching Europe by the Middle Ages. With an estimated population of 10–12 million in Europe alone, they form the continent's largest ethnic minority.

Origins and migration

The Romani language is Indo-Aryan, closely related to languages of north-western India, suggesting an origin in that region around the early medieval period. Romani groups travelled through Persia, Armenia and the Byzantine Empire, arriving in the Balkans by the 13th–14th centuries and in Western Europe by the 15th. Distinct subgroups — including the Sinti of Germany, the Calé of Spain, the Romanichal of Britain and the Kalderash — developed distinct dialects and customs.

History and persecution

Romani people were enslaved in the Romanian principalities until the mid-19th century and faced expulsion edicts, forced assimilation and restrictions across Europe. During the Second World War, Nazi Germany and its allies carried out the Porajmos (the Devouring), the genocide of the European Roma, in which between 220,000 and 500,000 or more were murdered. Discrimination, segregation in housing and education, and anti-Romani violence have persisted in many countries into the present.

Culture

Romani culture places strong emphasis on family, purity codes (marime), and respect for elders. Romani music — from the lăutari of Romania to flamenco in Spain and Romani jazz (jazz manouche) associated with Django Reinhardt — has deeply influenced European musical traditions. The first World Romani Congress in London in 1971 adopted the Romani flag and anthem ("Gelem, Gelem") and promoted the name "Roma". International Roma Day is marked on 8 April.
