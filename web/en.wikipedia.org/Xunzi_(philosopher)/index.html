Xunzi

Xunzi (c. 310 – c. 235 BCE), born Xun Kuang, was a Chinese Confucian philosopher of the late Warring States period. Together with Confucius and Mencius, he is one of the great early systematizers of Confucian thought, though his teaching that human nature is bad (xing e) set him apart from Mencius, who held that human nature is good. The text that bears his name, the Xunzi, is among the most carefully argued works of ancient Chinese philosophy.

Life

Xunzi was born in the state of Zhao. He travelled to Qi, where he became a leading figure — reportedly three times the libationer (senior scholar) — at the Jixia Academy, the era's great intellectual centre. He later served as magistrate of Lanling under the state of Chu, where he may have spent his final years. His students included the Legalist theorist Han Fei and Li Si, who became chancellor of the Qin dynasty.

Philosophy

Human nature is bad

Xunzi argued that people are born with desires for profit, sex and status; left unrestrained, these produce conflict and disorder. Goodness is therefore the product of "conscious activity" (wei) — deliberate cultivation through ritual, education and the accumulated artifice of civilisation, like straightening warped wood with a press-frame.

Ritual and learning

Ritual propriety (li) and righteousness (yi), devised by the sage kings, reshape desire and give social life form and beauty. Xunzi prized learning as lifelong accumulation, writing that "learning never ceases" and that blue dye comes from the indigo plant yet is bluer than the plant — the student surpasses the material through processing.

Heaven and language

His chapter "Discourse on Heaven" treats Heaven (tian) as impersonal nature: it does not respond to human virtue or vice, so one should understand natural regularities rather than pray. In "On the Correct Use of Names" he developed a sophisticated account of how names are fixed by convention and agreement.

Legacy

Despite his influence in the Han dynasty, orthodox Confucianism later followed Mencius, and Xunzi was dropped from the Confucian canon of venerated masters for much of imperial history; modern scholarship has revived interest in his naturalism and political realism.
