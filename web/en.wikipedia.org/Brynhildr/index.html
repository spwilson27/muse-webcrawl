Brynhildr (Old Norse: Brynhildr, "armour-battle") is a valkyrie and heroine of Norse mythology, best known from the poems of the Poetic Edda and the Völsunga saga. She is the Norse counterpart of Brunhild in the German Nibelungenlied. In the Norse sources she is a supernatural figure: a valkyrie punished by Odin for disobedience.

Mythology

According to Sigrdrífumál and the Völsunga saga, Brynhildr gave victory to a king other than the one Odin favoured. In revenge Odin pricked her with a sleep-thorn and decreed that she would marry. She was placed in a castle surrounded by a wall of fire, vowing to wed only the man who could ride through the flames. The hero Sigurd, riding the horse Grani and bearing the sword Gram, crossed the fire and woke her; in some versions they pledged marriage and had a daughter, Aslaug.

Betrayal and death

Through the trickery of the Gjúkungs and a potion of forgetfulness, Sigurd later wooed Brynhildr on behalf of King Gunnar, riding through her fire in Gunnar's shape. When the deception was revealed by Gudrun during a quarrel of the queens, Brynhildr goaded Gunnar and his brothers into murdering Sigurd. Grief-stricken, she stabbed herself and was burned on Sigurd's funeral pyre, as foretold in the Helreið Brynhildar, which describes her journey to the afterlife.

Interpretation

The Eddic poems differ in details, and scholars debate how the Norse valkyrie version relates to the German queen Brunhild and to possible historical figures such as the Visigothic queen Brunhilda of Austrasia.
