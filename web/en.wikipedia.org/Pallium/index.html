Pallium

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

This article is about the ecclesiastical vestment. For the anatomical structure, see Pallium (neuroanatomy).

The pallium (from Latin pallium, a cloak) is an ecclesiastical vestment in the Roman Catholic Church, originally worn only by the pope, and now bestowed by him on metropolitan archbishops as a sign of their jurisdiction and of communion with the Holy See. It is a narrow circular band of white lamb's wool, about two inches wide, with two pendants hanging front and back, decorated with six black (occasionally red, for the pope) crosses, and fastened with gold-headed pins.

History and symbolism

The pallium's origins are debated; by the 6th century it was a distinctive papal insignium granted to favoured bishops, and by the Middle Ages it had become the formal mark of metropolitan authority, to be sought from Rome within months of appointment. Canon law (canon 437) still requires metropolitans to request it. Traditionally the wool comes from lambs blessed on the feast of St Agnes (21 January), the pallia are kept at the tomb of St Peter, and since 2015, under a decision of Pope Francis, they are blessed by the pope on the feast of Saints Peter and Paul (29 June) but formally imposed in each archbishop's own cathedral by the papal nuncio.

Other uses

The term also names a similar vestment — the omophorion — worn in Eastern Christianity, and lends its name to heraldic usage. In anatomy, "pallium" refers to the cerebral cortex region of the vertebrate forebrain.
