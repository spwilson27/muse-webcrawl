Monotreme

The platypus and echidnas are the only living monotremes

Monotremes are mammals of the order Monotremata, the only mammals that lay eggs instead of giving birth to live young. The five living species — the duck-billed platypus and four species of echidna (spiny anteaters) — are found only in Australia and New Guinea. Their name, "single opening", refers to the cloaca through which urinary, digestive, and reproductive tracts all exit, as in reptiles and birds.

Characteristics

Monotremes combine mammalian traits — hair, milk production, a single lower jaw bone, and three middle-ear bones — with ancestral features: they lay leathery eggs, have a lower and more variable body temperature than other mammals, and lack nipples, secreting milk onto skin patches for their young to lap up. Adults lack true teeth. The platypus is one of the few venomous mammals — males bear hind-leg spurs — and hunts underwater with an electrosensitive bill, a sense echidnas share in weaker form for detecting prey in soil.

Evolution

Monotremes are the earliest-diverging branch of living mammals, splitting from the therian line (marsupials and placentals) probably in the Jurassic or earlier; fossils such as Steropodon and Teinolophos come from Cretaceous Australia, and a fossil monotreme (Monotrematum) from Palaeocene South America shows the group once ranged across Gondwana. Because of their mosaic of traits, monotremes are invaluable for reconstructing the biology of early mammals — including the discovery that their genome retains a bird-like sex chromosome system.
