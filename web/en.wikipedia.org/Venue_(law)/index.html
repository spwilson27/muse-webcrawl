Venue (law)

In law, venue is the proper or most convenient location for the trial of a case. It concerns the geographic place — typically a county, district, or other subdivision — in which a court with jurisdiction may hear and determine a case. Venue is distinct from jurisdiction, which is the court's authority over the subject matter and the parties; a court may have jurisdiction but be an improper venue.

Principles

Venue rules generally locate a trial where the defendant resides, where the events giving rise to the claim occurred, or where property in dispute is situated. They aim at convenience for parties and witnesses and at fairness — preventing a plaintiff from choosing an oppressive or wholly unrelated forum. In United States federal courts, general venue is governed by 28 U.S.C. § 1391, under which a civil action may be brought in a district where any defendant resides, where a substantial part of the events occurred, or, in some cases, where a defendant is subject to personal jurisdiction.

Change of venue

Courts may transfer a case to another venue for the convenience of parties and witnesses and in the interest of justice (in federal practice, under 28 U.S.C. § 1404(a)). In criminal cases, a change of venue may be granted where pretrial publicity threatens the defendant's right to an impartial jury. Forum selection clauses in contracts, by contrast, allow parties to agree in advance on venue, subject to reasonableness limits.

Criminal venue

Constitutions often guarantee trial in the place where the crime was committed; Article III and the Sixth Amendment of the U.S. Constitution both reflect this vicinage principle, protecting defendants from being hauled to distant tribunals.
