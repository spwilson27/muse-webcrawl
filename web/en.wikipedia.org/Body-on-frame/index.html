Body-on-frame

Body-on-frame construction
Also calledSeparate chassis, ladder frame construction
Main structureIndependent steel frame (chassis)
Common usesTrucks, full-size SUVs, off-road vehicles
Contrast withUnibody (monocoque) construction

Body-on-frame is a traditional automobile construction method in which a separate body is mounted on a relatively rigid vehicle frame or chassis that carries the engine and drivetrain. It was the dominant method of building cars and trucks for most of the twentieth century and remains common for pickup trucks, large sport utility vehicles, and heavy commercial vehicles.

Design

In a body-on-frame vehicle, the frame is typically a ladder frame: two longitudinal box-section or C-channel rails joined by cross members. The engine, transmission, axles, and suspension are attached to the frame, and the body is bolted on top, usually through rubber mounts that isolate vibration and noise. Because the body is not a primary load-bearing structure, it can be changed or restyled without redesigning the running gear, a flexibility that historically allowed coachbuilders to fit custom bodies to a manufacturer's chassis.

History

Early automobiles inherited the practice from horse-drawn carriages, with wooden or steel frames carrying separate bodywork. Mass-production cars such as the Ford Model T used ladder frames. From the 1930s onward, passenger cars progressively adopted unitary (unibody) construction, in which the body shell itself forms the structure; by the 1980s unibody dominated car production worldwide. Body-on-frame persisted in trucks and large SUVs, where its strength, towing capacity, and ease of repair remained valued.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include high torsional strength for towing and off-road use, simpler and cheaper repair of frame and body separately, easier adaptation to multiple body styles, and better isolation of the cabin from road noise. Disadvantages include greater weight, higher floor and centre of gravity, and typically poorer crash performance and fuel economy than a comparable unibody design. Modern examples include the Ford F-Series, Chevrolet Silverado, Toyota Land Cruiser, and Jeep Wrangler.
