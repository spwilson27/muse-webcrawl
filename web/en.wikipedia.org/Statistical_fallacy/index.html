Statistical fallacy

A statistical fallacy is a misuse of statistics or statistical reasoning that leads to invalid conclusions. Fallacies may arise from flawed sampling, misinterpretation of probability, inappropriate tests, or misleading presentation of data, and they occur in science, journalism, litigation, and everyday argument.

Common examples

Well-known statistical fallacies include the base rate fallacy, in which prior probabilities are ignored; Simpson's paradox, in which a trend reverses when groups are combined; survivorship bias, in which only successes are observed; the ecological fallacy, in which group-level data are used to infer individual behaviour; the prosecutor's fallacy, involving transposed conditional probabilities; and the Texas sharpshooter fallacy, in which patterns are claimed in random data after the fact.

Data presentation

Graphs can mislead through truncated axes, cherry-picked time ranges, and distorted scales. In research, fallacies include p-hacking, multiple comparisons without correction, and confusing correlation with causation.

Avoidance

Sound statistical practice—pre-registered hypotheses, representative samples, appropriate controls, and honest visualization—reduces these errors. Texts such as Darrell Huff's How to Lie with Statistics (1954) popularized critical awareness of statistical fallacies.
