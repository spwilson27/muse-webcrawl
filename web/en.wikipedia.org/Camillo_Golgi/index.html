Camillo Golgi

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Camillo Golgi (7 July 1843 – 21 January 1926) was an Italian biologist and pathologist who discovered the Golgi apparatus and developed the silver staining technique that first revealed the detailed structure of nerve cells. He shared the 1906 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Santiago Ramón y Cajal "in recognition of their work on the structure of the nervous system".

Life and career

Golgi was born in Corteno, Lombardy, and studied medicine at the University of Pavia, where he later spent most of his career, becoming professor of histology and of general pathology and eventually rector. Working in a small makeshift laboratory in the hospital of Abbiategrasso, he made the discovery that would transform neuroscience.

The black reaction

In 1873 Golgi announced the reazione nera ("black reaction"), a staining method in which nervous tissue hardened in potassium dichromate is impregnated with silver nitrate, randomly labelling a small fraction of neurons in their entirety in black against a yellow background. For the first time, whole nerve cells — cell body, dendrites, and axon — could be seen clearly. Using this method Golgi described several cell types (including the Golgi cells of the cerebellum and the type I and type II neurons) and discovered the Golgi tendon organ.

Golgi apparatus and other work

In 1898 Golgi identified an internal reticular apparatus within cells — the Golgi apparatus (or Golgi complex), now known to be central to the modification, sorting, and packaging of proteins for secretion. He also worked extensively on malaria, distinguishing the fevers caused by different Plasmodium species and correlating fever cycles with parasite development.

Neuron doctrine controversy

Despite his staining method having made the neuron visible, Golgi defended the reticular theory — the view that the nervous system is a continuous network — against Cajal's neuron doctrine. At the 1906 Nobel ceremony the two laureates publicly argued opposite positions; subsequent electron microscopy confirmed Cajal's view of discrete neurons separated by synapses, though Golgi's method made that discovery possible.
