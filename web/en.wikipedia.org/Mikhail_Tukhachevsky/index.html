Mikhail Nikolayevich Tukhachevsky (16 February 1893 – 12 June 1937) was a Soviet general and Marshal of the Soviet Union who served as chief of staff of the Red Army. A brilliant commander in the Russian Civil War and the theorist of "deep operations," he was executed during Stalin's Great Purge in the Trial of the Trotskyist Anti-Soviet Military Organization.

Career

Commissioned in the Imperial Russian Army, Tukhachevsky was captured in the First World War and escaped on his fifth attempt. Joining the Bolsheviks in 1918, he commanded armies on the Eastern Front and in the Caucasus, crushed the Kronstadt rebellion and the Tambov revolt, and was defeated before Warsaw in the Polish–Soviet War of 1920. As chief of staff (1925–28) and later deputy people's commissar for defence, he drove the mechanisation of the Red Army and developed, with colleagues such as Triandafillov and Isserson, the doctrine of deep battle and deep operations — successive echeloned offensives striking the enemy's whole depth.

Arrest and death

In May 1937 he was arrested, tortured, and tried in secret with seven other senior commanders on fabricated charges of espionage and conspiracy; all were shot on 12 June 1937. The purge of the officer corps that followed severely weakened the Red Army on the eve of the Second World War. Tukhachevsky was rehabilitated in 1957, and his writings on deep operations later influenced Soviet and foreign military thought.
