Nuclear deterrence

Nuclear deterrence is the military doctrine that the threat of using nuclear weapons against an adversary prevents that adversary from using nuclear weapons or undertaking other actions that would trigger a nuclear response. It is the centrepiece of the security policies of the nuclear-weapon states and is commonly summarised by the concept of mutually assured destruction (MAD), under which a full-scale use of nuclear weapons by two or more opposing sides would cause the complete annihilation of both the attacker and the defender.

Principles

Deterrence theory holds that a state can prevent an attack by credibly threatening a retaliatory strike that would inflict unacceptable damage on the aggressor. For deterrence to hold, three conditions are usually cited: the retaliatory capability must exist, it must be communicated to the adversary, and the threat to use it must be credible. A second-strike capability — the ability to absorb a first strike and still retaliate — is considered essential, which is why nuclear forces are dispersed among land-based missiles, submarines, and aircraft (the "nuclear triad").

History

The concept emerged soon after the first atomic weapons were developed in the 1940s. Bernard Brodie wrote in 1946 that the chief purpose of military establishments must shift from winning wars to averting them. During the Cold War, deterrence between the United States and the Soviet Union dominated strategic thinking; doctrines included massive retaliation, flexible response, and counterforce targeting. Thomas Schelling formalised much of the theory in The Strategy of Conflict (1960).

Criticism

Critics argue that deterrence rests on assumptions of rational decision-making that may fail under stress, miscalculation, or accidents, and that its apparent stability during the Cold War may owe as much to luck as to design. Supporters respond that no nuclear weapons have been used in war since 1945 and that deterrence has prevented great-power war.
