Tool use by animals

Tool use by non-human animals is any behaviour in which an animal employs an external object as an extension of its body to achieve a goal — for example, a chimpanzee using a twig to extract termites or a sea otter striking shellfish against a rock held on its chest. Once thought to be uniquely human, tool use is now documented across mammals, birds, fish, cephalopods and insects, and its study has reshaped ideas about animal cognition.

Definitions and debate

Definitions vary: Benjamin Beck's influential 1980 formulation describes tool use as the external employment of an unattached environmental object to alter the form, position or condition of another object or organism. Whether behaviours such as an elephant dropping a log on an electric fence qualify remains debated among ethologists.

Examples

Chimpanzees manufacture termite-fishing probes and nut-cracking hammer stones, with group-specific traditions that researchers describe as cultures. New Caledonian crows fashion hooked tools from twigs and pandanus leaves. Sea otters use anvil stones to open molluscs; bottlenose dolphins carry marine sponges to protect their rostrums while foraging; the veined octopus carries coconut shell halves for later use as shelter — a rare documented case of tool use in an invertebrate.

Cognitive implications

Tool manufacture and use are associated with causal reasoning, planning and social learning, and are used as comparative benchmarks in the study of the evolution of human technology.
