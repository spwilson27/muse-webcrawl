Public diplomacy

Public diplomacy refers to the various government-sponsored efforts aimed at communicating with and influencing foreign publics, in contrast to traditional diplomacy, which deals with relations between governments. It encompasses cultural exchanges, international broadcasting, educational programs, and strategic communication intended to build understanding and favorable views of a country, supporting its foreign policy goals through soft power.

Methods

Nicholas Cull's taxonomy divides public diplomacy into listening, advocacy, cultural diplomacy, exchange diplomacy, and international broadcasting. Well-known instruments include the Fulbright Program of academic exchanges, broadcasters such as Voice of America and the BBC World Service, cultural institutes like the Goethe-Institut, the British Council, and Confucius Institutes, and high-profile events such as World Expos and Olympic Games.

History

Although the practice is old, the term "public diplomacy" was coined in 1965 by Edmund Gullion at the Fletcher School. During the Cold War, the United States Information Agency (1953–1999) coordinated American efforts, from libraries and exhibitions to jazz tours. After the September 11 attacks, public diplomacy toward the Middle East received renewed attention and debate over its effectiveness.

Digital diplomacy

Social media has transformed the field ("digital diplomacy" or "Twiplomacy"), allowing governments and embassies to address foreign publics directly, while also creating challenges from disinformation and state-sponsored influence operations, sometimes described as "sharp power."
