Apex predator
DefinitionA predator at the top of a food chain, without natural predators of its own
ExamplesLion, tiger, grey wolf, orca, great white shark, saltwater crocodile
Related conceptsTrophic level, keystone species, trophic cascade

An apex predator, also known as a top predator or superpredator, is a predator at the top of a food chain, without natural predators of its own. Apex predators are usually defined in terms of trophic dynamics, occupying the highest trophic levels in their ecosystems. The concept is commonly applied in wildlife management, conservation and ecology. Apex predators have a crucial role in maintaining the health of ecosystems, regulating prey populations and influencing the behaviour and evolution of other species.

Examples

Well-known apex predators include large terrestrial carnivores such as the lion, tiger, leopard and grey wolf; marine predators such as the orca (killer whale), great white shark and sperm whale; raptors such as the harpy eagle and polar owl; and reptiles such as the saltwater crocodile and Komodo dragon. Some species are apex predators only within particular regions or life stages, and many were formerly considered apex until targeted by human hunting.

Ecological role

By limiting the density and altering the behaviour of herbivores and mid-level predators (a phenomenon called mesopredator release when it fails), apex predators exert top-down control over food webs. Their removal can trigger trophic cascades — for example, the reintroduction of wolves to Yellowstone National Park in 1995 is widely cited as influencing elk behaviour, vegetation recovery along streams, and consequently the populations of beavers and other species, though the scale of these effects remains debated.

Evolutionary history

Fossil apex predators include the megalodon shark, theropod dinosaurs such as Tyrannosaurus rex, and, in the early Cambrian, Anomalocaris, often cited as one of the earliest apex predators. Throughout Earth's history the apex position has been occupied successively by arthropods, fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals.

Relationship with humans

Humans are sometimes described as apex predators — or "superpredators" — because of their global impact on other species at all trophic levels, although human diets vary widely and many human populations consume mostly plant material. Humans also persecute natural apex predators, which has caused dramatic declines in many large carnivore populations; conservation efforts increasingly emphasise restoring apex predators for their ecosystem functions.
