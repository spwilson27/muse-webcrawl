Ownership

Ownership is the state or fact of legal possession and control of property, which may be any asset, tangible or intangible. Ownership can involve multiple rights, collectively referred to as title, which may be separated and held by different parties.

The concept of ownership has existed for thousands of years and is a foundation of property law in virtually every society. An owner holds rights — typically summarized as the rights to use, enjoy, exclude others from, transfer, and (in Roman-law terminology) even destroy the thing owned (ius utendi, fruendi, abutendi) — against the world, subject to legal limits such as taxation, zoning, and eminent domain.

Types of ownership

Ownership may be private (held by individuals or firms), public or state ownership, common/collective (held by a community, as in cooperative or communal property), or mixed. Joint forms include co-ownership, joint tenancy and tenancy in common for real property, and fractional or timeshare arrangements. Intellectual property law extends ownership-like rights to intangible creations such as patents, copyrights, and trademarks, though these are time-limited and governed by distinct doctrines.

Acquisition and transfer

Ownership can be acquired by first possession of an unowned thing, by creation, by purchase or other voluntary transfer (sale, gift, inheritance), by accession or adverse possession, or by operation of law. Registration systems — land registries, title deeds, share registers — record and publicize ownership of major assets in many jurisdictions.

Theories

Philosophers and jurists have debated the justification of ownership from Locke's labour theory of property, through Hegel's personality theory, to utilitarian and Marxist critiques of private property. In modern legal analysis ownership is often described not as a single right but as a "bundle of rights" (Hohfeld; Honoré's incidents of ownership).
