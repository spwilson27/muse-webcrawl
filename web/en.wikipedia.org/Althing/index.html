Althing

The Althing (Icelandic: Alþingi, "general assembly") is the national parliament of Iceland and the oldest surviving parliament in the world. Founded in 930 AD at Þingvellir ("assembly plains"), some 45 km east of modern Reykjavík, it met annually for nearly nine centuries before being suspended in 1800, and was re-established in 1844–45 as an advisory assembly, becoming a legislative body again in 1874. Today it is a unicameral legislature of 63 members (Alþingismenn) elected for four-year terms by proportional representation.

Medieval commonwealth

During the Icelandic Commonwealth (930–1262), the Althing was both a legislature and a supreme court. Chieftains (goðar) attended with their followers; the lawspeaker (lögsögumaður) recited the laws aloud at the Law Rock (Lögberg) over a three-year cycle, and the Law Council (Lögrétta) amended them. Quarter courts and, from about 1005, a Fifth Court heard cases. Iceland's conversion to Christianity was decided at the Althing in the year 1000 by the lawspeaker Thorgeir Thorkelsson. With no executive authority, judgments depended on private enforcement — the social world depicted in the Icelandic sagas. The Commonwealth ended when Iceland submitted to the Norwegian crown in 1262–64.

Later history

Under Norwegian and then Danish rule the Althing declined into a court, meeting at Þingvellir until 1798 and being abolished in 1800. A royal decree of 1843 revived it as a consultative assembly, which first met in Reykjavík in 1845. The constitution of 1874 granted it legislative power jointly with the crown; home rule followed in 1904, sovereignty in personal union with Denmark in 1918, and full independence as a republic in 1944 — proclaimed, fittingly, at Þingvellir. The Althing was bicameral from 1874/1934 until 1991, when it became unicameral.

Modern parliament

Members are elected from six multi-member constituencies by a form of proportional representation with national levelling seats. Iceland's parliament is notable for high female representation (at times near half of seats) and for electing, in Vigdís Finnbogadóttir's era and after, governments formed by coalition. The 2008 financial collapse and the "pots and pans revolution" protests outside the Althing building reshaped Icelandic politics and prompted an (ultimately unsuccessful) crowdsourced constitutional reform process.
