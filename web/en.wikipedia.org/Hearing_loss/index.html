Hearing loss is a partial or total inability to hear sound in one or both ears. It may be temporary or permanent, may affect people of any age, and ranges in severity from mild difficulty understanding speech to profound deafness. The World Health Organization estimates that over 1.5 billion people worldwide live with some degree of hearing loss, of whom several hundred million have disabling loss.

Types

Conductive hearing loss results from problems in the outer or middle ear that prevent sound from reaching the inner ear — for example earwax blockage, fluid from middle-ear infection, perforated eardrum, or otosclerosis (fixation of the stapes bone). Sensorineural hearing loss results from damage to the cochlea's hair cells or the auditory nerve, most commonly from ageing (presbycusis) and noise exposure. A mixed loss combines both mechanisms.

Causes

Causes include genetic factors (responsible for a large share of congenital deafness), chronic ear infections, diseases such as meningitis, measles and mumps, ototoxic medications (some antibiotics and chemotherapy agents), head injury, and prolonged exposure to loud noise at work or through personal audio devices. In newborns, causes include congenital infections and complications of prematurity.

Diagnosis and treatment

Hearing is assessed with pure-tone audiometry, which produces an audiogram charting thresholds across frequencies. Many conductive losses are medically or surgically treatable. Sensorineural loss is usually managed with hearing aids, which amplify sound; for severe-to-profound loss, cochlear implants can bypass damaged hair cells and stimulate the auditory nerve directly. Newborn hearing screening enables early intervention that greatly improves language outcomes for deaf children.

Prevention and impact

A substantial share of hearing loss is preventable through immunisation, treatment of ear infections, hearing protection, safe listening practices and avoiding ototoxic drugs where possible. Untreated hearing loss is associated with social isolation, depression, falls, and an increased risk of cognitive decline and dementia, and it imposes large educational and economic costs, especially in low-income countries where access to hearing care is limited.
