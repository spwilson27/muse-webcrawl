The Battle of Adrianople (9 August 378 AD) was fought between an Eastern Roman army led by Emperor Valens and Gothic rebels (Thervingi and Greuthungi, along with some Alans) commanded by Fritigern. It ended in an overwhelming Gothic victory and the death of Valens on the field.

Background

In 376, fleeing the Huns, large numbers of Goths were admitted into the Empire as refugees but were mistreated and exploited by Roman officials, provoking revolt. After two years of war in the Balkans — including the inconclusive Battle of the Willows (377) — Valens marched from Constantinople to deal with the Goths himself, declining to wait for reinforcements from his nephew Gratian, the Western emperor.

Battle

Valens's scouts underestimated Gothic numbers (the Greuthungi cavalry was away foraging). After a forced march in the summer heat, Roman troops engaged Fritigern's wagon laager while negotiations still seemed possible; grass fires set by the Goths added to the Romans' thirst and confusion. The returning Gothic cavalry struck the Roman flank, the Roman cavalry fled, and the infantry was encircled and slaughtered. Roman losses are estimated at 10,000–20,000 men — perhaps two-thirds of the army — including Valens and many senior officers. The contemporary historian Ammianus Marcellinus compared the disaster to Cannae.

Consequences

Adrianople shattered the myth of Roman invincibility and is traditionally seen as a milestone in the decline of the Western Roman Empire and the rise of heavy cavalry. Valens's successor, Theodosius I, eventually settled the Goths as foederati inside the Empire under their own leaders (382) — an arrangement with far-reaching consequences, culminating a generation later in the Gothic sack of Rome (410).
