Andalusia

Andalusia (Spanish: Andalucía)
Country: Spain
Capital: Seville
Area: 87,268 km²
Population: about 8.5 million
Provinces: 8

Andalusia is the southernmost autonomous community of peninsular Spain, comprising the provinces of Almería, Cádiz, Córdoba, Granada, Huelva, Jaén, Málaga, and Seville. It is the most populous and the second-largest autonomous community in the country, bordered by Portugal and the Atlantic to the west, the Mediterranean to the south and east, and the regions of Extremadura, Castile–La Mancha, and Murcia to the north.

History

The region was home to the Tartessian culture and Phoenician and Roman settlement—as the province of Baetica it supplied Rome with olive oil, wine, and emperors such as Trajan and Hadrian. From 711 it formed the heartland of Muslim al-Andalus, whose capital Córdoba became one of Europe's largest cities and whose monuments, above all the Great Mosque of Córdoba and Granada's Alhambra, endure today. The Christian conquest was completed in 1492 with the fall of the Nasrid kingdom of Granada; Seville then grew rich on trade with the Americas.

Geography and climate

The Guadalquivir river plain separates the Sierra Morena to the north from the Baetic ranges to the south, which include the Sierra Nevada and mainland Spain's highest peak, Mulhacén (3,479 m). Andalusia has a Mediterranean climate with hot, dry summers—among the hottest in Europe—and mild, wetter winters.

Culture

Andalusia is the homeland of flamenco, inscribed on UNESCO's heritage list, and of the April Fair of Seville, Holy Week processions, and bullfighting traditions. Its cuisine features olive oil, gazpacho, jamón, and the fortified wines of Jerez (sherry). Tourism along the Costa del Sol and to the historic cities is a mainstay of the economy, alongside agriculture and aerospace industry.
