Mooring

A mooring is any permanent structure to which a vessel is secured, or the act of securing it. A boat is said to be moored when it is fastened to a fixed object such as a bollard, pier, wharf or mooring buoy, or held by a permanent anchor system on the seabed. Moorings range from a single block or mushroom anchor connected by chain or rope to a buoy, to multi-leg systems holding oceanographic instruments, fish farms, and floating production platforms in deep water.

Types

  • Swing (single-point) mooring — one anchor and buoy; the vessel swings around it with wind and tide.
  • Fore-and-aft mooring — anchors or buoys at bow and stern, restricting swing, common in crowded harbours and trot moorings.
  • Mediterranean mooring — stern-to against a quay with an anchor or buoy holding the bow.
  • Permanent anchor systems — screw (helix) anchors, mushroom anchors, deadweight blocks and driven piles.

Oceanographic moorings

Scientific moorings carry instruments such as current meters, CTD sensors and acoustic releases along a line held taut by subsurface floats or anchored in depths of several thousand metres. Fishing or snagging of moorings, chafe, and biofouling are major practical concerns.
