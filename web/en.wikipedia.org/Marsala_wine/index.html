Marsala wine

Marsala is a fortified wine, dry or sweet, produced in the region surrounding the Italian city of Marsala in Sicily. Marsala first received Denominazione di Origine Controllata (DOC) status in 1969. The wine is made primarily from white Sicilian grape varieties — Grillo, Inzolia, Catarratto and Damaschino — while red (Rubino) versions use grapes such as Perricone and Nero d'Avola.

History

Marsala is most similar in style to Madeira and shares with sherry and port the technique of fortification. Credit for popularising it is generally given to the English merchant John Woodhouse, who in 1773 shipped local wine fortified with grape spirit so it would survive the sea voyage; it proved popular in England and for the British fleet. Other merchant houses — Ingham, Whitaker and Florio — developed the trade in the 19th century.

Production and styles

Traditional Marsala is blended using a perpetuum system akin to the solera method. The wine is classified by colour — Oro (gold), Ambra (amber, coloured with cooked must) and Rubino (ruby) — by sweetness (secco, semisecco, dolce) and by age: Fine (about one year), Superiore (two years), Superiore Riserva (four), Vergine (five) and Vergine Stravecchio (ten or more). Vergine styles are unsweetened and not coloured with mosto cotto.

Use

Dry Marsala is served as an aperitif and sweet Marsala as a dessert wine; it is also a classic cooking wine, essential to dishes such as chicken marsala and zabaglione.
