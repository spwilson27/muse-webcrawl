Section 7 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Section 7 cases are ultimately decided by the Supreme Court of Canada.

Section 7 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms protects the rights to life, liberty, and security of the person. It provides:

"Everyone has the right to life, liberty and security of the person and the right not to be deprived thereof except in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice."

Section 7 is among the most litigated provisions of the Charter. Unlike the American concept of substantive due process, Canadian courts have structured its analysis in two steps: whether a state action deprives a person of life, liberty, or security of the person, and whether that deprivation accords with the principles of fundamental justice, such as the rules against arbitrariness, overbreadth, and gross disproportionality.

Key cases

  • Re B.C. Motor Vehicle Act (1985) — established that the principles of fundamental justice are substantive, not merely procedural, and that absolute liability offences threatening imprisonment can violate section 7.
  • R v. Morgentaler (1988) — struck down Canada's abortion law, with several justices grounding the decision in security of the person.
  • Chaoulli v. Quebec (2005) — held that Quebec's ban on private health insurance, in the face of long public waiting lists, violated the Quebec Charter and, in a divided ruling, section 7.
  • Canada (AG) v. Bedford (2013) — struck down prostitution provisions that endangered sex workers' security of the person.
  • Carter v. Canada (2015) — held that the prohibition on physician-assisted dying violated section 7, leading to Canada's medical assistance in dying legislation.

Scope

The Supreme Court has held that section 7 generally protects individuals rather than corporations, does not guarantee economic rights such as property or welfare in themselves, and is engaged by state action causing serious physical or psychological harm, as in the Insite supervised-injection case (2011). It remains central to Canadian debates over healthcare, criminal law, and end-of-life rights.
