Laminar flow

Laminar flow is the smooth, orderly motion of a fluid in parallel layers, or laminae, with little or no mixing between them. It contrasts with turbulent flow, in which the fluid undergoes irregular fluctuations and vigorous mixing. In laminar flow, fluid particles follow smooth paths in layers that slide over one another, and the motion is dominated by viscous forces rather than inertial forces.

Reynolds number

Which regime occurs is predicted principally by the Reynolds number, Re = ρvL/μ, the ratio of inertial to viscous forces. For flow in a pipe, laminar flow generally occurs below Re ≈ 2,300, with a transitional zone up to about Re ≈ 4,000, beyond which the flow becomes turbulent. Osborne Reynolds demonstrated the transition in his classic 1883 dye-injection experiments: at low speeds a filament of dye remained a straight, intact thread, while at higher speeds it broke up and diffused through the water.

Characteristics

In fully developed laminar flow in a circular pipe, the velocity profile is parabolic — fastest at the centre and zero at the wall (the no-slip condition) — and the pressure drop follows the Hagen–Poiseuille relation, being proportional to the flow rate and the viscosity. Because the velocity field varies smoothly in space and time, laminar flows can often be solved exactly or modelled accurately with the Navier–Stokes equations.

Applications

Laminar behaviour governs the flow of blood in small vessels, groundwater movement, and microfluidic devices, whose tiny channels guarantee very low Reynolds numbers. Aircraft designers seek to preserve laminar flow over wings (laminar-flow airfoils) because skin-friction drag is far lower than in turbulent boundary layers. Laminar flow hoods, which produce a smooth stream of filtered air, are used in laboratories and semiconductor clean rooms to protect sterile or contaminant-sensitive work.
