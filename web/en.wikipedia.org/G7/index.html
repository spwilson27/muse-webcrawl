The Group of Seven (G7) is an intergovernmental political and economic forum consisting of Canada, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the United Kingdom and the United States; additionally, the European Union (EU) is a "non-enumerated member". It is organised around shared values of pluralism and representative government. The members are major IMF advanced economies, and together represent a large share of global wealth, though a declining share of world population (about 10 percent).

History

The forum originated with the 1973 "Library Group" of finance ministers and the first heads-of-government summit at Rambouillet in 1975 (the G6, without Canada), convened in response to the collapse of the Bretton Woods system and the oil crisis. Canada joined in 1976. Russia was admitted in 1997, making the G8, but was suspended in 2014 after the annexation of Crimea, and the format reverted to the G7.

Summits and work

Leaders meet annually at a summit hosted in rotation by member states, supported by ministerial tracks for finance, foreign affairs, health, climate and other portfolios. Communiqués are politically rather than legally binding. Recent summits have addressed the COVID-19 pandemic, support for Ukraine following Russia's 2022 invasion, climate finance, a global minimum corporate tax (agreed in 2021 at 15 percent), and coordination on China.

Criticism

Critics argue the G7 is unrepresentative of the modern world economy — excluding China, India and the wider Global South — and is overshadowed in economic governance by the broader G20. Supporters counter that its smaller, like-minded membership enables candid consensus among democracies.
