Tartan

Tartan is a patterned cloth of criss-crossed horizontal and vertical bands in multiple colours, created by weaving coloured threads in a simple two-over, two-under twill. Where the same colour crosses itself the colour remains solid; where two different colours cross, they blend optically to a halved tone, generating a grid of new shades. Particular patterns, called setts, are strongly associated with Scotland, its clans, and the Scottish kilt.

Origins

Patterned checked cloth is ancient in northern Europe; the Falkirk tartan fragment, dated to about the 3rd century AD, is the oldest known Scottish example. Tartan became the characteristic dress of the Scottish Highlands, where the Gaelic term breacan referred to the speckled or checked cloth.

Suppression and revival

Following the Jacobite defeat at Culloden in 1746, the Dress Act banned Highland dress for most civilians until its repeal in 1782. In the 19th century a romantic revival — fuelled by Sir Walter Scott and the pageantry of George IV's 1822 Edinburgh visit — made tartan a symbol of Scotland as a whole, and the idea of fixed clan tartans took hold.

Registration

The Scottish Register of Tartans, established by an Act of the Scottish Parliament in 2008, is the official repository of tartan designs, recording clan, district, military, commemorative, and fashion setts.
