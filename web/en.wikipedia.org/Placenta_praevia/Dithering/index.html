Dithering

Dithering is an intentionally applied form of noise used to randomise quantisation error, preventing large-scale patterns such as colour banding in images. In digital imaging, dithering creates the illusion of colour depth in images with a limited colour palette by arranging pixels of available colours so that, viewed from a distance, intermediate shades appear. The technique is fundamental to pixel art and to displaying images on devices with restricted palettes.

Algorithms

The best-known method is Floyd–Steinberg dithering (1976), an error-diffusion algorithm that distributes each pixel's quantisation error to neighbouring unprocessed pixels. Ordered dithering instead compares each pixel against a fixed threshold matrix, such as a Bayer matrix, producing regular cross-hatch patterns that are fast and stable for animation.

Applications

Dithering is used when converting images to formats with limited colours, such as GIF; in printing, where it is related to halftoning; and in digital audio, where dither noise added before quantisation decorrelates the error from the signal, reducing audible distortion. Early computer games and demoscene graphics used dithering extensively to simulate extra colours on hardware such as the CGA and the original Game Boy.
