Gingham

Gingham is a medium-weight, balanced plain-woven fabric made from dyed cotton or cotton-blend yarns. It is distinguished by its checked pattern — typically white crossed by one colour in equal stripes along warp and weft — which produces squares of full colour, half-tone, and white. Unlike printed checks, gingham's pattern is woven in, so both faces of the cloth look the same.

History

The name derives from the Malay genggang, meaning striped or separated. Gingham was produced in Malaysia, Indonesia, and India, and imported into Europe in the 17th century; by the 18th century it was milled in Manchester, England, where the characteristic checked version developed. In the 19th and 20th centuries gingham became a staple for shirts, dresses, aprons, curtains, and picnic cloths, and it acquired strong associations with rural and domestic life — famously worn by Dorothy in the 1939 film The Wizard of Oz.

Characteristics

Gingham is a plain weave (one thread over, one under), lighter than the twill weave of tartan-style cloths and distinguished from them by its even, two-colour check. It is inexpensive, durable, easy to launder, and available in sizes of check from fine to bold.
