Placenta praevia

Placenta praevia (also spelled placenta previa) is a problem of pregnancy in which the placenta grows in the lowest part of the womb (uterus) and covers all or part of the opening to the cervix. Normally the placenta attaches to the upper part of the uterus, away from the cervix. Placenta praevia occurs in about 1 in 200 pregnancies and is a leading cause of vaginal bleeding in the second half of pregnancy.

Signs and classification

The classic presentation is painless, bright-red vaginal bleeding during the second or third trimester, sometimes triggered by intercourse or a pelvic examination. Traditionally the condition was classified into four grades, from a low-lying placenta whose edge does not reach the cervix, to complete praevia in which the placenta fully covers the internal os. Modern classification divides cases into placenta praevia (covering the os) and low-lying placenta (edge within about 2 cm of the os).

Risk factors and diagnosis

Risk factors include previous caesarean delivery, previous placenta praevia, multiple gestation, multiparity, increasing maternal age, smoking, and assisted reproduction. The condition is usually detected on routine mid-pregnancy ultrasound; many low-lying placentas seen early resolve as the uterus grows, an apparent movement sometimes called placental "migration". Diagnosis is confirmed by transvaginal ultrasound, which is safe and more accurate than transabdominal scanning.

Management

If significant bleeding does not occur and the pregnancy is preterm, management is expectant, with pelvic rest and monitoring. Delivery is by caesarean section when the placenta covers the cervix, typically planned at 36–37 weeks, or earlier if there is heavy bleeding. Placenta praevia is associated with placenta accreta spectrum disorders, especially in women with previous caesarean scars, which can cause life-threatening haemorrhage at delivery.
