Pixel

Etymology: "picture element"
Coined: 1965 (Frederic C. Billingsley)
Related units: Voxel, texel, megapixel

In digital imaging, a pixel (abbreviated px), also called a pel, is the smallest addressable element in a raster image or the smallest point in an all-points-addressable display device. Each pixel is a sample of an original image; more samples typically provide a more accurate representation. The intensity of each pixel is variable, and in colour systems each pixel usually consists of red, green, and blue subpixels.

History

The word is a portmanteau of "picture" and "element" and appeared in print in 1965 in publications by Frederic C. Billingsley of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory, describing elements of video images from space probes to the Moon and Mars. The underlying concept dates to early television scanning and to descriptions of "Bildpunkte" in German patents of the early 20th century.

Pixels and resolution

Image resolution is often expressed in total pixels (megapixels) or pixels per inch. On displays, physical pixel density determines the size of interface elements; modern high-density ("Retina"-class) screens pack several hundred pixels per inch so that individual pixels are indistinguishable at normal viewing distances.

Related concepts

A pixel is not necessarily a square, and its colour is commonly stored as three bytes (24-bit "true colour"). Related notions include the voxel (volume element) in 3D imaging, the texel (texture element), and the sprite, a movable graphic composed of pixels. In pixel art, artists deliberately place individual pixels as an aesthetic medium.
