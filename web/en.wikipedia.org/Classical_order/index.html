Classical order
OriginAncient Greece and Rome
Principal ordersDoric, Ionic, Corinthian, Tuscan, Composite

A classical order is one of the ancient styles of classical architecture, each distinguished by its proportions and characteristic ornament, most obviously in the form of the column and its capital. An order consists of the column (with base, shaft and capital) and the entablature it supports, proportioned according to a module derived from the column's diameter. The orders provided architects from antiquity through the Renaissance and beyond with a complete grammar of design.

The five orders

Renaissance theorists, following Vitruvius, fixed a canon of five orders. The Tuscan is the plainest, a simplified Doric. The Doric, the earliest Greek order, has a simple cushion capital and a frieze of triglyphs and metopes; its Roman form adds a base. The Ionic is slimmer and more elegant, identified by the scroll-like volutes of its capital. The Corinthian, the most ornate, carries a capital carved with acanthus leaves. The Composite, a Roman invention, combines Ionic volutes with Corinthian foliage.

Proportion and the module

Every part of an order is sized in modules, conventionally halves of the column's lower diameter. Greek Doric columns stand about six to seven diameters high, Ionic about nine, and Corinthian about ten, giving each order its characteristic slenderness. Superimposition — placing lighter orders above heavier ones on multi-storey buildings, as on the Colosseum — became a standard classical device.

History and revival

The orders originated in Greek temple architecture of the seventh and sixth centuries BC and were adopted, adapted and theorized by Rome. Vitruvius's treatise De architectura preserved their theory through the Middle Ages. Renaissance writers — Alberti, Serlio, Palladio and Vignola — re-codified the orders, and Vignola's Regola delli cinque ordini (1562) became the standard pattern book. The orders underpin Renaissance, Baroque, Palladian and Neoclassical architecture, and remain central to traditional design today.
