Bering Strait

The Bering Strait is the Pacific's northernmost sea passage, separating the Chukchi Peninsula of the Russian Far East from the Seward Peninsula of Alaska, United States. About 82 kilometres (51 mi) wide at its narrowest point, it connects the Chukchi Sea (part of the Arctic Ocean) to the north with the Bering Sea to the south. The strait and the sea to its south are named after Vitus Bering, a Danish explorer in Russian service who sailed through the strait in 1728.

Geography

The Diomede Islands — Big Diomede (Russian) and Little Diomede (American) — sit near the middle of the strait, about 3.8 kilometres apart; the International Date Line passes between them, so the two islands are separated by almost a full day in local time. The strait is shallow, with depths of only about 30–50 metres. During winter much of it is covered by sea ice.

History

During the most recent ice age, sea levels were low enough that the strait was replaced by the broad land bridge known as Beringia, across which humans and other animals migrated between Asia and North America. Semyon Dezhnyov sailed through the strait in 1648, decades before Bering, though his report went largely unnoticed. The United States purchased Alaska from Russia in 1867, and the maritime boundary settled in the strait remains internationally significant.

Crossing proposals

A fixed crossing — bridge, tunnel, or a combination — spanning the Bering Strait has been proposed repeatedly since the nineteenth century, most prominently as part of trans-global rail and road schemes. No such project has been built, owing to the strait's remoteness, extreme weather, and the enormous infrastructure that would be required on both shores.
