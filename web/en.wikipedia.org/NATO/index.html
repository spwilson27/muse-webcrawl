The North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) is an intergovernmental military alliance of European and North American countries established by the North Atlantic Treaty of 4 April 1949. Its foundational commitment is Article 5: an armed attack against one member is considered an attack against all.

History

NATO was formed at the outset of the Cold War, its twelve founding members uniting to deter Soviet expansion in Europe. The alliance built an integrated military command and stationed large forces in West Germany, facing the Warsaw Pact across the Iron Curtain. France withdrew from the integrated command in 1966 (rejoining in 2009) but remained a member. After the Cold War ended, NATO reinvented itself: it intervened in Bosnia (1995) and bombed Yugoslavia over Kosovo (1999), invoked Article 5 for the only time after the 11 September 2001 attacks, and led forces in Afghanistan for two decades. Successive enlargement rounds admitted former Warsaw Pact and Baltic states, to Russia's vocal objection. Following Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022, Finland (2023) and Sweden (2024) abandoned decades of neutrality to join, bringing membership to 32.

Structure and operations

Decisions are taken by consensus in the North Atlantic Council, chaired by the Secretary General; military planning runs through headquarters including SHAPE near Mons. Members commit to a defence-spending guideline of 2% of GDP (raised in emphasis after 2022). NATO maintains air-policing missions over the Baltic, standing naval forces, and battlegroups on its eastern flank.
