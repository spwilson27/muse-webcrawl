Jupiter (mythology)

Abode: Mount Olympus (by Greek analogy)
Symbols: Thunderbolt, eagle, oak
Consort: Juno
Greek equivalent: Zeus

Jupiter (Latin: Iūpiter or Jove), also known as Jove, is the god of the sky and thunder, and king of the gods in ancient Roman religion and mythology. Jupiter was the chief deity of Roman state religion throughout the Republican and Imperial eras, until Christianity became the dominant religion of the Empire.

Role and character

Jupiter originated as a sky god, associated with weather, lightning, and the heavens. His name derives from the Proto-Indo-European *Dyēu-pəter, "sky father," making him cognate with the Vedic Dyaus Pita and the Greek Zeus Pater. In the Roman pantheon he presided over laws and social order, and formed the Capitoline Triad together with his wife Juno and his daughter Minerva. The great Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus on the Capitoline Hill was the centre of Roman state religion, where triumphing generals laid their laurels.

Mythology

Jupiter was the son of Saturn and Ops, and brother of Neptune, Pluto, Juno, Ceres, and Vesta. After overthrowing Saturn, the three brothers divided the cosmos: Jupiter took the sky, Neptune the seas, and Pluto the underworld. Roman myth adapted many stories of the Greek Zeus, including his numerous liaisons and offspring — among them Mars, Vulcan, Diana, Apollo, Minerva, Mercury, Proserpina, and Hercules — often recounted in Ovid's Metamorphoses.

Worship

Jupiter's flamen, the Flamen Dialis, was among the highest-ranking priests of Rome, hedged by numerous taboos. Jupiter was worshipped under many epithets, including Optimus Maximus ("best and greatest"), Victor, and Tonans ("the Thunderer"). The Ides of each month were sacred to him.
