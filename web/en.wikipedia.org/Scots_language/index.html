Scots language

Scots (endonym: Scots; also called Braid Scots or Lallans in some contexts) is a West Germanic language spoken in the Lowlands of Scotland and in parts of Ulster in Ireland, where it is known as Ulster Scots. It developed from Old English as spoken in the Anglo-Saxon kingdom of Northumbria and is a close relative of English, though its status as a separate language versus a dialect of English has long been debated. It is recognised as a regional or minority language under the European Charter for Regional or Minority Languages.

History

From the fourteenth century, Older Scots became the language of the Scottish court, law and literature: poets known as the Makars, including William Dunbar, Robert Henryson and Gavin Douglas, produced a distinguished literature. The "Scottis" of this period was a fully developed literary standard, distinct in spelling, vocabulary and pronunciation from the English of London.

After the Union of the Crowns in 1603 and the Union of Parliaments in 1707, English progressively displaced Scots from formal, written and high-status use, a process hastened by the printing of the Bible in English. Scots survived as the everyday speech of Lowland Scotland and underwent a literary revival in the eighteenth century with Allan Ramsay, Robert Fergusson and above all Robert Burns, whose poems and songs remain central to Scottish culture. In the twentieth century the "Scottish Renaissance" writer Hugh MacDiarmid attempted to forge a synthetic literary Scots (Lallans).

Varieties and status

Dialects include Central Scots, Southern Scots, Northern Scots (including the Doric of the north-east) and Insular Scots in Orkney and Shetland, the latter strongly influenced by the extinct Norn language. Census figures in 2011 recorded about 1.5 million people in Scotland reporting some ability in Scots. There is no universally accepted standard written form, and spelling remains variable; characteristic Scots words include bairn (child), kirk (church), ken (know) and dreich (dreary). Scottish English, the standard English of Scotland, is itself heavily influenced by Scots vocabulary and pronunciation.
