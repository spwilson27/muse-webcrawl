Oil painting

Oil painting is the process of painting with pigments bound with a medium of drying oil, such as linseed oil. Since the 15th century it has been the most widely used technique for artistic painting on canvas and wood panel, prized for its rich colour, subtle blending, durability, and the long working time afforded by its slow drying.

History

The earliest known oil paintings are Buddhist murals in the Bamiyan caves of Afghanistan, dated to around the 7th century AD. In Europe, oil technique was documented by Theophilus Presbyter in the 12th century and was developed to a high level in Early Netherlandish painting in the 1400s by artists such as Jan van Eyck and Rogier van der Weyden. Antonello da Messina helped transmit the technique to Italy, where it was taken up by Venetian painters including Giovanni Bellini and Titian.

Development

Oil painting became central to Renaissance, Baroque, and later European art, enabling the chiaroscuro of Caravaggio, the glazing of Rembrandt, and the virtuoso brushwork of Frans Hals and Velázquez. In the 19th century, portable tube paints and canvas supports encouraged plein air work and Impressionism; in the 20th century oil remained a leading medium even as acrylic paint emerged as an alternative.

Technique and supports

Traditional supports are wood panel and stretched canvas (linen or cotton) prepared with a ground such as gesso. Methods include underdrawing, monochrome underpainting (grisaille), layered glazing, direct alla prima painting, and impasto. Works are conventionally protected with varnish after the paint film has cured.

Conservation

Oil paintings can suffer from cracking, yellowing of oil and varnish, canvas degradation, and pigment changes (for example the fading of fugitive lakes or darkening of lead-based pigments). Conservation involves cleaning, relining, retouching, and controlled display conditions.
