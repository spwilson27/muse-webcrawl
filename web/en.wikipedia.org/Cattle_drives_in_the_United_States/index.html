Cattle drives in the United States

Cattle drives were a major economic activity in the 19th and early 20th centuries in the American West, particularly between 1850s Texas and the railheads in Kansas and other states. Longhorn cattle, which had multiplied in Texas during the Civil War, could be worth only a few dollars locally but many times more at northern and eastern markets, so herds of hundreds or thousands were driven north by cowboys on trails lasting months.

Trails

Famous routes included the Chisholm Trail (to Abilene and other Kansas rail towns), the Great Western Cattle Trail (to Dodge City and Ogallala), the Goodnight–Loving Trail (to New Mexico and Colorado) and the Shawnee Trail. A typical drive covered 15–25 km a day with a crew of about a dozen cowboys, a trail boss, a cook and a chuck wagon, and a remuda of spare horses.

Decline

The era faded in the 1880s–1890s: railroads reached Texas itself, barbed wire fenced the open range, quarantine laws against Texas fever restricted passage, and the brutal winters of 1886–87 devastated herds. Cattle driving nonetheless left an enduring mark on American culture through Western fiction, film and song.
