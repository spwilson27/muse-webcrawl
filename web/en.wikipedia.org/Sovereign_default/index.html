Sovereign default

A sovereign default is the failure or refusal of the government of a sovereign state to pay back its debt in full when due. Unlike companies or individuals, sovereign states cannot be forced into bankruptcy proceedings by creditors, which makes sovereign defaults a distinctive and politically charged feature of international finance.

Causes and consequences

Defaults may result from excessive borrowing, collapsing revenues, currency crises, war, or a government's unwillingness — rather than inability — to pay. Consequences can include exclusion from capital markets, sharp currency depreciation, banking crises (because domestic banks hold government debt), falling output, and litigation by holdout creditors. Because governments can tax and, for domestic-currency debt, inflate, defaults on foreign-currency obligations are the more common pattern.

Restructuring

Sovereign debt is usually resolved through restructuring — negotiated exchanges that extend maturities or reduce principal or interest, often coordinated under the shadow of the International Monetary Fund. Modern bond contracts commonly include collective action clauses that bind dissenting minorities once a supermajority of creditors agrees, a reform driven by Argentina's litigated 2001 default.

Notable examples

  • Spain's repeated defaults in the 16th–17th centuries under the Habsburgs.
  • Russia's 1998 default and ruble crisis.
  • Argentina's 2001 default, then the largest in history (about $82 billion).
  • Greece's 2012 restructuring, the largest in developed-world history.
  • Lebanon (2020), Zambia (2020), Sri Lanka (2022) and Ukraine's restructurings.
