Japanese asset price bubble

The Japanese asset price bubble (バブル景気, baburu keiki, "bubble economy") was an economic bubble in Japan from 1986 to 1991 in which real estate and stock market prices were greatly inflated. The Nikkei 225 stock index peaked at 38,915 on 29 December 1989. The bubble's collapse ushered in a long period of stagnation known as the Lost Decades.

Period: 1986–1991

Peak: Nikkei 225 at 38,915 (29 December 1989)

Aftermath: The Lost Decades of economic stagnation

Causes

After the 1985 Plaza Accord, the yen appreciated sharply, and the Bank of Japan cut interest rates to stimulate domestic demand. Financial deregulation let corporations raise funds on capital markets, so banks lent aggressively against real estate collateral. Land prices in Tokyo soared — at the peak it was famously said that the grounds of the Imperial Palace were worth more than all the land in California.

Collapse and aftermath

The Bank of Japan raised rates from 1989, the Ministry of Finance restricted real-estate lending, and equity prices fell by more than half within two years; land prices followed over the next decade. Banks were left with mountains of bad loans, and deflation, weak growth and corporate deleveraging persisted through the 1990s and 2000s. The episode became a central case study in monetary policy, financial supervision and the dangers of asset bubbles.
