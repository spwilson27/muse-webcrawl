Pseudepigrapha

Pseudepigrapha (also anglicized as pseudepigraph or plural pseudepigraphs) are works whose authorship is falsely attributed to a person of the past. The term comes from the Greek pseudḗs ("false") and epigraphḗ ("inscription" or "ascription"), and it is most commonly used in biblical studies for a body of writings produced roughly between 200 BCE and 200 CE that claim authorship by figures from the Hebrew Bible such as Enoch, Abraham, Moses, or Ezra.

Although pseudepigraphy was a widespread literary convention in the ancient world, the works collected under the heading "Old Testament Pseudepigrapha" were largely excluded from both the Jewish and Christian biblical canons, even though many of them circulated widely and influenced later theology, angelology, demonology, and apocalyptic expectation.

Terminology and scope

Scholars distinguish the Pseudepigrapha from the Apocrypha (or deuterocanonical books), which are included in some Christian canons, and from the New Testament apocrypha. The standard modern collection, edited by James H. Charlesworth and published in two volumes in 1983 and 1985, gathers 65 documents, including apocalypses, testaments, psalms, prayers, and expansions of biblical narratives.

Not all pseudepigrapha are religious. Classical antiquity produced works attributed to Pythagoras, Hippocrates, and other famous names, and the practice continued through the Middle Ages in texts such as the Corpus Areopagiticum, attributed to Dionysius the Areopagite.

Major works

Among the best-known Old Testament pseudepigrapha are the Book of Enoch (1 Enoch), which survives complete only in Ge'ez and is canonical in the Ethiopian Orthodox Church; the Book of Jubilees, a retelling of Genesis attributed to Moses; the Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs; the Ascension of Isaiah; 2 Baruch; 4 Ezra; the Psalms of Solomon; and the Sibylline Oracles. Several of these works are also represented among the Dead Sea Scrolls, showing that they were read and copied in Second Temple Judaism.

Authorship and intent

Modern scholars debate whether ancient readers regarded pseudepigraphal attribution as deception. Some argue that attribution to an ancient sage was an accepted way of placing a work within a revered tradition, much as legal or philosophical schools wrote in the name of their founders. Others note that ancient writers such as Tertullian criticized the practice, and that a presbyter of Asia Minor was reportedly removed from office for composing the Acts of Paul out of "love for Paul."
