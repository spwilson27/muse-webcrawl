The vertebral column, also known as the spine or backbone, is the central supporting structure of the body in vertebrate animals. It is a flexible column of small bones called vertebrae, separated by intervertebral discs, which encloses and protects the spinal cord and supports the head and trunk.

Structure

In adult humans the vertebral column is typically about 71 cm long in men and 61 cm in women. It consists of 33 vertebrae: 7 cervical, 12 thoracic, 5 lumbar, 5 sacral (fused into the sacrum), and 4 coccygeal (fused into the coccyx). The vertebrae are named by region and numbered from top to bottom, e.g. C1–C7, T1–T12, L1–L5. The first two cervical vertebrae, the atlas and axis, are specialized to support the skull and permit head rotation.

Curvatures

Viewed from the side, the human spine has four curves: the cervical and lumbar curves are convex forward (lordotic), while the thoracic and sacral curves are convex backward (kyphotic). These curves increase the strength and flexibility of the spine and help maintain balance in the upright posture. Abnormal curvatures include scoliosis (lateral curvature), hyperkyphosis, and hyperlordosis.

Function

The vertebral column protects the spinal cord within the vertebral canal, bears the weight of the body above the pelvis, provides attachments for the ribs and the muscles of the back and neck, and, through the cushioning intervertebral discs, absorbs shock during movement.

Clinical significance

Common disorders of the vertebral column include herniated ("slipped") discs, spinal stenosis, osteoporotic compression fractures, spondylolisthesis, and vertebral infections or tumours. Back pain arising from the spine and its associated structures is one of the most common reasons for medical consultation worldwide.
