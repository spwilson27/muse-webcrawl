Chess engine

A chess engine is a computer program that analyses chess positions and generates moves and evaluations. Modern engines play far beyond the level of the strongest human players and are an indispensable tool in professional preparation and commentary.

How engines work

Engines explore the game tree using alpha–beta pruning with many enhancements, ordering candidate moves and evaluating leaf positions with a scoring function that weighs material, piece activity, king safety and pawn structure. Traditional engines use hand-crafted evaluation; since 2020 the leading programs use efficiently updatable neural networks (NNUE). Engines communicate with graphical interfaces through protocols such as UCI.

History

Early programs of the 1960s and 1970s played weakly, but steady improvements in search and evaluation brought rapid progress. In 1997 IBM's Deep Blue, specialised hardware rather than a general engine, defeated world champion Garry Kasparov in a match. Open-source Stockfish and the neural-network projects Leela Chess Zero (inspired by DeepMind's AlphaZero, which taught itself in 2017) dominate engine competition today, with estimated ratings well above 3500 Elo.

Uses

Engines are used for game analysis, opening preparation, solving studies, engine-versus-engine tournaments, and anti-cheating detection in online chess.
