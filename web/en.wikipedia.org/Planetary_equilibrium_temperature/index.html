Planetary equilibrium temperature

The planetary equilibrium temperature is a theoretical temperature that a planet would be if it were a black body being heated only by its parent star. In this model, the planet is treated as a fast-rotating sphere that absorbs incoming stellar radiation over its sunlit cross-section and re-radiates the energy evenly over its entire surface, so that absorbed and emitted power balance.

Calculation

The equilibrium temperature is given by Teq = Tstar · sqrt(Rstar / 2a) · (1 − A)1/4, where Tstar is the star's effective temperature, Rstar its radius, a the orbital distance, and A the planet's Bond albedo. For Earth, the calculation gives about 255 K (−18 °C), well below the actual mean surface temperature of about 288 K (15 °C); the difference is caused by the greenhouse effect of Earth's atmosphere.

Applications

Equilibrium temperature is a standard first estimate of the climate of an exoplanet and is used to define the habitable zone around a star — the range of distances at which a planet with a suitable atmosphere could maintain liquid water on its surface. Because it ignores atmospheric effects, internal heat, and uneven heat distribution, it is only a rough guide to actual surface conditions.
