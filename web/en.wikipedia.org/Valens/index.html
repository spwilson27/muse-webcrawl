Valens ruled the Eastern Roman Empire for fourteen years until his death at the Battle of Adrianople.

Flavius Julius Valens Augustus (c. 328 – 9 August 378) was Roman emperor in the East from 364 to 378. He was appointed co-emperor by his elder brother Valentinian I, who retained the West. Valens's reign culminated in the Gothic War and his death at the Battle of Adrianople, one of the most consequential defeats in Roman history, often taken to mark the beginning of the fall of the Western Roman Empire.

Rise

Valens and Valentinian were sons of Gratianus, a successful general from Cibalae in Pannonia. Valens served as a protector domesticus and campaigned under Julian and Jovian. After Jovian's sudden death in February 364, the army elected Valentinian emperor at Nicaea; a month later, at Constantinople, Valentinian raised Valens to co-Augustus, dividing the empire's administration — and its armies — between them.

Reign

Revolt of Procopius

In 365 Procopius, a relative of Julian, seized Constantinople and much of Asia Minor while Valens was in Syria preparing for war with Persia. Valens nearly despaired, but his generals recovered the situation, and Procopius was defeated and executed in 366. Sassanid Persia remained a standing preoccupation: Valens campaigned on the Armenian and Mesopotamian frontiers without decisive result.

Religious policy

An Arian Christian, Valens promoted Arian bishops and exiled Nicene opponents such as Athanasius of Alexandria (briefly) and Basil of Caesarea's allies, though his persecution was milder than later tradition claimed. His Arianism shaped hostile portrayals in the Nicene sources through which his reign is known.

Gothic War and death

In 376, pressed by the Huns, large numbers of Goths under Fritigern petitioned to cross the Danube into Roman territory. Valens admitted them, but corrupt Roman officials starved and abused the refugees, who revolted. Gothic forces ravaged Thrace, defeating Roman armies at Marcianople and, when Valens arrived with the eastern field army, he chose to attack without waiting for Gratian's western reinforcements. At Adrianople on 9 August 378 the Roman army was encircled and destroyed; perhaps two-thirds of it perished, and Valens was killed — ancient accounts say he was wounded and carried to a farmhouse which the Goths burned, or died on the field; his body was never found.

Legacy

Adrianople shattered the myth of Roman invincibility and left the Balkans exposed to Gothic settlement within the empire. Theodosius I, appointed to succeed Valens, eventually settled the Goths as foederati in 382. Valens also left physical marks on Constantinople: he completed the Aqueduct of Valens, still standing in Istanbul.
