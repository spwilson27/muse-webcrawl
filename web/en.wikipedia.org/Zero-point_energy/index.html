Zero-point energy


Quantum vacuum fluctuations

Zero-point energy (ZPE) is the lowest possible energy that a quantum mechanical system may have. Unlike in classical mechanics, quantum systems constantly fluctuate in their lowest energy state as described by the Heisenberg uncertainty principle. Even at absolute zero, a quantum harmonic oscillator retains a residual energy of one half of ħω per mode, where ħ is the reduced Planck constant and ω is the oscillator's angular frequency.

History

The concept grew out of Max Planck's work on black-body radiation and was developed by Planck, Albert Einstein and Otto Stern in the 1910s. In quantum field theory, every field mode contributes its own zero-point energy, so the vacuum of empty space is pictured as seething with virtual particles. Walter Nernst and others speculated about the physical reality of this vacuum energy long before quantum field theory formalized it.

Experimental evidence

Several phenomena are attributed to zero-point energy. The Casimir effect, predicted by Hendrik Casimir in 1948, is an attraction between neutral conducting plates caused by the restriction of vacuum modes between them, and it has been measured experimentally. The Lamb shift in hydrogen's spectrum and spontaneous emission of photons by excited atoms are also commonly explained in terms of vacuum fluctuations. Liquid helium's refusal to freeze under its own vapour pressure at low temperature is a macroscopic consequence: its atoms retain enough zero-point motion to prevent solidification.

Cosmology

Summing the zero-point energies of all field modes up to the Planck scale gives a vacuum energy density enormously larger — by as much as 120 orders of magnitude — than the value inferred from the observed cosmological constant. This mismatch, sometimes called the worst theoretical prediction in physics, remains an unsolved problem at the boundary of quantum mechanics and general relativity.
