Deep ecology

Deep ecology is an environmental philosophy that promotes the inherent worth of all living beings regardless of their instrumental utility to human needs, plus the restructuring of modern human societies in accordance with such ideas. It argues that the natural world is a complex web of interrelations in which the existence of organisms depends on the existence of others within ecosystems.

Origins

The term was coined by Norwegian philosopher Arne Næss in a 1973 article, where he contrasted "deep" ecology with "shallow" environmentalism concerned mainly with pollution and resource depletion in the developed world. Næss drew on the thought of Spinoza, Gandhi and Rachel Carson, and his ideas were developed with George Sessions into an eight-point platform in 1984.

Principles

The platform holds that the flourishing of human and nonhuman life has intrinsic value, that the diversity of life forms contributes to that flourishing, and that humans have no right to reduce this diversity except to satisfy vital needs. It calls for substantial changes in economic, technological and ideological structures, and an appreciable decrease of human population pressure on the biosphere.

Criticism

Critics, including social ecologist Murray Bookchin, have charged deep ecology with vagueness, misanthropy and a failure to address social causes of ecological destruction; ecofeminist and environmental-justice writers have raised related objections. Supporters reply that the philosophy is a personal and political orientation rather than a complete social theory.
