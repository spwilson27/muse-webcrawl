Hornbostel–Sachs is the most widely used system for classifying musical instruments in ethnomusicology and organology. It was devised by Erich Moritz von Hornbostel and Curt Sachs and first published in 1914 in the Zeitschrift für Ethnologie, modelled in part on the Dewey Decimal system and on Victor-Charles Mahillon's museum catalogue scheme.

The classes

Instruments are classified by how their sound is produced: idiophones (the body of the instrument itself vibrates, e.g. bells, xylophones), membranophones (a stretched membrane vibrates, e.g. drums), chordophones (stretched strings vibrate, e.g. violins, pianos), aerophones (air itself vibrates, e.g. flutes, trumpets), and electrophones (added later for electrically produced or amplified sound).

Decimal notation

Each class subdivides by decimal suffixes describing how the vibration is excited and the instrument's form; a piano, for example, is 314.122-4-8, a struck box zither with keyboard. Suffixes indicate the means of excitation, such as hammers, bows or plectra.

Revisions

The system has been revised and expanded by scholars and museum consortia such as MIMO (Musical Instrument Museums Online), notably to handle electronic instruments and instruments that span categories.
