Cruciform

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cruciform is a term for physical manifestations resembling a common cross or Christian cross. The label can apply to architectural shapes, biology, heraldry, and man-made shapes, where the arrangement of parts forms a cross-like plan or pattern.

Architecture

Many Christian churches are built in a cruciform plan, with the nave, transepts and chancel arranged so that the ground plan forms a cross. In the Latin cross plan, the nave is longer than the transepts; in the Greek cross plan, all four arms are of roughly equal length. Cruciform churches became the standard form for cathedrals and parish churches throughout medieval Europe and remain a recognisable symbol of Christian sacred architecture.

Cruciform piers and pilasters — supports with a cross-shaped section — are also found in Romanesque and Gothic construction, giving greater bearing surface for arches and vaults.

Biology

In biology, cruciform structures occur in several contexts. Cruciferous plants (family Brassicaceae, formerly Cruciferae) bear four-petalled flowers arranged in a cross. DNA can adopt cruciform structures when inverted repeat sequences extrude into cross-shaped configurations on both strands, a conformation implicated in replication, recombination and the recognition of DNA by binding proteins.

Heraldry and design

In heraldry, a wide range of crosses — the cross crosslet, cross pattée, cross potent and others — are described with cruciform terminology. Cruciform motifs also appear in textiles, jewellery, tools (such as cruciform screw heads) and in the plan form of some aircraft and buildings outside religious contexts.
