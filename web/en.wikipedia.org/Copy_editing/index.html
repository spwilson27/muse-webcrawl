Copy editing

Copy editing (also known as copy-editing or manuscript editing) is the process of revising written material ("copy") to improve quality and readability, as well as ensuring that a text is free of errors in grammar, style and accuracy. The Chicago Manual of Style states that manuscript editing encompasses "the entire process by which a piece of writing is prepared for publication", which can include, but is not exclusive to, an editorial read for content and organization; light or heavy edits such as cutting redundancies, smoothing and restructuring; and proofreading. Some might use the terms interchangeably.

Scope

A copy editor's tasks typically include checking for correctness in spelling, punctuation, grammar, and usage; ensuring consistency with a house style guide (such as AP Stylebook or Chicago style); flagging unclear or inaccurate statements and querying the author; checking facts, cross-references, tables, and illustrations; and marking copy for typesetting. Copy editing is distinct from developmental editing, which addresses structure and content, and from proofreading, the final check of typeset pages before publication.

History

The function developed with the growth of newspapers and book publishing in the 19th and 20th centuries; the traditional "copy desk" of a newspaper, headed by a chief copy editor and staffed by "rim" editors under the rim of a U-shaped desk, became a hallmark of American journalism. Digital publishing has shifted much of the work to on-screen editing with tracked changes.

Light, medium and heavy edits

Copy editing is often described in three levels: a light edit corrects mechanical errors only; a medium edit also improves syntax and style; a heavy or substantive edit may reorganize and rewrite passages. The appropriate level is usually agreed in advance between editor and author or publisher.
