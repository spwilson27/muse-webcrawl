Arthropod

Arthropods (phylum Arthropoda, from Greek ἄρθρον arthron, "joint", and πούς pous, "foot") are invertebrate animals having an exoskeleton, a segmented body, and paired jointed appendages. They form one of the largest phyla in the animal kingdom: described species number over 1.2 million, and arthropods are estimated to constitute the great majority of all animal species on Earth.

Characteristics

The arthropod body is divided into segments, typically grouped into tagmata such as the head, thorax, and abdomen. The rigid cuticle, made largely of chitin, cannot stretch, so arthropods grow by moulting (ecdysis), shedding the old exoskeleton. Arthropods have an open circulatory system and a ventral nerve cord.

Classification

Living arthropods are divided into several major groups: the Chelicerata (spiders, scorpions, mites, ticks, and horseshoe crabs), the Myriapoda (centipedes and millipedes), the Crustacea (crabs, shrimp, barnacles, and woodlice), and the Hexapoda (insects and their kin). Trilobites, an entirely extinct group, were among the earliest arthropods in the fossil record, appearing in the Cambrian period over 520 million years ago.

Ecology and importance

Arthropods occupy nearly every habitat on Earth, from the deep sea to mountain peaks. Insects are vital pollinators of flowering plants; crustaceans form a major component of marine food webs; and some arthropods are significant pests or vectors of disease, such as mosquitoes transmitting malaria. Humans farm arthropods including honey bees, silkworms, and shrimp.
