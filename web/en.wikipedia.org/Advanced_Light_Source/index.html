Advanced Light Source

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The ALS dome overlooks San Francisco Bay from the Berkeley Hills.

The Advanced Light Source (ALS) is a research facility at Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory in Berkeley, California. One of the world's brightest sources of ultraviolet and soft x-ray light, the ALS is the first "third-generation" synchrotron light source in its energy range. It produces light from the infrared through the soft x-ray region of the spectrum, with particular emphasis on extreme ultraviolet and soft x-ray light, and is used by researchers from around the world to study the structure and electronic properties of matter.

History

The ALS was proposed in the early 1980s by the former LBNL director David Shirley, and the project was funded by the US Department of Energy. The accelerator complex was built within the historic dome that had housed Ernest Lawrence's 184-inch cyclotron. The facility produced its first light in March 1993 and was dedicated in October 1993. Initially built with the capacity for some 50 beamlines, it grew to support roughly 40 simultaneously operating beamlines and more than 2,000 users per year.

How it works

Electrons are generated by an electron gun, accelerated to 50 MeV in a linear accelerator, boosted to 1.5 GeV in a booster synchrotron, and injected into a 196.8-metre storage ring where they circulate at energies up to 1.9 GeV. As the electrons pass through bending magnets and insertion devices called undulators and wigglers, they emit intense synchrotron radiation tangent to their path. This light is channelled down beamlines to experimental endstations for techniques such as X-ray absorption spectroscopy, photoemission spectroscopy, X-ray microscopy, and protein crystallography.

Research

Research at the ALS spans materials science, biology, chemistry, physics, energy and environmental science, and archaeology. Notable work has included studies of high-temperature superconductors, magnetic nanostructures, battery and solar-cell materials, and the structure of proteins relevant to drug design. The ALS's X-ray microscopes have produced record-resolution images of magnetic domains and of processes inside operating batteries.

Upgrade

The ALS Upgrade (ALS-U) project will replace the existing storage ring with a multi-bend achromat lattice, shrinking the electron beam's emittance and increasing the brightness and coherence of soft x-ray beams by up to two orders of magnitude. The upgrade received US Department of Energy approval for construction in the early 2020s, with a planned shutdown for installation later in the decade.
