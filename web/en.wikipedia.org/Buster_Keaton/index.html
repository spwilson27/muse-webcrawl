Buster Keaton

Born: Joseph Frank Keaton, October 4, 1895, Piqua, Kansas, U.S.

Died: February 1, 1966 (aged 70), Los Angeles, California, U.S.

Occupation: Actor, comedian, filmmaker

Joseph Frank "Buster" Keaton (October 4, 1895 – February 1, 1966) was an American actor, comedian and filmmaker. He is best known for his silent films during the 1920s, in which he performed physical comedy and inventive stunts while maintaining a stoic, deadpan facial expression that earned him the nickname "The Great Stone Face".

Early life

Keaton was born into a vaudeville family in Piqua, Kansas. His parents, Joe and Myra Keaton, were travelling performers, and the young Keaton joined their act, The Three Keatons, as a small child. The act relied on rough slapstick, with his father throwing him around the stage; Keaton learned early how to fall safely, a skill that defined his later film work.

Film career

Keaton began working in films in 1917 alongside Roscoe "Fatty" Arbuckle. He soon directed and starred in his own two-reel comedies and then features, including One Week (1920), Sherlock Jr. (1924), The General (1926) and Steamboat Bill, Jr. (1928). The General, a Civil War epic featuring elaborate train sequences, was initially a commercial disappointment but is now widely regarded as his masterpiece and one of the greatest films ever made.

Keaton's independence ended in 1928 when he signed with Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer, a decision he later called the worst of his life. Loss of creative control, personal problems and alcoholism damaged his career during the sound era, though he continued working in smaller roles, as a gag writer, and on television for the rest of his life.

Legacy

Keaton was rediscovered by critics and audiences in the 1950s and 1960s. He received an Honorary Academy Award in 1959. His films are celebrated for their mechanical ingenuity, precise timing and emotional restraint, and he is consistently ranked among the greatest actor-directors in cinema history.
