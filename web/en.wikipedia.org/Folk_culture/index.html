Folk culture

Folk culture refers to the traditional culture of small, homogeneous, rural communities, transmitted informally from person to person and generation to generation. It encompasses the customs, stories, music, dance, dress, foodways and material crafts that arise within a community rather than being produced by mass media or professional institutions. Folk culture is generally contrasted with popular culture and high culture, both of which are more centrally produced and commercially distributed.

Characteristics

Folk culture tends to be localised, stable over long periods, and closely tied to the landscape, seasons and livelihoods of the people who practise it. Because it is passed down orally and through imitation, it changes slowly, though it is never entirely static; communities adapt traditions to new circumstances while retaining a sense of continuity. Anonymous authorship is typical: a folk song, tale or embroidery pattern rarely has a single identifiable creator.

Forms

Common forms of folk culture include folk tales and legends, ballads and folk songs, traditional dances, seasonal festivals, folk costume, traditional crafts such as weaving and woodcarving, and vernacular architecture. Proverbs, riddles and children's games are also widely studied examples.

Study and preservation

The academic study of folk culture is the concern of folklore studies, which emerged in the nineteenth century alongside romantic nationalism. Collectors such as the Brothers Grimm gathered tales they feared would disappear with industrialisation. In the modern era, museums, festivals and heritage programmes work to document and revive folk traditions threatened by urbanisation and global mass culture, although scholars note that commercialised "folklore" presented for tourists may differ considerably from living community practice.
