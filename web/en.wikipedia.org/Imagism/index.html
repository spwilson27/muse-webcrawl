Imagism

Imagism was a movement in early-20th-century Anglo-American poetry that favored precision of imagery and clear, sharp language. It is generally considered the first organized modernist literary movement in the English language. Imagism has been termed "a succession of brilliant ideas without any corresponding energy of execution," although it profoundly influenced the course of modernist poetry.

Origins

The origins of Imagism are found in the work of T. E. Hulme, whose essay "A Lecture on Modern Poetry" and poem "Autumn" (1908) anticipated the movement. In 1912, Ezra Pound, who had been promoting the work of contemporary poets such as Hilda Doolittle (H.D.) and Richard Aldington, coined the term "Imagiste" and attached it to their work.

Principles

In March 1913, Poetry magazine published "A Few Don'ts by an Imagiste" and a short statement of principles attributed to the movement:

  1. Direct treatment of the "thing" whether subjective or objective.
  2. To use absolutely no word that does not contribute to the presentation.
  3. As regarding rhythm: to compose in the sequence of the musical phrase, not in sequence of a metronome.

Pound defined the Image as "that which presents an intellectual and emotional complex in an instant of time."

Publications and legacy

The anthology Des Imagistes (1914), edited by Pound, introduced the movement to a wider audience. Later anthologies, edited by Amy Lowell under the title Some Imagist Poets (1915, 1916, 1917), broadened the group. Although the movement itself was short-lived, its insistence on clarity, economy, and free verse shaped poets including William Carlos Williams, Marianne Moore, and Wallace Stevens.
