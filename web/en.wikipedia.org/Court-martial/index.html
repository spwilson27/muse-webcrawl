Court-martial

Type: Military court
Jurisdiction: Members of the armed forces
Governed by: Military law (e.g., the UCMJ in the United States)

A court-martial (plural courts-martial) is a military court convened to try members of the armed forces for offences against military law, such as desertion, insubordination, cowardice, or conduct unbecoming an officer. In many jurisdictions courts-martial may also try certain civilian offences committed by service members, and in wartime may extend to prisoners of war and enemy combatants.

Composition

A court-martial is ordinarily presided over by a military judge and, for contested cases, a panel of officers (and, in some systems, senior enlisted members) who act as finders of fact. The accused is entitled to military defence counsel and may retain civilian counsel. Convening authorities — commanders empowered to refer charges — play a role that has been progressively curtailed by reforms aimed at reducing unlawful command influence.

United States practice

Under the Uniform Code of Military Justice (UCMJ), there are three forms: summary courts-martial for minor offences, special courts-martial for intermediate cases, and general courts-martial, which can impose the severest punishments including death for certain wartime offences. Convictions may be appealed through the service Courts of Criminal Appeals and ultimately to the Court of Appeals for the Armed Forces.

Notable cases

Famous courts-martial include the trial of Benedict Arnold's accomplice John André, the 1950s proceedings depicted in The Caine Mutiny, and the trial of Lieutenant William Calley for the My Lai massacre. The 1992 film A Few Good Men popularized the courtroom dynamics of a general court-martial.
