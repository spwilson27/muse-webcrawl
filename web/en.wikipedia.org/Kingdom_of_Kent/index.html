The Kingdom of Kent was an early medieval kingdom in south-eastern England, traditionally founded by the Jutes under Hengist and Horsa in the fifth century. One of the kingdoms of the Anglo-Saxon Heptarchy, Kent was the first Anglo-Saxon kingdom to convert to Christianity, following the arrival of Augustine of Canterbury in 597.

History

According to tradition, the Jutish brothers Hengist and Horsa were invited by the British king Vortigern to help defend against the Picts, then seized Kent for themselves. By the late sixth century Æthelberht of Kent was the dominant ruler in southern England and the third king listed by Bede as holding imperium over the other Anglo-Saxon kingdoms.

Æthelberht issued the earliest surviving code of laws in English and received Augustine’s mission in 597, making Canterbury the seat of the southern archbishopric. Kent later fell under the overlordship of Mercia and then Wessex, and in 871 it was fully absorbed into the Kingdom of Wessex, though it retained a distinct identity as a county.

Significance

Kent’s early conversion and its law code make it disproportionately well documented among the early Anglo-Saxon kingdoms. Its position facing the Frankish world across the Channel made it wealthy through trade, reflected in rich burials in east Kent cemeteries.
