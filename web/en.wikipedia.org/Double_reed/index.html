Double reed

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A double reed is a type of reed used to produce sound in various wind instruments. In contrast with a single reed instrument, where the reed vibrates against a mouthpiece, double-reed instruments sound when two pieces of cane — or, in modern times, synthetic material — vibrate against each other. The player places the reed between the lips and blows, and the rapid opening and closing of the two blades chops the airstream into pulses that drive the air column of the instrument.

Double-reed instruments

In the Western orchestra the principal double-reed instruments are:

  • Oboe and its relatives: cor anglais (English horn), oboe d'amore, bass oboe
  • Bassoon and contrabassoon
  • Heckelphone and other rarities

Historical and folk double-reed instruments include the shawm, crumhorn, dulcian, sarrusophone, and the zurna, suona, shehnai, duduk (with a very wide reed), hne and pi, found in folk traditions across Europe, the Middle East and Asia.

Construction

Double reeds are traditionally made from cane of the giant reed Arundo donax. For the oboe, a small tube of cane is split, gouged, folded and shaped, then tied onto a metal staple; the tip is scraped to a fraction of a millimetre thick. Bassoon reeds are made from two shaped pieces of cane bound onto a crook. Because tiny variations in scraping dramatically affect response, pitch and tone, most professional oboists and bassoonists make and continually adjust their own reeds, and a player's reeds are considered a core part of their craft. Synthetic reeds offer durability at the cost of some tonal flexibility.

The double-reed family is characterised by a penetrating, reedy timbre and by considerable resistance to the player's breath, giving fine control over dynamics and articulation.
