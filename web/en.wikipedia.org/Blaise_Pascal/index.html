Blaise Pascal (19 June 1623 – 19 August 1662) was a French mathematician, physicist, inventor, philosopher, and Catholic writer. He was a child prodigy who was educated by his father, a tax collector in Rouen. His earliest work was in the natural and applied sciences, where he made important contributions to the study of fluids, clarified the concepts of pressure and vacuum, and defended the scientific method.

Pascal was an important mathematician, helping to create two major new areas of research: he wrote a significant treatise on projective geometry at the age of 16, and later, corresponding with Pierre de Fermat, developed the foundations of probability theory. He also invented the mechanical calculator known as the Pascaline.

Life

Born in Clermont-Ferrand, Pascal lost his mother at three and was raised by his father Étienne, who educated his children at home. The family moved to Paris in 1631. By his teens Blaise was attending meetings of Marin Mersenne's circle. Following a religious experience on the night of 23 November 1654 — the so-called "Night of Fire," recorded in his Memorial — Pascal turned increasingly toward theology and the Jansenist community at Port-Royal. He died in Paris in 1662 at the age of 39, after years of frail health.

Mathematics and science

His Essai pour les coniques (1640) contains what is now called Pascal's theorem. In 1642, at 19, he began work on the Pascaline to assist his father's tax computations. His experiments with the barometer, confirming Torricelli's work on atmospheric pressure, led to the Puy de Dôme experiment of 1648 conducted by his brother-in-law Florin Périer. Pascal's law (the principle of transmission of fluid-pressure) bears his name, as does the SI unit of pressure, the pascal. His correspondence with Fermat in 1654, sparked by the Chevalier de Méré's questions on games of chance, laid the groundwork for probability theory and expected value.

Philosophy and theology

Pascal defended the Jansenists against the Jesuits in the Lettres provinciales (1656–57), a masterpiece of French prose satire. His unfinished notes for a defence of Christianity were published posthumously as the Pensées, which contain the famous Pascal's wager and reflections on the human condition: "Man is but a reed, the most feeble thing in nature; but he is a thinking reed."

Legacy

Pascal's name is given to Pascal's triangle, the programming language Pascal, and the wager. Craters on the Moon and an asteroid (4500 Pascal) also bear his name.
