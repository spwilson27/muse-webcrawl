Automation

Automation describes a wide range of technologies that reduce human intervention in processes, by predetermining decision criteria and embedding them in machines and control systems. It spans simple mechanical devices, industrial robots, process control in factories, and software that performs office or information-technology tasks.

History

Self-operating mechanisms date to antiquity, but automation accelerated with the Industrial Revolution, the feedback control theory of the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, and the introduction of programmable logic controllers and computers after the Second World War. The word "automation" was coined in the American automobile industry in the 1940s.

Benefits and social impact

Automation increases productivity, consistency, and safety, and reduces labor costs. It also displaces some jobs while creating others in design, maintenance, and supervision; the balance of these effects, and phenomena such as "lights-out" factories and robotic process automation in offices, are subjects of ongoing economic and policy debate.

Control systems

Modern automation relies on sensors, actuators, and feedback loops, often organized as supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) systems or distributed control systems in process industries.
