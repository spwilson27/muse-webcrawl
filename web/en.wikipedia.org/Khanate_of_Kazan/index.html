The Khanate of Kazan was a medieval Tatar Turkic state that existed from 1438 to 1552, centred on the city of Kazan at the confluence of the Volga and Kazanka rivers. A successor state of the Golden Horde and of Volga Bulgaria, it controlled a multi-ethnic territory of Tatars, Chuvash, Mari, Mordvins, Udmurts, and Bashkirs, and stood for over a century in rivalry with the Grand Duchy of Moscow until its conquest by Ivan the Terrible.

Foundation

The khanate emerged as the Golden Horde disintegrated. Ulugh Muhammad, a deposed khan of the Horde, seized Kazan in 1438 and founded a dynasty. The new state inherited the trade networks of Volga Bulgaria: Kazan became a major entrepôt for commerce between Russia, Siberia, Central Asia, and Persia, famous for its fairs, leather goods, and for a lucrative — and controversial — trade in Russian captives taken in raids.

Relations with Moscow

Kazan and Moscow alternated between war, trade, and Muscovite attempts to install client khans. Kazan forces raided Russian lands repeatedly, reaching Nizhny Novgorod and threatening Moscow's eastern frontier, while Russian armies campaigned down the Volga. In 1487 Ivan III captured Kazan and made its khan a vassal, a subordination that lasted intermittently until the 1520s, when Crimean Giray influence in Kazan turned the khanate firmly hostile to Moscow. Succession struggles among Chinggisid claimants supported by Moscow, Crimea, and the Nogai Horde made the khanate's politics chronically unstable.

Conquest

After failed campaigns in 1547–1550, Ivan IV built the fortress of Sviyazhsk upriver as a base and in 1552 laid siege to Kazan with a large army using artillery and mining. The city fell by storm on 2 October 1552; much of the population was massacred or enslaved, mosques were destroyed, and the khanate was annexed. Resistance in the countryside continued for several years. The conquest — commemorated by St Basil's Cathedral in Moscow, built to celebrate it — opened the Volga to Russian expansion and is a foundational, deeply contested event in Tatar historical memory.

Society and legacy

The khanate's ruling class was Tatar and Muslim; Islamic institutions, mosques, and madrasas flourished in Kazan, while many subject peoples retained animist or Orthodox beliefs. Today the khanate is regarded by Tatarstan's Tatars as a predecessor state, and its fall is commemorated annually in Kazan as a day of remembrance.
