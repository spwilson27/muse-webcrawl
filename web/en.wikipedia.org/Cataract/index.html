A cataract is a clouding of the normally clear lens of the eye, which lies behind the iris and pupil. As proteins in the lens break down and clump together, the lens becomes opaque, scattering light and reducing the sharpness of images reaching the retina. Cataracts develop slowly and are the most common cause of vision loss in people over 40 and the leading cause of blindness worldwide, though the condition is readily treatable with surgery in most settings.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms include blurred or cloudy vision, difficulty seeing at night, sensitivity to light and glare, seeing halos around lights, frequent changes in glasses prescription, fading or yellowing of colors, and double vision in a single eye.

Causes and types

Most cataracts are age-related (senile cataracts). Other types include:

  • Congenital cataracts — present at birth or developing in childhood, sometimes due to infection or genetic conditions.
  • Secondary cataracts — following other diseases such as diabetes, or use of corticosteroids.
  • Traumatic cataracts — after eye injury.
  • Radiation cataracts — after exposure to certain radiation, including ultraviolet light.

By location within the lens, cataracts are classed as nuclear (center), cortical (spoke-like opacities in the lens cortex), or posterior subcapsular (back of the lens).

Treatment

Early cataracts may be managed with updated glasses, brighter lighting, and anti-glare lenses. When vision loss interferes with daily activities, cataract surgery — removal of the clouded lens and replacement with an artificial intraocular lens — is the definitive and highly successful treatment.

Epidemiology

Age-related cataracts account for roughly half of all blindness worldwide (on the order of 20 million people), with the burden falling disproportionately on low- and middle-income countries where surgical access is limited.
