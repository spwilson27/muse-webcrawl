Caricature

MediumDrawing, print, painting
Italian origincaricare — to load, exaggerate

A caricature is a rendered image showing the features of its subject in a simplified or exaggerated way, whether through sketching, pencil strokes, or other artistic drawings. Caricatures can be either insulting or complimentary and can serve a political purpose, be drawn solely for entertainment, or combine both.

History

The earliest caricatures are found in the works of Leonardo da Vinci, who sought the "ideal type" of deformity. The art developed in 16th- and 17th-century Italy with artists such as the Carracci, and flourished in 18th-century Britain with James Gillray, Thomas Rowlandson, and George Cruikshank, whose satirical prints targeted politicians and royalty. Honoré Daumier carried the form to great heights in 19th-century France.

Political caricature

Editorial cartoonists use caricature to lampoon public figures and comment on current events; recognizable exaggeration of features such as a president's ears or hair makes subjects instantly identifiable. Caricatures have at times provoked controversy and censorship, most famously in debates over satirical depictions of religious figures.

Modern practice

Street and fairground caricaturists produce portraits in minutes, while the form remains a staple of magazines, newspapers, and animation design.
