Gelatin dessert

Gelatin desserts are desserts made with a sweetened and flavoured processed collagen product (gelatin), forming a clear, trembling jelly when dissolved in hot water and chilled. The most famous commercial form is the American brand Jell-O, whose name is often used generically in North America.

History

Gelled dishes date back to medieval aspics and jellies made by boiling calves' feet. Powdered gelatin was patented by Peter Cooper in 1845, and in 1897 Pearle Bixby Wait of Le Roy, New York, trademarked a flavoured version as "Jell-O". Sold to the Genesee Pure Food Company, it was marketed so successfully — through recipe booklets, radio and television advertising — that by the mid-20th century it was a fixture of American home cooking, especially in moulded desserts and "gelatin salads" containing fruit, vegetables, marshmallows or whipped cream.

Preparation and properties

Powdered gelatin is dissolved in hot liquid and sets on cooling into a thermoreversible gel that melts in the mouth. Some fresh fruits — pineapple, kiwi, papaya and fig — contain proteolytic enzymes (such as bromelain) that break down gelatin and prevent setting unless the fruit is cooked first. Vegetarian alternatives use seaweed hydrocolloids such as agar-agar or carrageenan.

Cultural presence

Gelatin desserts became emblematic of mid-century American convenience food and of Utah and the Mormon Corridor, where Jell-O consumption is famously high; Utah declared Jell-O its official state snack in 2001. Gummy candies and jelly sweets are firmer, concentrated relatives of the same basic preparation.
