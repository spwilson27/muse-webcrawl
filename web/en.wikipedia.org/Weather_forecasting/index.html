Weather forecasting

A surface analysis chart, the classic starting point of a synoptic weather forecast.

Weather forecasting is the application of science and technology to predict the conditions of the atmosphere for a given location and time. People have attempted to predict the weather informally for millennia and formally since the 19th century.

Weather forecasts are made by collecting quantitative data about the current state of the atmosphere, land, and ocean and using meteorology to project how the atmosphere will change at a given place. Once calculated manually based predominantly on changes in barometric pressure, current weather conditions, and sky conditions or cloud cover, weather forecasting now relies on computer-based models that take many atmospheric factors into account.

History

The ancient Babylonians, Chinese, and Indians all attempted weather prediction from observed patterns. The word "forecast" was coined by Admiral Robert FitzRoy, who established the British Meteorological Office in 1854 and issued the first daily published weather forecasts in The Times in 1861. The invention of the telegraph allowed rapid collection of observations over wide areas. In 1922 Lewis Fry Richardson published the first proposal for numerical weather prediction, and the first successful computer forecast was produced on the ENIAC in 1950 by a team led by Jule Charney.

Numerical weather prediction

Modern forecasting is dominated by numerical weather prediction (NWP): the atmosphere is divided into a three-dimensional grid, the equations of fluid motion are integrated forward on supercomputers, and the results are post-processed into forecasts. Major global models include the ECMWF Integrated Forecasting System and NOAA's Global Forecast System (GFS). Ensemble forecasting runs many simulations with slightly perturbed initial conditions to estimate forecast uncertainty.

Forecast types

Forecasts are issued for aviation, marine, agricultural, and public users; nowcasting covers the next few hours using radar and satellite data, while seasonal forecasts project anomalies months ahead. Human forecasters still add value by interpreting model output and communicating hazards such as hurricanes, floods, and heat waves.
