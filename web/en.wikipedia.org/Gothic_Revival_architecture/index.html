Gothic Revival

LocationBritain, Europe, North America, worldwide

Gothic Revival (also referred to as Victorian Gothic or Neo-Gothic) is an architectural movement that began in the late 1740s in England. The movement gained momentum in the late 18th and 19th centuries alongside a medievalist interest fostered by Romanticism, and sought to revive the forms of medieval Gothic architecture—pointed arches, ribbed vaults, flying buttresses, and elaborate tracery—in place of the classical styles derived from Greece and Rome.

Origins

An early landmark was Horace Walpole's villa Strawberry Hill in Twickenham (begun 1749), a whimsical, asymmetrical house decorated with Gothic detail. In the early nineteenth century the style was given a moral and religious programme by A. W. N. Pugin, whose books Contrasts (1836) and The True Principles of Pointed or Christian Architecture (1841) argued that Gothic was the only truly Christian architecture and that its forms embodied honesty of construction.

High Victorian Gothic

After the old Palace of Westminster burned in 1834, the new building was required to be Gothic or Elizabethan; Charles Barry's design, executed with Pugin's detailing, made the style the face of British national government. John Ruskin's writings, especially The Stones of Venice (1851–53), championed the polychrome, craft-centred Gothic of Italy. In France, Eugène Viollet-le-Duc restored medieval monuments including Notre-Dame de Paris and theorised a rationalist Gothic. The style spread worldwide and was applied to churches, universities, railway stations such as St Pancras in London, town halls, and country houses; in the United States it produced the "Carpenter Gothic" of wooden churches and houses.

Decline and legacy

By the early twentieth century the Gothic Revival had largely yielded to modernism for most building types, though collegiate and ecclesiastical Gothic continued for decades. The movement's insistence on truth to materials and the integration of art and craft fed directly into the Arts and Crafts movement.
