Disjunctivism

Disjunctivism is a position in the philosophy of perception. It holds that veridical perceptions and hallucinations are fundamentally different kinds of mental states or events, rather than instances of a single common kind of experience that the two share. The view is most closely associated with the British philosopher J. M. Hinton, and was later developed by Paul Snowdon, John McDowell, M. G. F. Martin and others.

The argument from hallucination

Disjunctivism is typically presented as a response to the argument from hallucination. That argument reasons that because a hallucination can be subjectively indistinguishable from a genuine perception, the two states must be of the same fundamental kind; since in hallucination no suitable object is present in the environment, perception in general must consist in awareness of something internal, such as a sense datum. Disjunctivists reject this "common kind" assumption: the indistinguishability of two states, they argue, does not show that the states share a fundamental nature, any more than the indistinguishability of real and counterfeit banknotes makes them the same thing.

Naïve realism

Disjunctivism is often motivated by naïve realism (or direct realism), the view that in genuine perception the mind-independent objects of the world, and their qualities, are literally constituents of the perceptual experience. On this picture, seeing a red tomato just is standing in a relation of acquaintance with the tomato and its redness; a hallucination of a red tomato contains no such constituent and must therefore be a different kind of state — often treated negatively, as a state that is merely indiscriminable from a perception.

Epistemological disjunctivism

A related but distinct position, epistemological disjunctivism, concerns perceptual knowledge. It holds that when a subject sees that p, the subject's rational support for believing p includes the factive reason that she sees that p — a reason unavailable in the hallucinatory case even when the subject cannot tell the difference. John McDowell is a leading proponent of this view.

Criticisms

Critics object that disjunctivism struggles to give a positive account of what hallucination is, and that causal arguments — for example, that the same proximal stimulation of the brain can produce either state — tell against treating them as fundamentally different kinds. Intentionalists about perception typically defend the common-kind picture instead.
