Jonas Salk

Jonas Edward Salk (28 October 1914 – 23 June 1995) was an American virologist and medical researcher who developed one of the first successful polio vaccines. His inactivated polio vaccine, announced as safe and effective in 1955, dramatically reduced the incidence of poliomyelitis worldwide.

Early life and research

Born in New York City to Russian Jewish immigrant parents, Salk studied at City College of New York and earned his medical degree from New York University. He worked on influenza vaccines with Thomas Francis at the University of Michigan before moving to the University of Pittsburgh in 1947, where he began his polio research with funding from the National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis (March of Dimes).

Polio vaccine

Salk developed a vaccine using killed poliovirus grown in cell culture. After preliminary trials, a massive field trial involving some 1.8 million children began in 1954. On 12 April 1955 the vaccine was declared safe, potent, and effective. Salk famously declined to patent it, asking, "Could you patent the sun?" Mass immunization followed, and polio cases in the United States fell precipitously.

Later work

In 1963 Salk founded the Salk Institute for Biological Studies in La Jolla, California, designed by architect Louis Kahn. In his later years he worked on a vaccine against HIV. He died in La Jolla in 1995.
