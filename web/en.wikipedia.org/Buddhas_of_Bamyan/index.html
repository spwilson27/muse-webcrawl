Buddhas of Bamiyan

LocationBamiyan, Afghanistan
Height55 m (180 ft) and 38 m (125 ft)
Built6th–7th century CE
DestroyedMarch 2001
UNESCOWorld Heritage Site (in danger since 2003)

The Buddhas of Bamiyan were two monumental statues of the Buddha carved into the side of a cliff in the Bamiyan valley of central Afghanistan, roughly 130 kilometres northwest of Kabul, at an elevation of about 2,500 metres. Built in the 6th and 7th centuries CE, the larger western Buddha stood 55 metres tall and the eastern 38 metres. They were the largest standing Buddha statues in the world and masterpieces of Gandharan art, blending Gupta, Sassanian and Hellenistic influences along the Silk Road.

History

The statues were carved by Buddhist monks when Bamiyan was a flourishing centre of trade and religion. The Chinese pilgrim Xuanzang visited around 643 CE and described the two great figures and the monasteries around them. The statues' faces were later damaged, variously attributed to campaigns by Genghis Khan's successors, the Mughal emperor Aurangzeb, and Afghan rulers, but the bodies survived for roughly 1,400 years.

Destruction

In February 2001, Taliban leader Mullah Mohammed Omar decreed that the statues were idols and must be destroyed. Despite international appeals, the Buddhas were demolished over several weeks in March 2001 using anti-aircraft guns, artillery and dynamite. The destruction was condemned worldwide as an act of cultural vandalism. The empty niches remain, with the surrounding caves and valley inscribed on UNESCO's World Heritage List in 2003. Fragments have been catalogued, and proposals for partial reconstruction or digital restoration — including a 3D light projection shown in 2015 — continue to be debated.
