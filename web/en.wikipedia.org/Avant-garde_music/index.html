Avant-garde music

Stylistic originsModernism, experimental music, futurism
Cultural originsEarly 20th century, Europe and the United States
Key figuresJohn Cage, Karlheinz Stockhausen, Pierre Schaeffer, La Monte Young

Avant-garde music is music that is considered to be at the forefront of innovation in its field, with the term "avant-garde" implying a critique of existing aesthetic conventions, rejection of the status quo in favour of unique and original elements, and the idea of deliberately challenging audiences.

History

Early twentieth-century movements such as Italian futurism (Luigi Russolo's "art of noises", 1913), atonality and the Second Viennese School, and later total serialism, musique concrète, tape music, indeterminacy, and minimalism all contributed to the avant-garde tradition. John Cage's 4′33″ (1952), in which the performer makes no intentional sounds, became an emblematic provocation.

Characteristics

Avant-garde composers have experimented with noise, silence, chance procedures, unconventional instruments and extended techniques, electronic synthesis, microtonality, and graphic scores. The boundary between avant-garde music and experimental music is debated; "experimental" often denotes a working method, whereas "avant-garde" implies a social and aesthetic stance.

In popular music

The term is also applied to experimental strains within jazz (free jazz), rock (avant-garde rock), and electronic music, where artists extend the norms of their genres.
