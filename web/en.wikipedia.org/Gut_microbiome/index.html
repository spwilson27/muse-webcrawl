Gut microbiome

The gut microbiome is the community of microorganisms — bacteria, archaea, fungi, viruses and other microbes — that inhabit the gastrointestinal tract of humans and other animals, together with their collective genetic material. The human gut harbours tens of trillions of microorganisms, dominated by bacterial phyla such as Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, with a combined gene count far exceeding that of the human genome.

Functions

Gut microbes ferment indigestible dietary fibre into short-chain fatty acids, synthesise certain vitamins (including vitamin K and some B vitamins), metabolise bile acids and drugs, and help train and regulate the immune system. The community also competes with pathogens for nutrients and attachment sites.

Dysbiosis and health

Disruption of the normal community, termed dysbiosis, has been associated with conditions including inflammatory bowel disease, obesity, type 2 diabetes, colorectal cancer and certain neurological disorders, though many relationships remain correlational. Faecal microbiota transplantation is an established treatment for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection and is being investigated for other indications.

Development

Colonisation begins at birth and is shaped by delivery mode, feeding, antibiotics and environment; the community broadly resembles an adult configuration by about three years of age and changes again in old age.
