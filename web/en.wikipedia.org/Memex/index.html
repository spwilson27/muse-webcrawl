Memex

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The memex is a theoretical device, never actually built, described by the American engineer Vannevar Bush in his influential 1945 essay "As We May Think" in The Atlantic. Bush envisioned it as a mechanized private library and filing system — a desk in which an individual could store books, records, and communications on microfilm and retrieve them rapidly by means of associative links.

Description

The memex would consist of a desk with slanting translucent screens on which material could be projected for convenient reading. A user could consult a book by typing a code, flip through its pages with levers, and — crucially — create "associative trails" linking any two items, building a personal web of references that could be stored and shared.

Influence

Bush's concept of associative indexing is widely credited as a conceptual forerunner of hypertext. It influenced pioneers such as Douglas Engelbart and Ted Nelson, and through them the development of the World Wide Web. The name "memex" is generally understood as a contraction of "memory extender".
