The Xianbei (Chinese: 鮮卑; pinyin: Xiānbēi) were an ancient nomadic people of the eastern Eurasian steppe, inhabiting what is today Mongolia, Inner Mongolia and northeastern China. Together with the Xiongnu, they were among the most consequential peoples of the northern frontier of imperial China, and Xianbei-led dynasties would eventually rule much of northern China for nearly three centuries.

Origins and early history

The Xianbei emerged from the Donghu confederation, which was defeated by the Xiongnu chanyu Modu around 208 BCE. Retreating eastward to the Xianbei and Wuhuan mountains, from which they took their name, the Xianbei gradually consolidated as an independent power. By the mid-2nd century CE, under the leader Tanshihuai, they formed a vast steppe confederation that raided Han dynasty China repeatedly and defeated coalitions raised against them.

Fragmentation and sinicization

After Tanshihuai's death in 181 CE the confederation fragmented. During the Three Kingdoms period, Xianbei groups such as the Murong, Tuoba and Duan moved into the Chinese frontier zone, serving as auxiliaries, founding regional states, and increasingly adopting Chinese language, dress and administrative practice.

The Xianbei states

During the Sixteen Kingdoms period (304–439), Xianbei clans founded Former Yan, Later Yan, Southern Yan, Western Qin and Southern Liang. Most importantly, the Tuoba clan founded Northern Wei (386–534), which reunified northern China in 439. Emperor Xiaowen's reforms of the 490s — adopting Han surnames, dress and the capital at Luoyang — completed a deep sinicization, and the Tuoba royal house took the surname Yuan.

Legacy

The Xianbei legacy includes the Northern dynasties, and through the mixed aristocracy of the northwest, the founding houses of the Sui and Tang dynasties had partial Xianbei ancestry. The Khitan and the Mongols are among peoples sometimes traced to Xianbei-related groups. The modern Mongolian city name Ulaanbaatar region and the Greater Khingan range preserve Xianbei toponymy.
