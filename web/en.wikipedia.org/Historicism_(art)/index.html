Historicism (art)

Historicism or historism in art and architecture is the conscious revival and imitation of styles of the past. It was a dominant tendency of the 19th century, when architects and designers drew on Classical, Gothic, Renaissance, Baroque, and other historical vocabularies — sometimes singly, sometimes eclectically combined — for new buildings and objects.

Revival styles

Historicism encompasses the many "neo-" and "revival" movements of the period: Neoclassicism and the Greek Revival, the Gothic Revival championed by A. W. N. Pugin and John Ruskin, Neo-Renaissance, Neo-Baroque, Romanesque Revival, and the Beaux-Arts style taught at the École des Beaux-Arts in Paris. In Britain the style wars between classical and Gothic partisans — the "Battle of the Styles" — shaped public buildings such as the Palace of Westminster and the Foreign Office.

Decline

By the end of the 19th century, historicism was challenged by Art Nouveau and then by modernism, whose theorists condemned imitation of the past as dishonest. Revivalist design nevertheless persisted in academic and vernacular building well into the 20th century.
