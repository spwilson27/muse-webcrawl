Universal algebra

Universal algebra (sometimes called general algebra) is the field of mathematics that studies algebraic structures in themselves, not examples ("models") of algebraic structures. For instance, rather than take particular groups as the object of study, in universal algebra one takes the class of groups as an object of study.

Basic ideas

An algebra in this sense is a set A together with a collection of operations on A, each of finite arity. The list of operation symbols and their arities is called the signature or type of the algebra. Familiar structures — groups, rings, lattices, Boolean algebras — are all algebras of suitable types, allowing theorems proved once in universal algebra to apply to all of them at once.

Key concepts include subalgebras, homomorphisms, congruences, and the constructions of products and quotients. A variety is a class of algebras defined by a set of identities (equations); Birkhoff's variety theorem (1935) characterizes varieties as exactly those classes closed under homomorphic images, subalgebras, and products.

History

The subject grew out of work by J. J. Sylvester, Arthur Cayley, and especially Alfred North Whitehead, whose 1898 A Treatise on Universal Algebra gave the field its name. Garrett Birkhoff's papers of the 1930s, including his variety theorem and his subdirect representation theorem, established the modern foundations, and the field expanded further through the work of Anatoly Maltsev and others on model-theoretic and equational approaches.

Applications

Universal algebra underlies parts of mathematical logic, lattice theory, and theoretical computer science, where its methods inform the study of term rewriting, type systems, and the semantics of algebraic specification languages.
