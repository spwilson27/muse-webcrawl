Golden plates

The golden plates are, according to Latter Day Saint belief, the source from which Joseph Smith translated the Book of Mormon, a sacred text of the faith. Smith said he found the plates in 1823, buried in a stone box on a hill near his home in Manchester, New York, after being directed there by the angel Moroni. He said the plates had the appearance of gold, were bound by three rings, and were engraved with characters in a language he called "reformed Egyptian."

History according to Smith

Smith reported first visiting the site on 22 September 1823 and returning annually for four years before obtaining the plates on 22 September 1827. With the plates, he said, were interpreters (the Urim and Thummim, and later a seer stone) by which he translated the record. After the translation was complete, Smith said he returned the plates to the angel Moroni; they have never been made available for public examination.

Witnesses

Eleven men signed testimonies published in the Book of Mormon. The Three Witnesses (Oliver Cowdery, David Whitmer and Martin Harris) stated that an angel showed them the plates; the Eight Witnesses stated that they had handled and examined the plates themselves. All maintained their testimony, though several later left or were expelled from Smith's church.

Significance

The plates' existence and nature are a central question of Mormon faith and scholarship. Believers regard them as physical evidence of the Book of Mormon's divine origin; sceptics have proposed naturalistic explanations, and historians note the absence of the artifact itself. Depictions of the plates feature prominently in Latter-day Saint art and teaching, and the Hill Cumorah in New York remains a significant site for the movement.
