Criminology

Criminology (from Latin crīmen, “accusation”, and Greek -λογία, -logia) is the interdisciplinary study of crime and deviant behaviour. Criminology is a multidisciplinary field in both the behavioural and social sciences, which draws primarily upon the research of sociologists, political scientists, economists, legal sociologists, psychologists and psychiatrists, as well as on research in law.

Schools of thought

The classical school, associated with Cesare Beccaria (On Crimes and Punishments, 1764) and Jeremy Bentham, treated offenders as free and rational agents and argued for proportionate, certain punishment as deterrence. The positivist school, led by Cesare Lombroso, Raffaele Garofalo and Enrico Ferri, sought biological, psychological and social causes of criminality; Lombroso's theory of the “born criminal” is now discredited, but the positivists' emphasis on empirical study and individual treatment endured.

Modern theories

Twentieth-century criminology developed social disorganization theory (Chicago School), strain theory (Robert K. Merton), differential association and social learning theory (Edwin Sutherland), labelling theory, and control theories. Critical and feminist criminology examine how crime definitions reflect power relations. Situational and environmental criminology focus on crime events and prevention through environmental design.

Crime statistics

Criminologists rely on official statistics (such as the FBI's Uniform Crime Reports), victimisation surveys (such as the National Crime Victimization Survey) and self-report studies, each with known biases; the gap between recorded crime and actual crime is called the “dark figure” of crime.

Subfields

Subfields include penology (punishment and corrections), victimology, biosocial criminology, comparative criminology and green criminology.
