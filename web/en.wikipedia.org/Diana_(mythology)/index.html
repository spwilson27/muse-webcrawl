Diana is a goddess in Roman and Hellenistic religion, primarily considered a patroness of the countryside and nature, hunters, the Moon, and crossroads. She is the Roman counterpart of the Greek goddess Artemis. Diana is traditionally the daughter of Jupiter and Latona, and the twin sister of Apollo. Like Artemis, she is a virgin goddess of the hunt, wild animals and the wilderness, and she was also associated with childbirth and with the protection of women, slaves and the lower classes in Roman society.

Origins and cult

Diana's worship appears to be an old Italic cult, centred on sacred groves. Her most famous early sanctuary stood at Lake Nemi in the Alban Hills, where she was worshipped as Diana Nemorensis, "Diana of the Wood." The priest of Nemi, the Rex Nemorensis ("King of the Wood"), was a runaway slave who held office until another slave challenged and killed him in single combat — a strange rite discussed at length by James Frazer in The Golden Bough. Her great temple on the Aventine Hill in Rome, traditionally dedicated in the 6th century BCE, served as a cult centre for the Latin League and a refuge for slaves; her festival, the Nemoralia, was held on the Ides of August (13 August), the "slaves' holiday."

Mythology

Roman writers adopted the myths of Artemis for Diana. In Ovid's Metamorphoses, the hunter Actaeon accidentally sees Diana bathing; she transforms him into a stag, and his own hounds tear him apart. She is also linked to the sacrifice of Iphigenia, to the moon-goddess Luna and to the underworld goddess Hecate in her triple form (Diana Triformis), and Virgil calls her Trivia, "she of the three ways." Diana was credited with aiding her mother Latona in delivering Apollo on Delos, immediately after her own birth.

Nature and worship

Unlike many Roman deities, Diana's cult retained strong roots among the rural poor and enslaved; Cicero and others note her popularity beyond the elite. Worshippers offered garlands hung in trees, votives, and prayers especially connected with hunting, safe childbirth and protection on journeys. In later European folklore, and controversially in Charles Leland's 1899 Aradia, or the Gospel of the Witches, Diana was portrayed as queen of the witches; this depiction influenced modern Neopagan and Wiccan practice, in which Diana is revered as a principal goddess.

Legacy

Diana remains a recurring figure in Western art and literature, frequently depicted with a crescent moon diadem, bow, quiver and a hunting dog or deer. Places named for her include sanctuaries across the former Roman world, and her name is borne by the French royal château at Anet's chapel sculptures and countless later works. The asteroid 78 Diana and lunar features also bear her name.
