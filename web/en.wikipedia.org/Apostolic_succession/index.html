Apostolic succession

Belief ofRoman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Anglican, and some Lutheran and other churches
Transmitted byEpiscopal consecration (laying on of hands)
First articulatedEarly Church; Irenaeus of Lyons, 2nd century

Apostolic succession is the doctrine, held by several Christian churches, that the ministry of bishops stretches back in an unbroken line to the Apostles of Jesus. Through the rite of ordination — in particular the laying on of hands at episcopal consecration — bishops are held to transmit the apostolic office and, with it, the authority to teach, sanctify, and govern, together with the grace of the Holy Spirit given to the Apostles at Pentecost. The doctrine underlies claims about the validity of sacraments, especially the Eucharist and ordination itself.

Origins

Early Christian writers appealed to succession lists of bishops in the great sees as a guarantee of authentic teaching against heresy: Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180 AD) traced the bishops of Rome from Linus, successor of Peter and Paul, in his Against Heresies. Clement of Rome, writing in the first century, already described the Apostles appointing bishops and deacons and providing for successors. The practice of consecrating a new bishop by at least three bishops, with the laying on of hands, was codified as early as the Council of Nicaea (325).

Understanding in different churches

The Roman Catholic Church teaches that apostolic succession is essential to the Church's structure and to valid ordination, and in the apostolic letter Apostolicae curae (1896) Pope Leo XIII declared Anglican orders "absolutely null and utterly void" — a judgement Anglicans reject. The Eastern Orthodox Church likewise insists on succession within the Orthodox episcopate while sometimes recognising the sacramental reality of other churches through oikonomia. Many Anglicans, Old Catholics, and some Lutherans (notably the Church of Sweden) maintain historic episcopates claiming succession. Most other Protestant traditions locate apostolicity instead in fidelity to apostolic teaching ("succession of doctrine") and reject the necessity of a tactile succession of bishops.

Ecumenical significance

Apostolic succession is a central issue in ecumenical dialogue. The World Council of Churches' Lima text Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry (1982) proposed that churches lacking the historic episcopate might recover the "sign" of apostolic succession without repudiating their existing ministries; the Porvoo Communion (1996) brought Anglican and Lutheran churches in northern Europe into full communion on this basis.
