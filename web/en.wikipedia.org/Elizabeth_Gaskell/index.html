Elizabeth Gaskell, novelist of industrial Manchester and biographer of Charlotte Brontë

Elizabeth Cleghorn Gaskell (née Stevenson; 29 September 1810 – 12 November 1865), often referred to as Mrs Gaskell, was an English novelist, biographer and short story writer. Her novels offer a detailed portrait of the lives of many strata of Victorian society, including the very poor. Her first novel, Mary Barton (1848), set among Manchester's working classes, brought her fame and the friendship of Charles Dickens, in whose magazines much of her later work appeared.

Life

Raised largely in Knutsford, Cheshire — the model for Cranford and Hollingford in Wives and Daughters — Elizabeth Stevenson married William Gaskell, a Unitarian minister, in 1832 and settled in industrial Manchester. Writing partly to occupy her grief after the death of her infant son, she published Mary Barton anonymously in 1848; its sympathy for striking workers provoked both praise and anger from mill owners.

Works

Gaskell's major novels are Mary Barton (1848), Cranford (1851–53), Ruth (1853), North and South (1854–55) — contrasting the industrial North with the rural South through its heroine Margaret Hale — Sylvia's Lovers (1863), and the unfinished Wives and Daughters (1864–66), often considered her masterpiece. She also wrote the first biography of Charlotte Brontë, The Life of Charlotte Brontë (1857), which remains a landmark of literary biography, and ghost stories including "The Old Nurse's Story".

Gaskell died suddenly of a heart attack in 1865 while visiting a house she had secretly bought in Hampshire. Her reputation, overshadowed in the early twentieth century, has been substantially revived by feminist and social-historical criticism.
