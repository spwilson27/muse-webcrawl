Breakwater (structure)

A breakwater is a permanent structure constructed at a coastal area to protect against tides, currents, waves, and storm surges. Breakwaters, usually placed parallel to the coast or enclosing a harbor, dissipate wave energy so that the water behind them is calm enough for ships to berth safely and for shorelines to resist erosion.

Types

Rubble-mound breakwaters are heaped piles of rock or concrete armor units, such as tetrapods and dolosse, whose rough, porous surfaces absorb wave energy. Vertical or caisson breakwaters are large concrete boxes filled with sand or rubble and set on a prepared seabed; they reflect rather than absorb waves. Floating breakwaters, used in marinas with modest wave climates, consist of anchored pontoons. Detached offshore breakwaters may encourage beach accretion in their lee, sometimes forming a tombolo.

Design considerations

Engineers design breakwaters for a chosen design wave height, accounting for wave reflection, overtopping, and the stability of armor units, commonly estimated with the Hudson formula or the van der Meer formulas. A poorly designed breakwater can suffer toe scour, armor displacement, or catastrophic failure in storms, as happened historically at Cherbourg and at Sines in Portugal in 1978.

Ecology and effects

Breakwaters create sheltered, hard-substrate habitat colonized by algae, mussels, and fish, but they can also alter longshore sediment transport, starving downdrift beaches of sand. Submerged and low-crested designs are sometimes used to reduce visual impact and improve water circulation.
