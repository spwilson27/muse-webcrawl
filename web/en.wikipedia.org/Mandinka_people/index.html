Mandinka people

Total population: about 11–13 million
Regions: The Gambia, Senegal, Guinea-Bissau, Mali, Guinea, Sierra Leone, Ivory Coast
Language: Mandinka (Manding)
Religion: predominantly Islam

The Mandinka (also spelled Mandingo or Malinke, and self-designated Mandinko) are a West African ethnic group, one of the largest Mandé-speaking peoples. They descend from the Mali Empire, which rose in the 13th century under Sundiata Keita, whose story is preserved in an epic recited by hereditary griots (jeliw).

History and society

From the Mali Empire's heartland on the upper Niger, Mandinka groups migrated westward to the Senegambia, founding states such as Kaabu. They are traditionally farmers of millet, maize and peanuts, organised in patrilineal clans with occupational castes — nobles, artisans, blacksmiths and griots. Islam, adopted by much of the Mandinka elite from the empire period onward, spread widely in the 19th century through the jihad states of leaders such as Samori Touré (himself of Mandinka/Dyula heritage).

Culture

Mandinka culture is known for its oral tradition and music centred on the kora, a 21-string harp-lute played by griot families. The Gambian Mandinka ancestor Kunta Kinte, whose enslavement in America was dramatised in Alex Haley's Roots (1976), became a widely known symbol of the transatlantic slave trade.
