Kenneth Geddes Wilson (8 June 1936 – 15 June 2013) was an American theoretical physicist who won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1982 for his theory of critical phenomena in connection with phase transitions, based on the renormalization group.

Early life and education

Wilson was born in Waltham, Massachusetts, the son of E. Bright Wilson, a prominent Harvard chemist. He studied mathematics at Harvard, finishing his undergraduate degree in 1956, and earned his PhD at the California Institute of Technology in 1961 under Murray Gell-Mann, working on the low-energy scattering of kaons.

Renormalization group and critical phenomena

Joining Cornell University in 1963, Wilson tackled the problem of critical phenomena — the behaviour of matter near continuous phase transitions — by developing Leo Kadanoff's scaling ideas into a full computational method. His renormalization group approach (1971) integrated out degrees of freedom scale by scale, explaining universality: why very different materials share identical critical exponents. The method transformed statistical physics and quantum field theory alike.

Other contributions

Wilson introduced the operator product expansion, Wilson loops in gauge theory, and in 1974 formulated lattice gauge theory, placing quantum chromodynamics on a space-time lattice in a way suited to numerical computation. He was also a pioneer of scientific computing, advocating supercomputing for physics, and in later life turned to education reform, co-authoring work on redesigning science education.

Awards

Besides the 1982 Nobel Prize, Wilson received the Wolf Prize in Physics (1980, shared with Michael Fisher and Leo Kadanoff), the Boltzmann Medal, and the Dirac Medal. He spent his later career at Ohio State University, where he held a professorship from 1988.
