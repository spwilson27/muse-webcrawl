Singing Revolution

Date1988–1991
LocationEstonia, Latvia, Lithuania (Baltic states)
OutcomeRestoration of independence of the Baltic states

The Singing Revolution is a commonly used name for the events between 1987 and 1991 that led to the restoration of the independence of Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania — the three Baltic states that had been occupied and annexed by the Soviet Union in 1940. The name, coined by the Estonian artist and activist Heinz Valk, reflects the central role of mass singing — especially at the region's historic song festivals — in the non-violent movement.

Background

Under Mikhail Gorbachev's policies of glasnost and perestroika, long-suppressed grievances surfaced: the secret protocols of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, environmental damage from Soviet industrial projects (such as the Estonian phosphorite mining dispute of 1987), and Russification. Popular fronts formed in 1988 — Sąjūdis in Lithuania, and the Popular Fronts of Estonia and Latvia — calling at first for autonomy and then for full independence.

Key events

In June 1988, after a television concert in Tallinn, crowds walked to the Song Festival Grounds and sang forbidden national songs late into the night; that autumn hundreds of thousands gathered to sing at festivals in all three republics. On 23 August 1989, the 50th anniversary of the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact, around two million people formed the Baltic Way, a human chain some 690 kilometres long linking Tallinn, Riga and Vilnius. Lithuania declared independence on 11 March 1990. In January 1991 Soviet forces killed demonstrators in Vilnius and Riga, but the Baltic peoples defended their parliaments with barricades and continued singing protests; following the failed August 1991 coup in Moscow, Estonia and Latvia proclaimed the restoration of independence, and the USSR formally recognised all three states in September 1991.

Legacy

The movement is celebrated as a model of successful non-violent resistance. The Estonian song festival tradition (Laulupidu), founded in 1869, and its Latvian and Lithuanian counterparts are inscribed on UNESCO's list of Intangible Cultural Heritage. Heinz Valk's remark that the Estonians had "sung themselves free" gave the revolution its enduring name.
