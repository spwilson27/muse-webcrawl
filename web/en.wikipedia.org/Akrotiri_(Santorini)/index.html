Akrotiri (Greek: Ακρωτήρι) is a Bronze Age settlement on the volcanic island of Santorini (Thera) in the Cyclades, Greece. It was buried under volcanic ash from the Minoan eruption in the second millennium BC, which preserved its buildings, frescoes and artefacts in remarkable detail, leading to comparisons with Pompeii.

History

The site was first settled in the Late Neolithic and grew during the Early Bronze Age into an important Cycladic town. By the Middle and Late Bronze Age it had become a prosperous harbour town with strong ties to Minoan Crete, evidenced by its architecture, pottery and wall paintings.

Before the eruption, earthquakes damaged the town; evidence suggests the inhabitants evacuated, as no human remains or valuables such as gold have been found among the ruins. The eruption of Thera, one of the largest volcanic events in recorded history, buried Akrotiri under metres of pumice and ash.

Excavations

Systematic excavations began in 1967 under Spyridon Marinatos of the Archaeological Society of Athens and continued under Christos Doumas. Only a small fraction of the town — roughly 2.5 acres — has been excavated. A modern protective canopy covers the site; the original shelter collapsed in 2005, and a replacement opened in 2012.

Findings

Excavators found multi-storey buildings with paved streets, drainage systems, storerooms filled with large pithoi, and an advanced plumbing system with what may be among the earliest indoor toilets. The town is famous for its vivid frescoes — including the Spring Fresco, the Boxing Boys, the Fisherman, and the miniature "West House" frieze depicting fleets, towns and landscapes — many now displayed in the National Archaeological Museum of Athens and the Museum of Prehistoric Thera in Fira.

Significance

Akrotiri provides one of the best-preserved windows into Aegean Bronze Age urban life and has been linked in popular speculation to the legend of Atlantis, a connection most scholars treat cautiously.
