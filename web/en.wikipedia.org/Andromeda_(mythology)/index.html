Andromeda (mythology)

In Greek mythology, Andromeda (/ænˈdrɒmɪdə/; Greek: Ἀνδρομέδα, Androméda) is the daughter of Cepheus, king of Aethiopia, and his wife Cassiopeia. When Cassiopeia boasted that she (or her daughter) was more beautiful than the Nereids, Poseidon sent the sea monster Cetus to ravage the coast. An oracle declared that only the sacrifice of Andromeda, chained to a rock by the sea, would appease the god. The hero Perseus, flying home with the head of Medusa, slew the monster, freed Andromeda and married her. After her death Andromeda was placed among the stars as the constellation Andromeda, near Perseus and Cassiopeia.

The myth

Cassiopeia's boast of beauty offended the sea nymphs, the Nereids, and Poseidon answered their complaint with a flood and the monster Cetus. The oracle of Ammon told Cepheus that his land would be delivered only if he exposed his daughter Andromeda to the monster. Andromeda was chained naked to a rock on the shore — ancient sources and later art being divided on details — when Perseus, returning from slaying the Gorgon Medusa, saw her, fell in love, and promised Cepheus he would kill the monster in exchange for her hand. Using Medusa's head or his adamantine sword (accounts differ), Perseus killed Cetus and freed her.

At the wedding feast, Andromeda's uncle and former fiancé Phineus attacked Perseus with a band of followers; Perseus held up the Gorgon's head and turned them to stone. Andromeda bore Perseus several children, among them Perses, said to be the ancestor of the Persians, and Gorgophone. The couple's descendants ruled Mycenae and Tiryns and included Heracles.

Sources

The story is told in Apollodorus's Library, Ovid's Metamorphoses (Books 4–5), and Hyginus; Sophocles and Euripides each wrote a lost tragedy titled Andromeda, the latter famous enough to be parodied by Aristophanes. The myth also attaches to the ancient port of Joppa (Jaffa), where the rock of Andromeda was shown to visitors.

In art and astronomy

Andromeda's rescue is a staple of Western art, depicted by Piero di Cosimo, Titian, Rubens, Ingres and Gustave Doré, among others. The constellation Andromeda, catalogued by Ptolemy, contains the Andromeda Galaxy (M31), and the neighbouring constellations Perseus, Cassiopeia, Cepheus and Cetus perpetuate the whole cast of the myth in the northern sky.
