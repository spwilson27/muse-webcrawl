American Indian boarding schools

American Indian boarding schools, later known as American Indian residential schools, were established in the United States from the mid-17th to the early 20th centuries with a primary objective of "civilizing" or assimilating Native American children and youth into Anglo-American culture. The US government, often working with Christian missionaries, removed children from their families and communities and placed them in schools where they were forbidden to speak their languages or practise their religions.

History

The model was pioneered at the Carlisle Indian Industrial School in Pennsylvania, founded in 1879 by Captain Richard Henry Pratt, whose maxim was "Kill the Indian in him, and save the man." By 1926, over 80 percent of Native American school-age children attended such institutions — more than 180 federal schools existed, alongside hundreds of mission schools. Children endured military discipline, manual labour, renamed identities, cropped hair, disease and physical and sexual abuse; many died, and burial grounds have been documented at numerous sites.

Policy and reform

The 1928 Meriam Report condemned conditions and the assimilation policy, and the Indian Reorganization Act of 1934 began a slow shift toward tribal self-determination, reinforced by the Indian Self-Determination and Education Assistance Act of 1975. Most boarding schools had closed by the 1980s, though four off-reservation schools remain open under the Bureau of Indian Education.

Legacy and investigations

In 2021, following discoveries of unmarked graves at Canadian residential schools, US Secretary of the Interior Deb Haaland launched the Federal Indian Boarding School Initiative. Its 2024 final report identified at least 973 children who died at the schools and 74 burial sites, and recommended a formal federal apology, which President Joe Biden delivered in October 2024. Survivors and descendants describe lasting intergenerational trauma, language loss and cultural disruption attributable to the system.
