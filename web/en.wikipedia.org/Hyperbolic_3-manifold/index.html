Hyperbolic 3-manifold

The interior of a hyperbolic 3-manifold as seen in the Poincaré ball model.

In mathematics, a hyperbolic 3-manifold is a 3-manifold equipped with a complete Riemannian metric of constant sectional curvature −1. In other words, it is the quotient of three-dimensional hyperbolic space by a subgroup of hyperbolic isometries acting freely and properly discontinuously. Hyperbolic 3-manifolds form the richest and most studied class of 3-manifolds; William Thurston's work on them, culminating in the geometrization conjecture (proved by Grigori Perelman), revolutionised low-dimensional topology.

Structure

By Mostow rigidity, a finite-volume hyperbolic structure on a 3-manifold, if it exists, is essentially unique: the fundamental group alone determines the geometry. Consequently geometric invariants such as volume are topological invariants. Finite-volume hyperbolic 3-manifolds have ends that are either cusps (homeomorphic to a torus times a ray, with a complete Euclidean metric on the torus) or geometrically finite flaring ends.

Examples

The complement of the figure-eight knot in the 3-sphere is a hyperbolic 3-manifold of finite volume, built from two ideal tetrahedra; its volume is about 2.02988. More generally, most knot and link complements are hyperbolic. Closed examples can be obtained by Dehn surgery on cusped manifolds: Thurston's hyperbolic Dehn surgery theorem says that all but finitely many surgeries on each cusp yield hyperbolic manifolds, whose volumes approach that of the original from below.

The smallest volumes

The Weeks manifold, obtained by Dehn surgery on the Whitehead link, is the closed hyperbolic 3-manifold of smallest known volume, about 0.9427, and is conjectured to be the minimum. The census of small hyperbolic manifolds, enumerated by programs such as SnapPea, is an active computational resource in the field.
