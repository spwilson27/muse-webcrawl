Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems

Frontispiece of the 1632 edition, showing Aristotle, Ptolemy, and Copernicus.

The Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems (Italian: Dialogo sopra i due massimi sistemi del mondo) is a 1632 book by Galileo Galilei comparing the Copernican heliocentric system with the traditional Ptolemaic geocentric system. Written in Italian rather than Latin so as to reach a broad audience, it became one of the most influential — and controversial — books of the Scientific Revolution.

Structure and characters

The book is cast as a conversation over four days among three characters: Salviati, a Florentine gentleman who argues Galileo's own Copernican views; Sagredo, an intelligent layman who moderates; and Simplicio, a dogged defender of Aristotelian and Ptolemaic astronomy whose name carried an unflattering double meaning in Italian. The dialogues range over the motion of the Earth, sunspots, the phases of Venus, the moons of Jupiter, and Galileo's (erroneous) theory that the tides prove the Earth's motion.

Publication and trial

Galileo obtained permission to publish on condition that he present both systems impartially and include the Church's preferred argument — that God could have made the world in any way, so human reasoning cannot constrain Him. Pope Urban VIII believed that argument, placed at the end of the book in the mouth of Simplicio, made him look foolish. In 1633 Galileo was tried by the Inquisition, the book was banned and placed on the Index of Forbidden Books, and Galileo spent the rest of his life under house arrest.

Legacy

The Dialogue remained on the Index until 1835. It is celebrated as a masterpiece of scientific prose and persuasive argument, and the Galileo affair surrounding it became the emblematic episode in debates about science and religious authority.
