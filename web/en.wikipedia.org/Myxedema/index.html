Myxedema


Labelled image: periorbital puffiness in myxedema

Myxedema (American English; also spelled myxoedema) is a term generally used to denote severe hypothyroidism. More specifically, it describes the characteristic skin and tissue changes caused by inadequate thyroid hormone: the deposition of mucopolysaccharides (glycosaminoglycans such as hyaluronic acid) in the dermis, producing a waxy, non-pitting swelling of the skin. The term was coined by William Miller Ord in 1877.

Signs and symptoms

Features include puffiness of the face and eyelids, swelling of the lips and tongue, dry and thickened skin, hair loss including the outer third of the eyebrows, cognitive slowing, fatigue, weight gain, cold intolerance, and a deep, husky voice. In its most dangerous form, myxedema coma (myxedema crisis) is a life-threatening decompensation, often triggered by infection, cold exposure, or sedatives, presenting with hypothermia, hypoventilation, hyponatremia and depressed consciousness, with substantial mortality even with treatment.

Causes and related conditions

The usual cause is long-standing untreated hypothyroidism, most commonly from Hashimoto's thyroiditis. A distinct form, pretibial myxedema (thyroid dermopathy), occurs paradoxically in Graves' disease — a hyperthyroid condition — where antibody-driven glycosaminoglycan deposition produces thickened plaques on the shins.

Treatment

Treatment of ordinary myxedema is thyroid hormone replacement, typically with levothyroxine, which gradually reverses the tissue changes. Myxedema coma is a medical emergency requiring intravenous thyroid hormone (levothyroxine and sometimes liothyronine), supportive care, warming, and glucocorticoids until adrenal insufficiency is excluded.
