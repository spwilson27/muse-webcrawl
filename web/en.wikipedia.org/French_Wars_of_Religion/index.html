French Wars of Religion

The French Wars of Religion were a series of civil wars between French Catholics and Protestants (called Huguenots) from 1562 to 1598. Between two and four million people died from violence, famine or disease directly caused by the conflict, and it severely damaged the power of the French monarchy. One of its most notorious episodes was the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre in 1572. The fighting ended with a compromise in 1598, when Henry of Navarre, who had converted to Catholicism and been crowned Henry IV, issued the Edict of Nantes, granting substantial rights and freedoms to the Huguenots.

Background

The Reformation spread into France in the early sixteenth century, and despite persecution under Francis I and Henry II, Calvinist congregations grew rapidly, drawing support from parts of the nobility including the House of Bourbon and the House of Coligny. Against them stood the ultra-Catholic House of Guise. The death of Henry II in 1559 left a succession of young or weak kings — Francis II, Charles IX, and Henry III — under the dominating influence of their mother, Catherine de' Medici, whose attempts at compromise repeatedly collapsed into violence.

The wars

The wars are conventionally divided into a series of conflicts punctuated by short-lived peace edicts. The massacre of Huguenots at Vassy in March 1562 by troops of the Duke of Guise triggered the first war. Subsequent wars in 1567–68, 1568–70, 1572–73, 1574–76, 1576–77, 1579–80, and 1585–98 saw fighting across the kingdom, foreign intervention by England, Spain, and German Protestant princes, and repeated attempts by the crown to impose settlements such as the Peace of Saint-Germain-en-Laye (1570).

The St. Bartholomew's Day massacre, beginning on 24 August 1572, saw the assassination of Admiral Gaspard de Coligny and thousands of Huguenots in Paris and the provinces. The final phase, the War of the Three Henrys (1585–1589), pitted Henry III against Henry of Guise and Henry of Navarre; both the king and Guise were assassinated, leaving the Protestant Navarre heir to the throne.

Conclusion

Henry IV's conversion to Catholicism in 1593 — famously summarised as "Paris is well worth a Mass" — opened the way to his coronation at Chartres in 1594. The Edict of Nantes (1598) granted Huguenots freedom of conscience, limited freedom of worship, and fortified places of security, while the Treaty of Vervins ended Spanish intervention. The settlement endured until Louis XIV revoked the edict in 1685.
