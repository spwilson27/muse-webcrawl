Steelmaking is the process of producing steel from iron ore and/or scrap. Steel is an alloy of iron and carbon (up to about 2%), whose properties can be tuned by composition and heat treatment; it is the world's most important engineering material, with global crude-steel production around 1.9 billion tonnes per year.

Processes

In the integrated route, iron ore is reduced with coke in a blast furnace to molten pig iron (hot metal). In the basic oxygen process (BOF/LD converter), pure oxygen is blown onto the hot metal, oxidising excess carbon, silicon and phosphorus into a slag; scrap is added as coolant. About 70% of world steel is made this way. In the electric arc furnace (EAF) route, recycled scrap (and/or direct-reduced iron) is melted by a powerful electric arc — the dominant method in the United States and for special steels. Secondary ("ladle") metallurgy then refines composition, and the steel is continuously cast into slabs, blooms and billets for rolling.

History

Early steel was made in small quantities by carburising wrought iron (cementation) or in crucibles. The Bessemer converter (1856) made steel cheap and mass-produced; the Siemens–Martin open-hearth furnace followed, then the basic oxygen process (1952, Linz–Donawitz) and continuous casting transformed productivity in the 20th century.

Environment

Steelmaking accounts for roughly 7–9% of global CO₂ emissions, mainly from coke-based reduction. Decarbonisation routes include hydrogen-based direct reduction, greater scrap-EAF recycling, and carbon capture — "green steel" projects are under way especially in Europe.
