The 1995 Quebec referendum was the second referendum to ask voters in Quebec whether the province should proclaim sovereignty and seek to become an independent country. Held on 30 October 1995, it produced the closest result in Canadian referendum history: the sovereigntist option was defeated by 50.58 percent to 49.42 percent, a difference of just 54,288 votes, on a turnout of 93.5 percent.

Background

After the failure of the Meech Lake and Charlottetown constitutional accords, support for sovereignty rose sharply. The Parti Québécois under Jacques Parizeau won the 1994 provincial election and promised a referendum within a year. The question asked whether Quebec should become sovereign after making a formal offer of economic and political partnership to the rest of Canada.

Campaign

The Yes campaign was invigorated when Lucien Bouchard, leader of the Bloc Québécois, took a leading role in its final weeks, pushing Yes support above 50 percent in some polls. The federalist No campaign, formally headed by provincial Liberal leader Daniel Johnson Jr., responded with a massive "Unity Rally" in Montreal on 27 October, drawing an estimated 100,000 or more supporters from across Canada. Prime Minister Jean Chrétien made televised appeals promising recognition of Quebec as a distinct society.

Result and aftermath

On referendum night Parizeau conceded and, in a bitter speech, blamed "money and ethnic votes" for the defeat — remarks for which he apologised and which ended his premiership; he resigned the next day and was succeeded by Bouchard. In response to the near-loss, the federal government asked the Supreme Court to rule on the legality of unilateral secession; the Court's 1998 Secession Reference held that Quebec could not secede unilaterally but that a clear majority on a clear question would oblige negotiations. Parliament then passed the Clarity Act in 2000. Disputes over No-side campaign spending, investigated by the Grenier report, continued for years afterwards.

The referendum remains the high-water mark of the Quebec sovereignty movement and a defining moment in modern Canadian history; see also the 1980 Quebec referendum.
