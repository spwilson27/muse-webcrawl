The House of Tudor was a royal house of Welsh origin that held the English throne from 1485 until 1603. Its five monarchs — Henry VII, Henry VIII, Edward VI, Mary I and Elizabeth I — presided over the end of the Wars of the Roses, the English Reformation, and England's emergence as a major European and maritime power. The dynasty's emblem is the Tudor rose, uniting the red rose of Lancaster with the white rose of York.

Origins and accession

The family's fortunes were founded by Owen Tudor, a Welsh courtier who secretly married Catherine of Valois, widow of Henry V. Their son Edmund Tudor married Margaret Beaufort, a descendant of Edward III, and their son Henry Tudor defeated Richard III at the Battle of Bosworth Field on 22 August 1485, ending the Wars of the Roses. Henry VII consolidated his claim by marrying Elizabeth of York and strengthened royal finances and administration.

The Tudor monarchs

  • Henry VII (r. 1485–1509) — restored stability and a solvent crown after decades of civil war.
  • Henry VIII (r. 1509–1547) — broke with Rome and established the Church of England, largely in pursuit of an annulment and a male heir; dissolved the monasteries.
  • Edward VI (r. 1547–1553) — a minor whose reign advanced Protestant reform under regents.
  • Mary I (r. 1553–1558) — attempted to restore Roman Catholicism and married Philip II of Spain; the succession crisis of Lady Jane Grey preceded her accession.
  • Elizabeth I (r. 1558–1603) — established a durable religious settlement, defeated the Spanish Armada in 1588, and presided over a flowering of English culture.

End of the dynasty

Elizabeth died unmarried and childless in 1603, and the crown passed peacefully to James VI of Scotland, of the House of Stuart, a descendant of Henry VII's daughter Margaret Tudor. The Tudor period is conventionally seen as a turning point between medieval and early modern England: the era of the Reformation, the growth of Parliament and bureaucracy, overseas exploration, and the drama of Shakespeare and his contemporaries.
