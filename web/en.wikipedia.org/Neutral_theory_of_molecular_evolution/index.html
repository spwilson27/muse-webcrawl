Neutral theory of molecular evolution

The neutral theory of molecular evolution holds that most evolutionary changes at the molecular level, and most of the variation within and between species, are caused not by natural selection but by the random genetic drift of mutant alleles that are selectively neutral — that is, mutations that have no effect on fitness. The theory was proposed by Motoo Kimura in 1968 and independently by Jack Lester King and Thomas H. Jukes in 1969.

Under the neutral theory, a small minority of mutations are advantageous and swept to fixation by selection, a substantial fraction are deleterious and removed by purifying selection, but the bulk of molecular substitutions observed when comparing species are neutral changes fixed by drift. A key prediction is that the rate of molecular evolution (substitutions per site per year) should be roughly constant — a molecular clock — equal to the neutral mutation rate, independent of population size.

Evidence and debate

The theory was motivated by unexpectedly high levels of protein polymorphism found by early electrophoresis studies, and by the roughly clock-like accumulation of amino-acid differences. It also explains observations such as the faster evolution of pseudogenes and of less functionally constrained protein regions. The neutral theory sparked the "neutralist–selectionist debate" of the 1970s–80s. Tomoko Ohta's nearly neutral theory (1973) extended the idea to mutations with very small effects, whose fate depends on effective population size. Modern consensus regards neutral drift as the null model of molecular evolution while recognising widespread roles for both purifying and positive selection.
