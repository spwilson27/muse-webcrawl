Raymond Fernand Loewy (5 November 1893 – 14 July 1986) was a French-American industrial designer often called "the father of industrial design." Across a career spanning five decades he shaped the look of mid-century America — locomotives, automobiles, refrigerators, cigarette packs, corporate logos and even spacecraft interiors — and appeared on the cover of Time magazine in 1949.

Career

After serving as a French officer in the First World War, Loewy emigrated to New York in 1919, working first as a fashion illustrator. His breakthrough came in 1929 with the streamlined Gestetner duplicating machine. For Sears, his Coldspot refrigerator (1934) dramatically lifted sales and established the annual-model styling cycle. He streamlined the Pennsylvania Railroad's GG1 electric locomotive and S1 steam locomotive, designed the Lucky Strike cigarette pack's familiar target motif (1940), and for Studebaker produced the 1947 "coming-or-going" Starlight coupe, the 1953 Starliner and the Avanti (1962).

Later work and philosophy

Loewy's firm created enduring logos for Shell, BP, Exxon and the United States Postal Service, the blue-and-white livery of Air Force One, and habitability concepts for NASA's Skylab space station. His MAYA principle — "Most Advanced Yet Acceptable" — held that consumers are drawn to novelty only up to the threshold of unfamiliarity, a rule he set out in his autobiography Never Leave Well Enough Alone (1951). He retired to France and died in Monte Carlo in 1986.
