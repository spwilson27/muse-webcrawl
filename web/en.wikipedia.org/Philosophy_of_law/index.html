Philosophy of law

Philosophy of law, also known as legal philosophy or jurisprudence, is the branch of philosophy that investigates the nature of law and law's relationship to other systems of norms, especially ethics and political philosophy. It asks questions like "What is law?", "What are the criteria for legal validity?", "What is the relationship between law and morality?", "What is the authority of law?", and "What is the right of a person to be punished?".

Traditionally, jurisprudence has been divided into analytical jurisprudence, which studies the essential features of law and legal systems, and normative jurisprudence, which examines what law ought to be. Major schools include natural law theory, legal positivism, legal realism, legal interpretivism, and critical legal studies.

Analytical jurisprudence

Natural law

Natural law theory holds that law is inherently connected to morality: laws that are unjust fail, in some strong sense, to be true laws. Ancient roots include Aristotle and Cicero; medieval development came with Thomas Aquinas, who held that an unjust law is a corruption of law. Modern natural lawyers such as John Finnis and Lon Fuller have defended versions of the view, Fuller emphasizing an "inner morality of law" consisting of procedural principles such as generality, publicity, and non-retroactivity.

Legal positivism

Legal positivism holds that the existence and content of law depend on social facts and not on its merits. Jeremy Bentham and John Austin developed early positivist accounts based on commands of a sovereign. H. L. A. Hart's The Concept of Law (1961) refined positivism by describing law as a union of primary rules of obligation and secondary rules of recognition, change, and adjudication. Joseph Raz defended an "exclusive" positivism grounded in law's claim to legitimate authority and the sources thesis.

Legal interpretivism

Ronald Dworkin criticized positivism and proposed interpretivism: propositions of law are true in virtue of the best constructive interpretation of a community's legal practice, which he called "law as integrity". Judges, on Dworkin's account, should decide hard cases by identifying the interpretation that best fits and justifies the legal record as a whole.

Legal realism

Legal realists, such as Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. and Karl Llewellyn, emphasized the role of judges, social context, and the indeterminacy of legal rules, urging attention to what officials actually do rather than abstract doctrine.

Normative jurisprudence

Normative jurisprudence addresses questions about the aims of law, including theories of justice, rights, punishment, and the obligation to obey the law. Theories of punishment include retributivism, deterrence, incapacitation, and rehabilitation. Debates about civil disobedience, from Plato's Crito to Martin Luther King Jr.'s "Letter from Birmingham Jail", ask when, if ever, citizens may justifiably break the law.

Philosophy of specific areas of law

Subfields examine the foundations of criminal law, contract law, tort law, property law, and constitutional law, as well as international law and questions about the rule of law.
