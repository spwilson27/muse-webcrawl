A video game console is a computer system designed primarily for playing video games, outputting a video signal to a television or monitor. Unlike general-purpose personal computers, consoles use standardised hardware sold by a platform holder, who licences and certifies games for the system. Consoles are generally divided into home consoles, handheld consoles with built-in screens, and hybrid systems such as the Nintendo Switch.

History

The industry began with the Magnavox Odyssey (1972), designed by Ralph Baer, and was popularised by Atari's home version of Pong (1975) and the cartridge-based Atari 2600 (1977). After the North American video game crash of 1983, Nintendo revived the market with the Nintendo Entertainment System (1985), introducing strict licensing. Successive "generations" brought the Sega–Nintendo rivalry of the 16-bit era, Sony's PlayStation (1994) and its CD-based format, the record-selling PlayStation 2 (2000), Microsoft's Xbox (2001) with online play via Xbox Live, and Nintendo's motion-controlled Wii (2006). The current generation features the PlayStation 5 and Xbox Series X/S (both 2020) alongside the Switch (2017) and its successor.

Business model

Consoles are frequently sold at or below manufacturing cost, with profits taken from game licensing fees, first-party software, and subscription services such as PlayStation Plus, Xbox Game Pass and Nintendo Switch Online. Digital storefronts have largely replaced physical media distribution, raising questions about game preservation.

Hardware

Modern consoles are built around custom system-on-a-chip designs (typically AMD CPU/GPU combinations in the PlayStation and Xbox lines), solid-state storage, and controllers descended from the gamepad standard established by the NES and refined with analogue sticks, triggers, haptics and motion sensing.
