Remote sensing

Remote sensing is the acquisition of information about an object or phenomenon without making physical contact with it, most commonly the observation of the Earth's surface and atmosphere from aircraft and satellites. Remote sensing detects reflected or emitted electromagnetic radiation — visible light, infrared, microwaves — and converts it into images and measurements used across the environmental sciences, agriculture, forestry, geology, archaeology, and defence.

Methods

Passive sensors record natural radiation: multispectral scanners exploit characteristic spectral signatures (healthy vegetation, for instance, reflects strongly in the near-infrared) to classify land cover. Active sensors emit their own energy: radar systems such as synthetic-aperture radar image in all weather and measure surface deformation by interferometry, while lidar times pulses of laser light to build precise elevation and canopy models. Data are characterized by spatial, spectral, temporal, and radiometric resolution.

Applications and history

Aerial photography from balloons and pigeons in the nineteenth century, aircraft reconnaissance in the World Wars, and the first weather and Landsat satellites opened the modern era. Today Earth observation satellites, including small CubeSat constellations, together with drones and geographic information systems support crop forecasting, deforestation and ice-sheet monitoring, urban mapping, disaster assessment, and climate records.
