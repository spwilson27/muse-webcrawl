Plainsong

Plainsong, or plainchant, is the traditional monophonic, unaccompanied sacred song of the Western Christian Church. Its melodies move in free rhythm, guided by the prose rhythm of the Latin text, and its tones are arranged according to the eight modes. The Latin name, cantus planus, contrasts it with measured, polyphonic "figured" music.

Repertories

The largest plainsong repertory is Gregorian chant, sung at Mass and in the monastic Divine Office. Other regional traditions survived alongside it: Ambrosian (Milan), Mozarabic (Visigothic Spain), Beneventan (southern Italy), and the chant of the Sarum use in medieval England, which influenced later Anglican chant practice.

Notation and performance

Medieval manuscripts notated plainsong with neumes — small signs indicating rising, falling, or repeated pitches — later aligned on a staff of four lines with a movable C or F clef. In modern editions the chant is written in square notation. Performances range from the rhythmic interpretations of the Solesmes school, based on nineteenth-century manuscript research, to equal-note and mensuralist approaches.

Legacy

Plainsong supplied the raw material for the birth of Western polyphony: organum, the motet, and countless Renaissance Mass settings are built over chant melodies. The chant heritage was also revived by twentieth-century religious communities and reached popular audiences through bestselling chant recordings in the 1990s.
