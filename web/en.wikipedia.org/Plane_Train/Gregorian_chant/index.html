Gregorian chant

Gregorian chant is the central tradition of Western plainchant, a form of monophonic, unaccompanied sacred song sung in Latin in the Roman Catholic Church. Named by tradition after Pope Gregory I (r. 590–604) — legendarily said to have received the melodies from the Holy Spirit in the form of a dove — the repertory was in fact compiled mainly in the eighth and ninth centuries, when Frankish cantors blended Roman chant with Gallican practice under Carolingian patronage.

Repertory and structure

The chant serves both the Mass (the unchanging Ordinary — Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus, Agnus Dei — and the seasonally changing Proper of introits, graduals, alleluias, offertories, and communions) and the Divine Office, where psalms are framed by antiphons. Melodies are classified by the eight church modes and range in style from syllabic (one note per syllable) through neumatic to the florid melismas of graduals and alleluias.

Notation and legacy

Chant was first written with unheighted neumes in the ninth century; staff notation and square note-heads followed, and the semiological restoration by the monks of Solesmes in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries shaped modern editions such as the Graduale Romanum. Gregorian chant stands behind much of Western music theory — modes, notation, and early polyphony all grew from it — and remains the Church's model of sacred music.
