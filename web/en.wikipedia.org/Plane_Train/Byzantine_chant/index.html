Byzantine chant

Byzantine chant is the sacred chant tradition of the Eastern Orthodox Church and the Greek-speaking Christian East, developed in the Byzantine Empire from late antiquity and preserved today in the liturgies of the Greek Orthodox and other Eastern churches. It is monophonic, sung unaccompanied (often over a sustained drone, the ison), and organized in the eight tones of the oktōēchos.

History

The tradition grew from early Christian psalmody and hymnody, took shape in Constantinople and the monasteries of Palestine and Mount Athos, and was enriched by hymnographers such as Romanos the Melodist, John of Damascus, and Kassia. Composers of the later Middle Ages, above all John Koukouzeles, expanded its melismatic ("kalophonic") style. After 1453 the tradition continued under Ottoman rule; in 1814–1821 the "Three Teachers" (Chrysanthos of Madytos, Gregorios the Protopsaltes, and Chourmouzios the Archivist) reformed its notation into the New Method still in use.

Characteristics

Byzantine notation is intervallic and mnemonic: signs indicate melodic movement from the previous note rather than absolute pitch, so performance depends on oral transmission. The repertory fills liturgical books such as the Heirmologion, Sticherarion, and Papadike, serving the services of the Divine Office and the Divine Liturgy.
