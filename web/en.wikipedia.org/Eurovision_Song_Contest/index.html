The Eurovision Song Contest is an international song competition organised annually by the European Broadcasting Union (EBU), featuring participants representing predominantly European countries. Each participating country submits an original song to be performed live, after which all countries cast votes — through national juries and public televoting — to determine the winner. It is one of the world's longest-running television programmes and one of the most watched non-sporting events, drawing audiences of well over 160 million.

History

The first contest was held in Lugano, Switzerland, on 24 May 1956, with seven countries competing; it was won by the host country's Lys Assia with "Refrain." Inspired partly by Italy's Sanremo Music Festival, the contest was conceived as an experiment in transnational live broadcasting. It grew through the decades as participation widened to countries across and beyond Europe, including Israel (from 1973), Turkey, and, since 2015, Australia as invited participants. The 2020 contest was cancelled due to the COVID-19 pandemic — the only cancellation in its history.

Famous acts and winners

Eurovision launched or boosted several major recording careers: ABBA won for Sweden in 1974 with "Waterloo," Céline Dion won for Switzerland in 1988, and Italian rock band Måneskin (2021 winners) achieved global success afterwards. Ireland and Sweden share the record of seven victories; Ireland's Johnny Logan is the only performer to win twice (1980 and 1987). Other celebrated winners include Conchita Wurst (Austria, 2014), Salvador Sobral (Portugal, 2017), and Loreen of Sweden, who won twice (2012 and 2023).

Format and rules

Today's contest consists of two semi-finals and a grand final, hosted — by tradition — by the previous year's winning country ("winner takes all" hosting). Songs must be original and no longer than three minutes; performing artists must be at least 16 on the day of the final. Since 2016 the jury and public votes have been presented separately, doubling the drama of the scoreboard. Voting patterns long notorious for neighbourly and diaspora blocs remain a perennial topic of discussion, as does the contest's intersection with politics and LGBTQ+ culture.
