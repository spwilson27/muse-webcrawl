Waterfall model

[Image placeholder: waterfall model diagram]

The waterfall model is a breakdown of development activities into linear sequential phases, where each phase depends on the deliverables of the previous one and corresponds to a specialization of tasks. Originating in manufacturing and construction, it was adapted to software development and is often contrasted with iterative and agile methodologies. The term "waterfall" describes the way progress flows steadily downwards through the phases like a waterfall.

Phases

A typical formulation, following Winston W. Royce's influential 1970 paper on managing large software systems, includes:

  1. Requirements analysis and definition — what the system must do.
  2. System and software design — architecture and detailed design.
  3. Implementation and unit testing — coding and verifying components.
  4. Integration and system testing — assembling and validating the whole.
  5. Operation and maintenance — deployment, support, and correction.

Ironically, Royce presented the sequential model to argue that projects must allow feedback between phases, and he is often wrongly credited as the waterfall model's advocate; the name itself came later, in a 1976 paper by Bell and Thayer. The model was formalized in standards such as the U.S. Department of Defense's DOD-STD-2167.

Advantages and criticism

Advocates value the waterfall model's clear structure, documentation, defined milestones, and suitability for projects with fixed, well-understood requirements, such as safety-critical systems. Critics argue that real projects rarely follow a purely sequential flow: requirements change, errors surface late and are expensive to fix, and working software appears only near the end of the process. These concerns motivated iterative models, the spiral model, and agile software development, which emphasize incremental delivery and adaptation. Modified waterfall variants allow overlap and feedback between phases.
