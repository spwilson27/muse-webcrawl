Flattery

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Flattery is the act of giving excessive or insincere praise, generally for the purpose of ingratiating oneself with the recipient. It is a strategy of impression management and social influence: the flatterer compliments another's appearance, abilities, possessions, or achievements, typically with an ulterior motive such as gaining favour, employment, protection, or advantage.

History and analysis

Flattery has been analysed since antiquity. Plutarch wrote an essay, "How to Tell a Flatterer from a Friend," warning that the flatterer imitates friendship while serving only himself. Theophrastus included the flatterer among his Characters. In Christian moral theology, flattery was treated as a sin opposed to fraternal correction; Aquinas distinguished praising someone to encourage good (acceptable) from praising to please or to deceive (sinful). Courts of monarchs were famously populated by flatterers, and the figure of the courtier in works by Castiglione and Shakespeare illustrates both the rewards and the moral dangers of the practice.

Psychology

Research in social psychology suggests that flattery is often effective even when the recipient suspects it is insincere: compliments tend to produce positive feelings toward the flatterer through automatic affective responses. Marketing studies show that flattery from salespeople can improve attitudes even when consumers consciously discount it. Flattery directed upward at superiors is a common workplace behaviour, sometimes studied under the heading of ingratiation.

In culture

The fable tradition repeatedly punishes flattery's victims — in Aesop's "The Fox and the Crow," the crow drops its cheese when flattered into singing. The proverb "Flattery will get you nowhere" is, empirically, often false, and its ironic reversal "Flattery will get you everywhere" circulates widely.
