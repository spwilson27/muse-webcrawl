M-theory

M-theory is a theory in theoretical physics that unifies all consistent versions of superstring theory. It was proposed in 1995 by Edward Witten, who suggested that the five known superstring theories — Type I, Type IIA, Type IIB, and the two heterotic string theories — are actually different limiting cases of a single eleven-dimensional theory.

Background

Before 1995, string theorists had found five separate supersymmetric string theories, each consistent but apparently distinct, and each formulated in ten spacetime dimensions. A web of relationships called dualities — including S-duality and T-duality — hinted that the theories were connected. Witten's insight was that at strong coupling, Type IIA string theory grows an eleventh dimension and becomes a theory of membranes, and that the strongly coupled heterotic E8×E8 theory can likewise be described in eleven dimensions.

Key features

  • The fundamental objects are not only one-dimensional strings but also membranes (2-branes) and higher-dimensional branes, including the M5-brane.
  • Its low-energy limit is eleven-dimensional supergravity, a theory first constructed in 1978.
  • The "M" has been variously interpreted as standing for "membrane", "matrix", "mother", "monster", or "mystery"; Witten later remarked that the choice could be left to the beholder.

Status

A complete, non-perturbative formulation of M-theory is not yet known. Proposals such as the BFSS matrix model — describing M-theory in flat spacetime as a matrix quantum mechanics of D0-branes — and the AdS/CFT correspondence give partial definitions in specific backgrounds. M-theory remains a central focus of research into quantum gravity.
