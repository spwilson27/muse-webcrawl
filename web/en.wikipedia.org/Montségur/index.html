Montségur

Montségur is a commune in the Ariège department in southwestern France, in the foothills of the Pyrenees. It is famous for the ruined fortress that crowns the steep rocky peak (the pog) above the village, at an altitude of about 1,200 metres. Montségur was the last stronghold of the Cathars, and its fall in 1244, followed by the burning of some 200 Cathar parfaits who refused to renounce their faith, is one of the defining episodes of the Albigensian Crusade.

History

A fortress existed on the pog from early times — its name means "mount secure". Around 1204, Raymond de Péreille rebuilt the castle at the request of the Cathar church, and Montségur became the seat and refuge of the Cathar hierarchy, home to a community of several hundred, including perfects, believers, and a small garrison.

The siege of 1243–44

After Cathars based at Montségur murdered inquisitors at Avignonet in 1242, royal forces under Hugues des Arcis besieged the castle beginning in May 1243. After ten months, and the capture of key defensive positions, the defenders negotiated a surrender in March 1244. Those who would not abjure Catharism — around 200 men and women — were burned on a pyre at the foot of the mountain, in the field still called the Prat dels Cremats ("field of the burned").

The ruins of Montségur fortress on the pog, rebuilt after 1244 in the form seen today.

The fortress today

The present ruins largely date from the fortress rebuilt by the victorious Lévis family after the siege. The site is a major destination for visitors interested in Cathar history and has accumulated legends, including associations with the Holy Grail.
