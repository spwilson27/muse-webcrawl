Pan-Arabism

Pan-Arabism (Arabic: العروبة) is an ideology that advocates the political unification of all Arab countries into a single state. Closely related to Arab nationalism, it holds that the Arabs form one nation bound by language, culture, and history, and that the borders drawn by colonial powers should be overcome.

History

Pan-Arab ideas circulated among Arab intellectuals in the late Ottoman period and gained momentum after the First World War, when the mandates system divided the Arab East. The Ba'ath Party, founded in the 1940s, made unity the first term of its slogan. Pan-Arabism surged in the 1950s under Egyptian president Gamal Abdel Nasser, whose popularity after the 1956 Suez Crisis made him the movement's leading figure.

Attempts at unity

The most substantial union was the United Arab Republic, which merged Egypt and Syria between 1958 and 1961 before collapsing amid Syrian resentment of Egyptian domination. Other short-lived projects included the Arab Federation of Iraq and Jordan (1958), the Federation of Arab Republics (1972–1977) linking Egypt, Libya, and Syria on paper, and repeated unity talks between the rival Ba'athist regimes of Syria and Iraq that foundered on mutual hostility. The Arab League, founded in 1945, coordinated member states but never approached political union.

Decline

The Arab defeat in the 1967 Six-Day War, the death of Nasser in 1970, Egypt's separate peace with Israel in 1979, and the 1990–91 Gulf crisis—when Arab states joined a coalition against Iraq—progressively hollowed out pan-Arabism as a practical program, though its themes continue to resonate in Arab public opinion.
