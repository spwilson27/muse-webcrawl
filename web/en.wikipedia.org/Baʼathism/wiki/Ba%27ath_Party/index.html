Ba'ath Party

The Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party (Arabic: حزب البعث العربي الاشتراكي, romanized: Ḥizb al-Baʿth al-ʿArabī al-Ishtirākī) was a political party founded in Syria in 1947 by Michel Aflaq, Salah al-Din al-Bitar, and Zaki al-Arsuzi. Its ideology, Ba'athism, combined Arab nationalism, pan-Arabism, and Arab socialism under the slogan "Unity, Freedom, Socialism." Branches were established across the Arab world, and the party ruled Syria from 1963 to 2024 and Iraq from 1968 to 2003.

History

The party's founding congress met in Damascus in April 1947. In 1952 it merged with Akram al-Hawrani's Arab Socialist Party. During the United Arab Republic period (1958–1961), the Syrian party was dissolved under pressure from Nasser, a decision its military supporters later regretted. Ba'athist officers seized power in Iraq in February 1963 (losing it within the year) and in Syria in March 1963.

Split

In 1966 a coup in Damascus by radical officers ousted the party's original leadership, including Aflaq and al-Bitar, splitting the movement into two rival parties: a Syrian-dominated Ba'ath and an Iraqi-dominated Ba'ath, each with its own "National Command" claiming legitimacy. The Iraqi branch retook power in 1968 and ruled until the 2003 invasion toppled Saddam Hussein. The Syrian branch ruled under Hafez al-Assad and then Bashar al-Assad until rebel forces overthrew the regime in December 2024.

Organization

The party was organized hierarchically, with cells, sections, branches, and regional commands in each country, topped by a National Command intended to span the whole Arab nation. In power, both branches built one-party states fused with the military and security services, with mass organizations for workers, peasants, students, and women.
