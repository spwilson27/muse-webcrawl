Arab nationalism

Arab nationalism (Arabic: القومية العربية, romanized: al-qawmīya al-ʿarabīya) is a political ideology that asserts that Arabs constitute a single nation and promotes the cultural and political unity of Arab peoples. It emerged in the late 19th and early 20th centuries in the Arab provinces of the Ottoman Empire, partly in reaction to Turkish nationalist policies, and became a dominant force in Middle Eastern politics in the mid-20th century.

Origins

Arab nationalist thought grew out of the Nahda, the Arab cultural renaissance of the 19th century, which revived interest in Arabic language and literature. Intellectuals such as Butrus al-Bustani and secret societies in Beirut and Damascus began articulating an Arab identity distinct from the Ottoman state. During the First World War, the Arab Revolt of 1916–1918, encouraged by the Hussein–McMahon Correspondence, sought independence from Ottoman rule.

Interwar period and the mandate era

After the war, the Arab lands were divided into British and French mandates, thwarting hopes for a unified Arab state. Thinkers like Sati' al-Husri argued that language and shared history, rather than religion, defined the Arab nation. In the 1940s Michel Aflaq and Salah al-Din al-Bitar founded the Arab Ba'ath movement, which fused Arab nationalism with socialism and the slogan "Unity, Freedom, Socialism."

Height and decline

Arab nationalism reached its zenith under Egypt's Gamal Abdel Nasser, whose pan-Arabism inspired the short-lived United Arab Republic (1958–1961) uniting Egypt and Syria. Ba'athist regimes took power in Syria and Iraq in the 1960s, though the two branches became bitter rivals. The Arab defeat in the Six-Day War of 1967 badly damaged the ideology's prestige, and from the 1970s it was increasingly eclipsed by Islamism and state-centered nationalisms.
