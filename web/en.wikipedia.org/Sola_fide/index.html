Sola fide

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Martin Luther, whose theology centred on justification by faith alone

Sola fide, meaning justification by faith alone, is a Christian theological doctrine held by most Protestant traditions, particularly the Lutheran and Reformed traditions, which teach that believers are forgiven of their sins and justified before God only through faith in Jesus Christ, apart from works of the law.

History

The doctrine became a defining issue of the Protestant Reformation. Martin Luther, drawing especially on Paul's letters to the Romans and Galatians, argued that human beings cannot merit salvation and are declared righteous by God solely through faith, the righteousness of Christ being imputed to them. Luther called it the article on which the Church stands or falls. The Council of Trent (1545–1563) rejected the doctrine as formulated by the Reformers, affirming instead that faith must be formed by charity and that good works cooperate in justification.

Relation to the other solae

Sola fide is one of the five solae of the Reformation, alongside sola scriptura, sola gratia, solus Christus, and soli Deo gloria. It is closely tied to sola gratia: salvation is by grace alone, received through faith alone.

Modern discussion

The 1999 Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification between the Lutheran World Federation and the Catholic Church stated a consensus on basic truths of justification, though differences in interpretation remain.
