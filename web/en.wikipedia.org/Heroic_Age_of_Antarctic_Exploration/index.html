Heroic Age of Antarctic Exploration

Era of exploration of Antarctica, c. 1897–1922, ending with the death of Ernest Shackleton.

The Heroic Age of Antarctic Exploration was an era in the exploration of the continent of Antarctica which began at the end of the 19th century and ended with the death of Ernest Shackleton in 1922. During this period the Antarctic continent became the focus of international efforts that resulted in intensive scientific and geographical exploration by seventeen major Antarctic expeditions launched from ten countries. The common factor in these expeditions was the limited nature of the resources they had at their disposal, before advances in transport and communication made the work of exploration easier; each was a feat of endurance by a small party, and several ended in disaster and death.

Milestones

The era opened with the Belgian Antarctic Expedition (1897–99) aboard Belgica, the first expedition to winter in the Antarctic, and Carsten Borchgrevink's Southern Cross Expedition, which wintered at Cape Adare and sledged to a furthest south of 78°50′S. Robert Falcon Scott's Discovery Expedition (1901–04) and Ernest Shackleton's Nimrod Expedition (1907–09) pushed the furthest south record ever closer to the Pole; Shackleton's party reached 88°23′S, 97 nautical miles from the Pole.

The South Pole was finally attained by Roald Amundsen's Norwegian party on 14 December 1911, beating Scott's Terra Nova expedition, whose five-man polar party reached the Pole on 17–18 January 1912 and perished on the return journey. Douglas Mawson's Australasian Antarctic Expedition (1911–14) survived one of the most extreme solo journeys in exploration history. The era closed with Shackleton's Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition (1914–17)—the loss of Endurance, the open-boat journey to South Georgia and the rescue of all hands—and with Shackleton's death aboard Quest at Grytviken on 5 January 1922.

Legacy

The expeditions made major contributions to Antarctic geography, meteorology, oceanography, biology and geomagnetism, and their narratives—Scott's journals, Apsley Cherry-Garrard's The Worst Journey in the World (1922), Shackleton's South—became classics of exploration literature. Historic huts at Cape Evans, Hut Point and Cape Royds survive as protected monuments under the Antarctic Treaty System.
