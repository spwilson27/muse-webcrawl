Insolvency

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In accounting, insolvency is the state of being unable to pay debts when they fall due. Two tests are commonly applied: the cash-flow test (inability to pay debts as they mature) and the balance-sheet test (liabilities exceeding assets). Insolvency may lead to formal insolvency proceedings — bankruptcy for individuals and liquidation or administration for companies — in which the debtor's assets are distributed among creditors under the supervision of a court or licensed insolvency practitioner.

Causes

Insolvency can result from poor cash management, declining sales, excessive leverage, fraud, or external shocks. A business may be profitable on paper yet insolvent if its assets cannot be converted to cash quickly enough.

Procedures

Legal frameworks vary by jurisdiction. In the United States, Chapter 7 provides for liquidation and Chapter 11 for reorganisation of businesses. In the United Kingdom, the Insolvency Act 1986 governs liquidation, administration, and company voluntary arrangements, while individuals may use bankruptcy or individual voluntary arrangements. Modern reforms increasingly emphasise rescue culture — preserving viable businesses rather than closing them.

Consequences

Insolvency triggers default provisions, may bar directors found guilty of wrongful trading, damages credit ratings for years, and can allow creditors to appoint receivers or administrators. For sovereign states, which cannot be liquidated, insolvency leads instead to debt restructuring and negotiation with creditors.
