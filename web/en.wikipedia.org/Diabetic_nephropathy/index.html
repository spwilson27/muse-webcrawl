Diabetic nephropathy

Diabetic nephropathy, also called diabetic kidney disease, is the chronic loss of kidney function occurring in people with diabetes mellitus. It is the leading cause of end-stage kidney disease in many countries. Damage to the glomeruli — the kidney's filtering capillaries — causes increasing amounts of albumin to leak into the urine and a progressive decline in glomerular filtration rate (GFR).

Glomerular changes in diabetic nephropathy include basement-membrane thickening and mesangial expansion.

Pathophysiology

Chronic hyperglycaemia drives thickening of the glomerular basement membrane, mesangial expansion, podocyte injury, and eventually glomerulosclerosis, classically with Kimmelstiel–Wilson nodules. Hyperfiltration in early disease raises intraglomerular pressure, accelerated by activation of the renin–angiotensin system; hypertension and dyslipidaemia add further injury.

Stages

The classic Mogensen stages progress from hyperfiltration, through silent disease, to incipient nephropathy marked by microalbuminuria (moderately increased albuminuria), then overt proteinuria with declining GFR, and finally kidney failure. Modern KDIGO classification stages chronic kidney disease by GFR category and albuminuria category together.

Treatment

Tight glycaemic control, blood-pressure control, and blockade of the renin–angiotensin system with ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers slow progression. SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 receptor agonists provide additional kidney and cardiovascular protection. Advanced disease is treated with dialysis or kidney transplantation.
