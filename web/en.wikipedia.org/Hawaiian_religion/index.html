Hawaiian religion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hawaiian religion refers to the traditional religious beliefs and practices of Native Hawaiians. It is a polytheistic animistic religion centred on the akua (gods and spirits) and ʻaumākua (deified ancestors), and on maintaining right relationships (pono) among people, the gods, and the land (ʻāina).

Deities

Four major gods stand at the head of the pantheon: Kāne, creator and god of the sky and fresh water; Kū, god of war and of the forest; Lono, god of rain, agriculture, and peace, celebrated in the annual Makahiki festival; and Kanaloa, god of the ocean and the underworld. Other important deities include Pele, the volcano goddess of Kīlauea; Kamapuaʻa, the hog-man demigod; and the trickster demigod Māui, who is credited in legend with fishing the islands up from the sea.

The kapu system

Society was governed by the kapu system, a set of sacred laws and prohibitions regulating everything from eating (men and women ate separately; women were forbidden certain foods) to contact with chiefs. Temples called heiau were built for worship and, in the case of the luakini heiau, human sacrifice. Priests (kāhuna) mediated between gods and people. In 1819, shortly after the death of Kamehameha I, the kapu system was abolished in the ʻAi Noa ("free eating") by Kamehameha II, his mother Keōpūolani, and the regent Kaʻahumanu — just before the arrival of Christian missionaries in 1820.

Modern revival

Suppressed in the nineteenth century, Hawaiian religious and cultural practices have been revived since the Hawaiian Renaissance of the 1970s, including hula, chant (mele), and ceremonies at heiau and on Mauna Kea and Kīlauea, where practitioners continue to honour Pele. Practitioners today seek legal protection for sacred sites and traditional access under Native Hawaiian religious rights.
