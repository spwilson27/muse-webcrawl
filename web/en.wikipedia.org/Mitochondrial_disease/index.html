Mitochondrial disease

Mitochondrial disease
SpecialtyMedical genetics, neurology
CausesMutations in mitochondrial or nuclear DNA
InheritanceOften maternal (matrilineal)

Mitochondrial diseases are a group of genetic disorders caused by mutations in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) or in nuclear DNA encoding mitochondrial proteins, which impair the ability of mitochondria to produce energy through oxidative phosphorylation. Because mitochondria are present in nearly all cells, symptoms can affect many organ systems, especially energy-hungry tissues such as the brain, muscles, heart, and eyes.

Examples

Well-characterised mitochondrial diseases include Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy (LHON), which causes sudden vision loss in young adults; MELAS (mitochondrial encephalomyopathy, lactic acidosis, and stroke-like episodes); MERRF (myoclonic epilepsy with ragged red fibres); Leigh syndrome; and Kearns–Sayre syndrome. Muscle biopsies in some of these conditions show characteristic ragged-red fibres.

Inheritance and heteroplasmy

mtDNA is inherited maternally. Cells contain many copies of mtDNA, and a mixture of normal and mutant copies — heteroplasmy — is common; disease severity often depends on the proportion of mutant DNA and the threshold requirements of each tissue. Nuclear-gene mitochondrial diseases follow ordinary Mendelian inheritance.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis combines clinical features, blood and cerebrospinal fluid lactate measurement, imaging, muscle biopsy, and genetic sequencing of the mitochondrial genome. There is no cure for most mitochondrial diseases; management is supportive and may include exercise, treatment of complications such as diabetes and seizures, and avoidance of certain drugs (e.g., valproate in some conditions). Mitochondrial donation techniques were developed to prevent transmission of mtDNA disease.
