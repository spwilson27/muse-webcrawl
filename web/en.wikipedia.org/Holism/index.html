Holism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Holism is the interdisciplinary idea that systems (physical, biological, chemical, social, economic, mental, linguistic) and their properties should be viewed as wholes, not just as a collection of parts. This often includes the view that systems somehow function as wholes and that their functioning cannot be fully understood solely in terms of their component parts. The term was coined by South African statesman and scholar Jan Smuts in his 1926 book Holism and Evolution.

Reductionism is often viewed as the opposite of holism. Reductionism in science says that a complex system can be explained by reduction to its fundamental parts. Social scientist and physician Nicholas A. Christakis notes that "for the last few centuries, the Cartesian project in science has been to break matter down into ever smaller pieces, in pursuit of understanding. And this works, to some extent, but putting things back together in order to understand them is harder, and typically comes later in the development of a scientist or in the development of a science."

History

The word holism derives from the Ancient Greek holos (ὅλος), meaning "all, whole, entire." Jan Smuts introduced the term in 1926, using it to describe a tendency in nature to produce wholes from the ordered grouping of unit structures. Smuts argued that holism was a fundamental factor in evolution, driving the universe toward ever more complex and integrated wholes.

Ideas related to holism long predate the term. Aristotle summarised the idea with the famous phrase "the whole is more than the sum of its parts," and similar concepts appear in the philosophies of Spinoza, Hegel, and in Gestalt psychology, whose slogan — associated with Kurt Koffka — held that "the whole is other than the sum of the parts."

Applications

Holistic approaches appear in many fields. In medicine, holistic care treats the whole person — body, mind, spirit, and emotions — rather than an isolated set of symptoms. In ecology, ecosystems are studied as integrated wholes whose emergent properties cannot be predicted from species lists alone. In physics, holism has been invoked in discussions of quantum entanglement, where the state of a composite system cannot always be described by the states of its parts. In the social sciences, methodological holism holds that social phenomena must be explained by reference to social structures and institutions, a position associated with Émile Durkheim.

Criticism

Critics argue that holism is vague and can serve as a label rather than an explanation. Defenders of reductionist methods point to the enormous success of analytical science, while holists counter that emergence — the appearance of novel properties at higher levels of organisation — makes purely reductive accounts incomplete in principle.
