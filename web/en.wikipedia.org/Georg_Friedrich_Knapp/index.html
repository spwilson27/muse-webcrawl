Georg Friedrich Knapp (7 March 1842 – 20 February 1926) was a German economist and statistician, best known for founding the state theory of money, or chartalism, in his book Staatliche Theorie des Geldes (The State Theory of Money, 1905).

Life and career

Knapp studied in Munich, Berlin, and Göttingen, and directed the statistical office of Leipzig before becoming a professor at the University of Strasbourg, where he taught for several decades. He was associated with the younger German historical school of economics. His earlier scholarship included important work on agricultural history, notably on the emancipation of the Prussian peasantry, and on demography and mortality statistics.

The State Theory of Money

Knapp argued that money is fundamentally a creature of law: a currency is money because the state declares what it will accept in payment of taxes and other obligations, not because the material of coins or notes has intrinsic value. "The money of a state," he wrote, "is what is accepted at the public pay offices." The theory — for which John Maynard Keynes expressed qualified admiration in his Treatise on Money — is an intellectual ancestor of modern monetary theory (MMT) and remains influential in heterodox economics, in opposition to metallist and commodity theories of money.
