Randomness

In common usage, randomness is the apparent or actual lack of definite pattern or predictability in information. A random sequence of events, symbols or steps often has no order and does not follow an intelligible pattern or combination. Individual random events are, by definition, unpredictable, but if the probability distribution is known, the frequency of different outcomes over repeated events can be predicted — as with dice throws or coin flips.

History

Randomness was long associated with divination, gambling and fate. Games of chance drove the mathematical study of probability in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries through the work of Cardano, Pascal and Fermat. In the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, statistics formalised random variables and distributions, while physics debated whether nature is fundamentally random — a question sharpened by quantum mechanics, where measurement outcomes are held to be intrinsically probabilistic. Religious and philosophical traditions have variously treated chance as illusion, divine plan, or a real feature of the world.

In science and mathematics

Mathematical definitions of randomness include statistical randomness (passing frequency and correlation tests) and algorithmic randomness, developed by Kolmogorov, Chaitin and Martin-Löf, in which a string is random if it cannot be generated by any shorter program. Random processes underpin statistical inference, Monte Carlo methods, randomised algorithms, cryptography and the generation of random numbers — either pseudo-randomly by deterministic generators or from physical entropy sources.

Applications and perception

Randomness is used in sampling, clinical trials, lotteries, procedural generation and noise in art and music. Humans are poor intuitive judges of randomness, often seeing patterns in random data (the clustering illusion) or expecting alternation too frequently (the gambler's fallacy), which psychology studies under heuristics and biases.
