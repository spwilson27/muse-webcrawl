Castile (historical region)

Castile (Spanish: Castilla) is a historical region of Spain, roughly corresponding to the central plateau known as the Meseta Central. Its name derives from the many castles (castillos) built along its frontier with Muslim al-Andalus during the early Middle Ages. Traditionally divided into Old Castile (Castilla la Vieja) in the north and New Castile (Castilla la Nueva) in the south, the region became one of the principal Christian kingdoms of the Iberian Peninsula.

History

The County of Castile emerged in the 9th century as a frontier march of the Kingdom of León. It became an independent kingdom in 1035 under Ferdinand I, and in 1230 Ferdinand III permanently united the crowns of Castile and León. Under rulers such as Alfonso X and the Catholic Monarchs, the Crown of Castile drove the Reconquista to its conclusion with the conquest of Granada in 1492, and sponsored the voyages of Christopher Columbus that opened the Americas to Spanish colonisation. Isabela I of Castile and Ferdinand II of Aragon laid the foundations of the modern Spanish state.

Geography

Castile occupies the high central plateau of Iberia, bounded by mountain ranges including the Cantabrian Mountains, the Sistema Ibérico and Sierra Morena. The Duero, Tagus and Guadiana rivers drain its two sub-plateaus. Today its territory is divided mainly among the autonomous communities of Castile and León, Castile-La Mancha, Madrid and parts of surrounding regions.

Language and culture

The Castilian dialect of Spanish (castellano) originated here and became the standard language of Spain and the Spanish-speaking world. The region is associated with figures such as El Cid and with the literary setting of Cervantes' Don Quixote in La Mancha.
