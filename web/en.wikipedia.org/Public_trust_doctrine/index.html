Public trust doctrine

The public trust doctrine is the principle that the sovereign holds in trust certain natural resources — traditionally navigable waters, submerged lands and tidelands — for public use, and that these resources cannot be alienated in a way that deprives the public of their use. The doctrine has roots in Roman law, English common law and the Institutes of Justinian.

United States

In the United States, the doctrine was prominently articulated in Illinois Central Railroad Co. v. Illinois (1892), in which the Supreme Court held that the state held title to lands under navigable waters in trust for the people and could not abdicate control of them. States have applied the doctrine to navigation, commerce, fishing and increasingly to recreation and environmental protection, as in California's Mono Lake case (National Audubon Society v. Superior Court, 1983).

Other jurisdictions

Versions of the doctrine have been recognized in India, South Africa, Kenya, the Philippines and other countries, often as a basis for environmental rights and state duties toward natural resources.
