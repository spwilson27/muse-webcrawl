Deafblindness

Deafblindness is the condition of having significant combined loss of both vision and hearing, such that the two impairments together create distinctive challenges in communication, mobility, and access to information. Most deafblind people are not completely deaf and completely blind; the condition covers a spectrum, from mild dual sensory loss to total deafness combined with total blindness.

Causes

Deafblindness may be congenital or acquired. The commonest cause of congenital deafblindness historically was rubella infection during pregnancy; other congenital causes include CHARGE syndrome and complications of prematurity. The leading cause in children born deaf is Usher syndrome, in which retinitis pigmentosa progressively destroys vision. Acquired deafblindness is most common in older adults, where it results from the combination of age-related hearing loss and conditions such as macular degeneration, cataracts, or glaucoma; illness, injury, and meningitis can also cause it.

Communication

Communication methods depend on the person's residual senses and the age at which the impairments developed. They include:

  • Tactile sign language and the deafblind manual alphabet (fingerspelling into the hand)
  • Print on palm and Braille, including refreshable braille displays
  • Tadoma, in which the listener touches the speaker's lips and throat
  • Speech, lipreading, hearing aids, and cochlear implants where residual function allows

Notable people

The most famous deafblind person was Helen Keller (1880–1968), who lost her sight and hearing in infancy and, taught by Anne Sullivan, became an author and lecturer. Earlier pioneers of deafblind education include Laura Bridgman, the first deafblind person in the United States to be formally educated. Notable people with Usher syndrome include the artist and campaigner Haben Girma, the first deafblind graduate of Harvard Law School.

Support

In several countries, deafblind people are entitled to interpreter-guide (communicator-guide) services. Organisations such as Sense (UK), the Helen Keller National Center (US), and Deafblind International advocate for recognition of deafblindness as a distinct disability rather than the sum of two separate impairments.
