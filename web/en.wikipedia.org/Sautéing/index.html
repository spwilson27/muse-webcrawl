Sautéing

Sautéing is a cooking method that uses a relatively small amount of oil or fat in a shallow pan over relatively high heat. The word comes from the French sauter, "to jump", referring to the tossing of food in the pan so that it browns evenly without steaming.

Food tossed in a hot sauté pan

Technique

Ingredients are usually cut into pieces or thin slices to facilitate fast, even cooking. The pan and fat are heated first; food is added in a single layer without crowding, and is tossed or turned frequently. Browning comes from the Maillard reaction and caramelisation, producing flavours that distinguish sautéing from boiling or steaming. A wide, low-sided pan called a sauteuse or sauté pan is preferred, since its sloped or straight sides allow tossing and rapid evaporation.

Related methods

Sautéing is related to stir-frying, which uses a wok and typically even higher heat, and to pan-frying, which uses more fat and less movement. After sautéing, the browned residues in the pan (fond) are often deglazed with wine or stock to form the basis of a sauce.
