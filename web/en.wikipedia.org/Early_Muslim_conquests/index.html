Early Muslim conquests

The early Muslim conquests, also known as the Arab conquests, were a series of campaigns in the 7th and 8th centuries that brought a vast territory — from the Iberian Peninsula in the west to the Indus Valley and Central Asia in the east — under the rule of the caliphate. Beginning under the prophet Muhammad and continuing under the Rashidun and Umayyad caliphs, they permanently reshaped the political and cultural map of the Middle East, North Africa and beyond.

Background

Arabia was unified under Islam by Muhammad's death in 632. The Ridda Wars under the first caliph, Abu Bakr, consolidated Muslim control of the peninsula. Both great powers to the north, the Byzantine Empire and Sasanian Persia, had been weakened by a long war (602–628) and by plague, leaving their frontier provinces exposed.

Conquest of Persia and the Levant

Under Caliph Umar (634–644), Muslim armies won decisive victories at Yarmouk (636) against the Byzantines and at al-Qadisiyyah (636) against the Sasanians. Syria, Egypt and Iraq fell within a few years; the Sasanian Empire collapsed entirely by 651 with the death of Yazdegerd III. Jerusalem surrendered in 637 or 638, and Alexandria in 642.

Expansion under the Umayyads

After a period of civil war, the Umayyad Caliphate (661–750) resumed expansion. North Africa was subdued by the early 8th century; in 711 forces under Tariq ibn Ziyad crossed into Iberia, destroying the Visigothic kingdom, while in the east armies reached Transoxiana and Sindh. The advance in the west was halted in Francia at the Battle of Tours (732), and two great sieges of Constantinople (674–678 and 717–718) failed.

Administration and society

The conquerors were initially a small military elite ruling largely Christian, Jewish and Zoroastrian populations. Non-Muslims paid the jizya tax and retained communal autonomy; conversion to Islam was gradual over centuries. Arabic became the language of administration under Abd al-Malik, who also built the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem and reformed the coinage.

Legacy

The conquests created the framework of the medieval Islamic world, spread the Arabic language from Iraq to Spain, and established trade and scholarly networks that transmitted learning between Europe, Africa and Asia.
