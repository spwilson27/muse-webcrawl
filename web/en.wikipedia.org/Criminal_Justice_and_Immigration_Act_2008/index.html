Criminal Justice and Immigration Act 2008

Criminal Justice and Immigration Act 2008
Parliament of the United Kingdom
Citation: 2008 c. 4
Royal assent: 8 May 2008

The Criminal Justice and Immigration Act 2008 (c. 4) is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom. It is a wide-ranging statute covering criminal law, sentencing, anti-social behaviour, immigration, and other matters. Royal assent was received on 8 May 2008, and its provisions came into force at various dates thereafter.

The Act was passed by the UK Parliament at Westminster

Provisions

Among its many provisions, the Act reformed the law on self-defence, restating the common law on the use of reasonable force; amended the arrangements for appeals and for the Criminal Cases Review Commission; introduced changes to sentencing, including provisions about the release of prisoners; created offences concerning extreme pornographic images ("extreme pornography"); and made various changes to immigration law, including a "special immigration status" for certain foreign criminals who could not be deported because of human rights obligations.

Incitement to hatred

During its passage, the Act became the vehicle for an amendment, proposed by Lord Waddington, intended to protect free speech in relation to the new offence of incitement to hatred on grounds of sexual orientation. A modified version of the amendment was included in the Act, providing that discussion or criticism of sexual conduct or practices, or urging persons to refrain from or modify such conduct or practices, shall not be taken of itself to be threatening or intended to stir up hatred. Successive attempts in later legislation to remove the provision failed.

Blasphemy

The Act also abolished the common law offences of blasphemy and blasphemous libel in England and Wales, following a campaign by parliamentarians and public figures who argued that the offences were obsolete and discriminatory in a multi-faith society.

Other measures

Other provisions included a new offence of causing a nuisance or disturbance on National Health Service premises, reforms relating to youth justice and anti-social behaviour orders, the introduction of immigration status controls, and powers relating to the designation of areas for protests around Parliament.
