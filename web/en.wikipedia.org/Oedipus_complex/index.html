In classical psychoanalytic theory, the Oedipus complex is a child's supposed unconscious desire for the parent of the opposite sex and a sense of rivalry with the parent of the same sex. Sigmund Freud introduced the concept in The Interpretation of Dreams (1899) and developed it as the centrepiece of his theory of psychosexual development, locating it in the phallic stage, roughly between the ages of three and five. He named it after the Greek legend of Oedipus, who unknowingly killed his father and married his mother.

Freud's account

Freud held that the boy desires his mother and wishes to displace his father, while fearing castration by the father as punishment. Successful resolution — through repression of the desire and identification with the father — was, in Freud's theory, the origin of the superego and of adult sexual identity. For girls Freud proposed a more complicated path beginning with "penis envy", a formulation later singled out for criticism; Carl Jung's suggestion of a parallel "Electra complex" was rejected by Freud.

Criticism

The Oedipus complex has been criticised from within and outside psychoanalysis. Anthropologist Bronisław Malinowski argued from his Trobriand Islands fieldwork that the complex was not universal but shaped by family structure. Feminist writers criticised its phallocentrism. Modern empirical psychology generally regards the concept as unfalsifiable and unsupported by experimental evidence, though it remains influential in literary criticism and cultural theory, where Sophocles' Oedipus Rex continues to be read through it.
