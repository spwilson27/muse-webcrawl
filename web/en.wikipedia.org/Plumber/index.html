Plumber

A plumber at work on domestic pipework

A plumber is a tradesperson who specialises in installing and maintaining systems used for potable (drinking) water, hot-water heating, drainage and sewage in buildings. The word derives from the Latin plumbum for lead, the material of water pipes in Roman times; a worker in lead was a plumbarius. Modern plumbers also install and service gas lines, water heaters, boilers, sanitary fixtures (sinks, toilets, baths and showers) and appliances such as dishwashers and washing machines.

History

Plumbing originated during ancient civilisations. The Indus Valley civilisation used earthenware pipes by around 2700 BC, Egyptian palaces had copper piping, and the Romans built extensive systems of lead pipes and aqueducts. The trade in its modern form emerged in the 19th century as cities built water supply and sewerage networks: cast-iron soil pipes, flush toilets and trapped drainage required skilled installation, and public-health legislation made sound plumbing a legal requirement in new buildings. Lead piping was progressively abandoned during the 20th century as its toxicity to drinking water became clear.

Training and licensing

In most jurisdictions plumbers train through an apprenticeship combining classroom instruction with supervised work, typically lasting four to five years, progressing from apprentice to journeyman to master plumber. Many countries and states license plumbers to protect public health, since cross-connections between drinking water and sewage can spread disease. Plumbers must know building codes, blueprint reading, mathematics for pipe sizing and gradients, and safe handling of materials from copper and PVC/PEX plastics to cast iron and steel. Common tools include pipe wrenches, tube cutters, soldering and brazing torches, press-fit tools, drain augers and inspection cameras.

Work and hazards

Plumbing work ranges from emergency repairs — burst pipes, leaks, blocked drains — to installing complete systems in new construction and renovation. It is among the better-paid skilled trades, with persistent labour shortages reported in many countries. Occupational hazards include exposure to sewage-borne pathogens, asbestos in old pipe lagging, lead, mould, burns from soldering, and injuries from working in confined spaces and at heights. Plumbers may also require separate certification for gas fitting and backflow prevention.
