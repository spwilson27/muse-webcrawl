A New Account of the Tales of the World is an English rendering of tales from Liaozhai zhiyi (Strange Tales from a Chinese Studio), the celebrated collection of nearly five hundred short stories by the Qing-dynasty writer Pu Songling (1640–1715). The phrase echoes an older Chinese classic, Shishuo Xinyu ("A New Account of the Tales of the World", 5th century), a compilation of anecdotes about historical figures compiled under Liu Yiqing — a title that has also been applied in scholarship to translations of Pu's supernatural tales, connecting his work to that earlier tradition of anecdotal literature.

Pu Songling's collection

Pu Songling, a Shandong schoolteacher who repeatedly failed the imperial civil service examinations, compiled the Liaozhai zhiyi over much of his life. The stories — written in elegant Classical Chinese and drawing on the zhiguai ("records of the strange") and chuanqi traditions — feature fox spirits, ghosts, flower fairies, dreams, corrupt officials and examination candidates. The collection was first printed in 1766 and became one of the most beloved works of Chinese fiction.

Themes and style

Beneath their supernatural surfaces, the tales frequently satirise corruption, praise steadfast love and scholarship, and extend sympathy to marginal figures. Fox-spirit heroines such as Yingning and Nie Xiaoqian are among the most famous characters in Chinese literature, and the stories have been adapted innumerable times for film, television and opera, including the Hong Kong film A Chinese Ghost Story (1987).

English translation

The first substantial English translation was Herbert A. Giles's Strange Stories from a Chinese Studio (1880), which presented just over 160 tales in a polished Victorian style. Later translations, including those of John Minford and Denis and Victor Mair's selections, have restored more of Pu's range and completeness for English readers.
