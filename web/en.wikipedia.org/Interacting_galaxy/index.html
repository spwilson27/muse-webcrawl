Interacting galaxies
PhenomenonGravitational interaction between galaxies
EffectsTidal tails, distorted shapes, starbursts, mergers
Famous examplesAntennae Galaxies, Whirlpool Galaxy, Mice Galaxies

Interacting galaxies (also called colliding galaxies) are galaxies whose gravitational fields disturb one another as they pass close by or merge. Such interactions are a major force in galactic evolution: they can distort spiral structure, draw out long tidal tails and bridges of stars and gas, trigger intense bursts of star formation, and ultimately merge the galaxies into a single, often elliptical, system.

Process

When two galaxies approach, tidal forces stretch them; material is pulled into tails and bridges, and gas clouds collide and compress, igniting starbursts. If the galaxies are gravitationally bound, they may pass through or around each other several times, losing energy to dynamical friction until they coalesce. The whole process can take billions of years. Individual stars almost never collide, because of the vast distances between them, even when galaxies merge.

Examples

The Antennae Galaxies (NGC 4038/4039) are a merging pair with long tidal tails and vigorous star formation. The Whirlpool Galaxy (M51) interacts with its smaller companion NGC 5195. Catalogues by Halton Arp and by Vorontsov-Velyaminov collected many such peculiar systems.

Future of the Milky Way

The Milky Way and the Andromeda Galaxy are approaching each other and are expected to interact and merge in roughly 4–5 billion years, forming a combined galaxy sometimes nicknamed "Milkomeda".
