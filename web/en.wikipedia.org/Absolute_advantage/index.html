Absolute advantage

In economics, the principle of absolute advantage is the ability of a party (an individual, firm, region or country) to produce a good or service more efficiently than its competitors: with a given quantity of inputs (such as labour hours), the party with absolute advantage can produce a greater quantity of output, or can produce the same quantity using fewer inputs. The concept was developed by Adam Smith in The Wealth of Nations (1776) as part of his argument against mercantilism and in favour of international trade and the division of labour.

Smith's argument

Smith argued that if one country can produce a good more cheaply than another, it is in both countries' interest for the first to specialise in that good and trade for the goods in which the other has the advantage — just as a tailor does not make his own shoes but buys them from a shoemaker. Trade based on absolute advantage increases total output and wealth for both parties, contradicting the mercantilist view of trade as a zero-sum contest over bullion.

Example

Suppose Country A can produce 10 units of cloth or 20 of wine per worker-day, while Country B can produce 4 units of cloth or 8 of wine. Country A has an absolute advantage in both goods. Smith's principle alone would suggest no basis for trade between them — a gap resolved by David Ricardo's theory of comparative advantage (1817), which showed that trade benefits both sides whenever their relative efficiencies differ, even if one party is absolutely more efficient at everything.

Relation to comparative advantage

Absolute advantage compares productivity across parties for the same good; comparative advantage compares opportunity costs within each party across goods. Modern theories of international trade therefore rest mainly on comparative advantage, although absolute advantages in technology, resources and productivity still matter empirically and feature in later trade models.
