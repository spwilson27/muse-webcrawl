Milling (machining)

Milling is the process of machining using rotary cutters to remove material by advancing a cutter into a workpiece. It is one of the most versatile of all machining processes: a milling machine can produce flat surfaces, slots, gears, threads, and complex three-dimensional contours. The rotating tool — the milling cutter — usually carries multiple cutting edges, and the workpiece is moved relative to it on a table that feeds in several axes. Most modern milling is performed on computer numerical control (CNC) machining centres.

A vertical milling machine cutting metal, with coolant applied to the cut.

Process basics

In milling, each tooth of the cutter takes an intermittent cut, producing chips whose thickness varies through the cut. Two basic modes are distinguished: peripheral (slab) milling, where the cutter axis is parallel to the surface, and face milling, where the cutter axis is perpendicular to it. When the cutter rotation pulls the workpiece against the table motion it is called climb (down) milling, generally preferred on rigid CNC machines; the opposite is conventional (up) milling. Key parameters are cutting speed, feed per tooth, axial and radial depth of cut, all chosen according to the workpiece material, cutter material (high-speed steel, carbide, ceramics or diamond coatings) and desired surface finish.

Machine types

  • Vertical mills (including the ubiquitous Bridgeport-style knee mill) have a spindle perpendicular to the table.
  • Horizontal mills mount cutters on an arbor parallel to the table, suited to heavy slab milling and gear cutting.
  • CNC machining centres add automatic tool changers and three to five (or more) controlled axes, enabling complete machining of prismatic and sculptured parts in one setup.
  • Bed mills, gantry mills and plano-mills handle very large workpieces such as die blocks and machine beds.

Cutters and operations

Common cutters include end mills (for profiling and pocketing), face mills, ball-nose cutters for 3D contours, slab mills, side-and-face cutters, slitting saws, form cutters and thread mills. Typical operations are face milling, end milling, chamfering, drilling (using the spindle), tapping, boring and gear cutting with a dividing head. Coolant or compressed air clears chips and controls heat; high-speed machining and trochoidal toolpaths are used for hardened steels and aerospace alloys such as titanium and Inconel.

History

Rotary filing preceded true milling; the first recognisable milling machines appeared in the early 19th century in the armories of New England — Eli Whitney and Simeon North are among those associated with their adoption for interchangeable gun parts. Joseph Brown's universal milling machine of 1861 added a spiral-flute cutter and dividing head. Frederick W. Howe, Cincinnati Milling Machine Company and others industrialised the type, and the advent of numerical control in the 1950s, then CNC in the 1970s, made milling the backbone of modern manufacturing.
