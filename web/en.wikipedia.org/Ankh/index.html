Ankh

The ankh is an ancient Egyptian hieroglyphic symbol representing the word for "life" (ꜥnḫ) and, by extension, life itself. It is shaped like a cross with a loop at the top, and is also known as the crux ansata ("cross with a handle" in Latin). The ankh appears frequently in Egyptian art, where gods are shown holding it to the nose or mouth of a pharaoh, conferring the breath of life.

Origin and meaning

The symbol's origin is uncertain. Proposed derivations include a sandal strap, a knot of cloth or reeds, and anatomical or sexual symbolism; Egyptologist Alan Gardiner suggested the sandal strap, whose name was also pronounced ꜥnḫ. In texts the ankh formed part of formulaic blessings such as "life, stability, and dominion" and "may he live, be prosperous, be healthy".

Use in art and religion

Ankhs were made as amulets, mirrors, and decorative motifs in gold, faience, wood, and stone. Deities including Isis, Osiris, Anubis, and Sekhmet carry the ankh; in tomb paintings the deceased receives it as a promise of life after death. The symbol was adopted by Egyptian Christians (Copts) in late antiquity, blending with the Christian cross.

Modern use

The ankh remains a popular cultural symbol, used in jewellery, neo-pagan and Kemetic religious practice, Afrocentric iconography, and popular media, often signifying life, vitality, or African heritage. It appears in the Unicode standard as U+2625 (☥).
