Hemiola

In music, hemiola (from the Greek hemiolios, "one and a half") is the ratio 3:2, applied in two contexts. In rhythm, it is the superimposition or alternation of two groups of three beats with three groups of two beats — for example, six eighth notes accented as 3+3 instead of 2+2+2, or vice versa. In pitch, the ratio 3:2 describes the interval of the perfect fifth.

Rhythmic hemiola

The rhythmic hemiola is especially characteristic of the Baroque era, where it frequently appears at cadences in triple-time dances such as the courante and sarabande, temporarily implying duple metre before the final chord. Earlier, in Renaissance mensural music, sesquialtera and hemiola (including "blackened" notation) were systematic devices for shifting accent. A horizontal hemiola unfolds the 3:2 grouping successively in time, while a vertical hemiola sounds the two groupings simultaneously — a simple polyrhythm.

Composers from Handel and Bach to Brahms (a particularly devoted user) employed hemiola for rhythmic tension. Leonard Bernstein's "America" from West Side Story is a famous twentieth-century example, alternating 6/8 and 3/4 bars. Three-against-two patterns are also fundamental in much West African and Afro-Cuban music, where the six-beat bell pattern is inherently hemiolic.

Intervallic usage

In ancient Greek theory and Pythagorean tuning, the hemiolic ratio 3:2 yields the perfect fifth, one of the primary consonances; the corresponding term for 2:1 (octave) is diplasion and for 4:3 (fourth) epitritos.
