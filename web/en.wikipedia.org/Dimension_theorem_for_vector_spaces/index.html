Dimension theorem for vector spaces

A basis of a three-dimensional vector space

In mathematics, the dimension theorem for vector spaces states that all bases of a vector space have equally many elements. This number of elements is called the dimension of the vector space. If the vector space is finite-dimensional, the theorem can be derived from the first isomorphism theorem together with an exchange argument.

Statement

If V is a vector space over a field F, then any two bases of V have the same cardinality. Consequently the dimension dimF(V), defined as the cardinality of any basis, is well defined. For finite-dimensional spaces the dimension is a natural number; infinite-dimensional spaces have dimensions that are infinite cardinals.

Proof ideas

The standard proof for the finite case uses the Steinitz exchange lemma: if {v1, …, vm} is linearly independent and {w1, …, wn} spans V, then m ≤ n, and after reordering, some of the w's can be exchanged for the v's while preserving the spanning property. Applying this in both directions to two bases shows they have the same size. The infinite case extends the argument using cardinal arithmetic.

Consequences

The theorem underlies the notion that Rn has dimension n, that isomorphic finite-dimensional spaces are exactly those of equal dimension, and the rank–nullity theorem for linear maps. It fails for modules over general rings, where a module may have bases of different cardinalities.
