Ultra-high-definition television

Resolution comparison of UHDTV formats with earlier television standards.

Ultra-high-definition television (also known as Ultra HD television, Ultra HD, UHDTV, UHD, and Super Hi-Vision) today includes 4K UHD and 8K UHD, which are two digital video formats that were first proposed by NHK Science & Technology Research Laboratories. They were then defined and approved by the International Telecommunication Union (ITU) in the Recommendation ITU-R BT.2020.

The Consumer Electronics Association announced on October 17, 2012, that "Ultra High Definition", or "Ultra HD", would be used for displays with an aspect ratio of 16:9 or wider and a minimum native resolution of 3840 × 2160 pixels. UHD has since become the dominant standard for consumer television sets, computer monitors, and streaming video.

Formats

  • 4K UHD (2160p) — a resolution of 3840 × 2160 pixels, four times the pixel count of 1080p Full HD. The related cinema standard, DCI 4K, uses 4096 × 2160.
  • 8K UHD (4320p) — a resolution of 7680 × 4320 pixels, sixteen times the pixel count of 1080p. 8K broadcasts began in Japan, where NHK launched a satellite 8K channel in 2018.

Beyond resolution, the UHD ecosystem includes wider colour gamuts (the Rec. 2020 colour space), high dynamic range (HDR) formats such as HDR10 and Dolby Vision, higher frame rates of up to 120 frames per second in the standard, and object-based audio.

History

NHK researchers in Japan began work on what became Super Hi-Vision in the 1990s, demonstrating an 8K system publicly in the 2000s and broadcasting experimental footage of the 2012 London Olympics. Meanwhile, the first commercial 4K displays appeared around 2012–2013, and prices fell rapidly through the 2010s. Streaming services such as Netflix and YouTube began offering 4K content in 2014, and the Ultra HD Blu-ray format was launched in 2016. By the end of the decade, 4K sets accounted for the majority of televisions sold in major markets.

Delivery and standards

UHD content is delivered over broadcast (DVB, ATSC 3.0), cable, satellite, and internet streaming, typically compressed with the HEVC (H.265) or VP9 codecs, and later AV1, because uncompressed 4K video requires very high bit rates. Consumer connections use HDMI 2.0 or later for 4K at 60 Hz. Standards bodies involved include the ITU, the Consumer Technology Association, and the UHD Alliance, an industry group that defines certification for "UHD Premium" devices.

Adoption and criticism

At typical viewing distances, the sharpness advantage of 4K over 1080p is most apparent on large screens, while the visible benefits of 8K on consumer-sized displays are debated. Many viewers report that HDR and wide colour produce a more noticeable improvement than resolution alone. Nonetheless, UHD resolution has become a default feature of new televisions, and production of films and television programmes in 4K is now routine.
