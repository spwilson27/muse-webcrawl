Corundum is a crystalline form of aluminium oxide (Al2O3) and one of the hardest naturally occurring minerals, rating 9 on the Mohs scale — second only to diamond among common minerals. Transparent gem-quality varieties are among the world's most prized gemstones: red corundum is ruby, and all other colours, most famously blue, are sapphire.

Properties and occurrence

Corundum crystallises in the trigonal system, typically forming barrel-shaped or tabular crystals. Its colours arise from trace impurities: chromium produces the red of ruby, while iron and titanium produce blue sapphire; other impurities give yellow, pink, green, and orange ("padparadscha") stones. The mineral occurs in metamorphic rocks such as gneiss and marble and in silica-poor igneous rocks, and is concentrated in placer deposits. Important sources have included Myanmar (Mogok), Sri Lanka, Thailand, Vietnam, Madagascar, and Montana in the United States.

Uses

Beyond gemstones, corundum's hardness makes it valuable industrially. The impure granular variety emery — corundum mixed with magnetite and other minerals — has long been used as an abrasive, and manufactured fused alumina is now the standard abrasive and refractory material. Synthetic corundum, produced by the Verneuil flame-fusion process since the early twentieth century, supplies inexpensive rubies and sapphires for jewellery, watch bearings, and the first laser (the ruby laser of 1960). Sapphire glass, a single-crystal form, is used for scratch-resistant watch crystals, optical windows, and electronic substrates.

Name

The word "corundum" derives, via Tamil kuruntam, from Sanskrit kuruvinda, a name for the ruby.
