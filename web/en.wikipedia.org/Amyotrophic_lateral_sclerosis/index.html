Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), also known as motor neurone disease (MND) or Lou Gehrig's disease, is a rare, terminal neurodegenerative disorder that results in the progressive loss of both upper and lower motor neurons that normally control voluntary muscle contraction. ALS is the most common form of motor neurone disease.

SymptomsProgressive muscle weakness, stiffness, fasciculations, slurred speech, difficulty swallowing
Usual onsetAges 50–70
CausesUsually unknown; about 10% inherited
TreatmentSupportive care; riluzole, edaravone modestly extend survival
PrognosisLife expectancy typically 2–4 years from onset

Signs and symptoms

ALS usually begins with painless weakness in a limb, or with slurred speech and difficulty swallowing in bulbar-onset cases. Weakness spreads to involve most voluntary muscles; fasciculations, spasticity, and eventually respiratory failure follow. Sensation, eye movements, and bladder function are typically spared, while a substantial minority of patients develop frontotemporal dementia.

Causes and genetics

Most cases are sporadic with no known cause. Around 10% are familial; the most common genetic causes are repeat expansions in C9orf72 and mutations in SOD1, TARDBP, and FUS. Aggregates of the protein TDP-43 are found in the neurons of nearly all patients.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is clinical, supported by electromyography and the exclusion of mimicking conditions; the El Escorial and Gold Coast criteria are used to classify certainty. There is no cure. Multidisciplinary care, non-invasive ventilation, gastrostomy feeding, and medications such as riluzole are the mainstays of management. The disease gained wide public attention through the Ice Bucket Challenge of 2014, which raised substantial research funding.
