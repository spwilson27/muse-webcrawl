Switched-mode power supply

Image placeholder: the internals of a typical SMPS.

A switched-mode power supply (SMPS), also called a switching power supply or switcher, is an electronic power supply that incorporates a switching regulator to convert electrical power efficiently. Unlike a linear power supply, the pass transistor of an SMPS continually switches between low-dissipation full-on and full-off states, wasting very little energy in transition.

Operation

Most SMPS designs first rectify the AC mains input, then chop the resulting DC at high frequency (tens of kilohertz to several megahertz) through a small, light transformer, and finally rectify and filter the output. A feedback circuit adjusts the duty cycle to regulate the output voltage. Common topologies include buck, boost, buck–boost, flyback and forward converters.

Advantages and disadvantages

Because the transformer operates at high frequency it can be far smaller and lighter than a mains-frequency transformer, so SMPS units are compact and typically 70–95% efficient. Their disadvantages include greater circuit complexity and the generation of high-frequency electrical noise that must be filtered to meet electromagnetic compatibility rules. SMPS technology underlies nearly all modern consumer electronics chargers and computer power supplies.
