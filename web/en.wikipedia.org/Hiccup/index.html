Hiccup

SpecialtyPulmonology, gastroenterology
Technical nameSingultus
MechanismInvoluntary diaphragmatic contraction with sudden glottic closure
DurationUsually minutes; "intractable" if over one month

A hiccup (medically, singultus) is an involuntary contraction of the diaphragm followed almost immediately by closure of the vocal cords, which produces the characteristic "hic" sound. A bout of hiccups is normally harmless and resolves within minutes, though hiccups can occasionally persist for days, months, or longer.

Mechanism and triggers

The hiccup reflex arc involves afferent fibres of the vagus, phrenic, and sympathetic nerves, a central processing area in the brainstem, and efferent fibres driving the diaphragm and accessory inspiratory muscles. Common triggers include eating quickly, carbonated drinks, alcohol, sudden excitement, laughing, and gastric distension. Persistent hiccups may signal irritation of the vagus or phrenic nerves, central nervous system disorders, metabolic disturbances, or side effects of medications.

Remedies

Folk remedies — holding one's breath, breathing into a paper bag, drinking cold water, or being startled — mostly aim to raise carbon dioxide levels or stimulate the vagus nerve to interrupt the reflex. For persistent or intractable hiccups, doctors treat underlying causes and may use drugs such as chlorpromazine, baclofen, or gabapentin.

Notable cases

American Charles Osborne hiccupped continuously from 1922 to 1990 — an estimated 430 million hiccups — the longest recorded bout. Hiccups are also common in fetuses and newborns, leading some researchers to propose the reflex is a developmental leftover or aids in learning to suckle.
