The Democratic National Convention is a series of presidential nominating conventions held every four years since 1832 by the United States Democratic Party. The convention formally nominates the party's candidates for president and vice president, adopts a party platform, and unifies the party for the general election campaign.

Delegates

Delegates are allocated to states largely on the basis of primary and caucus results. In addition, "superdelegates" — automatic seats for party leaders and elected officials — historically played a role; reforms adopted after 2016 restricted their voting on the first ballot if it would change the outcome. The convention typically opens with a roll-call vote of the states.

History

The first convention, in 1832, renominated Andrew Jackson. Conventions were once genuine decision-making bodies: the 1924 convention needed 103 ballots to choose John W. Davis. The chaotic 1968 convention in Chicago, marked by anti-Vietnam War protests and police violence, prompted reforms (the McGovern–Fraser Commission) that shifted power to primary voters. Modern conventions are largely ceremonial, serving as televised showcases culminating in the nominee's acceptance speech.

Recent conventions

The 2020 convention was held largely virtually because of the COVID-19 pandemic, and the 2024 convention was held in Chicago, where Kamala Harris accepted the nomination.
