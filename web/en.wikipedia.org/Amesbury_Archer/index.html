Amesbury Archer

The Amesbury Archer is an early Bronze Age man whose grave was discovered in May 2002 at Amesbury, near Stonehenge in Wiltshire, England. Dating to about 2300 BCE, it is one of the richest Bronze Age burials found in Britain, and the man is sometimes nicknamed the "King of Stonehenge."

The burial

The grave contained the skeleton of a man aged about 35 to 45, buried with an unusually large assemblage of goods: numerous flint arrowheads, wrist guards of stone, copper knives, gold hair ornaments (among the earliest gold objects found in Britain), boars' tusks, and several Beaker pots. A second, younger man was buried nearby, likely a relative.

Origins

Oxygen isotope analysis of the Archer's teeth indicated that he grew up in a colder climate than Britain — probably in the Alps region of central Europe — suggesting he traveled a great distance, perhaps as a metalworker bringing new skills to Britain. He had suffered a serious knee injury and a dental abscess. His remains and grave goods are displayed at the Salisbury Museum.
