Procedural due process

Procedural due process is a principle of United States constitutional law requiring that government, before depriving a person of life, liberty, or property, follow fair procedures — typically notice and an opportunity to be heard. It derives from the Due Process Clauses of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments.

Elements

At minimum, procedural due process requires (1) adequate notice of the action and its grounds, and (2) a meaningful opportunity to present one's case before a neutral decision-maker. In Mathews v. Eldridge (1976), the Supreme Court set a balancing test weighing the private interest, the risk of erroneous deprivation and value of added safeguards, and the government's interest.

Applications

The doctrine applies to criminal prosecutions, civil litigation, administrative actions such as benefit terminations (Goldberg v. Kelly), school discipline, and professional licensing. It is distinguished from substantive due process, which limits what government may regulate at all.
