A gametophyte (from Greek gamete and phyton, "plant") is one of the two alternating multicellular phases in the life cycles of plants and algae, a phenomenon called the alternation of generations. The gametophyte is haploid: it develops from a spore produced by meiosis in the diploid sporophyte, and it produces gametes by mitosis. Fusion of two gametes forms a zygote that grows into the next sporophyte generation.

Variation among plant groups

In bryophytes (mosses, liverworts and hornworts) the gametophyte is the dominant, long-lived, photosynthetic phase; the sporophyte grows attached to and dependent upon it. The moss gametophyte comprises a protonema and leafy shoots bearing antheridia (male) and archegonia (female). In ferns and other vascular plants the balance reverses: the familiar plant is the sporophyte, while the gametophyte is reduced to a small free-living prothallus. In seed plants the gametophytes are reduced still further and are entirely dependent on the sporophyte — the male gametophyte is the pollen grain, and the female gametophyte is the embryo sac within the ovule.

Reproduction

Gametophytes produce gametes in multicellular organs called gametangia: antheridia produce sperm and archegonia produce eggs. In non-seed plants the sperm are typically flagellated and must swim through water to reach the egg, one reason these plants favour moist habitats. Some gametophytes are bisexual (bearing both kinds of gametangia) and others unisexual.

Evolution

The evolutionary trend across land plants is progressive reduction of the gametophyte and dominance of the sporophyte, interpreted as an adaptation to life on land: the diploid phase delays the expression of deleterious recessive mutations and its tissues (cuticle, vascular system, seeds) protect reproduction from desiccation. In animals, by contrast, no multicellular haploid phase exists.
