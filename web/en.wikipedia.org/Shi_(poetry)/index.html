Shi (poetry)

Chinese: 詩 / 诗
Pinyin: shī
Meaning: "poetry", "poem", "verse"

Shi (Chinese: 詩) is the Chinese generic word for "poetry" or "poem", and also came to denote a specific, highly influential form of Classical Chinese poetry. In the broadest sense, shi can refer to any Chinese poetic verse; in the narrow sense it describes the lyric poetry developed from the ancient Classic of Poetry (Shijing) onwards.

Development

The earliest shi poems, collected in the Shijing, used lines of four characters. During the Han dynasty, poets developed five-character and seven-character line forms, which became the dominant metres of Chinese poetry for the next two thousand years. The folk-song bureau (yuefu) collected ballads in shi form that deeply influenced later literati poets such as Cao Cao and Tao Yuanming.

Regulated verse

In the Tang dynasty, shi poetry reached a highly refined form in the "regulated verse" (lüshi) and quatrain (jueju) styles, with strict tonal patterns, parallelism and line counts. Masters such as Li Bai and Du Fu wrote in these forms, which remained canonical models for poets throughout East Asia.

Influence

Shi poetry shaped the literary cultures of Japan (as kanshi), Korea and Vietnam, and anthologies such as the Three Hundred Tang Poems remain widely read and memorised today.
