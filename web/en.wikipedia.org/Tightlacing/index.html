Tightlacing


A tightlaced corset, late 19th century

Tightlacing (also called corset training) is the practice of wearing a tightly laced corset to achieve extreme modifications of the waist and figure. It is associated with the desire for a very small waist, sometimes as little as 16 to 18 inches, and was both practised and controversial during the height of corset fashion in the 19th century.

History

Corsets became a standard part of women's dress in Europe from the 16th century onward. During the Victorian era, fashion favoured a pronounced hourglass silhouette, and some women laced their corsets extremely tightly. Contemporary medical and moral critics debated the health effects fiercely, attributing a wide range of ailments to tightlacing, and the letters pages of magazines such as The Englishwoman's Domestic Magazine carried a long-running correspondence on the subject.

Practice and effects

Gradual waist training is achieved by wearing progressively smaller corsets over months or years. Sustained tightlacing can displace the lower (floating) ribs, compress the abdominal organs, and reduce lung capacity while the corset is worn; the modifications are largely temporary, though long-term wearers report semi-permanent changes to the lower ribcage. Critics in the dress reform movement condemned the practice, while wearers often defended it as a matter of fashion, discipline, or personal preference.

Tightlacing survives today as a niche practice among corsetry enthusiasts and in fetish subcultures. Notable modern tightlacers have included Ethel Granger and Cathie Jung, who holds the Guinness record for the smallest waist on a living person.
