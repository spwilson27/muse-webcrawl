Classical test theory

Classical test theory
Field: Psychometrics
Core model: Observed score = true score + error
Key statistics: Reliability, Cronbach's alpha, standard error of measurement
Alternative: Item response theory

Classical test theory (CTT) is a body of related psychometric theory that predicts outcomes of psychological testing, such as the difficulty of items or the ability of test-takers. It has been the dominant framework for test construction and interpretation in psychology and education. Its ideas were developed from early 20th-century work on correlation and measurement error, notably Charles Spearman's theory of reliability, and were systematically presented in Lord and Novick's Statistical Theories of Mental Test Scores (1968).

The model

The central axiom of CTT is that an observed test score X is composed of a true score T and measurement error E: X = T + E, where errors are assumed random, uncorrelated with true scores, and with a mean of zero. From this model follows the theory of reliability: the proportion of observed-score variance attributable to true scores. Common reliability coefficients include test–retest reliability, parallel-forms reliability, and internal consistency measured by Cronbach's alpha. Measurement error is summarized by the standard error of measurement.

Item analysis

In CTT, items are described by statistics such as item difficulty (the proportion of examinees answering correctly) and item discrimination (the item–total correlation). These statistics are sample-dependent, a limitation that motivated the development of item response theory (IRT), in which item and person parameters are modelled on a common scale. Despite this, CTT remains widely used because of its simplicity and modest assumptions.
