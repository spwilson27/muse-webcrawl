Manchukuo was a puppet state of the Empire of Japan in Northeast China (Manchuria) that existed from 1932 until its collapse in 1945. It was ostensibly founded as a republic and later proclaimed an empire, ruled in name by Puyi, the last emperor of the Qing dynasty, but it was in fact controlled by the Japanese Kwantung Army, whose officers staffed the state and directed its policies.

History

Following the Mukden Incident of 18 September 1931, the Kwantung Army invaded Manchuria. On 1 March 1932 the "State of Manchuria" (Manchukuo) was proclaimed, with Puyi installed first as Chief Executive and, from 1934, as the Kangde Emperor. The League of Nations' Lytton Report refused to recognise the state as genuinely independent, prompting Japan's withdrawal from the League in 1933; only Japan, its Axis partners, and a few other states extended recognition. China never recognised it.

Government and society

The capital, Changchun, was renamed Hsinking ("New Capital") and extensively rebuilt. Under the slogan "Five Races Under One Union", the state promoted a multi-ethnic ideology, but Japanese settlers and officials held privileged positions, resources were exploited for Japan's war economy, and the region saw heavy industrialisation alongside harsh repression. Unit 731, based near Harbin, conducted lethal human experimentation under the cover of epidemic prevention. Opium production and sales were a significant state revenue source. Manchukuo's population grew to over 40 million, mostly Han Chinese, with Manchu, Korean, Mongol, Japanese, and Russian minorities.

Collapse

On 8 August 1945 the Soviet Union declared war on Japan and launched the invasion of Manchuria, overwhelming the Kwantung Army within days. Puyi abdicated on 17 August and was captured by Soviet forces while attempting to flee to Japan. Manchukuo ceased to exist and its territory returned to China.

Legacy

Manchukuo remains a sensitive symbol of Japanese imperialism in China. Its rapid industrial development — railways, steel at Anshan, and planned cities — left infrastructure that later served the People's Republic, but official Chinese historiography treats the state purely as an illegitimate occupation regime.
