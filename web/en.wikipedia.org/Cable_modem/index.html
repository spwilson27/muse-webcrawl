A cable modem is a type of network bridge that provides bi-directional data communication via radio frequency channels on a hybrid fibre-coaxial (HFC) network. Cable modems are primarily used to deliver broadband Internet access over cable television infrastructure, in the form of cable Internet.

History

Cable modems emerged in the mid-1990s as cable operators began offering internet services. Early proprietary systems were displaced by the interoperable DOCSIS standard developed by CableLabs, which allowed customers to buy retail modems that would work on different networks. Cable internet became one of the principal forms of residential broadband, competing with DSL and later fibre networks.

How it works

The modem modulates data onto radio-frequency carriers carried over the coaxial cable plant, using channels also used for television signals. At the operator's headend, a cable modem termination system (CMTS) manages the connection. Downstream and upstream traffic use different frequency ranges; later DOCSIS versions bond many channels together and employ advanced modulation such as OFDM to reach gigabit speeds.

Features

Many modern cable modems are combined with a router, Wi-Fi access point, Ethernet switch and telephone adapter in a single gateway device supplied by the operator. Standalone modems typically connect to a separate router through an Ethernet port.

Comparison with other technologies

Cable offers higher speeds than traditional DSL because the shared coaxial medium has greater bandwidth, though capacity is shared among subscribers in a neighbourhood. Fibre-to-the-premises generally offers higher and more symmetric speeds, but cable networks remain widely deployed and have been upgraded through successive DOCSIS generations.
