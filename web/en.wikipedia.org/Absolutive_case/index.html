Absolutive case

The absolutive case (abbreviated ABS) is the case of nouns in ergative–absolutive languages that would generally be the subjects of intransitive verbs or the objects of transitive verbs. It is thus the case borne by the single argument (S) of an intransitive clause and by the patient (P) of a transitive clause, while the transitive agent (A) takes the ergative case.

Alignment

In an ergative–absolutive system, "the man arrived" and "the woman saw the man" mark "the man" identically (absolutive), while "the woman" as agent is ergative. This contrasts with nominative–accusative languages such as English, where S and A share the nominative case. The absolutive is typically the citation form of the noun and is often unmarked (zero-marked), with the ergative carrying the overt case suffix — as in Basque, where absolutive gizon "man" contrasts with ergative gizonak.

Distribution

Ergative–absolutive alignment with an absolutive case is found in Basque, in most Mayan languages, in Inuit languages, in many languages of the Caucasus (such as Georgian in some tense series), in Sumerian, in Burushaski, and in many Australian Aboriginal languages such as Dyirbal. Some languages show split ergativity, using absolutive alignment only in certain tenses, aspects, or clause types. Case syncretism and syntax complicate the picture: in some languages nominal morphology is ergative while syntactic processes (such as coordination) are accusative.
