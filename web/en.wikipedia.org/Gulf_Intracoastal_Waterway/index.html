Gulf Intracoastal Waterway

The Gulf Intracoastal Waterway (GIWW) is a navigable inland waterway running approximately 1,050 miles (1,690 km) along the Gulf of Mexico coast of the United States, from Brownsville, Texas, to Carrabelle, Florida. Formed from natural bays, lagoons and rivers connected by dredged canals, it provides a sheltered route for barges and pleasure craft parallel to the coast, and links with the Atlantic Intracoastal Waterway at the Mississippi River and via the Okeechobee Waterway in Florida.

History

A continuous Gulf waterway was proposed in the 19th century and authorised piecemeal by Congress; major construction took place in the 1930s and 1940s, accelerated by the strategic need during the Second World War to move petroleum and bulk cargoes safe from submarine attack. The route was essentially complete by 1949.

Traffic and importance

The waterway is one of the busiest inland navigation routes in the United States, carrying on the order of 100 million tons of cargo annually, chiefly petroleum products, chemicals, crude oil, aggregates and grain moving to and from the ports and refineries of Texas and Louisiana. Key junctions include the Mississippi River at New Orleans, the Houston Ship Channel, and the Calcasieu and Sabine–Neches waterways. Locks, notably at Harvey and Algiers near New Orleans and at the Inner Harbor Navigation Canal, connect the GIWW with the Mississippi system.

Challenges

Maintenance dredging, bank erosion from barge wakes, saltwater intrusion into coastal marshes, and damage from hurricanes such as Katrina (2005) are persistent concerns, as is funding for lock modernisation.
