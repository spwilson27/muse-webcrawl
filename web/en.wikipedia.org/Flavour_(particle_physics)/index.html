Flavour (particle physics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In particle physics, flavour or flavor is the term for the different types or species of elementary particles that share the same interactions but differ in mass. The six flavours of quarks are up, down, charm, strange, top, and bottom; the six flavours of leptons are the electron, muon, and tau and their corresponding neutrinos. Particles of different flavours are grouped into three generations, each containing two quarks and two leptons.

Flavour quantum numbers

Associated with flavour are several quantum numbers — strangeness, charm, bottomness, topness, and the lepton flavour numbers (electron number, muon number, tau number) — that are conserved by the strong and electromagnetic interactions but can be changed by the weak interaction. It is the weak force alone that permits flavour change: a neutron decays because a down quark turns into an up quark, and quarks of one generation mix into another through the CKM matrix.

Flavour-changing processes

Flavour-changing neutral currents, in which a quark changes flavour without changing charge, are heavily suppressed in the Standard Model (the GIM mechanism) and are therefore sensitive probes of new physics. Neutrino oscillation, in which neutrinos change lepton flavour as they propagate, demonstrated that neutrinos have mass and that lepton flavour is not strictly conserved — the first confirmed physics beyond the simplest Standard Model picture.

The flavour problem

Why there are exactly three generations, and why their masses and mixing angles take the values they do, is unexplained in the Standard Model; this is the flavour problem. The huge hierarchy from the electron neutrino's tiny mass to the top quark's 173 GeV is one of the outstanding puzzles motivating theories beyond the Standard Model.
