Klemens von Metternich

Klemens Wenzel Lothar von Metternich (15 May 1773 – 11 June 1859) was an Austrian diplomat and statesman who served as Foreign Minister of the Austrian Empire from 1809 and as State Chancellor from 1821 until the revolutions of 1848 forced his resignation. He was the central architect of the Congress of Vienna and the conservative European order — the "Concert of Europe" — that followed the Napoleonic Wars.

Early life and career

Metternich was born in Koblenz into a Rhenish noble family in Habsburg service. Educated at Strasbourg and Mainz, he entered the diplomatic service and served as ambassador to Saxony, Prussia, and Napoleonic France, where he dealt directly with Napoleon.

Foreign minister

Appointed foreign minister in 1809 after Austria's defeat at Wagram, Metternich pursued a cautious policy of survival, including the marriage of Archduchess Marie Louise to Napoleon in 1810. As Napoleon's power crumbled after the Russian campaign, Metternich positioned Austria as armed mediator, then joined the coalition that defeated France in 1813–14.

Congress of Vienna and the Metternich system

At the Congress of Vienna (1814–15) Metternich worked with Castlereagh, Talleyrand, and Alexander I to redraw Europe's map on the principles of legitimacy and balance of power. The resulting settlement gave Europe nearly four decades without a general war. Domestically and across the German and Italian states, the "Metternich system" defended monarchy against liberalism and nationalism, using censorship, police surveillance (notably through the Carlsbad Decrees of 1819), and congress diplomacy.

Downfall and later life

In March 1848 revolution reached Vienna; Metternich resigned and fled, famously disguised, to England. He returned to Vienna in 1851 and acted as an unofficial adviser until his death in 1859.

Assessment

Metternich remains a polarising figure: praised as a master diplomat who preserved peace and stability, and criticised as the reactionary "coachman of Europe" who suppressed legitimate national and liberal aspirations.
