Count Zero is a science fiction novel by American-Canadian writer William Gibson, originally published in 1986. It is the second volume of the Sprawl trilogy, following Neuromancer (1984) and preceding Mona Lisa Overdrive (1988). The novel was nominated for the Nebula Award and the Hugo Award for Best Novel in 1987. Portions appeared in serial form in Isaac Asimov's Science Fiction Magazine before book publication, beginning with the story that became the novel's opening chapters.

Plot summary

The novel interweaves three storylines. Turner, a corporate mercenary, is hired to extract Christopher Mitchell, a researcher defecting from Maas Biolabs, but the operation goes disastrously wrong and Turner flees with Mitchell's young daughter Angie, whose brain has been fitted with experimental biochips that let her access cyberspace without a deck. Bobby Newmark, a young would-be console cowboy who goes by "Count Zero," is nearly killed testing stolen software and is saved by apparent voodoo loa manifesting in the matrix. Marly Krushkhova, a disgraced art dealer, is hired by dying billionaire Josef Virek to find the unknown maker of a series of Cornell-like boxes.

Themes

Seven years after the events of Neuromancer, the artificial intelligences Wintermute and Neuromancer have fragmented into entities that present themselves as the gods of Haitian Vodou. Gibson explores the intersection of technology, religion, and art, as well as the reach of multinational corporations (zaibatsus) that dominate the Sprawl setting. The loa use human agents, including the voodoo-practicing hackers of a suburban squat, to pursue their own inscrutable ends.

Reception

Count Zero consolidated Gibson's reputation as a leading figure of the cyberpunk movement. Critics praised its dense, allusive prose and its layered structure, in which the three plot strands converge in the final chapters.
