The Kuiper belt is a circumstellar disc in the outer Solar System, extending from the orbit of Neptune at 30 astronomical units (AU) to approximately 50 AU from the Sun. It is similar to the asteroid belt, but is far larger—20 times as wide and 20 to 200 times as massive—and consists mainly of small remnants from the formation of the Solar System, composed largely of frozen volatiles ("ices") such as methane, ammonia and water. It is home to three officially recognized dwarf planets—Pluto, Haumea and Makemake—and is believed to be the source of most short-period comets.

A band of icy bodies beyond Neptune was hypothesised by Kenneth Edgeworth (1943) and Gerard Kuiper (1951), among others, but long went undetected because its members are small and extremely faint. After Pluto (1930) and Charon (1978), the first further object, 15760 Albion, was found in 1992 by David Jewitt and Jane Luu; thousands of Kuiper belt objects (KBOs) are now catalogued, and the region is estimated to contain more than 100,000 bodies over 100 km across.

Structure

The classical Kuiper belt (c. 42–48 AU) contains "cold" classicals on near-circular, low-inclination orbits—pristine survivors of the original disc—and dynamically "hot" classicals on more excited orbits. Resonant objects are trapped in mean-motion resonances with Neptune; the largest group, the plutinos (including Pluto itself), occupy the 3:2 resonance at 39.4 AU. The scattered disc, whose members were flung outward by encounters with Neptune, extends much farther and blends into the domain of detached objects such as Sedna.

Exploration

NASA's New Horizons spacecraft flew past Pluto in July 2015, revealing a geologically active world with nitrogen glaciers and a thin atmosphere, and on 1 January 2019 performed the most distant flyby ever conducted, of the cold classical object Arrokoth—a pristine contact binary unchanged since the Solar System's formation.
