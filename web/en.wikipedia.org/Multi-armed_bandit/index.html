Multi-armed bandit

In probability theory and machine learning, the multi-armed bandit problem (sometimes called the K-armed bandit problem) is a classic model of the conflict between exploration and exploitation. A gambler must repeatedly choose among several slot machines ('one-armed bandits') whose reward distributions are unknown, so as to maximise the total reward gained over a sequence of plays.

The problem formalises sequential decision-making under uncertainty and underlies modern algorithms for online advertising, recommendation and adaptive clinical trials.

Formulation

Each arm i yields rewards drawn from an unknown probability distribution. The gambler must balance exploiting the arm with the highest observed average reward against exploring other arms that might prove better. Performance is measured by regret: the difference between the expected reward of the best strategy and that of always playing the best arm.

Variants include contextual bandits, in which side information about each trial is available, stochastic and adversarial bandits, and bandit problems with changing reward distributions.

Strategies

Common strategies include ε-greedy, which explores with probability ε and otherwise exploits; optimism in the face of uncertainty, implemented in the UCB (Upper Confidence Bound) family of algorithms, which picks the arm with the highest plausible reward; and Thompson sampling, a Bayesian method that samples from posterior distributions of each arm's reward. The Gittins index provides an optimal solution for discounted infinite-horizon versions of the problem.

The problem was originally described by Herbert Robbins in 1952 and has since become foundational in reinforcement learning and operations research.
