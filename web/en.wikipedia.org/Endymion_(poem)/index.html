Endymion (poem)

AuthorJohn Keats
CountryEngland
LanguageEnglish
GenreNarrative poem; romance
Publication date1818
FormFour books, heroic couplets, about 4,000 lines

Endymion is a poem by John Keats first published in 1818 by Taylor and Hessey of Fleet Street in London. John Keats dedicated this poem to the late poet Thomas Chatterton. Endymion begins with the line "A thing of beauty is a joy for ever". The poem is written in rhyming couplets in iambic pentameter (also known as heroic couplets). Keats based the poem on the Greek myth of Endymion, the shepherd beloved of the moon goddess Selene. The poem elaborates on the original story and renames Selene "Cynthia" (an alternative name for Artemis).

Composition

Keats began work on the poem in April 1817 at Carisbrooke on the Isle of Wight, setting himself the discipline of writing a fixed number of lines each day. He completed the draft at Burford Bridge in November 1817 and revised it before publication the following spring. The poem runs to some 4,000 lines divided into four books.

Plot

The poem tells of the shepherd-prince Endymion, who falls into a reverie after dreaming of the moon goddess Cynthia. He undertakes a quest — descending into the earth and diving into the sea — to find his ideal love. Along the way he encounters and pities other lovers of myth, including Alpheus and Arethusa and Glaucus and Scylla. In the final book he falls for an Indian maiden, only to discover that she and Cynthia are one and the same.

Reception

Upon its release, Endymion was attacked fiercely by the conservative literary establishment. John Gibson Lockhart, writing in Blackwood's Magazine under the signature "Z", mocked Keats as a member of the "Cockney School" and advised him to return to his apothecary's shop. John Wilson Croker's review in the Quarterly Review was similarly scathing. Percy Bysshe Shelley defended Keats, and later critics came to see the poem, despite its diffuseness, as a significant apprenticeship work containing the seeds of Keats's mature style.

Legacy

The opening line, "A thing of beauty is a joy for ever", has become one of the most quoted lines in English poetry. The poem's lush imagery and its meditation on the imagination's power to transcend mortality anticipate the great odes Keats would write in 1819.
