Common tern

Scientific name: Sterna hirundo
Family: Laridae
Conservation status: Least Concern

The common tern (Sterna hirundo) is a seabird of the family Laridae with a circumpolar breeding distribution across temperate and subarctic coasts and islands of Europe, Asia, and North America. It is strongly migratory, wintering in tropical and subtropical waters as far south as South Africa and Australia.

Description

Adults in breeding plumage are pale grey above and white below, with a black cap, orange-red legs, and a red bill with a black tip. At 31–35 cm long with a deeply forked tail, it closely resembles the Arctic tern, from which it is distinguished by bill colour, leg length, and wing pattern.

Behaviour

Common terns nest colonially on beaches, islands, and artificial rafts, laying two or three eggs in a scrape. They feed by plunge-diving for small fish — sand eels, sprats, and young herring — often hovering before striking. Colonies defend their nests aggressively, dive-bombing intruders including humans.

Conservation

Once persecuted for the millinery trade, the species recovered under legal protection but remains vulnerable to predation by gulls, foxes, and mink, and to disturbance and loss of nesting habitat; conservationists provide rafts and wardening at many colonies.
