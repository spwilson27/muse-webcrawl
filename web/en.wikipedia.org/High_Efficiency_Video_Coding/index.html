High Efficiency Video Coding (HEVC), also known as H.265 and MPEG-H Part 2, is a video compression standard designed as part of the MPEG-H project as a successor to the widely used Advanced Video Coding (AVC, H.264, or MPEG-4 Part 10). In comparison to AVC, HEVC offers from 25% to 50% better data compression at the same level of video quality, or substantially improved video quality at the same bit rate. It supports resolutions up to 8192×4320, that is, 8K UHD, and unlike the primarily 8-bit AVC, HEVC's higher fidelity Main10 profile has been incorporated into nearly all supporting hardware.

Whereas AVC uses the integer discrete cosine transform (DCT) with 4×4 and 8×8 block sizes, HEVC uses integer DCT and discrete sine transform (DST) with varied block sizes between 4×4 and 32×32. The improved compression comes at the cost of substantially greater computational complexity for encoding.

History

HEVC was jointly developed by the ITU-T Video Coding Experts Group (VCEG) and the ISO/IEC Moving Picture Experts Group (MPEG), working together as the Joint Collaborative Team on Video Coding (JCT-VC). The first version of the standard was completed in January 2013 and ratified by the ITU-T in April 2013 and by ISO/IEC later that year. Subsequent versions added range extensions, scalable coding (SHVC), multiview extensions (MV-HEVC), and screen content coding extensions.

Features

Coding tree units

HEVC replaces AVC's 16×16-pixel macroblocks with coding tree units (CTUs), which can be as large as 64×64 pixels and can be recursively subdivided in a quadtree structure into smaller coding units. This allows the encoder to choose large blocks for smooth image regions and small blocks for detailed ones, improving compression efficiency.

Other tools

  • Intra-picture prediction with 35 directional modes, compared to 9 in AVC
  • Improved motion compensation with more precise motion vector prediction
  • Sample adaptive offset (SAO) filtering to reduce banding and ringing artefacts
  • Tiles and wavefront parallel processing for parallel encoding and decoding
  • Improved entropy coding using context-adaptive binary arithmetic coding (CABAC)

Profiles and levels

The first version of HEVC defines three profiles: Main (8-bit 4:2:0), Main10 (up to 10-bit), and Main Still Picture. Later range extensions added profiles supporting higher bit depths, 4:2:2 and 4:4:4 chroma sampling, and screen content. Thirteen levels define maximum picture size, frame rate, and bit rate, from Level 1 up to Level 6.2, which supports 8K video at 120 frames per second.

Licensing

Unlike its predecessor, HEVC's patent licensing was fragmented among multiple pools (MPEG LA, HEVC Advance/Access Advance, and Velos Media) as well as companies licensing independently, with higher and less predictable royalty terms. This complexity slowed adoption on the open web—major browsers long declined to ship software decoding—and encouraged the Alliance for Open Media to develop the royalty-free AV1 format as an alternative. HEVC nevertheless achieved wide deployment in broadcast television, 4K Ultra HD Blu-ray, video streaming services, smartphones, and operating systems, where hardware decoding support is common.

Implementations

The open-source x265 encoder is a widely used software implementation. Hardware encoders and decoders are integrated into most modern GPUs, mobile system-on-chips, and televisions. Apple's adoption of HEVC across iOS and macOS in 2017, including the HEIF image format based on HEVC intra coding, significantly expanded its installed base.
