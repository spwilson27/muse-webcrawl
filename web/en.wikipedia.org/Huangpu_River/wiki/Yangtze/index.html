Yangtze

The Yangtze (Chinese: 长江, Cháng Jiāng) is the longest river in Eurasia, the third-longest in the world, and the longest river to flow entirely within one country. It rises on the Tibetan Plateau in Qinghai and flows about 6,300 kilometres eastward through China to the East China Sea near Shanghai. Its drainage basin covers roughly one-fifth of China's land area and is home to nearly a third of the country's population.

Course

In its upper reaches, known as the Jinsha River, the Yangtze descends steeply from the plateau, at one point running close to the Mekong and Salween in the Three Parallel Rivers region. It passes Chongqing, then flows through the dramatic Three Gorges, now impounded by the Three Gorges Dam, the world's largest power station by installed capacity. Below the dam it meanders across the lakes and plains of Hubei, Anhui, and Jiangsu, receiving tributaries such as the Han, and the outflow of Dongting and Poyang lakes, before reaching its delta at Shanghai.

The Huangpu and the estuary

The river's last major tributary is the Huangpu River, which joins the Yangtze at Wusongkou after flowing through Shanghai. The broad estuary, divided by Chongming Island — China's third-largest island, formed from Yangtze silt — carries enormous volumes of sediment to the East China Sea, continually extending the delta.

Importance

The Yangtze is central to China's economy: its valley is a major rice- and fish-producing region, its waters irrigate farmland, and its navigable channels, the busiest inland waterway in the world, link the coast with the deep interior. It has also flooded catastrophically many times, most severely in 1931 and 1954.
