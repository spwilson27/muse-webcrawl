The Huangpu River (Chinese: 黄浦江; pinyin: Huángpǔ Jiāng; Shanghainese: Waonphu Tshan), formerly romanized as the Whangpoo River, is a 113-kilometre (70 mi) river flowing through Shanghai, China, that was first excavated by Lord Chunshen during the Warring States period. It is the last significant tributary of the Yangtze before the Yangtze empties into the East China Sea. The Bund and Lujiazui are located along the river, which divides the city into its two traditional halves: Pudong ("east of the Pu") and Puxi ("west of the Pu").

The Huangpu is the largest river in Shanghai and Suzhou Creek is its major tributary. It is on average about 400 metres wide and 9 metres deep, and is a major source of drinking water for Shanghai as well as its principal shipping artery; the Port of Shanghai developed along its banks before expanding to the deep-water port at Yangshan.

Geography

The river rises at Dianshan Lake in the western suburbs of Shanghai (in Qingpu District) and flows generally northeast through the city. It receives the Suzhou Creek (Wusong River) at the northern end of the Bund, then continues past Lujiazui and the former Expo 2010 site to join the Yangtze at Wusongkou. Its principal tributaries include the Dianpu, Wusong (Suzhou Creek), Yunzaobang, and Chuanyang rivers. The Huangpu has a notable tidal range, and the bore and tides at its mouth have long influenced navigation and flood control along its banks.

History

According to tradition, the river's channel was dug under Huang Xie, Lord Chunshen (d. 238 BC), a statesman of Chu, from whom the names "Huangpu" and "Shen" (an old name for Shanghai) derive. Historically the Wusong River (modern Suzhou Creek) was the main channel to the sea; after it silted up in the early Ming dynasty, the official Xia Yuanji directed works in 1403–1404 that diverted the flow into the Huangpu, making it the principal waterway. With the opening of Shanghai as a treaty port after 1842, foreign concessions developed along the western bank — the Bund becoming the famous waterfront of banks and trading houses — while wharves, warehouses, and shipyards made the river one of China's busiest commercial arteries. Jiangnan Shipyard, founded in 1865, stood on its bank for over a century.

Crossings

Because the river cuts through the centre of the metropolis, it is spanned by many bridges, tunnels, and metro lines. Major crossings include the Nanpu Bridge (1991), Yangpu Bridge (1993), Lupu Bridge (2003, once the world's longest arch bridge), Xupu Bridge, Minpu bridges, the historic Waibaidu (Garden) Bridge over Suzhou Creek at its confluence, and the Bund Tunnel. Five ferry lines and numerous vehicle and metro tunnels also cross the river; Shanghai Metro lines 2, 4, 7, 8, 9, 12 and others pass beneath it.

Tourism

River cruises from the Bund promenade are a signature Shanghai experience, offering views of the colonial architecture of the Bund on the west bank and the skyscrapers of Lujiazui — the Oriental Pearl Tower, Jin Mao Tower, Shanghai World Financial Center, and Shanghai Tower — on the east. In 2017–2018 the city completed a continuous 45-kilometre system of riverside walking and cycling paths along both banks, linking parks, museums (such as the Power Station of Art), and former industrial sites.
