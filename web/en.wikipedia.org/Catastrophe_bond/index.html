Catastrophe bond

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A catastrophe bond (often shortened to cat bond) is a high-yield insurance-linked security that transfers a specified set of risks from an insurer, reinsurer or government sponsor to capital-market investors. If a defined catastrophe — such as a hurricane, earthquake, flood or pandemic — occurs during the bond's life, the investors lose part or all of their principal, which is used to pay the sponsor's claims. If no qualifying event occurs, investors receive their principal back at maturity plus an elevated coupon.

Structure

Typically, the sponsor pays premiums to a special-purpose vehicle (SPV), often domiciled in Bermuda or the Cayman Islands, which issues the bonds and invests the proceeds in safe collateral. Payments are governed by a trigger: an indemnity trigger (based on the sponsor's actual losses), an industry-index trigger (based on total insured industry losses), a parametric trigger (based on physical measurements such as earthquake magnitude or wind speed at a location), or a modelled-loss trigger. Parametric triggers pay fastest and with least moral hazard but carry "basis risk" — the payout may not match actual losses.

History and market

The market emerged in the mid-1990s after Hurricane Andrew (1992) overwhelmed reinsurance capacity, and grew following Hurricane Katrina (2005). The World Bank and governments, including Mexico's FONDEN programme, have issued cat bonds for disaster relief. The outstanding market has reached tens of billions of dollars, and pension funds and specialist funds value the bonds because catastrophe risk is largely uncorrelated with financial markets. Notable payouts include bonds triggered by the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake and various US hurricanes; pandemic bonds issued for the World Bank in 2017 paid out during the COVID-19 pandemic, though their slow, complex triggers drew criticism.

Risks

Investors face the risk of losing their entire principal, model uncertainty in catastrophe risk assessment, and historically higher spreads than corporate bonds of similar rating. Most cat bonds are unrated or rated below investment grade.
