Ancient Egyptian religion

Ancient Egyptian religion was a complex system of polytheistic beliefs and rituals integral to ancient Egyptian culture. Centred on the Egyptians' interactions with many deities believed to be present in, and in control of, the world, it developed over more than three thousand years, from the Predynastic Period (before c. 3100 BC) until the adoption of Christianity in the early centuries AD.

Deities

Egyptians worshipped hundreds of gods and goddesses, among them the sun god Ra, Osiris and Isis, Horus, Seth, Thoth, Hathor, Bastet, and Amun, who rose to supremacy in the New Kingdom. Deities were frequently combined (syncretised), depicted with animal heads on human bodies, and associated with particular cities and natural phenomena.

Cosmology

Central concepts included ma'at — truth, order, and cosmic balance, upheld by the pharaoh — and its opposite, chaos (isfet). Creation accounts from Heliopolis, Memphis, and Hermopolis described the world arising from the primordial waters of Nun. The sun god's nightly journey through the underworld (Duat), battling the serpent Apep, symbolised the daily renewal of creation.

Temples and priesthood

Temples were believed to be the gods' houses on earth, where priests performed daily rituals of washing, clothing, and feeding the cult statue. The king was theoretically the chief priest of every cult, and temple complexes such as Karnak grew into vast economic as well as religious institutions.

Funerary beliefs

Egyptians believed in an afterlife in which the soul — comprising elements including the ka, ba, and akh — faced judgment: the heart was weighed against the feather of ma'at before Osiris. Mummification, tomb goods, and texts such as the Book of the Dead were meant to secure a blessed existence in the Field of Reeds.

Decline

The religion gradually gave way under Roman rule and Christianisation; the last hieroglyphic inscription dates to AD 394 at Philae, whose temple of Isis was closed under Justinian in the 6th century.
