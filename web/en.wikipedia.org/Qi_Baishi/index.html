Qi Baishi (Chinese: 齊白石; 1 January 1864 – 16 September 1957) was a Chinese painter noted for the whimsical, often playful style of his works. Born to a peasant family in Xiangtan, Hunan, Qi rose from humble origins — he worked first as a carpenter — to become one of the most celebrated masters of Chinese painting in the twentieth century. His oeuvre spans some 10,000 works and ranges across flowers, birds, insects, fish, landscapes and figures.

Life and career

Qi taught himself to paint after encountering a borrowed copy of the Manual of the Mustard Seed Garden. In his late twenties he was taken on as a pupil by the scholar-painter Hu Qinyuan and the seal carver Wang Xiangyi. He travelled widely through China between 1902 and 1909, a period he called his "five departures and five returns," before settling in Beijing in 1917.

After the age of forty Qi's style matured into the distinctive xieyi ("sketching the idea") manner for which he is best known: bold ink outlines, economical brushwork and vivid colour contrasts. His lively paintings of shrimps — rendered in transparent ink washes that seem to swim on the page — became his signature subject, along with crabs, frogs, cicadas and peonies.

Qi was also a master seal carver, developing a forceful single-knife cutting technique, and he wrote extensively on painting in poems and inscriptions carved or brushed onto his works.

Honours and legacy

In 1953 Qi was named a "People's Artist" and became the first chairman of the China Artists Association. In 1956 he received the International Peace Prize. His former Beijing residence is preserved as a memorial museum, and his paintings rank among the most valuable works of modern Chinese art at auction, with his Twelve Landscape Screens (1925) achieving a record price for Chinese artwork in 2017.
