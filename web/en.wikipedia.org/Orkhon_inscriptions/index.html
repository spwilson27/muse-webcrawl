The Orkhon inscriptions, also known as the Khushoo Tsaidam monuments, are two large stone stelae erected by the Göktürks of the Second Turkic Khaganate in the early 8th century in the Orkhon Valley of what is now Mongolia. They were set up in honour of the prince Kül Tigin (erected 732 CE) and his elder brother, the emperor Bilge Khagan (erected 735 CE). Written in the Old Turkic script, they are the oldest extensive texts in any Turkic language.

Content

Narrated largely in the voice of the khagan, the inscriptions recount the legendary origins of the Turks, the fall of the First Khaganate to Tang China, the fifty years of subjugation that followed, and the restoration of Turkic independence under Ilterish Qaghan and his sons. They contain famous passages warning the Turks against Chinese diplomacy — "the Chinese people, who give in abundance gold, silver, millet and silk, have always used sweet words and alluring riches" — and urging them to remain in the Ötüken homeland. The texts were composed by Yollug Tigin, a nephew of the rulers.

Discovery and decipherment

The monuments were found in 1889 by the Russian explorer Nikolai Yadrintsev. In December 1893 the Danish philologist Vilhelm Thomsen announced he had deciphered the script, using the bilingual Chinese text on the Kül Tigin stele as a key; Wilhelm Radloff published the first full translations soon after. The site forms part of the UNESCO-listed Orkhon Valley Cultural Landscape.
