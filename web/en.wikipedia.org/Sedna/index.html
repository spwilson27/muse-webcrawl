90377 Sedna

Artist's conception of Sedna, a distant red trans-Neptunian object with an extraordinarily elongated orbit.

90377 Sedna is a dwarf planet in the outermost reaches of the Solar System, discovered in 2003 by Michael Brown, Chad Trujillo and David Rabinowitz at Palomar Observatory. It is named after Sedna, the Inuit goddess of the sea and marine animals. Sedna has one of the longest orbital periods of any known large object in the Solar System, taking roughly 11,400 years to complete one orbit of the Sun.

Orbit

Sedna's orbit is exceptionally elliptical: its perihelion is about 76 astronomical units (AU) — roughly two and a half times the distance of Neptune — and its aphelion is estimated at about 937 AU, deep in the inner Oort cloud. Because its perihelion lies well beyond Neptune's gravitational influence, Sedna is classified as a detached object, and together with a handful of similar bodies it defines the tentative class of sednoids. How such orbits arose is debated; hypotheses include a passing star in the Sun's birth cluster, capture from another star, or the gravitational influence of an undiscovered distant planet.

Physical characteristics

Sedna is roughly 1,000 kilometres in diameter, comparable to the dwarf planet Ceres, and is one of the reddest objects in the Solar System, similar in colour to Mars. Its surface is thought to consist of water, methane and nitrogen ices coloured by tholins produced by irradiation. It rotates slowly, with a period of roughly 10 hours according to later measurements (early estimates suggested a much longer period, prompting searches for a moon, but no satellite has been found). Sedna reached perihelion around 2076, making the present era the best opportunity to study it.

Significance

Sedna's discovery demonstrated that the Solar System contains large bodies far beyond the Kuiper belt and provided the first observational evidence bearing on the population of the inner Oort cloud. The clustering of the orbits of Sedna and other detached objects was one of the observations that motivated the Planet Nine hypothesis.
