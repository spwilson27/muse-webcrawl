Korematsu v. United States, 323 U.S. 214 (1944), was a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States that upheld the internment of Japanese Americans during World War II, applying the legal test applied in Hirabayashi v. United States (1943). The decision has been widely described as an infamous and egregiously wrong decision.

Background

After the attack on Pearl Harbor, President Franklin D. Roosevelt issued Executive Order 9066 in February 1942, authorising the exclusion of persons of Japanese ancestry from the West Coast. Fred Korematsu, a 23-year-old Japanese American welder, refused to report for relocation and was arrested and convicted of violating the exclusion order.

Decision

In a 6–3 decision written by Justice Hugo Black, the Court held that the need to protect the country against espionage outweighed Korematsu's individual rights and the rights of Americans of Japanese descent. Justice Frank Murphy's dissent called the exclusion a "legalization of racism".

Later developments

In 1983, a federal court vacated Korematsu's conviction on a writ of coram nobis after evidence emerged that the government had suppressed and altered reports contradicting claims of military necessity. The Civil Liberties Act of 1988 issued a formal apology and reparations to surviving internees. In Trump v. Hawaii (2018), Chief Justice John Roberts stated that Korematsu "was gravely wrong the day it was decided" and "has been overruled in the court of history".
