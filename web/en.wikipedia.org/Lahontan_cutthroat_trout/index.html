The Lahontan cutthroat trout, Nevada's state fish

The Lahontan cutthroat trout (Oncorhynchus henshawi, formerly O. clarkii henshawi) is the largest subspecies (or species) of cutthroat trout and the state fish of Nevada. It is native to the Lahontan Basin of northern Nevada, eastern California and southeastern Oregon, the remnant drainage of Pleistocene Lake Lahontan.

Description and habitat

Lahontan cutthroats are distinguished by the red-orange "cutthroat" slash beneath the jaw and by heavy spotting over the body. Uniquely among cutthroats, they evolved in alkaline desert lakes, tolerating water chemistry lethal to most fish. The lake-dwelling form grew to exceptional size: the world record, caught in Pyramid Lake in 1925, weighed 18.6 kg (41 lb), and nineteenth-century accounts describe fish of 27 kg or more.

Decline and recovery

The original Pyramid Lake strain was driven to extinction by the 1940s by commercial fishing, diversion of the Truckee River to the Derby Dam, and introduction of lake trout and rainbow trout. The lake was restocked from surviving populations elsewhere in the basin, and in the 1970s–2000s biologists rediscovered a remnant of the original giant strain in a tiny stream on Pilot Peak near the Utah border, where fish had been transplanted in the early twentieth century. Broodstock from these "Pilot Peak" fish has been used to restore large Lahontan cutthroats to Pyramid Lake, where fish exceeding 9 kg are again caught. The trout was listed as endangered in 1970 and reclassified as threatened in 1975, and recovery efforts continue across its basin.
