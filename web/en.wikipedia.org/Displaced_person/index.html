Displaced person

A displaced person (sometimes abbreviated DP) is a person who has been forced to leave their home or place of habitual residence, especially as a result of armed conflict, persecution, violence, human-rights violations, or disasters. The term came into wide use during and after the Second World War for the millions of people uprooted across Europe, including liberated forced labourers, concentration-camp survivors, and prisoners of war who could not or would not return home.

Post-war Europe

At the end of the war in 1945 there were an estimated 11 million displaced persons in Europe outside the Soviet sphere. Allied authorities and the United Nations Relief and Rehabilitation Administration (UNRRA), later the International Refugee Organization, ran DP camps — often in former barracks and camps — providing food, shelter, and tracing services while organizing repatriation and, for those unable to return, resettlement in countries such as the United States, Canada, Australia, and Israel. The last DP camps closed in the late 1950s.

Modern usage

Today the broader category of forcibly displaced people includes refugees who cross international borders and internally displaced persons (IDPs) who remain within their own country; UNHCR reported the global total exceeding 100 million in the 2020s. Legal protection differs by category: refugees are covered by the 1951 Refugee Convention, while IDPs rely primarily on their own governments and the non-binding Guiding Principles on Internal Displacement.
