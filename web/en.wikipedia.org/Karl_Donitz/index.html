Karl Donitz

Karl Dönitz (16 September 1891 – 24 December 1980) was a German admiral who commanded the Kriegsmarine's U-boat arm, served as Commander-in-Chief of the navy from 1943, and succeeded Adolf Hitler as head of state of Germany in the final days of the Second World War in Europe. He authorised the German surrender documents signed in May 1945 and was afterwards convicted of war crimes at Nuremberg.

Early career

Dönitz joined the Imperial German Navy in 1910 and served in the Mediterranean during the First World War. Commanding the submarine UB-68, he was captured by the British in October 1918 after his boat was forced to the surface. He remained in the small postwar Reichsmarine and rose steadily between the wars.

U-boat commander

When Germany openly rebuilt its submarine force in 1935, Dönitz was put in charge of the new U-boat arm. He developed the Rudeltaktik — the "wolfpack" tactic of coordinated group attacks on convoys — and pressed, unsuccessfully, for a much larger submarine fleet. During the Battle of the Atlantic his U-boats sank millions of tons of Allied shipping, but from 1943 Allied air cover, radar and code-breaking turned the campaign decisively against Germany.

Commander-in-Chief and head of state

In January 1943 Dönitz replaced Erich Raeder as Commander-in-Chief of the Kriegsmarine and was promoted to Grand Admiral. Named in Hitler's political testament as his successor as President of the Reich, he took power on Hitler's suicide on 30 April 1945 and headed the short-lived Flensburg Government. He authorised the unconditional surrender signed at Reims on 7 May and Berlin on 8 May 1945, and sought in his final days to move soldiers and refugees westward away from the advancing Red Army. His government was dissolved when its members were arrested on 23 May 1945.

Trial and later life

At the Nuremberg trials Dönitz was convicted of crimes against peace and war crimes and sentenced to ten years' imprisonment, served at Spandau Prison. Released in 1956, he wrote memoirs and lived quietly near Hamburg until his death in 1980.
