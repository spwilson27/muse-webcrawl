Spina bifida

Spina bifida
SpecialtyPaediatrics, neurosurgery, medical genetics
TypesOcculta, meningocele, myelomeningocele

Spina bifida is a birth defect in which the spine and spinal cord do not form or close properly. It is a type of neural tube defect, arising in the first month of embryonic development when the neural tube — the precursor of the brain and spinal cord — fails to close completely. The name is Latin for "split spine".

Types

  • Spina bifida occulta — the mildest form: one or more vertebrae are malformed but the cord and meninges remain in place, often with no symptoms and discovered incidentally on X-ray. A tuft of hair or dimple may mark the site.
  • Meningocele — the meninges herniate through the vertebral opening forming a sac of cerebrospinal fluid, but the spinal cord stays in its normal position; neurological damage is usually minor.
  • Myelomeningocele — the most severe form: the spinal cord and nerves protrude into the sac, causing paralysis and loss of sensation below the lesion, bladder and bowel dysfunction, and frequently hydrocephalus associated with a Chiari II malformation.

Causes and prevention

The cause is multifactorial — genetic susceptibility combined with environmental factors. The best-established risk factor is insufficient folate (vitamin B9) around conception; certain anti-seizure medicines (especially valproate), maternal diabetes and obesity also raise risk. Taking 400 micrograms of folic acid daily before conception and through the first 12 weeks of pregnancy greatly reduces occurrence, and many countries fortify flour with folic acid. A landmark 1991 Medical Research Council trial showed folic acid prevented about 70% of recurrences.

Diagnosis and treatment

Spina bifida can be detected before birth by maternal serum alpha-fetoprotein screening and ultrasound; the open lesion of myelomeningocele is usually closed surgically within the first days of life, and a landmark 2011 trial (the MOMS trial) showed prenatal surgery can improve outcomes for selected cases. Lifelong multidisciplinary care addresses mobility (often with wheelchairs or braces), urological management, hydrocephalus shunting and orthopaedic problems. Most people with spina bifida now live into adulthood.
