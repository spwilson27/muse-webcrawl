Fuselage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The fuselage (from the French fuseler, "to shape like a spindle") is an aircraft's main body section. It holds crew, passengers, or cargo, and in a single-engine aircraft it also carries the engine. The fuselage also serves as the structure to which the wings and empennage (tail surfaces) are attached.

Typical cross-sectionCircular or oval (pressure vessel) for airliners

Design

Early aircraft used truss structures covered with fabric; by the 1930s stressed-skin metal construction predominated. Modern airliners use a semi-monocoque design: a skin supported by circumferential frames and longitudinal stringers, with the skin itself carrying much of the load. A circular cross-section efficiently resists the internal pressure of a pressurised cabin and minimises weight. Pressurisation loads and repeated cycles make fatigue a central concern; the 1988 Aloha Airlines Flight 243 accident, in which a section of fuselage skin tore away, led to stricter inspection regimes for ageing aircraft.

Geometry

Fuselage fineness ratio (length to maximum diameter) balances internal volume against drag; subsonic transports favour ratios around 6–8. Wide-body airliners use double-deck or oval sections; the Boeing 747's partial upper deck produced its distinctive hump. Newer types such as the Boeing 787 and Airbus A350 use composite barrels wound or laid up as single pieces, reducing joints and allowing higher cabin humidity and pressure.

Other types

Flying boats use hull-shaped fuselages for water landings; some designs (the "lifting body" and blended wing body concepts) merge the fuselage with the wing. Glider fuselages are slender pods, while helicopters call their main body a fuselage or airframe enclosing the cabin and tail boom.
