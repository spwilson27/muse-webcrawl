Flamenco

Flamenco
OriginAndalusia, Spain
ElementsCante (song), toque (guitar), baile (dance), palmas (handclapping)
RecognitionUNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage, 2010

Flamenco is an art form based on the musical traditions of southern Spain, above all Andalusia, encompassing singing (cante), guitar playing (toque), dance (baile), and rhythmic handclapping, finger-snapping, and shouts of encouragement (jaleo). It developed over several centuries from the intermingling of Romani (Gitano), Andalusian, and other musical cultures, and in 2010 UNESCO inscribed it on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

History

Flamenco emerged in recognisable form in the late eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries in the towns of Lower Andalusia — Seville, Cádiz, and Jerez de la Frontera. Romani communities, who had arrived in Spain centuries earlier, were central to its creation, fusing their own traditions with Andalusian folk song and dance. In the later nineteenth century flamenco moved from private gatherings to commercial cafés cantantes, where professional singers performed for paying audiences; this "golden age" fixed many of its classic forms.

Forms (palos)

Flamenco songs are classified into palos — traditional forms distinguished by rhythm, scale, and mood. There are more than fifty, including the profound soleá and seguiriya (cante jondo, "deep song"), the festive alegrías and bulerías of Cádiz and Jerez, the stately tangos, and the farruca. Most are built on a twelve-beat rhythmic cycle (compás) with characteristic accent patterns.

Music and dance

The flamenco guitar developed from the classical instrument, adding techniques such as rasgueado (strumming) and percussive tapping. Great twentieth-century guitarists such as Ramón Montoya, Niño Ricardo, and Paco de Lucía transformed its role from accompaniment to solo artistry. Singers like La Niña de los Peines, Antonio Mairena, and Camarón de la Isla are revered as masters of the cante. The dance is known for its expressive arm movements, proud posture, and intricate footwork (zapateado).

Duende and cultural role

Flamenco aesthetics prize duende — a heightened state of emotion and authenticity, celebrated in an essay by Federico García Lorca. Long stigmatised as the music of the marginalised, flamenco was alternately suppressed and exploited as a national symbol under Franco; since Spain's return to democracy it has been embraced as a living art, with festivals such as the Bienal de Flamenco in Seville and constant fusion with jazz, rock, and world music.
