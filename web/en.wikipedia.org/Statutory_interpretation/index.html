Statutory interpretation

Legal area: Public law
Key approaches: Literal rule, golden rule, mischief rule, purposive approach
Related: Textualism, legislative intent

Statutory interpretation is the process by which courts interpret and apply legislation. Some amount of interpretation is often necessary when a case involves a statute, because the words of a statute may be ambiguous, a statute may have been drafted with future circumstances unforeseen, or a literal application may produce an absurd result.

Approaches in common law

English courts historically applied three principal rules. The literal rule gives words their plain, ordinary meaning. The golden rule permits departure from the literal meaning to avoid an absurd or repugnant result. The mischief rule, from Heydon's Case (1584), directs the court to consider the defect the statute was intended to remedy. Modern practice favours a purposive approach, reading the text in context and in light of the statute's purpose, an approach also dominant in civil law jurisdictions and in the interpretation of European Union law.

Canons and aids

Judges may use internal aids such as definitions sections, headings and long titles, and external aids such as dictionaries, precedent, and — in England and Wales, under Pepper v Hart — statements made by ministers in Parliament. Latin canons of construction include ejusdem generis (general words following specific words take their colour from the specific words) and expressio unius est exclusio alterius.
