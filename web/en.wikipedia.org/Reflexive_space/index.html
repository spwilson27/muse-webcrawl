Reflexive space

A Banach space is reflexive when the canonical embedding into its bidual is surjective.

In the area of mathematics known as functional analysis, a reflexive space is a locally convex topological vector space for which the canonical evaluation map into its bidual (the dual of its dual) is an isomorphism. For Banach spaces, reflexivity means that the natural embedding of the space into its second dual is surjective (and hence, by the open mapping theorem, an isometric isomorphism with an equivalent norm). The concept was introduced in the 1930s in work of Eduard Helly and Hans Hahn, and developed extensively by later functional analysts.

Examples

All finite-dimensional normed spaces are reflexive. Every Hilbert space is reflexive, as are the Lp spaces for 1 < p < ∞, including the sequence spaces ℓp. The Sobolev spaces Wk,p for 1 < p < ∞ are likewise reflexive. Non-examples include c0, ℓ1, ℓ∞, L1, L∞ and the space C([0,1]) of continuous functions; the James space is a famous non-reflexive space that is nonetheless isomorphic to its bidual.

Properties

Reflexive Banach spaces are characterised by the weak compactness of their closed unit ball (Kakutani's theorem combined with the Banach–Alaoglu theorem), which yields powerful existence results: bounded sequences have weakly convergent subsequences, and continuous convex functions on reflexive spaces attain their minima — a fact used throughout the calculus of variations and the theory of partial differential equations. Reflexivity is inherited by closed subspaces and passes to duals and quotients; a Banach space is reflexive if and only if its dual is reflexive. Uniformly convex spaces, including Hilbert spaces and Lp for 1 < p < ∞, are reflexive by the Milman–Pettis theorem.
