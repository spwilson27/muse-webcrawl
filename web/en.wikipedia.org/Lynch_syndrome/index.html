Lynch syndrome

Autosomal dominant inheritance pattern

Also known as: Hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC)

Inheritance: Autosomal dominant

Genes: MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, PMS2, EPCAM

Lynch syndrome, often called hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer (HNPCC), is an inherited condition that greatly increases the risk of many types of cancer, particularly cancer of the colon and rectum. It is caused by germline mutations in DNA mismatch repair genes — principally MLH1, MSH2, MSH6, and PMS2, or deletions in EPCAM — and is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. It is the most common cause of inherited colorectal cancer, affecting roughly 1 in 280–440 people.

Clinical features

People with Lynch syndrome have lifetime colorectal cancer risks of up to about 50–80% depending on the gene, often with onset before age 50, and women face a similarly elevated risk of endometrial cancer. Risks are also increased for cancers of the stomach, small intestine, ovary, urinary tract, biliary tract, brain, pancreas, and sebaceous glands (the Muir–Torre variant). Tumours typically show microsatellite instability (MSI) and loss of mismatch repair protein expression on immunohistochemistry.

Diagnosis and management

Clinical criteria such as the Amsterdam criteria and revised Bethesda guidelines identify candidate families; modern practice increasingly tests all new colorectal and endometrial tumours for mismatch repair deficiency, followed by germline genetic testing. Management includes colonoscopy every 1–2 years from early adulthood — shown to reduce colorectal cancer mortality — risk-reducing gynaecological surgery after childbearing, consideration of aspirin chemoprevention (supported by the CAPP2 trial), and immunotherapy for advanced mismatch-repair-deficient cancers, which respond well to PD-1 inhibitors.
