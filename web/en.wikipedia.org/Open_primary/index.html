Open primary

An open primary is a primary election that does not require voters to be affiliated with a political party in order to vote for partisan candidates. In a traditional open primary, voters may select one party's ballot and vote for that party's candidates regardless of their own party registration or lack of it. Political parties may legally require their primary elections to be open to all voters, as in the case of the Supreme Court decision California Democratic Party v. Jones (2000), which struck down a blanket primary law.

Variations

Open primaries in the United States vary considerably. In some states, a voter must make a public choice of which party's primary to vote in before entering the voting booth. In others, the choice is made privately inside the booth. Some states, such as Michigan and Minnesota, operate effectively open systems in which party affiliation plays no role in primary voting at all.

Arguments

Supporters argue that open primaries produce more moderate candidates, increase voter participation, and allow independent voters a say in elections that may effectively decide the outcome in safe districts. Critics argue that open primaries allow "raiding" or crossover voting by members of an opposing party seeking to nominate a weaker candidate, and that they dilute the ability of parties to define their own membership and message.
