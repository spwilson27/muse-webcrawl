Fourteen Points

Woodrow Wilson presenting his programme for world peace to Congress, 8 January 1918

The Fourteen Points was a statement of principles for peace that was to be used for peace negotiations to end the First World War. The principles were outlined in a speech on war aims and peace terms delivered by United States President Woodrow Wilson to a joint session of Congress on 8 January 1918.

Background

Wilson's address responded to the upheavals of 1917: the Russian Revolution and Bolshevik publication of the Allies' secret treaties, growing war weariness, and the need to articulate liberal war aims. The speech was based on research by The Inquiry, a group of academics assembled to study European conditions.

The points

The fourteen points called for open diplomacy without secret treaties; absolute freedom of navigation on the seas; removal of economic barriers and equality of trade; reduction of armaments; impartial adjustment of colonial claims; evacuation of occupied territories including Russia, Belgium, and France (with Alsace-Lorraine restored); readjustment of Italy's frontiers along lines of nationality; autonomous development for the peoples of Austria-Hungary and the Ottoman Empire; an independent Poland with secure access to the sea; and, in the fourteenth point, the creation of a general association of nations — realised as the League of Nations.

Reception and legacy

The Fourteen Points were widely disseminated as Allied propaganda and were embraced by many in the Central Powers' populations. Germany's armistice request of October 1918 was based on them. At the Paris Peace Conference, however, Allied leaders such as Clemenceau and Lloyd George pursued national interests that diverged from Wilson's vision; the resulting Treaty of Versailles disappointed many, though the League of Nations was established. The points remain a landmark statement of liberal internationalism and national self-determination.
