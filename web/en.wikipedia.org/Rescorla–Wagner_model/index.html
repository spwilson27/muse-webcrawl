Rescorla–Wagner model

The Rescorla–Wagner model is a model of classical conditioning in which learning is understood as changes in the associative strength of stimuli, driven by the discrepancy — the prediction error — between what an animal expects and what actually occurs. Proposed by Robert A. Rescorla and Allan R. Wagner in 1972, it remains one of the most influential formal theories of learning.

The equation

On each trial, the change in associative strength V of a stimulus is ΔV = αβ(λ − ΣV), where λ is the maximum strength supportable by the unconditioned stimulus, ΣV is the total associative strength of all stimuli present, and α and β are learning-rate parameters reflecting the salience of the conditioned and unconditioned stimuli respectively. When the outcome is fully predicted (ΣV = λ), no further learning occurs.

Phenomena explained

The model elegantly accounts for several effects that earlier contiguity-based theories could not, notably blocking (prior conditioning to one stimulus prevents learning about a redundant added stimulus), overshadowing, and conditioned inhibition. Learning is greatest when outcomes are surprising, and the model also predicts the shape of acquisition curves and the effects of extinction when λ = 0.

Limitations and influence

The model does not by itself explain latent inhibition, retrospective revaluation, or changes in stimulus associability; these motivated successors such as the Pearce–Hall model and attentional theories, as well as temporal-difference learning in reinforcement learning, which generalises the same error-correcting idea and links it to dopamine signalling.
