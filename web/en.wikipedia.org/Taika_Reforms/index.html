Taika Reforms

Date: 645–649 CE
Country: Japan (Asuka period)
Leaders: Emperor Kōtoku; Prince Naka no Ōe; Nakatomi no Kamatari
Preceded by: Isshi incident (645)
Followed by: Ritsuryō system

The Taika Reforms (大化の改新, Taika no Kaishin) were a series of political and administrative reforms enacted in Japan beginning in 645 CE, under Emperor Kōtoku. Named after the Taika era, the first Japanese nengō (era name), they aimed to remake the Japanese state on the model of Tang-dynasty China, centralising power in the emperor.

Background

The reforms followed the Isshi incident of 645, a coup in which Prince Naka no Ōe (later Emperor Tenji) and Nakatomi no Kamatari assassinated Soga no Iruka, breaking the dominance of the powerful Soga clan. Reform edicts issued in 646 proclaimed the abolition of private estates and hereditary titles of control over people, declaring all land and subjects to belong to the emperor.

Measures

The reforms introduced a centralised bureaucracy modelled on Chinese practice, provincial governors appointed by the court, household registers and periodic land redistribution under the handen system, taxes paid in rice, labour and cloth, and new administrative units and capitals. Implementation was gradual and incomplete in the seventh century, but the reforms laid the foundations for the later ritsuryō codes that structured imperial Japan.
