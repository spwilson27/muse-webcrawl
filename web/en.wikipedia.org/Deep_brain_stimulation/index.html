Deep brain stimulation
SpecialtyNeurosurgery, neurology
ApproachStereotactic implantation of electrodes
Common targetsSubthalamic nucleus, globus pallidus interna, ventral intermediate nucleus of thalamus

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is a neurosurgical procedure in which one or more electrodes are implanted in specific deep structures of the brain and connected to an implanted pulse generator ("neurostimulator") placed under the skin of the chest. The device delivers controlled electrical pulses that modulate abnormal neural activity. The stimulation is adjustable and reversible, distinguishing DBS from older ablative procedures.

Medical uses

DBS is most established for movement disorders. It is used for Parkinson's disease patients with motor fluctuations or tremor not adequately controlled by medication — typically targeting the subthalamic nucleus or globus pallidus interna — and for essential tremor and dystonia, targeting the ventral intermediate nucleus of the thalamus or pallidum respectively. It is also approved for certain forms of drug-resistant epilepsy (anterior nucleus of the thalamus) and, under a humanitarian device exemption in the United States, for severe obsessive–compulsive disorder. Investigational targets include depression and Tourette syndrome.

Procedure

Using stereotactic imaging and often microelectrode recording, the surgeon places thin leads with multiple contacts into the target nucleus through small burr holes. A pulse generator is implanted in a second, shorter procedure and connected by a tunneled extension wire. Programming sessions over subsequent weeks adjust amplitude, pulse width, and frequency (commonly high-frequency stimulation around 130 Hz).

Mechanism and adverse effects

The precise mechanism remains debated; hypotheses include depolarization blockade, synaptic inhibition, and disruption of pathological oscillatory synchrony in basal ganglia circuits. Risks include intracranial haemorrhage, infection, hardware malfunction, and stimulation-induced effects such as speech disturbance, paraesthesia, mood changes, and (in Parkinson's disease) possible worsening of gait or cognition in some patients.

History

Chronic therapeutic brain stimulation was pioneered in the late 1980s by Alim Louis Benabid and colleagues in Grenoble, who showed that high-frequency thalamic stimulation suppressed tremor. Regulatory approvals for tremor (1997) and Parkinson's disease (2002) in the United States followed large clinical series.
