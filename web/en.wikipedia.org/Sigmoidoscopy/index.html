Sigmoidoscopy is the minimally invasive medical examination of the large intestine from the rectum through the last part of the colon, the sigmoid colon. There are two types of sigmoidoscopy: flexible sigmoidoscopy, which uses a flexible endoscope, and rigid sigmoidoscopy, which uses a rigid device. Flexible sigmoidoscopy is generally the preferred procedure when the term sigmoidoscopy is used, though a rigid scope may still be used in some settings.

Procedure

The patient is usually positioned on their left side. A thin, flexible tube fitted with a light and camera is inserted through the anus and advanced through the rectum into the sigmoid colon, typically reaching about 60 cm. Air may be gently insufflated to expand the bowel wall for better viewing. The procedure usually takes 10 to 20 minutes and often requires no sedation, though bowel preparation with enemas or laxatives is needed beforehand.

Uses

Sigmoidoscopy is used to investigate symptoms such as rectal bleeding, changes in bowel habit, and abdominal pain, and to detect inflammation, ulcers, polyps, and cancers of the lower colon. Tissue samples (biopsies) can be taken and small polyps removed during the examination.

Screening

Several large randomized trials have shown that screening sigmoidoscopy reduces the incidence of and mortality from colorectal cancer, chiefly by detecting and removing precancerous polyps in the distal colon. Some national screening programs offer a one-off flexible sigmoidoscopy around age 55–60, sometimes combined with stool-based testing.

Risks

Sigmoidoscopy is generally very safe. Uncommon complications include bleeding, especially after polyp removal, and perforation of the bowel wall, which is rare. Because it examines only the distal colon, abnormalities in the proximal colon may be missed, and a full colonoscopy may be recommended if findings warrant it.
