Heat pump

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A heat pump is a device that consumes energy (usually electrical) to transfer heat from a cold space to a hot space via a refrigeration cycle, moving thermal energy in the opposite direction of spontaneous heat transfer. By switching the direction of refrigerant flow, a heat pump can provide both space heating and space cooling in buildings, and can also be used for domestic hot water.

Because a heat pump moves heat rather than generating it by burning fuel or resistance heating, it can deliver several times more thermal energy than the electrical energy it consumes. Typical efficiencies, expressed as a coefficient of performance (COP), are between 3 and 5 under moderate conditions, meaning one unit of electricity can move three to five units of heat.

Principle of operation

A heat pump works on the vapour-compression refrigeration cycle. A refrigerant circulates through four main components: an evaporator, a compressor, a condenser, and an expansion valve. In the evaporator the refrigerant absorbs heat from the cold source (outside air, ground, or water) and evaporates. The compressor raises its pressure and temperature. In the condenser the refrigerant releases heat into the heated space and condenses back into a liquid. The expansion valve then drops the pressure, cooling the refrigerant so the cycle can repeat.

Reversing the cycle with a four-way valve allows the same machine to cool a building in summer, acting as an air conditioner.

Types

  • Air-source heat pumps extract heat from outdoor air. They are the cheapest and most common type but lose capacity and efficiency in very cold weather.
  • Ground-source (geothermal) heat pumps exchange heat with the ground through buried pipe loops, benefiting from stable underground temperatures.
  • Water-source heat pumps use a lake, river, or well as the heat source or sink.
  • Exhaust-air heat pumps recover heat from a building's ventilation exhaust.
  • Absorption heat pumps are driven by a heat source such as natural gas or solar-heated water rather than electricity.

Efficiency and climate impact

Heat pumps are widely promoted as a key technology for decarbonising heating, because they can be powered by low-carbon electricity and displace gas or oil boilers. Their performance is rated by the COP for instantaneous efficiency and by seasonal measures such as the seasonal coefficient of performance (SCOP) or, in North America, the heating seasonal performance factor (HSPF). Refrigerants historically used in heat pumps, such as R-410A, have high global warming potential if leaked; newer systems increasingly use lower-impact refrigerants such as R-32 or propane (R-290).

History

The underlying thermodynamic principle was described by Lord Kelvin in 1852. Peter von Rittinger is credited with building the first practical heat pump in the 1850s for evaporating salt brine in Austria. Robert C. Webber built an early ground-source system in the United States in the 1940s, and commercial adoption grew after the energy crises of the 1970s.
