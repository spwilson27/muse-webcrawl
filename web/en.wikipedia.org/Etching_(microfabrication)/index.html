Etching (microfabrication)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In semiconductor and microsystems manufacturing, etching is the process of removing material from a wafer's surface in a pattern defined by a protective mask, usually patterned photoresist laid down by photolithography. Etching transfers the mask pattern into the underlying film or substrate and is one of the fundamental steps of microfabrication, repeated many times in building an integrated circuit.

Wet etching

Wet etching immerses the wafer in a liquid chemical etchant. Classic examples include hydrofluoric acid mixtures (buffered oxide etch) for silicon dioxide, and potassium hydroxide (KOH) for silicon. KOH etches different crystal planes of silicon at very different rates, giving it strong anisotropy useful for carving V-grooves and membranes in MEMS. Most liquid etches, however, are isotropic — they undercut the mask as fast as they etch downward — which limits the fineness of features they can produce.

Dry etching

Dry etching uses gases or plasmas. In plasma etching and reactive-ion etching (RIE), a radio-frequency plasma generates reactive species that chemically attack exposed material, while ion bombardment directed by an electric field makes the etch strongly vertical (anisotropic), enabling the narrow, deep features of modern chips. The Bosch process alternates etching and passivation steps to drill very deep, straight-walled holes in silicon (deep RIE). Ion milling and sputter etching remove material purely physically, with little chemical selectivity.

Key parameters

  • Selectivity: the ratio of etch rates between the target material and the mask or underlying layer.
  • Anisotropy: the degree to which etching is directional rather than undercutting.
  • Uniformity and rate: consistency of the etch across the wafer and between wafers.
