The Voynich manuscript is an illustrated codex, hand-written in an unknown script referred to as Voynichese. The vellum on which it is written has been carbon-dated to the early 15th century (1404–1438). It is named after Wilfrid Voynich, a Polish book dealer who purchased it in 1912. Despite more than a century of study by professional and amateur cryptographers—including American and British codebreakers from both World Wars—no one has succeeded in deciphering its contents, and it has become famous in the history of cryptology.

Contents

The remaining manuscript consists of about 240 pages, and internal evidence suggests some pages are missing. Based on its illustrations, the book appears to be divided into sections dealing with herbs, astronomy and astrology, biology (including drawings of nude female figures bathing in interconnected pools), cosmology (circular diagrams), pharmaceuticals (jars and plant parts), and recipes (short paragraphs of text).

History

The manuscript's early history is murky. It has been associated with the court of Holy Roman Emperor Rudolf II, who supposedly bought it for 600 gold ducats, and with the alchemist John Dee and his associate Edward Kelley. A letter from Johannes Marcus Marci to the Jesuit scholar Athanasius Kircher (1665/66), still attached to the manuscript, is the earliest firm evidence of its existence. Voynich acquired the manuscript in 1912 from the Jesuit college at Villa Mondragone in Italy. After his death it passed to his widow Ethel Voynich, then to Anne Nill, who sold it in 1961 to dealer Hans P. Kraus; Kraus donated it to Yale University's Beinecke Library in 1969.

Hypotheses

Proposed explanations include that the text is written in a natural language disguised by a cipher; that it encodes a constructed language; that it is a glossolalic or otherwise meaningless text; or that the whole book is an elaborate hoax—whether medieval or created by Voynich himself. Statistical analyses show the text has word structures and frequency patterns resembling natural language, with low entropy, but no decipherment has won scholarly acceptance. Claims of solutions continue to appear periodically—involving, variously, proto-Romance language, Hebrew, Turkish, or Nahuatl—but none has been verified by independent scholars.
