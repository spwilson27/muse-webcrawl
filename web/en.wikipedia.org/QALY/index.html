Quality-adjusted life year

QALYs gained shown as the area between treatment curves

The quality-adjusted life year (QALY) is a generic measure of disease burden, including both the quality and the quantity of life lived. It is used in economic evaluation to assess the value of medical interventions. One QALY equates to one year in perfect health; health outcomes are assigned a value on a scale from 1 (perfect health) to 0 (dead), with states considered worse than death sometimes given negative values.

Calculation

A year of life in a health state valued at u contributes u QALYs; for example, ten years lived at a utility of 0.6 equals 6 QALYs. Utilities are commonly measured with standard instruments such as the EQ-5D questionnaire, the time trade-off, or the standard gamble. Health-technology assessment bodies, including the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE) in the United Kingdom, compare interventions by cost per QALY gained and often use thresholds (commonly quoted as £20,000–£30,000 per QALY) in deciding whether to recommend funding.

Criticism

The QALY has been criticised on ethical and methodological grounds: it can appear to value the lives of younger or healthier people more highly, it may not capture all benefits of treatment (such as convenience or effects on carers), and different measurement methods give different values. The related disability-adjusted life year (DALY), used by the World Health Organization, measures overall disease burden as years of healthy life lost.
