Operation Priboi

Date25–28 March 1949
LocationEstonian, Latvian, and Lithuanian SSRs, Soviet Union
Organized byUSSR Ministry of State Security (MGB)
DeportedOver 90,000 people
OutcomeMass deportation to Siberia and Kazakhstan

Operation Priboi ("Coastal Surf") was the code name for the Soviet mass deportation from the Baltic states on 25–28 March 1949. More than 90,000 Estonians, Latvians, and Lithuanians, labeled as "enemies of the people" — including kulaks (wealthy peasants), nationalists, and the families of partisans — were deported to forced settlements in Siberia and other remote regions of the Soviet Union.

Background

The Baltic states had been occupied and annexed by the Soviet Union in 1940, briefly occupied by Nazi Germany from 1941 to 1944, and reoccupied by the Red Army. Armed resistance by the "Forest Brothers" continued after the war, supported by parts of the rural population. Soviet authorities also sought to accelerate collectivization of agriculture, which the independent peasantry resisted. An earlier wave of deportations had taken place in June 1941, and further deportations from Lithuania occurred in 1948.

The operation

The deportation was authorized by a secret decree of the USSR Council of Ministers of 29 January 1949. Lists of deportees were compiled by local organs of the Ministry of State Security (MGB). In the early hours of 25 March 1949, families were arrested at their homes, given little time to pack, and transported by truck to railway stations, where they were loaded into cattle wagons. The operation involved tens of thousands of security troops, party activists, and local auxiliaries. Approximately 30,000 families — over 90,000 people, a majority of them women and children — were deported within a few days.

Consequences

The deportees were settled in special settlements, mainly in Siberia and Kazakhstan, under the supervision of commandants, and compelled to work on collective farms and in forestry. Conditions were harsh and mortality, especially among children and the elderly, was significant in the first years. The operation broke the support base of the partisan resistance and was followed rapidly by mass collectivization in the Baltic countryside. Most deportees were allowed to return only after Stalin's death, in the late 1950s, though many never regained their confiscated property. After the restoration of independence in 1991, the March deportation has been commemorated in Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania as a crime of the Soviet regime and is remembered with annual memorial ceremonies.
