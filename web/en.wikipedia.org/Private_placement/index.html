Private placement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A private placement memorandum used in an unregistered securities offering

A private placement is the sale of stock shares or bonds to pre-selected investors and institutions rather than publicly on the open market. It is an alternative to an initial public offering (IPO) for a company seeking to raise capital for expansion. Investors invited to participate in private placement programs include wealthy individual investors, banks and other financial institutions, mutual funds, insurance companies, and pension funds.

Private placement is the opposite of a public issue, in which securities are made available for sale on the open market to any type of investor. Since private placements are not offered to the general public, they are prospectus-exempt, meaning they do not have to comply with the strict disclosure and registration requirements that apply to public offerings in many jurisdictions.

United States regulation

In the United States, private placements are typically conducted under Regulation D of the Securities Act of 1933, which provides exemptions from registration for offers sold to a limited number of accredited investors and sophisticated purchasers. Rule 506 of Regulation D is the most commonly used exemption. Securities sold in a private placement are "restricted securities" and generally cannot be resold publicly unless registered or sold under another exemption, such as Rule 144A, which permits resale to qualified institutional buyers.

Advantages and disadvantages

For issuers, private placements are faster and less expensive than public offerings, require less disclosure of commercially sensitive information, and allow management to retain greater control. For investors, private placements may offer higher yields or returns in exchange for reduced liquidity and greater risk. Because the securities are not traded on public exchanges, investors may be unable to sell them quickly, and valuations can be difficult to establish.
