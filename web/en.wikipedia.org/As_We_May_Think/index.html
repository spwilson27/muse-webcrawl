As We May Think

"As We May Think" is a 1945 essay by Vannevar Bush which has been described as visionary and influential, anticipating many aspects of information society. It was first published in The Atlantic in July 1945 and subsequently in Life magazine. In the essay, Bush predicted the development of the personal computer, hypertext, the World Wide Web, speech recognition, and online encyclopedias such as Wikipedia.

Background

Bush, who had directed the United States Office of Scientific Research and Development during the Second World War, wrote the essay out of concern that scientists should turn their attention to peacetime problems, particularly the growing difficulty of accessing the expanding record of human knowledge. He argued that the methods of storing and consulting information were inadequate, comparing information retrieval unfavourably with advances in data generation.

The memex

The essay's central proposal is the memex: a device in which an individual stores all of his or her books, records, and communications, mechanised so that it may be consulted with exceeding speed and flexibility. The memex was conceived as a desk with microfilm storage, translucent screens, levers for browsing, and the crucial ability to create "associative trails" — links between documents that users could build, annotate, and share. Bush argued that associative indexing mirrors the way the mind operates, by association rather than by hierarchical filing.

Influence

"As We May Think" influenced the pioneers of computing and hypertext: J. C. R. Licklider acknowledged its impact, and Douglas Engelbart read it as a young serviceman and later cited it as inspiration for his work on interactive computing and the system demonstrated in "The Mother of All Demos". Ted Nelson's hypertext concept and the architects of the World Wide Web worked in its intellectual lineage.
