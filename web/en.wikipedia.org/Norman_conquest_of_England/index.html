Norman Conquest of England

The Norman Conquest was the invasion and occupation of England in the 11th century by an army of Norman, Breton, and Flemish soldiers led by William, Duke of Normandy, later William the Conqueror. It transformed England's ruling class, language, church, and architecture, and is one of the defining events of English history.

The succession crisis and Hastings

When Edward the Confessor died childless in January 1066, the English council chose Harold Godwinson as king, but William claimed Edward had promised him the throne, and the Norwegian king Harald Hardrada also pressed a claim. Harold defeated the Norwegians at Stamford Bridge on 25 September, only to march south to meet William's landing at Pevensey. At the Battle of Hastings on 14 October 1066, after a day-long fight, Harold was killed — tradition says by an arrow in the eye, as shown on the Bayeux Tapestry — and his army destroyed. William was crowned in Westminster Abbey on Christmas Day.

Consolidation

Resistance continued for years, culminating in the "Harrying of the North" (1069–70), a devastating campaign that wasted Yorkshire and surrounding counties. William replaced almost the entire English landholding aristocracy with his followers, built castles across the kingdom — including the Tower of London — and in 1086 commissioned the Domesday Book, an unprecedented survey of land and resources. Norman French became the language of court and government, flooding English with thousands of French words, while Anglo-Norman prelates took over the English church and Romanesque cathedrals rose over the towns.

Legacy

The Conquest bound England to France politically for centuries — a link that led eventually to the Angevin empire and the Hundred Years' War — and set England on a distinct path of centralised feudal monarchy. 1066 remains the most famous date in English history, and the last time the island was successfully conquered by a foreign invader.
