Open government

Open government is the governing doctrine which maintains that citizens have the right to access the documents and proceedings of the government to allow for effective public oversight. In its broadest construction it opposes reason of state and other considerations that have tended to legitimise extensive state secrecy. The origins of open-government arguments can be dated to the Age of Enlightenment, when philosophers debated the proper construction of a then-nascent democratic society.

Principles and components

Open government is conventionally built on three pillars, as articulated by international bodies such as the OECD and the Open Government Partnership:

  • Transparency — government information, spending, and decision-making are proactively published, including freedom of information laws and open data portals.
  • Participation — citizens are given meaningful opportunities to influence policy, through consultations, petitions, and participatory budgeting.
  • Accountability — rules, mechanisms, and independent institutions (auditors, ombudsmen, courts) hold officials answerable for their actions.

History

Sweden's Freedom of the Press Act of 1766, incorporating the principle of public access (offentlighetsprincipen), is regarded as the world's first freedom-of-information law. The United States passed its Freedom of Information Act in 1966, and over 100 countries now have access-to-information laws. In the digital era the concept expanded from documents to data: the 2009 United States Open Government Directive and the launch of data.gov, the United Kingdom's data.gov.uk, and the 2011 founding of the Open Government Partnership — a multilateral initiative in which member countries commit to national action plans co-created with civil society — turned open government into a global reform movement.

Related concepts and criticism

Open government overlaps with open data, e-government, freedom of information, whistleblower protection, and anti-corruption policy. Critics note that transparency alone does not guarantee accountability — data dumps can overwhelm rather than inform, secrecy can migrate to informal channels, and governments may publicise openness while resisting meaningful disclosure. Researchers accordingly distinguish between nominal openness and openness that genuinely redistributes scrutiny and power.
