Torah reading (Hebrew: קריאת התורה, Kri'at ha-Torah, "reading of the Torah") is a Jewish religious tradition that involves the public reading of a set passage from a handwritten Torah scroll, chanted according to traditional cantillation marks (trop). The full Torah is read in the course of a year in 54 weekly portions (parashot), the reading forming the centrepiece of the Shabbat morning service.

Origins and times

Public Torah reading is traditionally attributed to Moses and was institutionalised by Ezra the Scribe after the return from Babylonian exile, as described in the Book of Nehemiah. Portions are read on Shabbat morning (seven people are called up, olim, plus the maftir), on Shabbat afternoon and on Monday and Thursday mornings (three olim, reading the beginning of the coming week's portion), and on festivals, fast days and other occasions, each with prescribed selections.

Procedure

The scroll is taken from the ark with prayers and carried in procession. Individuals called to the Torah recite blessings before and after their section (aliyah); a trained reader (ba'al koreh) usually chants the text while the honouree follows, since the scroll lacks vowel points and punctuation. A pointer (yad) is used to keep the place without touching the parchment. The gabbai supervises and corrects errors; after the Torah reading, the haftarah, a related passage from the Prophets, is chanted. The annual cycle concludes and recommences on Simchat Torah, when the final portion of Deuteronomy and the opening of Genesis are read on the same day.

Triennial cycle

In ancient Israel a triennial cycle, completing the Torah in roughly three and a half years, was customary; it survives in adapted forms in some Conservative and Reform congregations, while the Babylonian annual cycle is universal in Orthodox practice.
