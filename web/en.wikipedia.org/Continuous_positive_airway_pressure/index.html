Continuous positive airway pressure

Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is a form of positive airway pressure ventilation in which a constant level of pressure above atmospheric pressure is continuously applied to the upper airway. The application of positive pressure may be intended to prevent upper airway collapse, as occurs in obstructive sleep apnea, or to reduce the work of breathing in conditions such as acute decompensated heart failure.

Sleep apnea treatment

CPAP is the standard first-line treatment for moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea. Developed by Australian physician Colin Sullivan in 1980 and tested in patients from 1981, the therapy uses a machine that delivers pressurized air through a nasal or full-face mask, acting as a pneumatic splint that keeps the throat open during sleep. Effective treatment reduces apneic events, improves daytime alertness and lowers blood pressure in many patients.

Equipment and variants

Modern devices are compact, often include heated humidifiers, and record usage data. Auto-titrating machines (APAP) adjust pressure through the night; bilevel devices (BiPAP) deliver higher pressure on inhalation and lower on exhalation. Pressures are typically in the range of 4–20 cm H2O, set by sleep study titration or auto-titration.

Adherence and side effects

Long-term adherence is the principal limitation; many patients discontinue therapy because of mask discomfort, nasal congestion, dryness or claustrophobia. Mask fitting, humidification and gradual acclimatisation improve compliance. CPAP is also used, in modified forms, for premature infants with respiratory distress and for some patients with hypoxemia in emergency care.
