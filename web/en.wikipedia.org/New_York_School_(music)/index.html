New York School (music)

The New York School is a loosely defined circle of experimental composers active in New York City in the 1950s and 1960s, centred on John Cage and including Morton Feldman, Earle Brown and Christian Wolff, with the pianist David Tudor as their principal interpreter. The name was coined by analogy with the New York School of abstract expressionist painters, with whom the composers were closely associated.

Style and ideas

Rather than a shared technique, the group shared an attitude: music as an open field of sounds rather than a vehicle for self-expression. Cage developed chance operations (using the I Ching) and indeterminacy; his Music of Changes (1951) and the silent piece 4′33″ (1952) became landmarks. Feldman wrote extremely quiet, slowly unfolding works inspired by the scale and surface of abstract expressionist canvases, and later adopted graphic notation, as did Earle Brown, whose December 1952 is an iconic abstract score. Christian Wolff introduced cue-based pieces in which performers respond to one another's sounds, embedding social and political models in performance.

Context and influence

The circle intersected with the Merce Cunningham Dance Company, Black Mountain College, and the downtown art scene. Its ideas — indeterminacy, open form, graphic scores, the dissolution of the composer/performer hierarchy — profoundly influenced subsequent experimental music, Fluxus, minimalism and sound art.
