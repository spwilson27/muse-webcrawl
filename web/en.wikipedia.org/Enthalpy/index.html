Enthalpy

Enthalpy is a property of a thermodynamic system, defined as the sum of the system's internal energy and the product of its pressure and volume: H = U + pV. It is a state function used in many measurements in chemical, biological and physical systems at constant pressure. The unit of measurement in the International System of Units (SI) is the joule.

Definition and use

Because enthalpy is a state function, only its change, ΔH, is physically meaningful. For a process at constant pressure with only expansion work, the change in enthalpy equals the heat absorbed or released by the system. This makes enthalpy the natural quantity for describing chemical reactions carried out in open vessels: an exothermic reaction has ΔH < 0 and an endothermic reaction ΔH > 0.

Enthalpies of reaction

Tabulated standard enthalpies of formation allow the enthalpy of almost any reaction to be computed via Hess's law, which states that the total enthalpy change is independent of the path taken. Related quantities include the enthalpy of combustion, the enthalpy of fusion and the enthalpy of vaporization.

History

The concept grew out of the nineteenth-century thermodynamics of Rudolf Clausius and Josiah Willard Gibbs; the word "enthalpy", from the Greek enthalpein ("to warm in"), was coined by Heike Kamerlingh Onnes in the early twentieth century.
