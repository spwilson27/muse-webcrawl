The Cayuga people (Cayuga: Gayogo̱hó꞉nǫʼ, "People of the Great Swamp") are one of the five original constituent nations of the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois Confederacy), an alliance of Indigenous peoples of northeastern North America. Their traditional homeland lay between the territories of the Seneca and Onondaga around Cayuga Lake in the Finger Lakes region of present-day New York State.

History

The Cayuga joined the Haudenosaunee confederacy — traditionally credited to the Peacemaker and Hiawatha — as one of its founding nations, and their sachems held seats on the Grand Council at Onondaga. During the American Revolutionary War the confederacy split; most Cayuga allied with the British. The Sullivan Expedition of 1779 devastated Cayuga towns and crops, and afterward many Cayuga removed to Canada or westward. The 1794 Treaty of Canandaigua confirmed a Cayuga reservation, but New York State purchases soon reduced it; by the early nineteenth century the Cayuga in New York had lost their recognised land base, a loss at the heart of modern land-claim litigation.

Cayuga today

Today Cayuga citizens belong principally to three governments: the Cayuga Nation of New York, which has reacquired land in Seneca and Cayuga counties and litigated its status for decades; the Seneca-Cayuga Nation in Oklahoma, descended from those who moved west; and Six Nations of the Grand River in Ontario, where most Grand River Cayuga live. The Cayuga language, a Northern Iroquoian language, is critically endangered, with revitalisation programmes under way at Six Nations and elsewhere.

Culture

Traditional Cayuga society was matrilineal, organised in clans, with longhouse villages sustained by maize-bean-squash ("Three Sisters") agriculture, hunting, and fishing on Cayuga Lake. Ceremonies of the Longhouse religion and lacrosse remain central to community life.
