Sepsis is a life-threatening condition that arises when the body's response to an infection damages its own tissues and organs. Under the Sepsis-3 definitions (2016), sepsis is life-threatening organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection, and septic shock is the subset with profound circulatory and metabolic abnormalities — persistent hypotension requiring vasopressors and elevated lactate — carrying substantially higher mortality. Global estimates suggest around 49 million cases and 11 million deaths annually, roughly one in five of all deaths worldwide.

Signs and diagnosis

Common sources include pneumonia, urinary tract, abdominal, and skin infections; causative organisms may be bacterial, viral, or fungal. Warning signs include fever or low temperature, tachycardia, tachypnoea, confusion, mottled skin, and falling urine output. Diagnosis is clinical, supported by organ-dysfunction scores such as SOFA, blood cultures, and lactate measurement. The older SIRS criteria remain in use in some settings but are neither sensitive nor specific enough alone.

Treatment

Sepsis is a medical emergency. The Surviving Sepsis Campaign's "hour-1 bundle" calls for measuring lactate, taking blood cultures, giving broad-spectrum antibiotics, beginning intravenous crystalloid fluids for hypotension, and starting vasopressors (typically noradrenaline) if blood pressure does not respond. Source control — draining an abscess or removing infected tissue or devices — is essential. Supportive care in intensive care may include ventilation, renal replacement therapy, and glucose control. Survivors may experience post-sepsis syndrome: lasting physical, cognitive, and psychological impairment.

Prevention and awareness

Prevention rests on vaccination, clean water and sanitation, hand hygiene, and prompt treatment of infections. The World Health Assembly recognised sepsis as a global health priority in 2017, and World Sepsis Day is marked each 13 September.
