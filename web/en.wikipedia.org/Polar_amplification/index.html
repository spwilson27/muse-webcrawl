Polar amplification

PhenomenonAmplified warming at the poles
Strongest regionThe Arctic
Key mechanismsIce–albedo feedback, lapse-rate feedback

Polar amplification is the phenomenon that any change in the net radiation balance, for example from intensified greenhouse forcing, tends to produce a larger change in near-surface temperature near the poles than the planetary average. On Earth, the Arctic has warmed several times faster than the global mean in recent decades, a pattern often called Arctic amplification.

Mechanisms

The best-known mechanism is the ice–albedo feedback: as snow and sea ice retreat, darker land and ocean surfaces are exposed, absorb more solar radiation, and warm further. Other contributors include the lapse-rate feedback, which is negative in the tropics but positive in the stable polar atmosphere; increases in water vapour and cloud cover; and changes in atmospheric and oceanic heat transport toward the poles.

Observations

Instrumental records show the Arctic warming at roughly two to four times the global average rate since the late twentieth century, alongside a steep decline in summer sea ice extent. Antarctic amplification is weaker and slower, in part because of the Southern Ocean's large heat uptake and the elevation of the Antarctic ice sheet.

Consequences

Amplified polar warming drives permafrost thaw, ice-sheet mass loss and sea-level rise, and ecosystem shifts. A debated research question is whether shrinking Arctic–midlatitude temperature gradients alter the jet stream and mid-latitude weather extremes.
