Pendentive

In architecture, a pendentive is a constructive device permitting the placing of a circular dome over a square room or space. The pendentives, which are triangular segments of a sphere, taper to points at the bottom and spread at the top to meet the circular base of the dome, transferring its weight to four piers at the corners of the square bay.

History

Although Roman builders experimented with related forms, the pendentive was first fully exploited in Byzantine architecture. Its masterpiece is the Hagia Sophia in Constantinople (completed 537 AD), where architects Anthemius of Tralles and Isidore of Miletus set a dome over 30 metres across on four pendentives. Pendentives became a hallmark of Byzantine churches and were later widely used in Ottoman mosque architecture and in Renaissance and Baroque buildings in Europe.

Comparison with squinches

The squinch—an arch or series of arches across the corner of a square bay—achieves a similar effect and was common in Persian and Islamic architecture. The pendentive, however, forms part of a continuous spherical surface with the dome, creating a more unified geometry.

Decoration

Pendentive surfaces are often decorated with mosaics or paintings; in Byzantine churches they frequently bear images of the four Evangelists.
