Carlism

Carlism (Basque: Karlismo; Spanish: Carlismo) is a Traditionalist and Legitimist political movement in Spain aimed at establishing an alternative branch of the Bourbon dynasty — one descended from Don Carlos, Count of Molina (1788–1855) — on the Spanish throne. The movement was founded as a consequence of the succession dispute at the death of Ferdinand VII in 1833, when pragmatic sanction changes put his daughter Isabella II on the throne ahead of his brother Don Carlos.

The Carlist Wars

Carlism was the driving force in three civil wars: the First Carlist War (1833–1840), the Second Carlist War (1846–1849) and the Third Carlist War (1872–1876), each ending in defeat for the Carlist pretenders. Carlists principally drew support from the rural Basque Country, Navarre and Catalonia, defending Catholicism, regional fueros (historic charters) and the slogan "Dios, Patria, Rey" (God, Fatherland, King).

20th century

Reorganised as the Traditionalist Communion, the Carlists' paramilitary requetés were a significant component of the Nationalist forces in the Spanish Civil War (1936–1939), though Franco's unification decree subordinated them and the movement never recovered the throne. Carlism later fragmented, including a left-wing Carlist Party that reinterpreted the tradition in federalist and self-management socialist terms.
