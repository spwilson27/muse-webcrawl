The Almagest is a second-century Greek treatise on mathematical astronomy by Claudius Ptolemy, written around AD 150 in Alexandria, Egypt. Its original title was Mathēmatikē Syntaxis ("The Mathematical Treatise"); the name Almagest derives from the Arabic al-majisṭī ("the greatest"), reflecting the reverence with which the work was held. It is the principal surviving source on ancient Greek astronomy and remained the authoritative astronomical text in the Byzantine, Islamic and European worlds for more than 1,400 years.

Contents

The work is divided into thirteen books. The opening books set out the geocentric cosmology — a spherical, stationary Earth at the centre of a rotating celestial sphere — and develop the mathematics of chords (equivalent to trigonometry) needed for computation. Later books treat the motions of the Sun and Moon, eclipses, the fixed stars (including Ptolemy's catalogue of 1,022 stars in 48 constellations, still recognisable in the modern constellations), and the planets. The final books present the planetary models for Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn.

The Ptolemaic model

To reconcile the circular, uniform motions demanded by Greek philosophy with the observed retrograde loops of the planets, Ptolemy refined the system of deferents and epicycles inherited from Apollonius and Hipparchus: each planet moves on a small circle (the epicycle) whose centre travels along a larger circle (the deferent) around the Earth. Ptolemy's own innovations — the eccentric, which displaces the deferent's centre from Earth, and especially the equant, a point about which motion appears uniform — made the model remarkably accurate at predicting planetary positions, though the equant's violation of uniform circular motion troubled later astronomers from Ibn al-Haytham to Copernicus.

Transmission and influence

The Almagest was preserved and extended in the Islamic world, where astronomers such as al-Battani and the Maragha school refined its parameters and models, and it returned to Latin Europe through a translation made in Toledo by Gerard of Cremona in the twelfth century. It was the foundation of university astronomy until it was displaced by the Copernican system and, definitively, by Kepler's laws and Newtonian gravitation in the seventeenth century.
