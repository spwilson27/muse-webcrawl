Polyphony

In music, polyphony is a type of musical texture consisting of two or more simultaneous lines of independent melody, as opposed to a texture with just one voice (monophony) or a texture with one dominant melodic voice accompanied by chords (homophony). The term derives from the Greek polyphonia, "many sounds."

History

Polyphony emerged in European art music during the Middle Ages, developing out of organum — the practice of adding a parallel voice to Gregorian chant. The Notre Dame school of the 12th and 13th centuries (Léonin and Pérotin) produced the first substantial repertory of composed polyphony. During the Renaissance, polyphonic writing reached great sophistication in the masses and motets of composers such as Josquin des Prez, Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina, and William Byrd. In the Baroque era, counterpoint — the art of combining melodic lines according to systematic rules — culminated in the fugues of Johann Sebastian Bach.

Techniques

Polyphonic music relies on imitation (one voice repeating another's melody, as in canon and fugue), invertible counterpoint, and careful control of consonance and dissonance between voices. The study of species counterpoint, codified by Johann Joseph Fux in his Gradus ad Parnassum (1725), long formed the foundation of composition training.

World traditions

Polyphonic traditions also exist outside Europe, notably in the vocal music of the Aka Pygmies of Central Africa, Georgian folk singing, Balkan women's choirs, and the gamelan musics of Indonesia, all recognized for their complex interlocking parts.
