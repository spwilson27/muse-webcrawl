A public aquarium or public water zoo is the aquatic counterpart of a zoo, which houses living aquatic animal and plant specimens for public viewing. Most public aquariums feature tanks larger than those kept by home aquarists, as well as smaller tanks. Since the first public aquariums were built in the mid-19th century, they have become popular attractions and their numbers have increased. Most modern accredited aquariums stress conservation issues and educating the public.

History

The first public aquarium exhibition opened at London Zoo in 1853, sparking a Victorian "aquarium craze"; P. T. Barnum's American Museum in New York followed with aquatic displays. Early aquariums struggled with oxygenation and water chemistry until advances in filtration, aeration, and artificial seawater made large collections viable. Landmark institutions include the Monaco Oceanographic Museum (1910), the Shedd Aquarium in Chicago (1930), the Monterey Bay Aquarium (1984), noted for its kelp forest and open-ocean exhibits, the Georgia Aquarium (2005), and the giant aquariums of Asia such as S.E.A. Aquarium in Singapore and Chimelong Ocean Kingdom in China.

Design and operation

Aquariums depend on life-support systems that filter, sterilise, heat, and recirculate huge volumes of water. Acrylic panels—far thicker and stronger than glass—permit the panoramic windows and walk-through tunnels of modern ocean tanks. Exhibits range from local freshwater habitats to coral reefs, kelp forests, and open-ocean communities with sharks, rays, and sea turtles; many also keep marine mammals and penguins. Staff include aquarists, veterinarians, water-quality specialists, and dive teams that feed animals and maintain tanks.

Conservation and debate

Modern aquariums participate in captive breeding, stranding rescue and rehabilitation, and public education; associations such as the AZA and WAZA accredit institutions and coordinate species survival plans. Criticism focuses on the ethics of keeping wide-ranging marine mammals such as orcas and dolphins in captivity—a debate intensified by the 2013 documentary Blackfish—and on the collection of wild specimens, leading several facilities to end cetacean breeding or phase out mammal shows.
