Embryo transfer

Embryo transfer is the step in assisted reproduction in which one or more embryos are placed into the uterus of a female with the intention of establishing a pregnancy. In human medicine it is the final stage of in vitro fertilisation (IVF); in animal breeding it allows a genetically valuable donor female to produce many more offspring than she could carry herself.

In human IVF

Embryos created in the laboratory are cultured for two to six days, graded, and selected; a thin catheter passed through the cervix deposits the embryo(s) into the uterine cavity, usually under ultrasound guidance. Transfer may be "fresh" in the same cycle as egg retrieval or "frozen", using cryopreserved embryos thawed later. Modern practice increasingly favours single embryo transfer to reduce the risks of multiple pregnancy. Surplus embryos may be frozen for later use, donated, or discarded, raising ethical and legal questions that vary by jurisdiction.

In livestock

Embryo transfer is routine in cattle breeding: a donor cow is superovulated, inseminated, and her embryos flushed and transferred to synchronised recipient cows or frozen for transport and sale. The technique, commercialised in the 1970s, accelerates genetic improvement and allows embryos to be sexed and genomically tested before transfer.

History

The first successful embryo transfer in mammals was performed by Walter Heape in rabbits in 1890. Human IVF embryo transfer led to the birth of Louise Brown in 1978, work for which Robert Edwards later received the Nobel Prize.
