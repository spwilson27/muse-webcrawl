Intracytoplasmic sperm injection

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI, pronounced "ick-see") is an in vitro fertilisation technique in which a single sperm cell is injected directly into the cytoplasm of an egg (oocyte) using a fine glass micropipette. Developed at the Vrije Universiteit Brussel, with the first pregnancies reported in 1992 by Gianpiero Palermo and colleagues, ICSI revolutionised the treatment of male-factor infertility because it can succeed with very few, poorly motile, or surgically retrieved sperm.

Indications

ICSI is used mainly for severe male infertility — very low sperm count (oligospermia), poor motility (asthenozoospermia) or abnormal morphology — for sperm obtained by testicular or epididymal aspiration in cases of obstructive azoospermia, after failed fertilisation in conventional IVF, and when preimplantation genetic testing requires minimising contamination by extraneous sperm. It is performed in a large share of IVF cycles worldwide, sometimes also for non-male-factor indications, a practice whose benefits are debated.

Procedure

Following ovarian stimulation and egg retrieval, mature (metaphase II) oocytes are denuded of surrounding cumulus cells. The embryologist selects a single sperm, immobilises it, and, holding the oocyte with a suction pipette, injects the sperm through the zona pellucida into the ooplasm. Fertilisation is checked the following day by the appearance of two pronuclei; embryos are cultured and transferred as in standard IVF.

Outcomes and risks

Fertilisation rates per injected oocyte are typically around 70%, and live-birth rates are broadly comparable to conventional IVF for male-factor cases. Concerns studied in the literature include possible transmission of genetic causes of male infertility (such as Y-chromosome microdeletions and CFTR mutations associated with congenital absence of the vas deferens), slightly increased rates of imprinting and sex-chromosome abnormalities reported in some cohorts, and birth-defect findings that remain contested against background risk.
