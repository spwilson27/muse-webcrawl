Ibn Arabi

Ibn ʿArabī (1165–1240), in full Abū ʿAbd Allāh Muḥammad ibn ʿAlī ibn Muḥammad ibn ʿArabī al-Ḥātimī al-Ṭāʾī, was an Andalusian Muslim scholar, mystic, poet and philosopher, extremely influential within Islamic thought. He is known among Sufis as al-Shaykh al-Akbar ("the Greatest Master") and as Muḥyī al-Dīn ("Reviver of the Faith").

Life

Born in Murcia in al-Andalus, Ibn Arabi travelled extensively through the Islamic world — to Seville, Tunis, Mecca, Anatolia and Baghdad — before settling in Damascus, where he died in 1240. His tomb there remains a place of veneration.

Works and thought

His two most celebrated works are The Meccan Illuminations (Al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya), a vast encyclopedia of spiritual knowledge, and The Bezels of Wisdom (Fuṣūṣ al-Ḥikam). His doctrine is often summarised by the phrase waḥdat al-wujūd ("unity of being"), holding that existence is one and that all things are manifestations of the divine Reality. His thought profoundly influenced later Sufism and thinkers including Rumi's teachers, and remains both revered and controversial.
