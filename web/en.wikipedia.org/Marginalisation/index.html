Marginalisation

Marginalisation (American spelling: marginalization), also called social exclusion, is the process by which individuals or groups are pushed to the edge — the "margin" — of a society and denied full participation in its economic, political, cultural, and social life. Marginalised people typically have reduced access to resources, services, rights, and opportunities, and less influence over decisions that affect them.

Concepts and causes

The idea descends in part from Robert Park's 1928 essay on the "marginal man", describing individuals caught between two cultures. Modern usage is broader: marginalisation is understood as a relational and structural process produced by institutions, markets, laws, and cultural norms, rather than simply by individual misfortune. It is closely related to poverty and social exclusion, but emphasises position and power — a group may be relatively numerous yet still marginalised, and poverty can be one consequence rather than the whole of the condition.

Characteristics frequently associated with marginalisation include race and ethnicity, caste, religion, gender and sexuality, disability, age, migration and citizenship status, mental illness, addiction, homelessness, and rurality. Because these can overlap, analysts often discuss intersectionality: the way multiple disadvantages compound one another. Marginalisation can be produced or reinforced by discriminatory law, segregation in housing and schooling, labour-market exclusion, disenfranchisement, stigma, and lack of political representation.

Consequences

Research links marginalisation to worse physical and mental health, lower educational attainment, higher exposure to violence and environmental hazards, and shorter life expectancy. At the societal level, sustained marginalisation is associated with social unrest, distrust of institutions, and loss of human potential. Psychologists also note internal consequences — feelings of invisibility, powerlessness, and exclusion from mainstream identity.

Responses

Responses to marginalisation include anti-discrimination and equality legislation, affirmative action and other redistributive policies, inclusive education, legal aid and political enfranchisement, social protection floors, and community organising by marginalised groups themselves. International frameworks — the Universal Declaration of Human Rights and the UN Sustainable Development Goals, with their pledge to "leave no one behind" — explicitly target the inclusion of marginalised populations.
