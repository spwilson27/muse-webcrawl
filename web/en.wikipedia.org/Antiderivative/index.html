Antiderivative

In calculus, an antiderivative, inverse derivative, primitive function, primitive integral or indefinite integral of a function f is a differentiable function F whose derivative is equal to the original function f. This can be stated symbolically as F′ = f. The process of solving for antiderivatives is called antidifferentiation (or indefinite integration), and its opposite operation is differentiation.

Properties

If F is an antiderivative of f on an interval, then every antiderivative of f on that interval has the form F(x) + C, where C is an arbitrary constant known as the constant of integration. Essentially, antiderivatives differ only by a constant because the derivative of a constant function is zero.

Techniques

Common techniques for finding antiderivatives include substitution (the reverse of the chain rule), integration by parts (the reverse of the product rule), trigonometric substitution, and partial fractions. Not every elementary function has an elementary antiderivative: for example, e^(x²) and sin(x)/x cannot be expressed in terms of elementary functions, a result formalized by Liouville's theorem and the Risch algorithm.

Relation to definite integrals

The fundamental theorem of calculus states that if F is an antiderivative of a continuous function f over [a, b], then the definite integral of f from a to b equals F(b) − F(a). Antiderivatives are therefore a primary tool for evaluating definite integrals.
