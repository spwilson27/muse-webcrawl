Segregated cycle facilities


A segregated cycle track in Denmark

Segregated cycle facilities are cycling infrastructure marked or physically separated from general motor traffic and, in many cases, from pedestrians. They include cycle tracks (physically separated one- or two-way paths alongside a road), cycle lanes separated by buffer zones or barriers, and fully independent cycle paths. The concept is sometimes known as separation of modes.

Types

Facilities range along a spectrum of separation: painted bicycle lanes on the carriageway; buffered lanes with painted or hatched separation; kerb-, bollard-, or planter-protected tracks; and off-road paths. Junction design is regarded as critical, since most serious collisions involving cyclists occur at intersections — designs such as protected intersections and separate signal phases address this.

Debate

Advocates argue that perceived safety is the key barrier to mass cycling, and that countries with extensive segregated networks — notably the Netherlands and Denmark — achieve high cycling mode shares and low casualty rates. Critics, including some vehicular-cycling proponents, argue that poorly designed facilities increase conflict at junctions, restrict experienced riders, and can imply cyclists do not belong on ordinary roads. Research reviews generally find that well-designed cycle tracks reduce injury risk compared with cycling in mixed traffic.

History

Early cycle paths appeared in the Netherlands in the early 20th century; the Dutch network expanded greatly from the 1970s following the Stop de Kindermoord protests. Similar networks have since been adopted in cities such as Copenhagen, and increasingly in North America and elsewhere under "complete streets" and Vision Zero policies.
