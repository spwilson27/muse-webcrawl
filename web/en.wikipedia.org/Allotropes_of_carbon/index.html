Allotropes of carbon

Allotropes of carbon are the different structural forms in which the element carbon can occur. Carbon is capable of forming many allotropes because it can bond in several hybridisation states — sp3, sp2 and sp — producing materials with dramatically different properties. The best known are diamond and graphite, known since antiquity, while fullerenes, nanotubes and graphene were characterised only in the late twentieth century.

Diamond

In diamond, each carbon atom is bonded tetrahedrally to four neighbours in a rigid three-dimensional lattice. Diamond is the hardest known natural material, has the highest thermal conductivity of any bulk solid, and is a wide-bandgap electrical insulator. Its refractive index gives cut stones their brilliance.

Graphite

Graphite consists of planar layers of hexagonal sp2-bonded carbon (graphene sheets) held together by weak van der Waals forces. The layers slide easily, making graphite a lubricant and the "lead" in pencils, while delocalised electrons make it an electrical conductor along the planes.

Graphene

Graphene, a single atomic layer of graphite isolated in 2004 by Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov, combines exceptional strength, flexibility and conductivity. Its study earned the 2010 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Fullerenes and nanotubes

Fullerenes are closed cage molecules such as buckminsterfullerene (C60), discovered in 1985 by Kroto, Curl and Smalley. Carbon nanotubes are cylindrical sp2 structures with remarkable tensile strength and varied electronic behaviour depending on their chirality.

Amorphous carbon

Amorphous carbon — soot, charcoal, activated carbon and carbon black — lacks long-range crystalline order and is produced by incomplete combustion or carbonisation of organic matter. Other forms include lonsdaleite (hexagonal diamond), glassy carbon, carbyne chains and the predicted Q-carbon phase.
