High-performance computing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

High-performance computing (HPC) uses supercomputers and computer clusters to solve advanced computation problems. Today, computer systems approaching the teraflops-region are counted as HPC-computers. The term is most commonly associated with computing used for scientific research or computational science. A related term, high-performance technical computing (HPTC), generally refers to the engineering applications of cluster-based computing (such as computational fluid dynamics and building virtual prototypes). HPC has also been applied to business uses such as data warehouses, line-of-business (LOB) applications, and transaction processing.

As of recent years, the fastest supercomputers have broken into the exascale era, performing more than 1018 floating-point operations per second (FLOPS). Machines such as Frontier at Oak Ridge National Laboratory and El Capitan at Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory in the United States, and Fugaku in Japan, exemplify the field. Performance is conventionally measured with the LINPACK benchmark and published twice a year in the TOP500 list.

Overview

The most common users of HPC systems are scientific researchers, engineers, and academic institutions. Some government agencies, particularly in the military, also rely on HPC for complex applications. High-performance computers are used for problems that are either too large for standard computers or would take too long to solve: weather forecasting, climate research, molecular modelling, physical simulations (such as computational fluid dynamics), cryptanalysis, nuclear weapons research, and the training of large artificial intelligence models.

Techniques

Parallel processing

HPC systems achieve their performance by dividing problems across many processors working simultaneously. A single HPC system may contain hundreds of thousands of processor cores connected by a high-speed, low-latency interconnect. Programs are typically written using message-passing interfaces such as MPI for communication between nodes and thread- or directive-based models such as OpenMP within a node. Since the 2010s, graphics processing units (GPUs) have become central accelerators in HPC systems, providing very high throughput for arithmetic-heavy workloads.

Computer clusters

A computer cluster is a set of loosely or tightly connected computers that work together so that, in many respects, they can be viewed as a single system. Clusters are usually deployed to improve performance and availability over that of a single computer, while being much more cost-effective than individual computers of comparable speed or availability. Beowulf clusters, built from commodity hardware running open-source software, made cluster computing widely accessible from the 1990s onward.

Measuring performance

The performance of an HPC system is typically measured in FLOPS. The LINPACK benchmark, which solves a dense system of linear equations, supplies the Rmax figure used by the TOP500. Because LINPACK does not reflect every workload, alternative benchmarks such as HPCG (High Performance Conjugate Gradients) have been introduced to give a more representative picture of application performance. Energy efficiency, increasingly important as systems grow, is tracked by the Green500 list in FLOPS per watt.

Costs and challenges

Large HPC installations cost tens or hundreds of millions of dollars to build and consume megawatts of electricity. Writing software that scales efficiently to hundreds of thousands of cores remains a central challenge: communication overhead, load imbalance, and memory bandwidth can all limit real-world performance far below peak FLOPS. The end of Dennard scaling and the slowing of Moore's law have pushed the field toward heterogeneous architectures combining CPUs with accelerators.

Applications

  • Numerical weather prediction and climate modelling
  • Computational fluid dynamics for aircraft and vehicle design
  • Molecular dynamics and drug discovery
  • Genomics and bioinformatics
  • Seismic processing for energy exploration
  • Cryptography and cryptanalysis
  • Training and inference for large-scale artificial intelligence models
  • Financial modelling and risk analysis
