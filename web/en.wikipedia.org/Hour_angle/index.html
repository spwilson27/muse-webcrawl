Hour angle

In astronomy and celestial navigation, the hour angle is the angle between the half plane determined by the Earth's axis and the zenith (the meridian plane) and the half plane determined by the Earth's axis and a given point of interest, typically a star or other celestial body.

The hour angle is measured westward along the celestial equator from the local meridian.

The hour angle is paired with the declination to fully specify the location of a point on the celestial sphere in the equatorial coordinate system. It is expressed in angular measure, commonly in hours, minutes, and seconds, with 24 hours corresponding to a full 360 degrees — one hour of hour angle being equal to 15 degrees.

Definition

The hour angle of a point is the angle between the two planes: one containing the Earth's axis and the zenith (the meridian plane), and the other containing the Earth's axis and the given point (the hour circle through the point). It may be given in degrees, time, or rotations depending on the application.

The angle may be expressed as negative east of the meridian plane and positive west of the meridian plane, or it may be measured westward from 0 to 360 degrees (24 hours). In practice, at the moment a star transits the local meridian — its upper culmination — its hour angle is zero.

Relation to right ascension

The hour angle is related to the local sidereal time (LST) and the right ascension (α) of the object by the relation:

HA = LST − α

Thus, if the hour angle of an object is negative, the object is approaching the meridian, while a positive hour angle indicates that the object has already crossed the meridian and is moving westward across the sky as the Earth rotates.

Uses

Hour angle is essential in telescope pointing, celestial navigation with a sextant, and the design of sundials, where the angle of the gnomon and the spacing of hour lines depend on it.
