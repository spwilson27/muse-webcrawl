South African pilchard

South African pilchard
Scientific name: Sardinops sagax
Family: Clupeidae (herrings)
Range: cold-temperate waters of the southern hemisphere, including the Benguela Current

The South African pilchard (Sardinops sagax) is a species of pilchard — a small, oily forage fish in the herring family Clupeidae — found in the cold-temperate waters off southern Africa, where it is a keystone species of the Benguela Current ecosystem. It is one of several regional populations of the widely distributed Sardinops sagax, which also occurs off Japan, California, Peru, Chile, Australia and New Zealand.

Description and biology

Adults typically reach 20–25 cm in length. They are silvery, schooling fish that feed on phytoplankton and zooplankton by filter feeding, and in turn support large populations of predators including Cape gannets, African penguins, Cape fur seals, dolphins, game fish and sharks. Spawning occurs mainly over the Agulhas Bank, with eggs and larvae carried by currents to nursery grounds on the west coast.

Fishery

The pilchard supports one of South Africa's most valuable purse-seine fisheries, supplying canneries — canned pilchards in tomato sauce are a staple food — as well as fishmeal and bait. The stock famously collapsed in the 1960s under heavy fishing and environmental change, recovered in the 1990s, and has fluctuated since; it is managed by an operational management procedure setting annual total allowable catches.

The sardine run

A related phenomenon is the annual sardine run, in which vast shoals of pilchards move north-east along the KwaZulu-Natal coast in winter, accompanied by thousands of predators in one of the largest marine migrations on Earth.
