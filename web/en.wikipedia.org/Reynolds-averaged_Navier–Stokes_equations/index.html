Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes equations

The Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes equations (RANS equations) are time-averaged equations of motion for fluid flow. The idea behind the equations is Reynolds decomposition, whereby an instantaneous quantity is decomposed into its time-averaged and fluctuating quantities. The RANS equations are primarily used to describe turbulent flows, and form the basis of most practical computational fluid dynamics (CFD) simulations of turbulence.

Derivation

Following Osborne Reynolds (1895), each instantaneous variable φ is decomposed as φ = φ̄ + φ′, where φ̄ is the time average and φ′ is the fluctuation. Averaging the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations yields a momentum equation for the mean velocity that contains an additional term, the divergence of the Reynolds stress tensor:

∂(ρūi)/∂t + ∂(ρūiūj)/∂xj = −∂p̄/∂xi + ∂/∂xj[ μ(∂ūi/∂xj + ∂ūj/∂xi) − ρ u′iu′j̅ ] + fi

The Reynolds stresses −ρ u′iu′j̅ are unknown a priori, giving rise to the closure problem: the averaged equations contain more unknowns than equations and cannot be solved without additional modelling assumptions.

Turbulence models

The Boussinesq hypothesis (1877) relates the Reynolds stresses to the mean strain rate through an eddy viscosity. Widely used eddy-viscosity models include:

  • Spalart–Allmaras – a one-equation model popular in aerospace applications.
  • k–ε model – a two-equation model solving for turbulent kinetic energy k and its dissipation rate ε.
  • k–ω model and its SST (shear stress transport) variant – two-equation models solving for k and the specific dissipation rate ω.
  • Reynolds stress models – which solve transport equations for each Reynolds stress component, at greater computational cost.

Applications

RANS modelling remains the workhorse of industrial CFD — in aerodynamics, turbomachinery, ship hydrodynamics, automotive design and building ventilation — because it provides engineering accuracy at a fraction of the cost of large eddy simulation (LES) or direct numerical simulation (DNS). Its weaknesses include poor prediction of strongly separated flows, swirl and transition, motivating hybrid RANS–LES approaches.
