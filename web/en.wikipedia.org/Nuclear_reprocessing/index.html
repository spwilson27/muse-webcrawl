Nuclear reprocessing is the chemical separation of fission products and unused uranium and plutonium from spent nuclear fuel. Originally developed to produce plutonium for nuclear weapons, it is used commercially to recover material for reuse as fresh fuel, notably in the form of MOX (mixed oxide) fuel, and to reduce the volume and long-term radiotoxicity of high-level waste.

The PUREX process

The industrial standard is the PUREX process: chopped fuel is dissolved in nitric acid, and uranium and plutonium are extracted into an organic phase of tributyl phosphate in kerosene, leaving the fission products and minor actinides in the aqueous raffinate. Plutonium and uranium are then separated from each other by adjusting the oxidation state of the plutonium. The remaining high-level liquid waste is vitrified — incorporated into borosilicate glass — for storage and eventual geological disposal.

Policy and controversy

Countries differ sharply on reprocessing. France, Russia and Japan pursue closed fuel cycles with reprocessing; the United States abandoned commercial reprocessing in the 1970s, chiefly over fears that separated plutonium could be diverted to weapons, and relies on a once-through cycle. Critics also question reprocessing's economics and the historic discharge of radioactive effluents from plants such as Sellafield. The UK's THORP plant closed in 2018 and Magnox reprocessing ended in 2022, ending the British reprocessing era.
