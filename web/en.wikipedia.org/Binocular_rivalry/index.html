Binocular rivalry

Binocular rivalry is a phenomenon of visual perception in which perception alternates between different images presented to each eye. When incompatible images — for example a house to one eye and a face to the other — cannot be fused, the observer sees one, then the other, in irregular alternation.

Characteristics

Dominance durations are stochastic, typically a few seconds, and can be influenced by stimulus strength: increasing contrast, motion or salience of one image lengthens its dominance, as described by Levelt's propositions. Observers can exert limited voluntary control over alternation.

History and study

Rivalry was described by early vision scientists and studied systematically from the 19th century. Modern neuroscience uses rivalry, and its flash-suppression variants, to study visual awareness, because perception changes while the retinal stimulus remains constant — making it a tool for isolating neural correlates of consciousness.

Mechanisms

Models involve reciprocal inhibition between competing representations, adaptation and noise, operating at multiple stages from eye-based competition in early visual cortex to pattern-level competition in higher areas.
