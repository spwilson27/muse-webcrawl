Edward I of England

Edward I (17/18 June 1239 – 7 July 1307), also known as Edward Longshanks and the Hammer of the Scots, was King of England from 1272 to 1307. He conquered Wales, subdued Scotland temporarily, and reshaped English law and government through a series of statutes and parliamentary reforms, making him one of the most formidable monarchs of medieval England.

Edward I, conqueror of Wales and reformer of English law.

Early life and the Barons' Wars

The son of Henry III, Edward was captured with his father at the Battle of Lewes in 1264 during the Second Barons' War, but escaped and defeated Simon de Montfort at Evesham in 1265. He then joined the Ninth Crusade, surviving an assassination attempt at Acre. He was on his way home when he learned of his father's death and his own accession in 1272.

Conquest of Wales

Two campaigns (1276–77 and 1282–83) destroyed the independence of Gwynedd under Llywelyn ap Gruffudd. Edward consolidated his conquest with a ring of great castles — Conwy, Caernarfon, Harlech, and Beaumaris among them — built by Master James of St George. The Statute of Rhuddlan (1284) annexed Wales to the English crown, and in 1301 Edward invested his son, the future Edward II, as the first English Prince of Wales.

Scotland and France

Invited to arbitrate the Scottish succession, Edward asserted overlordship and invaded in 1296, beginning the Wars of Scottish Independence. He defeated William Wallace at Falkirk (1298) but never secured the country against Robert the Bruce. He also fought Philip IV of France over Gascony and expelled England's Jewish population by the Edict of Expulsion of 1290.

Government and legacy

Edward's parliaments and statutes — including Westminster I and II and the Statute of Mortmain — systematised English law, earning him the nickname "the English Justinian". He died at Burgh by Sands in 1307 while marching north against Bruce once more, and was buried in Westminster Abbey.
