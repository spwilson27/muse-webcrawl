Invasion of the Waikato

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The invasion of the Waikato was the largest and most important campaign of the New Zealand Wars. Fought from July 1863 to April 1864, it pitted British Imperial troops under Lieutenant-General Duncan Cameron, supported by colonial forces and Māori allies, against the Kīngitanga (Māori King movement) and its supporters in the Waikato region of New Zealand's North Island. The campaign ended with the defeat of the Kīngitanga at Ōrākau and their retreat into what became known as the King Country, followed by the confiscation of more than a million acres of Māori land.

Background

Governor George Grey and the colonial government sought to break the authority of the Kīngitanga, which resisted further land sales and stood as an alternative centre of power to the Crown. After fighting in Taranaki, Grey concentrated some 12,000–14,000 imperial troops in Auckland and issued an ultimatum requiring Waikato Māori to swear allegiance or retire beyond the Mangatāwhiri Stream. On 12 July 1863 imperial troops crossed the stream — an act the Kīngitanga regarded as the start of invasion.

The campaign

Cameron's forces advanced south along the Great South Road and the Waikato River, supplied by steamers and protected by redoubts, while the defenders built fortified pā designed to withstand artillery. Major engagements included Koheroa, the siege and assault of Rangiriri (20 November 1863), where heavy British casualties accompanied the capture of part of the garrison, the battles of Rangiaowhia and Hairini, and the final stand at Ōrākau (31 March – 2 April 1864), where some 300 defenders under Rewi Maniapoto refused surrender with the famous reply "Ka whawhai tonu mātou, āke, āke!" ("We will fight on for ever and ever"). A parallel campaign opened in Tauranga, where imperial troops were defeated at Gate Pā in April 1864.

Consequences

Under the New Zealand Settlements Act 1863, the government confiscated about 1.2 million acres of Waikato land (raupatu), much of it belonging to Māori who had not fought. Military settlers were placed on the confiscated land and towns such as Hamilton were founded. The grievance of the confiscations endured; in 1995 the Crown formally apologised to Waikato-Tainui and paid $170 million in a settlement acknowledging the injustice of the invasion and confiscation.
