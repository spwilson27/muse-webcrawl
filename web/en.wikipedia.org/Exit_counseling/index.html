Exit counseling

Exit counseling is a voluntary intervention in which a counselor — often working with a concerned family — helps a member of a group regarded as a cult or high-control group to reconsider their involvement and, potentially, to leave it. The term was promoted in the 1980s as a non-coercive alternative to deprogramming, the controversial earlier practice in which members were sometimes abducted and held against their will.

Approach

Exit counselors typically present information about the group's history, doctrines, and practices; discuss models of undue influence and thought reform, drawing on writers such as Robert Jay Lifton and Margaret Singer; and encourage the member to think critically about their commitment. Steven Hassan, a prominent exit counselor and author of Combating Cult Mind Control (1988), developed the BITE model (control of Behavior, Information, Thoughts, and Emotions) to describe how high-control groups regulate members. Sessions usually last several days and are arranged by families, though the member is free to leave the process at any time.

Controversy

Scholars of new religious movements have criticized exit counseling and the anti-cult movement more broadly, arguing that "brainwashing" models lack scientific consensus and that most people join and leave such groups voluntarily. Exit counselors respond that they neither use coercion nor oppose religious belief itself, but target deceptive recruitment and authoritarian control. The voluntariness of counseling arranged under family pressure remains a point of debate, as does the professional status of exit counselors, who are generally not licensed therapists.
