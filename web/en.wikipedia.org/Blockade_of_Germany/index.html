Blockade of Germany

The Blockade of Germany refers to naval blockades mounted by the Allied powers against Germany in the two World Wars. The term is most often applied to the Royal Navy's distant blockade of 1914–1919, which cut Germany off from overseas trade and is credited with contributing significantly to the collapse of the Central Powers. A second blockade operated during the Second World War, alongside the Allied economic warfare effort coordinated by the British Ministry of Economic Warfare.

First World War

From August 1914, Britain used its geographical position to close the two exits from the North Sea — the Strait of Dover and the gap between Scotland and Norway — and systematically intercepted neutral shipping carrying food, raw materials, and nitrates to Germany. The blockade tightened over time, particularly after the United States entered the war in 1917. Germany responded with unrestricted submarine warfare. Severe shortages of food and raw materials followed, culminating in the "turnip winter" of 1916–17 and widespread civilian malnutrition. The blockade was maintained after the Armistice of November 1918 until Germany signed the Treaty of Versailles in mid-1919, partly to compel acceptance of the peace terms.

Second World War

Britain reimposed a blockade in September 1939, though its effectiveness was limited while Germany could draw on resources from the Soviet Union (until 1941) and from occupied Europe. Allied economic warfare expanded with American involvement and the strategic bombing campaign against German industry and transport.
