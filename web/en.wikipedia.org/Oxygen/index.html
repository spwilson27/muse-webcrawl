Oxygen is a chemical element; it has symbol O and atomic number 8. It is a member of the chalcogen group in the periodic table, a highly reactive nonmetal, and an oxidizing agent that readily forms oxides with most elements as well as with other compounds.

Oxygen is the third most abundant element in the universe by mass and the most abundant element in Earth's crust (about 46% by mass). At standard temperature and pressure, two atoms of the element bind to form dioxygen (O2), a colourless, odourless diatomic gas that makes up 20.95% of Earth's atmosphere. Another allotrope, ozone (O3), forms the stratospheric layer that absorbs ultraviolet radiation.

History

Carl Wilhelm Scheele obtained oxygen by heating various compounds by 1772 but published late; Joseph Priestley independently isolated it in 1774 and called it "dephlogisticated air." Antoine Lavoisier recognized it as an element, disproved the phlogiston theory of combustion, and gave it the name oxygène, from Greek roots meaning "acid-former" (reflecting his mistaken belief it was essential to all acids).

Biological role

Oxygen first accumulated in Earth's atmosphere about 2.4 billion years ago in the Great Oxidation Event, produced by photosynthetic cyanobacteria; accumulation of this toxic gas drove a mass extinction of anaerobic life but enabled aerobic respiration, on which nearly all complex life depends. In animals, oxygen is carried by haemoglobin and used in oxidative phosphorylation. In industry, oxygen is produced by fractional distillation of liquefied air and used in steelmaking, welding, rocket propellant, and medicine.
