In the United States, a design patent is a form of legal protection granted to the ornamental design of a functional item. Design patents are a type of industrial design right. Unlike a utility patent, which protects how an article works, a design patent protects only the article's appearance — its shape, configuration, and surface ornamentation — as shown in the patent's drawings.

Requirements and scope

To be patentable, a design must be new, original, and ornamental. Infringement is assessed from the perspective of the "ordinary observer": if an ordinary observer would be deceived into buying one article supposing it to be the patented design, the patent is infringed. Famous design patents include those for the Statue of Liberty, the original Coca-Cola bottle, and many consumer electronics shapes.

International equivalents

Many other countries protect appearance through registered design systems, such as the European Union's Community design and Japan's design registration system. The Hague Agreement provides an international registration route administered by WIPO.

Design patents vs. other rights

Design protection differs from copyright, which does not protect functional articles' shapes as such, and from trade dress under trademark law, which requires proof that a design identifies the source of goods and is non-functional.
