The history of algebra traces the development of the study of equations and algebraic structures from ancient problem-solving traditions to modern abstract algebra. Historians often distinguish three stages of notation: rhetorical algebra, in which solutions are written out in words; syncopated algebra, using abbreviations; and symbolic algebra, with a full notation for unknowns, operations and relations.

Ancient origins

Babylonian scribes (c. 1800 BC) solved quadratic problems by algorithmic recipes, and Egyptian texts such as the Rhind Papyrus solved linear problems by false position. Greek mathematics treated algebra geometrically: Book II of Euclid's Elements encodes algebraic identities as statements about areas. Diophantus of Alexandria (3rd century AD), in his Arithmetica, introduced abbreviations for the unknown and solved indeterminate equations, earning him the title "father of algebra" in some traditions. Indian mathematicians such as Brahmagupta (7th century) worked systematically with negative numbers and zero.

Islamic development

Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi's Compendious Book on Calculation by Completion and Balancing (c. 820) presented a systematic, geometric treatment of linear and quadratic equations; its Arabic term al-jabr ("restoration/completion") became the word "algebra", and al-Khwarizmi's name gave "algorithm". Successors such as Omar Khayyam classified cubic equations and solved them geometrically using conic sections.

Europe and the symbolic turn

Italian mathematicians of the 16th century found general solutions of the cubic and quartic equations (del Ferro, Tartaglia, Cardano — whose Ars Magna of 1545 published them — and Ferrari). François Viète introduced letters for parameters and unknowns, and Descartes standardised much modern notation. The 19th century transformed the subject: Abel and Galois proved the unsolvability of the general quintic by radicals, founding group theory, and algebra expanded into the abstract study of groups, rings, fields and vector spaces — the abstract algebra codified in van der Waerden's Moderne Algebra (1930).
