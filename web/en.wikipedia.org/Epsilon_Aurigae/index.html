Epsilon Aurigae (ε Aurigae, abbreviated Eps Aur) is an eclipsing binary star system in the northern constellation of Auriga. It is famous for its extraordinarily long eclipses: every 27.1 years the primary star is dimmed for roughly two years by an enormous, opaque companion object.

Observational history

The variability was first noticed by Johann Heinrich Fritsch in 1821. Eclipses were observed in 1847–48, 1874–75, 1901–02, 1928–30, 1955–57, 1982–84 and 2009–11. The visible star is an F-type supergiant; unusually, no significant light from the eclipsing companion is seen, leading to the hypothesis that the companion is a smaller hot star embedded in a vast, dusty disc viewed nearly edge-on.

The companion and disc

Observations during the 2009–2011 eclipse, including interferometric imaging with the CHARA array, produced the first direct images of the eclipsing body and confirmed it to be a dark disc of material — several astronomical units across — surrounding a B-type main-sequence star. The disc causes the eclipse's distinctive flat-bottomed light curve and mid-eclipse brightening is consistent with a central clearing in the disc.
