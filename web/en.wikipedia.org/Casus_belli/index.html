Casus belli

A casus belli (Latin for "occasion for war") is an act or event that provokes or is used to justify a war. It is distinguished from casus foederis, the situation in which a treaty ally is obliged to go to war in support of a partner. The term came into wide use in the 17th and 18th centuries through the writings of legal theorists such as Hugo Grotius and Emer de Vattel.

Usage

A casus belli may be an actual attack, such as the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor in 1941, or an incident presented as justification, such as the Gleiwitz incident staged by Nazi Germany in 1939. Governments commonly set out the alleged casus belli in a formal statement when declaring or entering war.

International law

Under modern international law, including the Charter of the United Nations, war is generally prohibited except in self-defence or under Security Council authorisation, so claims of a valid casus belli are scrutinised against the principles of necessity and proportionality. Manufactured or exaggerated incidents used as pretexts are frequently criticised as violating the prohibition on aggression.
