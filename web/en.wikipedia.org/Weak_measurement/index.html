Weak measurement

In quantum mechanics, a weak measurement is a type of measurement in which the interaction between the measuring device and the system is so weak that the system is barely disturbed, yielding correspondingly little information from any single trial. Averaged over many trials, and often combined with a later strong measurement (post-selection), weak measurements reveal quantities called weak values.

History

The theory was introduced by Yakir Aharonov, David Albert, and Lev Vaidman in 1988. Their formalism showed that the weak value of an observable, for a pre- and post-selected ensemble, can fall far outside the observable's eigenvalue range — an effect since demonstrated experimentally, including in optical setups where it is used to amplify tiny effects such as the spin Hall effect of light.

Applications and debate

Weak measurements have been used to probe questions such as the past of a photon in interferometers, to measure quantum states directly, and as a practical signal-amplification technique. The interpretation of anomalous weak values remains debated among physicists and philosophers of physics.
