Engineered wood

Engineered wood, also known as mass timber, composite wood, human-made wood, or manufactured board, includes a range of derivative wood products which are manufactured by binding or fixing the strands, particles, fibres, or veneers of wood, together with adhesives, or other methods of fixation to form composite materials. The panels vary in size but can range upwards of 19.5 by 8 feet (5.9 by 2.4 m) and in the case of cross-laminated timber (CLT) can be of any thickness from a few millimetres to several metres.

These products are engineered to precise design specifications, which are tested to meet national or international standards and provide uniformity and predictability in their structural performance. Engineered wood products are used in a variety of applications, from home construction to commercial buildings to industrial products.

Types

Plywood

Plywood is made by gluing together thin sheets of wood veneer with the grain of adjacent layers at right angles, giving strength in both directions. It is used for sheathing, flooring and furniture.

Laminated veneer lumber and glulam

Laminated veneer lumber (LVL) bonds veneers with grains aligned in the same direction for beams and headers. Glued laminated timber (glulam) stacks dimension lumber with grain parallel, producing long, sometimes curved, structural members used in bridges and large roofs.

Cross-laminated timber

Cross-laminated timber (CLT) consists of layers of lumber stacked crosswise and glued, forming large panels used for floors and walls in "mass timber" buildings, some exceeding 80 metres in height.

Particle and fibre boards

Oriented strand board (OSB), particleboard and medium-density fibreboard (MDF) are made from strands, chips or fibres bonded with resin under heat and pressure, widely used in sheathing, furniture and cabinetry.

Advantages and concerns

Engineered wood uses smaller, faster-growing trees and wood residues efficiently, can be stronger and more dimensionally stable than solid timber of the same size, and stores carbon in buildings. Concerns include the formaldehyde emissions of some adhesives, fire performance (addressed by charring behaviour of mass timber), and end-of-life recycling.
