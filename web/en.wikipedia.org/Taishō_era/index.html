Taishō era

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Taishō era (大正時代, Taishō jidai, "period of great righteousness") was a period in the history of Japan dating from 30 July 1912 to 25 December 1926, coinciding with the reign of Emperor Taishō. The health of the new emperor was weak, which prompted the shift in political power from the old oligarchic group of elder statesmen (or genrō) to the Imperial Diet and the democratic parties. It is thus regarded as a period of liberal movement known as Taishō Democracy in Japan; it is usually distinguished from the preceding chaotic Meiji era and the following militaristic-driven first part of the Shōwa era.

Legacy of Meiji

On 30 July 1912, Emperor Meiji died and Crown Prince Yoshihito succeeded to the throne as Emperor of Japan. In his coronation address, the newly crowned emperor announced his reign's era name, Taishō, meaning "great righteousness". The end of the Meiji period was marked by huge government domestic and overseas investments and defence programmes, nearly exhausted credit, and a lack of foreign reserves to pay debts.

The influence of Western culture experienced in the Meiji era continued. Kobayashi Kiyochika continued to produce ukiyo-e prints adopting a Western style in painting. Growing urbanization, the spread of mass media such as radio and magazines, and the emergence of a modern consumer culture transformed Japanese society during the period.

World War I and after

Japan entered World War I on the side of the Allies in 1914, seizing German possessions in China and the Pacific. The war brought an economic boom, and Japan emerged with expanded influence. The 1920s saw the flowering of party government, with Hara Takashi becoming the first commoner prime minister in 1918. Universal male suffrage was enacted in 1925.

The Great Kantō earthquake of 1923 devastated Tokyo and Yokohama. Emperor Taishō, whose health had long been fragile, died on 25 December 1926, and the era passed to his son Hirohito, inaugurating the Shōwa era.
