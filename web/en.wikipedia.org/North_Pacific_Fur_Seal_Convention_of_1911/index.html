North Pacific Fur Seal Convention of 1911

Signed7 July 1911, Washington, D.C.
PartiesUnited States, Great Britain (for Canada), Japan, Russia
SubjectProhibition of pelagic sealing; conservation of northern fur seals
EffectHerd recovered from ~125,000 to about 1.3 million by the 1950s
SupersededInterim Convention of 1957

The North Pacific Fur Seal Convention of 1911, formally the Convention between the United States and Other Powers Providing for the Preservation and Protection of Fur Seals, was a treaty signed on 7 July 1911 by the United States, Great Britain (on behalf of Canada), Japan and Russia. It is often described as the first international treaty for the conservation of wildlife.

Background

Commercial pelagic sealing — killing fur seals at sea, where most carcasses were lost and many were breeding females — had devastated the northern fur seal herds of the Pribilof Islands and the Commander and Robben islands, reducing them from millions to a remnant by the early twentieth century. An earlier dispute led to the Bering Sea arbitration of 1893, which imposed regulations but failed to protect the herds, since non-party nationals remained free to hunt.

Terms and effect

The convention prohibited pelagic sealing in the North Pacific north of the 30th parallel, while the United States and Russia continued regulated land harvests on the rookery islands and shared a portion of skins and revenue with Canada and Japan. With the at-sea hunt ended, the Pribilof herd recovered dramatically, growing from about 125,000 animals to roughly 1.3 million by mid-century. Japan withdrew in 1940; a new interim convention among the four parties followed in 1957.
