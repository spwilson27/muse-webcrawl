Eid al-Adha

Eid al-Adha

TypeIslamic religious festival

Eid al-Adha (Arabic: عيد الأضحى, "Feast of the Sacrifice") is one of the two main Islamic festivals, the other being Eid al-Fitr. It honours the willingness of the prophet Ibrahim (Abraham) to sacrifice his son as an act of obedience to God, and God's provision of a ram to be sacrificed in the son's place. It falls on the 10th day of Dhu al-Hijjah, the final month of the Islamic lunar calendar, and coincides with the culmination of the annual Hajj pilgrimage to Mecca.

Observances

The festival begins with a special congregational prayer (Salat al-Eid) held in mosques and open grounds, followed by sermons and greetings of "Eid Mubarak" ("Blessed Eid"). Those who can afford it perform qurbani, the ritual sacrifice of a sheep, goat, cow or camel, distributing the meat in three parts: one for the family, one for relatives and friends, and one for the poor. Charity, visiting relatives, festive meals and new clothes are central to the celebration, which lasts three to four days in many countries and is a public holiday across the Muslim world.

Significance

Eid al-Adha commemorates devotion, submission and divine mercy. The sacrifice recalls Ibrahim's test of faith, while the distribution of meat emphasises generosity and social solidarity. During the Hajj, pilgrims at Mina perform the ritual stoning of the pillars (ramy al-jamarat) on the same day, reenacting Abraham's rejection of Satan.

Names around the world

The festival is known as Kurban Bayramı in Turkey, Tabaski in West Africa, Bakra Eid or Bari Eid in South Asia, Hari Raya Haji in Malaysia and Indonesia, and Idul Adha in Indonesian. Dates vary slightly by country according to local moon-sighting practices.
