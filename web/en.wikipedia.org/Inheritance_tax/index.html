Inheritance tax

An inheritance tax is a tax paid by a person who inherits money or property of a person who has died, as opposed to an estate tax, which is a levy on the estate (money and property) of a person who has died. However, this distinction is not always observed; for example, the UK's "inheritance tax" is a levy on the deceased's estate.

History

Taxes on transfers at death date to antiquity: Rome levied the vicesima hereditatium, a 5 per cent tax on inheritances instituted by Augustus. Modern inheritance and estate taxes developed in the 19th and early 20th centuries — the United Kingdom introduced estate duty in 1894, and the United States enacted a federal estate tax in 1916.

By jurisdiction

In the United Kingdom, Inheritance Tax is charged at 40% on the value of an estate above the nil-rate band threshold (with additional residence allowances), subject to exemptions for spouses, civil partners, and charities. In the United States, the federal government levies an estate tax with a large per-person exemption, while several states impose their own estate or inheritance taxes. Many countries, including Canada, Australia, and Sweden, have abolished death duties, though capital gains tax may apply to inherited assets.

Debate

Supporters argue such taxes reduce wealth concentration and raise revenue progressively; opponents call them "death taxes", arguing they penalize saving and can burden family businesses and farms. Most systems include thresholds and reliefs that exempt the great majority of estates.
