An oceanarium is a facility in which marine animals are kept for study, display and performance. The term is used in two main senses: as a synonym for a large public saltwater aquarium, and more specifically for a marine mammal park — an establishment, often outdoors and built around very large seawater tanks, where dolphins, orcas, sea lions and other marine mammals are displayed and traditionally perform in shows.

History

The first oceanarium in the modern sense was Marine Studios at Marineland, Florida, opened in 1938: originally designed as an underwater film studio where marine life could be photographed through portholes, it became an enormously popular attraction and pioneered the keeping of bottlenose dolphins in captivity and their display in performances. Its success inspired imitators such as SeaWorld (founded 1964) and oceanaria across Europe, Asia and Oceania, where the word often denotes a large aquarium complex with walk-through underwater tunnels.

Exhibits and operation

Oceanaria typically maintain large-volume tanks — some holding millions of litres — with powerful filtration and life-support systems maintaining seawater chemistry, temperature and clarity. Exhibits range from shark lagoons and ray touch pools to cold-water kelp forests and polar areas. Institutions increasingly emphasise education, rescue and rehabilitation of stranded animals, and participation in captive breeding programmes for threatened species.

Criticism

The keeping of cetaceans in captivity has become controversial on animal-welfare grounds: critics argue that large, social, wide-ranging predators such as orcas cannot thrive in tanks, while operators cite advances in veterinary care, enrichment and the value of public education and conservation funding. Several countries have banned or restricted cetacean captivity, and prominent parks have ended orca breeding and theatrical shows.
