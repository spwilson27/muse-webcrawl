Retinol, also called vitamin A1, is a fat-soluble vitamin in the vitamin A family, found in food and used as a dietary supplement. It is a diterpenoid alcohol essential for vision, growth, immune function, and the maintenance of epithelial tissues.

Biological role

In the body, retinol is stored mainly in the liver as retinyl esters and circulates bound to retinol-binding protein. It is oxidised to retinal, the chromophore of rhodopsin and the other visual pigments, and further to retinoic acid, a hormone-like molecule that regulates gene expression through nuclear retinoic acid receptors (RAR and RXR), controlling embryonic development, cell differentiation, and immunity. Animals obtain retinol from animal foods directly or by converting plant carotenoids such as beta-carotene.

Deficiency and excess

Vitamin A deficiency remains a leading preventable cause of childhood blindness worldwide: early night blindness progresses to xerophthalmia and corneal destruction, and deficiency also increases mortality from infections such as measles. Supplementation programmes in low-income countries have substantially reduced child deaths. Conversely, excessive intake of preformed retinol is toxic (hypervitaminosis A) and teratogenic, so intake in pregnancy must be controlled; overdoses of polar-bear or other carnivore liver feature in accounts of Arctic exploration.

Uses

Retinol and related retinoids are used in medicine and cosmetics. Tretinoin (all-trans retinoic acid) treats acne and acute promyelocytic leukaemia; isotretinoin is used for severe acne; and over-the-counter retinol creams are marketed for photoaging, since retinoids increase collagen production and epidermal turnover, though they commonly cause irritation and photosensitivity.
