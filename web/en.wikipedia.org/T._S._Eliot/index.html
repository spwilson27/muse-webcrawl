T. S. Eliot

Born: 26 September 1888, St. Louis, Missouri, US
Died: 4 January 1965, London, England
Occupation: Poet, essayist, playwright, critic
Notable works: The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock, The Waste Land, Four Quartets
Awards: Nobel Prize in Literature (1948)

Thomas Stearns Eliot (26 September 1888 – 4 January 1965) was a poet, essayist, playwright, literary critic and editor, widely regarded as one of the twentieth century's major poets and a central figure in English-language Modernism. Born in St. Louis, Missouri, he moved to England in 1914 and became a British citizen in 1927.

Poetry

Eliot first attracted attention with "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock" (1915). His long poem The Waste Land (1922), edited with the help of Ezra Pound, became one of the most influential poems of the century, capturing post-war disillusionment. Later works include "The Hollow Men" (1925), Ash Wednesday (1930) and Four Quartets (1943), the last often considered his masterpiece.

Criticism and drama

As a critic, Eliot reshaped literary taste with essays such as "Tradition and the Individual Talent" (1919). He worked for many years at the publishing house Faber and Faber. His verse dramas include Murder in the Cathedral (1935) and The Cocktail Party (1949); his light verse for children, Old Possum's Book of Practical Cats (1939), later inspired the musical Cats.

Honours

Eliot received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1948 "for his outstanding, pioneer contribution to present-day poetry". He died in London in 1965 and his ashes were interred at East Coker, Somerset.
