Michelson–Morley experiment

The Michelson–Morley experiment was an attempt in 1887 by American physicists Albert A. Michelson and Edward W. Morley to detect the Earth's motion through the luminiferous aether, the hypothetical medium then thought necessary for the propagation of light. Using a highly sensitive interferometer, they found no significant difference in the speed of light in perpendicular directions — a "null result" that became some of the strongest evidence against aether theories and a foundational influence on Einstein's theory of special relativity (1905).

Design

The interferometer split a beam of light into two perpendicular paths, reflected each from mirrors, and recombined them to produce interference fringes. If the Earth moved through the aether, the beam travelling parallel to that motion should take slightly longer than the perpendicular beam, shifting the fringes as the apparatus was rotated. The optical path was lengthened to about 11 metres by multiple reflections, and the whole instrument floated in a trough of mercury to permit smooth rotation. The expected fringe shift was about 0.4 of a fringe; the observed displacement was less than a twentieth of that, consistent with zero.

Aftermath and interpretation

The null result puzzled physicists for two decades. FitzGerald and Lorentz proposed that motion through the aether physically contracts objects, rescuing the aether hypothesis; Einstein's special relativity instead made the aether superfluous by postulating the constancy of the speed of light in all inertial frames. Later repetitions — including Dayton Miller's disputed positive claims on Mount Wilson, and modern resonator experiments — have confirmed the null result to precisions better than one part in 1017. Michelson received the 1907 Nobel Prize in Physics, the first American so honored in the sciences.
