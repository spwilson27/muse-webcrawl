Triple junction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A triple junction is the point where the boundaries of three tectonic plates meet. At a triple junction each of the three boundaries is one of three types: a ridge (R), a trench (T), or a transform fault (F). Combinations are described with these letters — for example, an RRR junction joins three spreading ridges. The theory of triple junctions was developed in the late 1960s by W. Jason Morgan, Dan McKenzie, and Robert Parker as plate tectonics became established.

Stability

Some triple junctions are stable, persisting over geological time as the plates move, while others are unstable and exist only briefly before evolving into other configurations. RRR junctions are always stable. The Mendocino Triple Junction off northern California, where the San Andreas Fault, the Cascadia subduction zone, and the Mendocino Fracture Zone meet, is an example of an FFT-type junction that migrates northward with time.

Examples

The Afar Triple Junction in East Africa joins the Red Sea Rift, the Gulf of Aden Rift, and the East African Rift, and is splitting the African Plate into the Nubian and Somali plates. The Azores Triple Junction joins the North American, Eurasian, and African plates along the Mid-Atlantic Ridge. The Chile Triple Junction, where the Chile Rise is subducted beneath South America, is a rare ridge-trench-trench example.
