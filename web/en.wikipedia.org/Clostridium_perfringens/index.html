Clostridium perfringens is a Gram-positive, rod-shaped, anaerobic, spore-forming bacterium of the genus Clostridium. It is ever-present in nature and can be found as a normal component of decaying vegetation, marine sediment, the intestinal tract of humans and other vertebrates, insects, and soil. It is one of the most common causes of food poisoning, and some strains cause gas gangrene, a rapidly progressive and potentially fatal infection of wounded tissue.

Characteristics

C. perfringens forms hardy spores that survive cooking and germinate when food cools slowly or is held warm. It grows unusually fast, with generation times under optimal conditions of less than ten minutes, and at warmer temperatures than most pathogens. Strains are classified into types (A–G) according to which of several major toxins they produce — alpha, beta, epsilon, iota, enterotoxin and NetB among them. The alpha toxin, a phospholipase, is the chief agent of tissue destruction in gas gangrene.

Disease

In foodborne illness, large numbers of ingested bacteria produce enterotoxin in the intestine, causing watery diarrhoea and cramps that typically begin 6–24 hours after eating contaminated meat or poultry and resolve within a day or two. In gas gangrene (clostridial myonecrosis), spores contaminating deep wounds germinate in oxygen-poor tissue; the bacteria ferment muscle carbohydrates, producing gas that dissects the tissue, while toxins destroy cells and blood vessels, demanding urgent surgery and antibiotics. A rare, often fatal variant of infection, enteritis necroticans ("pigbel"), is caused by beta-toxin-producing type C strains.

Prevention

Control depends on thorough cooking, prompt refrigeration of leftovers, and reheating to steaming temperatures, since the vegetative cells are heat-sensitive even though spores are not. C. perfringens food poisoning ranks among the most common foodborne illnesses in the United States, with an estimated million cases a year.
