Paganism

Paganism
EtymologyFrom Latin paganus, "country dweller"
Historical useNon-Christian (later non-monotheistic) religions
Modern useNeopagan movements

Paganism is a term first used in the fourth century by early Christians for people in the Roman Empire who practiced polytheism or ethnic religions other than Judaism. It derives from the Latin paganus, meaning a villager or rustic, and came to denote those who adhered to the old religions as Christianity spread from the cities into the countryside.

Historical usage

In the Christian Roman Empire, "pagan" became a catch-all label for practitioners of traditional Greco-Roman religion, Egyptian and Near Eastern cults, and the religions of northern Europe. Theodosius I banned pagan sacrifices in the 390s CE, and over subsequent centuries Christianization largely displaced the old cults in Europe, though folk practices survived in rural areas — the origin of the word's association with country people.

Modern Paganism

From the mid-twentieth century, the word was reclaimed by practitioners of new religious movements — collectively called modern Paganism or Neopaganism — that revive or reconstruct pre-Christian traditions. These include Wicca, founded in England in the 1950s by Gerald Gardner; Druidry; Heathenry, which reconstructs Germanic religion; and revivals of Hellenic, Roman, Slavic, and Egyptian traditions. Modern Pagans commonly celebrate a Wheel of the Year of eight seasonal festivals, revere nature, and practice in small groups or solitarily. Census and survey data suggest over a million adherents in the United States and tens of thousands in the United Kingdom.
