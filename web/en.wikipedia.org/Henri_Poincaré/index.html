Henri Poincaré

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jules Henri Poincaré (29 April 1854 – 17 July 1912) was a French mathematician, theoretical physicist, engineer, and philosopher of science. Often described as a polymath and the last person to excel in all branches of mathematics as then known, he made fundamental contributions to topology, celestial mechanics, mathematical physics, and the theory of dynamical systems, and he is regarded as a co-founder of the theory of relativity's mathematical framework and of chaos theory.

Life

Poincaré was born in Nancy into an influential family — his cousin Raymond Poincaré became President of France. He studied at the École Polytechnique and the École des Mines, taking his doctorate in 1879. After a brief period as a mining engineer and teaching at Caen, he joined the University of Paris (the Sorbonne) in 1881 and held chairs there for the rest of his life. He was elected to the Académie des sciences in 1887 and to the Académie française in 1908, a rare double honour.

Work

Celestial mechanics and chaos. In his prize memoir on the three-body problem (1889–1890) for King Oscar II of Sweden, Poincaré discovered that even simple gravitational systems could behave in extraordinarily complicated, effectively unpredictable ways — the phenomenon of homoclinic tangles, now recognised as deterministic chaos. His three-volume Les méthodes nouvelles de la mécanique céleste (1892–1899) transformed the field.

Topology. With Analysis Situs (1895) and its supplements, Poincaré founded algebraic topology, introducing the fundamental group and homology. In 1904 he posed the Poincaré conjecture — that every simply connected, closed 3-manifold is a 3-sphere — which stood for a century until proved by Grigori Perelman in 2002–2003.

Mathematical physics and relativity. Poincaré developed the theory of automorphic functions, introduced the Poincaré disk model of hyperbolic geometry, and, in papers of 1900–1906 on the dynamics of the electron, arrived at much of the mathematics of special relativity, including the Lorentz transformations and their group structure, contemporaneously with Einstein's 1905 paper.

Philosophy of science. In popular books such as Science and Hypothesis (1902), he argued for conventionalism — that the axioms of geometry and parts of physics are conventions chosen for convenience rather than truths forced on us — and gave classic accounts of mathematical creativity and intuition.

Legacy

The Institut Henri Poincaré in Paris, the Poincaré Medal, and numerous mathematical objects — the Poincaré conjecture, Poincaré duality, the Poincaré–Hopf theorem, Poincaré maps and recurrence — bear his name.
