Tulum (Yucatec Maya: Tulu'um) is the site of a pre-Columbian Maya walled city on the Caribbean coast of Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula, in the modern state of Quintana Roo. It served as a major port for the inland city of Coba and was one of the last cities built and inhabited by the Maya, flourishing between the thirteenth and fifteenth centuries and surviving for some seventy years after the Spanish conquest began.

Description

Tulum stands on 12-metre-high cliffs above a sandy cove. It is protected on the landward side by a great wall, an unusual feature that gave the site its modern name—tulum means "wall" or "fence" in Yucatec Maya; its ancient name may have been Zama, "dawn", for its eastward outlook over the sea. The principal structure, El Castillo, combines temple and shrine functions and may have served as a landmark to guide trading canoes through the break in the offshore reef.

Other notable buildings include the Temple of the Frescoes, whose interior murals depict Maya deities, and the Temple of the Descending God, named for a distinctive upside-down figure carved above its doorway, a motif found across the site.

Trade

Tulum was a hub of maritime trade, receiving goods such as copper, jade, obsidian, ceramics and salt from across Mesoamerica and exporting local products. In 1518 the expedition of Juan de Grijalva sighted the city, comparing its size to Seville.

Tourism

Today Tulum is one of Mexico's most visited archaeological sites, and the modern town and beach zone south of the ruins have become a major resort destination on the Riviera Maya.
