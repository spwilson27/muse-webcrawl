Emergence

Emergence
FieldPhilosophy of science, complex systems
Key distinctionWeak vs. strong emergence
Related conceptsReductionism, supervenience, self-organization

In philosophy, systems theory, science, and art, emergence occurs when a complex entity has properties or behaviours that its parts do not have on their own; the properties or behaviours emerge only when the parts interact in a wider whole. Emergent properties are central to the study of complex systems such as ant colonies, brains, ecosystems, economies, and the weather.

Weak and strong emergence

Weak emergence describes properties that are unexpected given the parts but can in principle be derived from them — usually by simulation — once the interactions are understood. Examples include flocking behaviour, traffic jams, and the patterns of cellular automata such as Conway's Game of Life. Most scientists who use the term mean weak emergence.

Strong emergence is the more controversial claim that higher-level properties cannot, even in principle, be reduced to or predicted from lower-level facts, and may exert their own causal powers ("downward causation"). Consciousness is the most frequently cited candidate. Critics, following arguments by Jaegwon Kim and others, argue that strong emergence conflicts with the causal closure of the physical world; defenders reply that reductionist accounts omit genuinely novel structure at higher levels. The British emergentists of the 1920s, including C. D. Broad and Samuel Alexander, developed early versions of the doctrine.

Examples

Classical examples include the wetness of water, which is not a property of individual H₂O molecules considered in isolation; the temperature of a gas, which is a statistical property of molecular motion; the flocking murmurations of starlings; and the global behaviour of ant colonies arising from simple local rules followed by individuals with no central control. Philip W. Anderson's 1972 essay "More Is Different" argued that at each level of complexity entirely new laws and concepts are needed, a slogan often used to defend the scientific legitimacy of emergent phenomena.
