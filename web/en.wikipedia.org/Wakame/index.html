Wakame

Wakame
Scientific name: Undaria pinnatifida
Type: Brown algae (kelp)
Origin: Cold-temperate coasts of Japan, Korea, China
Uses: Soups, salads, furikake seasoning

Wakame (Japanese: ワカメ, wakame) is an edible seaweed — the kelp species Undaria pinnatifida — widely cultivated and eaten in Japan, Korea (where it is called miyeok), and China. It has a subtly sweet flavour and a smooth, slippery texture, and its fronds expand greatly when rehydrated from the dried form in which it is usually sold.

Culinary use

In Japan, wakame is most familiar in miso soup and in sunomono salads, often served with cucumber and vinegar; it is also blanched and dressed as wakame no su or tossed with sesame. In Korea, miyeok-guk, a wakame soup seasoned with soy sauce, garlic, and beef or seafood, is traditionally eaten on birthdays and by new mothers, who consume it for its iron and calcium. In China the seaweed is called qundaicai and used in soups and salads. Wakame is rich in iodine, calcium, omega-3 fatty acids (EPA), and the carotenoid fucoxanthin, and is low in calories.

Cultivation and invasiveness

Japan and Korea produce most of the world crop, grown on ropes suspended in the sea, mainly from autumn to spring. Because it spreads readily on ships' hulls and aquaculture equipment, Undaria pinnatifida has become an invasive species in many regions, including New Zealand, Australia, the Mediterranean, and the Atlantic coasts of Europe and the Americas, and it is listed among the world's worst invasive alien species; several countries regulate its harvesting and sale.
