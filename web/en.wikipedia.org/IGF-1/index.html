Insulin-like growth factor 1

Insulin-like growth factor 1 (IGF-1), also called somatomedin C, is a hormone similar in molecular structure to insulin which plays an important role in childhood growth, and has anabolic effects in adults. In the 1950s IGF-1 was called a "sulfation factor" and in the 1970s "somatomedin". It consists of 70 amino acids in a single chain with three intramolecular disulfide bridges, and has a molecular weight of about 7,649 Da.

Production and regulation

IGF-1 is produced primarily by the liver as an endocrine hormone as well as in target tissues in a paracrine/autocrine fashion. Production is stimulated by growth hormone (GH) and suppressed by undernutrition, GH insensitivity, and lack of GH receptors. About 98% of circulating IGF-1 is bound to one of six IGF-binding proteins (IGFBPs), most abundantly IGFBP-3, which extend its half-life and regulate its activity.

Mechanism of action

IGF-1 binds the IGF-1 receptor, a receptor tyrosine kinase on the surface of many cell types, triggering intracellular signaling through the PI3K/AKT and MAPK pathways. It can also bind the insulin receptor, though with lower affinity, and a hybrid receptor. Its effects include stimulating cell growth and proliferation, inhibiting programmed cell death, increasing protein synthesis in skeletal muscle, and promoting the growth of bone and cartilage — the principal mediator of the effects of growth hormone.

Clinical significance

Deficiency of IGF-1, as in Laron syndrome (growth hormone receptor deficiency), causes severe growth failure and short stature, while excess production (usually from a pituitary tumor causing excess GH) leads to acromegaly in adults and gigantism in children. Recombinant human IGF-1, mecasermin, is used to treat severe primary IGF-1 deficiency. Blood IGF-1 levels are used diagnostically in evaluating growth disorders and acromegaly. Research also links IGF-1 signaling to aging, cancer risk, and metabolic disease, though findings remain an active area of study.
