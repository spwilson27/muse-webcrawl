E-book

An e-reader displaying an e-book (placeholder image)

An electronic book, commonly called an e-book or eBook, is a book publication made available in digital form, consisting of text, images, or both, that can be read on computers and other electronic devices. E-books are delivered via the internet and read on dedicated e-readers, tablets, smartphones or computers.

History

The conceptual origins of e-books are often traced to Project Gutenberg, founded by Michael S. Hart in 1971 to digitize and distribute public-domain texts. Early dedicated readers appeared in the late 1990s, but the market expanded rapidly after Amazon launched the Kindle in 2007, followed by the iPad and other tablets in 2010. E-book sales grew to a substantial share of trade publishing before stabilizing alongside print.

Formats

Common formats include EPUB, the open standard supported by most platforms; Amazon's Kindle formats (AZW/KFX); PDF, which preserves fixed layout; and plain text and HTML. Many commercial e-books are protected by digital rights management (DRM), which restricts copying and transfer between devices.

Advantages and disadvantages

E-books are instantly downloadable, portable in large numbers, searchable, and can offer adjustable font sizes and built-in dictionaries, aiding accessibility. Criticisms include eye strain on some screens, dependence on proprietary ecosystems, uncertain ownership rights compared with print, and generally lower resale or lending flexibility.

Publishing and libraries

Digital distribution enabled widespread self-publishing through platforms such as Kindle Direct Publishing. Public libraries lend e-books through services that mimic print lending with limited licences, an arrangement that has produced ongoing disputes between libraries and publishers over price and availability.
