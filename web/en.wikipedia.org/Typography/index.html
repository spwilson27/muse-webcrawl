Typography

Typography
FieldGraphic design, printing, publishing
Key termsTypeface, font, kerning, leading, point size
OriginsMovable-type printing, c. 1040s (China); Gutenberg, c. 1440 (Europe)

Typography is the art and technique of arranging type to make written language legible, readable and appealing when displayed. It involves selecting typefaces, point sizes, line lengths, line spacing (leading), letter spacing (tracking) and the adjustment of space between letter pairs (kerning), as well as the layout of text on a page or screen.

History

Typography in its modern sense began with movable-type printing: ceramic and wooden type in China under Bi Sheng (c. 1040s), metal type in Korea, and Johannes Gutenberg's printing press in Mainz around 1440, which spread printing across Europe. Type design developed through the punchcutting traditions of Claude Garamond and others, the industrial type foundries of the nineteenth century, hot-metal composition (Linotype, 1884), phototypesetting, and the digital type revolution of the 1980s with PostScript and desktop publishing.

Scope

Typography covers everything from book and newspaper design to signage, interfaces and motion graphics. Its conventions — hierarchies of headings, margins, widows and orphans, hyphenation and justification — differ between languages, scripts and media.

Classification of typefaces

Typefaces are commonly grouped as serif, sans-serif, script and display designs, with finer historical classifications such as old-style, transitional, modern (Didone), slab serif and grotesque.
