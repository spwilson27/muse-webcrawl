Limenitis is a genus of brush-footed butterflies in the family Nymphalidae, commonly known as the admirals. The genus was established by Johan Christian Fabricius in 1807. Depending on the classification used, it includes roughly thirty to fifty species distributed across the temperate Northern Hemisphere — North America, Europe and Asia. Some authorities place the North American admirals in this genus while others merge parts of it into related genera.

Notable species

  • Limenitis arthemis — the white admiral (northern subspecies) and red-spotted purple (southern subspecies) of North America; the red-spotted purple is a mimic of the poisonous pipevine swallowtail.
  • Limenitis camilla — the (Eurasian) white admiral, a woodland butterfly of Europe and Asia whose caterpillars feed on honeysuckle.
  • Limenitis reducta — the southern white admiral, of southern Europe.
  • Limenitis lorquini — Lorquin's admiral, of western North America.
  • Limenitis weidemeyerii — Weidemeyer's admiral, of the North American interior west.
  • Limenitis archippus — the viceroy, a famous Müllerian mimic of the monarch butterfly, sometimes placed here.

Biology

Adults are strong fliers that frequent woodland edges, rides and riverbanks, feeding on nectar, honeydew, sap and carrion or dung juices. Caterpillars feed on a range of trees and shrubs, including willows, poplars, birches, cherries and honeysuckles. Several species overwinter as young larvae in a silken shelter (hibernaculum) rolled from a leaf. The striking colour differences within L. arthemis — banded white in the north, iridescent blue-black in the south — reflect both local mimicry and intergradation where the forms meet.
