Columba

For the bird genus, see Columba (genus). For the constellation, see Columba (constellation).

Columba or Colmcille (7 December 521 – 9 June 597) was an Irish abbot and missionary evangelist credited with spreading Christianity in what is today Scotland at the start of the Hiberno-Scottish mission. He founded the important abbey on Iona, which became a dominant religious and political institution in the region for centuries. He is the patron saint of Derry, where the town's precise founding is uncertain; by tradition, he founded a settlement at Derry in the mid-6th century.

Early life

Columba was born in County Donegal to Fedhlimidh and Eithne of the Cenél Conaill, of royal Uí Néill descent. He was educated at the monastic schools of Movilla under Finnian and at Clonard under another Finnian, becoming one of the "Twelve Apostles of Ireland". His name means "dove" (Latin columba), rendered Colum Cille, "dove of the church", in Irish.

Ireland

Columba founded monasteries at Derry (c. 546), Durrow, and Kells. Tradition holds that a quarrel over his unauthorised copying of a psalter (the Cathach) belonging to Finnian led to the Battle of Cúl Dreimhne (561), in which many men were killed; in penance or exile, Columba left Ireland in 563, vowing to win as many souls for Christ as had been lost in the battle.

Scotland

Sailing with twelve companions, Columba settled on Iona in the Inner Hebrides, granted by King Conall mac Comgaill of Dál Riata. From Iona he and his monks undertook missions among the Picts, tradition crediting him with converting King Bridei I at his fortress near Inverness. Iona's daughter houses spread across Scotland and northern England; his life was recorded by Adomnán in the Vita Columbae (c. 697), and the Book of Kells is traditionally associated with Iona's scribes.

Legacy

Columba is one of the three patron saints of Ireland, with Patrick and Brigid, and his feast day is 9 June. Relics of the saint were later enshrined at Dunkeld. The monastery on Iona, repeatedly raided by Vikings, was eventually refounded as a Benedictine abbey, and Iona remains a centre of pilgrimage today through the Iona Community.
