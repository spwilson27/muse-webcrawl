Leap year

A leap year (also known as an intercalary year or bissextile year) is a calendar year that contains an additional day compared with a common year. The extra day, February 29 in the Gregorian calendar, is added to keep the calendar year synchronised with the astronomical year, since the Earth takes approximately 365.2422 days to complete one orbit around the Sun.

Gregorian rule

In the Gregorian calendar, a year is a leap year if its number is divisible by 4, except for years divisible by 100, which are leap years only if they are also divisible by 400. Thus 2000 was a leap year, but 1700, 1800, and 1900 were not, and 2100 will not be. This rule gives an average year length of 365.2425 days, very close to the tropical year.

History

The Julian calendar, introduced by Julius Caesar in 45 BC, simply added a leap day every four years, giving an average year of 365.25 days. The small overestimate caused the calendar to drift against the seasons by about one day per 128 years, which Pope Gregory XIII corrected in 1582 with the Gregorian reform, skipping ten days and refining the leap rule. February 24 was originally the doubled day in the Julian system; the modern February 29 convention developed later.

Other calendars

Lunisolar calendars such as the Hebrew and Chinese calendars insert an entire leap month in some years to keep lunar months aligned with the solar year. The Islamic calendar uses leap years with an extra day in a 30-year cycle, while the Solar Hijri calendar of Iran computes leap years astronomically.

Customs and effects

People born on February 29, sometimes called "leaplings", usually celebrate their birthdays on February 28 or March 1 in common years. In some traditions, leap day was the one occasion when women could propose marriage to men. Leap years also affect payroll calculations, interest accrual, and software, where mishandling of February 29 has caused notable bugs.
