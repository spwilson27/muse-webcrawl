Panic disorder

Panic disorder is an anxiety disorder characterised by recurrent, unexpected panic attacks — sudden episodes of intense fear accompanied by physical symptoms such as a pounding heart, sweating, trembling, shortness of breath, chest pain, nausea, dizziness and feelings of unreality (derealisation or depersonalisation), often with a fear of dying or losing control. Attacks typically peak within minutes. In panic disorder, sufferers also worry persistently about further attacks or change their behaviour to avoid them, which distinguishes the disorder from isolated panic attacks.

Causes and risk factors

Panic disorder tends to run in families and has a heritable component. Onset often follows stressful life events. Smoking, caffeine and other substances can trigger attacks in susceptible people. The condition frequently co-occurs with agoraphobia, depression and other anxiety disorders.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical, based on criteria such as those in the DSM-5, and requires ruling out medical conditions that can mimic panic, including hyperthyroidism, cardiac disease and pheochromocytoma, as well as substance-related causes.

Treatment

Effective treatments include cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), particularly with exposure to feared bodily sensations (interoceptive exposure), and medication, usually selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs). Benzodiazepines may be used short-term but carry risks of dependence. Many people improve substantially with treatment, though the course can be chronic with relapses.
