Popery

Popery is a derogatory term used by Protestants and others to refer to the teachings, practices, and authority of the Roman Catholic Church, especially the authority of the pope. Emerging in English usage in the 1530s during the English Reformation, the word carried strongly negative connotations of superstition, idolatry, and foreign political allegiance, and "anti-popery" became a powerful force in British and colonial American politics for over two centuries.

History of usage

The term appeared as English reformers broke with Rome; statutes and sermons denounced "popery" as un-English and tyrannical. Fear of popery intensified under Mary I's restoration of Catholicism, during the Spanish Armada crisis, and after the Gunpowder Plot of 1605. Anti-popery ideology held that Catholicism was incompatible with political liberty because Catholics owed allegiance to a foreign ruler, the pope.

Political consequences

Anti-popery drove major events: the fabricated Popish Plot (1678) sparked executions and the Exclusion Crisis; Penal Laws in Britain and Ireland restricted Catholic worship, education, and landholding; the cry of "No Popery" fuelled the Gordon Riots of 1780 in London, among the most destructive riots in British history, after Parliament modestly relaxed the penal laws. In the American colonies, anti-popery shaped laws against Catholics and fed hostility to the Quebec Act of 1774. Agitation against "popery" also opposed Catholic emancipation (achieved 1829) and flared again in the 1850 "papal aggression" controversy. In later centuries the word survived in Ulster unionist rhetoric and in the slogan associated with Ian Paisley, though it is now largely archaic outside historical and sectarian contexts.
