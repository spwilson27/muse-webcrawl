Edwardian era

The Edwardian era or Edwardian period of British history spanned the reign of King Edward VII, from 1901 to 1910, and is sometimes extended to the start of the First World War in 1914. The death of Queen Victoria in January 1901 marked the end of the Victorian era. Edward VII, unlike his famously strict mother, embodied a more relaxed and cosmopolitan society, influenced by European art and fashions.

The period is often remembered as a golden age of long summer afternoons and garden parties, but beneath the surface it was a time of profound social and political change: the rise of the Labour Party, the campaign for women's suffrage, sweeping Liberal welfare reforms, and growing international tension that would culminate in the First World War.

Politics

The Conservative government of Lord Salisbury and Arthur Balfour gave way in 1906 to a landslide Liberal victory under Henry Campbell-Bannerman and later H. H. Asquith. The Liberal government introduced old-age pensions (1908), labour exchanges, and National Insurance (1911), laying foundations of the modern welfare state. David Lloyd George's "People's Budget" of 1909 provoked a constitutional crisis with the House of Lords, resolved by the Parliament Act 1911, which removed the Lords' power of veto over money bills.

Society and culture

Edwardian society was rigidly stratified, with a wealthy elite enjoying country-house weekends, motoring, and foreign travel, while much of the working class lived in poverty documented by investigators such as Charles Booth and Seebohm Rowntree. The decade saw the flourishing of writers including H. G. Wells, E. M. Forster, Joseph Conrad, and Arnold Bennett, the popular music hall, early cinema, and the first stirrings of modernism. Women's fashion moved away from the Victorian silhouette toward the S-bend corset and large hats, and the suffragettes of the Women's Social and Political Union, founded by Emmeline Pankhurst in 1903, escalated their militant campaign for the vote.

Science and technology

The era saw rapid technological change: Marconi's transatlantic wireless signal (1901), the Wright brothers' first powered flight (1903), the launch of HMS Dreadnought (1906), the spread of the motor car and the telephone, and advances in medicine and physics including the early development of quantum theory and the discovery of the electron's properties. The Anglo-German naval race intensified as both empires expanded their fleets.

End of the era

Edward VII died in 1910 and was succeeded by George V. Historians often extend the Edwardian period to 1914, when the outbreak of the First World War shattered the old order; others end it in 1910 with the king's death. In retrospect the era has acquired a powerful nostalgic glow as the last summer of the British Empire's pre-eminence.
