In mathematics, a polygonal number is a number represented as dots arranged in the shape of a regular polygon. Polygonal numbers are a type of figurate number, studied since antiquity by Greek mathematicians including the Pythagoreans, Hypsicles and Diophantus. The nth s-gonal number — the number of dots in a polygon with s sides built up to n dots per side — is given by the formula:

P(s,n) = (s−2)n² − (s−4)n, all over 2

Examples

For s = 3 the formula gives the triangular numbers 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, ...; for s = 4 the square numbers 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, ...; for s = 5 the pentagonal numbers 1, 5, 12, 22, 35, ...; and for s = 6 the hexagonal numbers 1, 6, 15, 28, 45, .... Every hexagonal number is also a triangular number. Some numbers belong to several families: 36 is both square and triangular.

The Fermat polygonal number theorem

In 1638 Fermat claimed that every positive integer is a sum of at most s s-gonal numbers. Lagrange proved the case of squares in 1770 (the four-square theorem), Gauss proved the triangular case in 1796 — recording "ΕΥΡΗΚΑ! num = Δ + Δ + Δ" in his diary — and Cauchy proved the full theorem in 1813. Related classical results include the characterisation of which numbers are polygonal of a given order, generalised by the concept of centred polygonal numbers.
