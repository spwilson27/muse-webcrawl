Microbial mat

A microbial mat is a multi-layered sheet of microorganisms — predominantly bacteria and archaea — held together by extracellular polymeric substances, growing at interfaces between water and solid surfaces or sediments. Mats are complete miniature ecosystems with steep chemical gradients over just millimetres.

Structure

A typical mat is stratified: cyanobacteria at the sunlit top photosynthesize and produce oxygen and organic carbon; below lie purple sulfur bacteria, then green sulfur bacteria, fermenters, and sulfate reducers in the anoxic base, with sulfide oxidation and methanogenesis completing the cycle. Day–night cycles reverse some gradients as photosynthesis starts and stops.

Significance

Microbial mats were Earth's dominant ecosystems for billions of years; cyanobacterial mats oxygenated the atmosphere during the Great Oxidation Event (~2.4 billion years ago) and built stromatolites, whose fossils date to ~3.5 billion years ago. Modern analogues survive in hypersaline lagoons, hot springs (e.g., Yellowstone), and Shark Bay, Australia, where grazing animals are excluded.
