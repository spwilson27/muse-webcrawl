Roman roads

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Roman roads (Latin: viae Romanae; singular via Romana) were physical infrastructure vital to the maintenance and development of the Roman state, built from about 300 BC through the expansion of the Roman Republic and Empire. The network ultimately comprised some 50,000 miles (80,000 km) of paved roads among roughly 200,000 miles of roads in total, enabling armies, officials, and the imperial post (the cursus publicus) to move quickly. Many Roman roads remained in use for centuries and underlie modern routes.

Construction

Roman roads were built in straight alignments wherever terrain allowed, surveyed by the gromatici. A trench was filled with layers — large stones (statumen), rubble and concrete (rudus), a finer bedding (nucleus) — and capped with tightly fitted polygonal paving slabs (summum dorsum), cambered for drainage with ditches and raised footpaths alongside. Bridges, cuttings, and embankments carried the road over rivers and marshes. Milestones recorded distances and often the emperor under whom the road was built or repaired.

Major roads

The Via Appia, the "queen of roads", ran from Rome to Brundisium. The Via Flaminia crossed the Apennines to the Adriatic; the Via Aurelia followed the Tyrrhenian coast; the Via Domitia linked Italy to Spain; and in Britain, Watling Street and the Fosse Way formed the backbone of the province. In the east, roads connected Antioch and the Euphrates frontier.

Legacy

The saying "all roads lead to Rome" reflects the network's hub-like design. Roman itineraries such as the Antonine Itinerary and the Peutinger Table map the system. Many routes survive as modern roads, footpaths, or parish boundaries across Europe and the Mediterranean.
