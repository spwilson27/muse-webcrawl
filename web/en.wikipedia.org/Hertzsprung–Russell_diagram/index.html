Hertzsprung–Russell diagram

Created by: Ejnar Hertzsprung; Henry Norris Russell (c. 1911–1913)
Axes: Luminosity vs. effective temperature (or colour)

The Hertzsprung–Russell diagram (abbreviated H–R diagram) is a scatter plot of stars showing the relationship between their absolute magnitudes or luminosities and their stellar classifications or effective temperatures. Created independently around 1911 by Ejnar Hertzsprung and in 1913 by Henry Norris Russell, it is one of the most important tools in stellar astrophysics for understanding stellar evolution.

Features

Most stars fall along a diagonal band, the main sequence, running from hot, luminous blue stars at upper left to cool, dim red dwarfs at lower right; the Sun lies near its middle. Above the main sequence lie the luminous, cool red giants and supergiants, while below it sit the hot but faint white dwarfs. Temperature increases to the left on the horizontal axis — a legacy of plotting spectral classes O, B, A, F, G, K, M in that order.

Interpretation

A star's position reflects its mass, age, and composition. Stars spend most of their lives on the main sequence fusing hydrogen; when core hydrogen is exhausted they move off it toward the giant branch. Plotting all stars of a single cluster — born together — yields a diagram whose main-sequence "turn-off point" reveals the cluster's age. Modern versions built from Gaia data contain over a billion stars and show fine structure such as the red clump and the white dwarf cooling sequence.
