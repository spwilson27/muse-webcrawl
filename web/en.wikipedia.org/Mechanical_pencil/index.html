Mechanical pencil

Other namesPropelling pencil, clutch pencil, automatic pencil
Lead sizes0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.9 mm; clutch leads 2 mm and up
First patent1822 (Sampson Mordan and John Isaac Hawkins)

A mechanical pencil (American English) or propelling pencil (British English) is a pencil with a replaceable and mechanically extendable solid pigment core called a "lead". The lead, made of graphite mixed with a clay binder (or polymer), is advanced through the tip by a spring-loaded clutch, twist mechanism or shaker action, so the pencil never requires sharpening and maintains a constant line width.

History

Early mechanical pencils are documented from the 16th century; a lead holder was found in the wreck of HMS Pandora (sunk 1791). The first patent for a refillable propelling pencil with a lead-advancing mechanism was granted in Britain in 1822 to Sampson Mordan and John Isaac Hawkins. Charles R. Keeran's Eversharp (from 1915) and Tokuji Hayakawa's "Ever-Ready Sharp Pencil" in Japan (1915) — the product from which the Sharp Corporation takes its name — popularized the modern fine-lead design.

Types and mechanisms

Click-advance (ratchet), twist, shake-advance and automatic-feed mechanisms are common; drafting pencils add features such as lead-grade indicators and cushioned tips. Clutch pencils hold thicker 2 mm leads that are pointed in a separate sharpener. Leads are graded on the same hardness scale as wooden pencils (9H to 9B) and are sold in standard diameters, most commonly 0.5 mm and 0.7 mm.
