Thanksgiving dinner

The centrepiece of contemporary Thanksgiving in the United States is the Thanksgiving dinner (informally called turkey dinner), a large meal generally centred on a large roasted turkey. The dinner is traditionally held on the fourth Thursday of November in the United States and on the second Monday of October in Canada. Thanksgiving dinner is the largest eating event in the United States; around 88 percent of Americans surveyed report eating turkey at the meal.

Traditional foods

The customary centrepiece is a roasted or deep-fried turkey, usually served with stuffing (or dressing), gravy, mashed potatoes, sweet potatoes or candied yams, cranberry sauce, green beans (often as green bean casserole), corn, and dinner rolls. Dessert is dominated by pies — pumpkin pie above all, along with apple, pecan, and sweet potato pie. Regional variations include macaroni and cheese and collard greens in the South, and lasagne in some Italian-American families. Drinks typically include apple cider and wine; many households serve the meal in the afternoon rather than the evening.

History

The meal's mythology traces to the harvest feast shared by the Plymouth colonists and the Wampanoag in 1621, though the documented menu — venison and wild fowl — bore little resemblance to the modern table. The canonical menu crystallised in the nineteenth century, promoted by writers such as Sarah Josepha Hale, whose campaign also helped establish Thanksgiving as a national holiday under Abraham Lincoln in 1863. Turkey became dominant because it was native, sizeable enough for a crowd, and, unlike chickens or cows, not more valuable alive for eggs or milk.

Preparation and scale

The National Turkey Federation estimates that some 46 million turkeys are eaten at Thanksgiving in the United States. The meal is also associated with the busiest travel period of the American year, post-dinner American football broadcasts, and "Friendsgiving" gatherings among friends.
