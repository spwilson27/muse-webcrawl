Tahitian (Reo Tahiti) is a Polynesian language spoken mainly on the Society Islands in French Polynesia, in particular on the island of Tahiti. It belongs to the Eastern Polynesian branch of the Austronesian family and is closely related to Cook Islands Māori, New Zealand Māori, and Hawaiian. Alongside French, it is used in administration, media, and education in French Polynesia, though French remains the sole official language of the territory.

Written Tahitian uses a Latin alphabet with the ʻokina and macrons marking vowel length

Phonology

Tahitian is notable for its very small sound inventory: only nine consonants (/p, t, ʔ, f, v, m, n, r, h/) and five vowels, each of which may be short or long. The glottal stop, written with the ʻeta (ʻ), and vowel length are phonemic, distinguishing otherwise identical words. The language has undergone extensive sound mergers compared with other Polynesian languages; for example, Proto-Polynesian *k and *ŋ both became the glottal stop.

History

Tahitian was first written down by missionaries of the London Missionary Society in the early 19th century, who created its orthography, printed a Bible translation, and taught literacy widely. After French annexation, the use of Tahitian in schools was long discouraged, and the language lost ground to French. Since the 1970s, cultural revival movements have promoted Tahitian (and the other languages of French Polynesia) in education and broadcasting; it is now taught in schools and used on territorial television and radio.

Grammar

Tahitian is an analytic language with verb–subject–object tendencies, extensive use of particles to mark tense, aspect, and case, and two sets of possessive markers distinguishing alienable from inalienable possession—a feature characteristic of Polynesian languages.
