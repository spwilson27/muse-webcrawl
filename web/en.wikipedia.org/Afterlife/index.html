Afterlife

The afterlife (also referred to as life after death or the hereafter) is a purported existence in which the essential part of an individual's stream of consciousness or identity continues to exist after the death of their physical body. Belief in an afterlife is a central theme of most religions and many philosophical traditions, though views differ profoundly on its nature: heaven and hell, reincarnation, an ancestral spirit world, or a shadowy underworld.

Models in world religions

In Christianity and Islam, the soul is judged and assigned to heaven or hell, with resurrection and a final judgment in eschatology. Judaism's beliefs developed over time from Sheol, a dim abode of the dead, toward resurrection and the World to Come. Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism teach samsara — rebirth governed by karma — from which liberation (moksha, nirvana) is the ultimate goal. Ancient Egyptians prepared elaborately for judgment before Osiris and life in the Field of Reeds; Greeks imagined Hades, with Elysium for the blessed; Norse tradition told of Valhalla and Hel.

Philosophical perspectives

Plato argued for the immortality of the soul in dialogues such as the Phaedo. Materialist philosophers and modern neuroscience generally hold that consciousness depends on the brain and ceases at death, regarding afterlife beliefs as psychologically comforting but unsupported by evidence. Debates involve personal identity, dualism, and the interpretation of near-death experiences.

Near-death experiences

Reports of tunnels, lights, and life reviews among people revived from clinical death are studied in medicine and psychology; explanations range from neurochemical accounts to claims of veridical perception, and the phenomenon remains contested.
