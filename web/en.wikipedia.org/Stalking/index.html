Stalking

Stalking is a pattern of unwanted, obsessive attention toward another person.

Stalking is unwanted and/or repeated surveillance or harassment by an individual or group toward another person. Stalking behaviours are interrelated to harassment and intimidation and may include following the victim in person or monitoring them. The term stalking is used with some differing definitions in psychiatry and psychology, as well as in some legal jurisdictions as a term for a criminal offence.

Definitions and behaviours

Typical stalking behaviours include persistent unwanted communication (calls, messages, letters, gifts), following the victim, appearing at their home or workplace, monitoring them physically or electronically (including "cyberstalking" through social media, GPS, or spyware), threats, and in some cases vandalism or violence. Most stalkers are known to their victims; a large proportion are former intimate partners. Research with victims commonly reports severe psychological effects including anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress, and stalking is recognised as a risk factor for serious violence, including homicide, particularly in domestic contexts.

Typologies

Psychological research has proposed several typologies of stalkers, such as the rejected stalker (seeking reconciliation or revenge after a relationship ends), the resentful stalker (seeking to frighten a perceived wrongdoer), the intimacy seeker and incompetent suitor, and the predatory stalker preparing a sexual attack. A small number stalk celebrities or public figures, sometimes in connection with erotomanic delusions — high-profile murders, including that of John Lennon, and the attempted assassination of Ronald Reagan were committed by obsessed stalkers.

Legal responses

California enacted the first modern anti-stalking law in 1990, following several murders by stalkers, and all US states, the federal government, and many other countries have since made stalking a criminal offence. England and Wales introduced a specific stalking offence in 2012 alongside strengthened harassment law and Stalking Protection Orders. Laws typically require a "course of conduct" — repeated acts that would cause a reasonable person to feel fear or distress — and remedies include restraining and non-harassment orders as well as imprisonment.
