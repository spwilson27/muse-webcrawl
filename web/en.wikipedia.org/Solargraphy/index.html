Solargraphy

Solargraphy is a method of photography based on the observation of the Sun's path across the sky, recorded with an extremely long exposure — typically from a few weeks to several months, and in exceptional cases more than a year — using a simple pinhole camera loaded with photographic paper.

Technique

A solargraph is made by fixing a blackened can or similar light-tight container, pierced with a tiny hole, so that it faces the Sun's daily arc. Sunlight entering the pinhole gradually darkens the photographic paper inside, tracing a bright trail for each day. The trails form a family of arcs whose height varies with the season, bounded by the paths of the Sun at the summer and winter solstices. Because the image forms directly on the paper without chemical development, the paper is afterwards scanned and the colours inverted digitally.

History

The technique grew out of experiments with long-exposure pinhole photography around the year 2000 and was developed and popularised by photographers including Tarja Trygg, Diego López Calvín and Paweł Kula, who coined and promoted the term "solargraphy" and the Solarigraphy project. One of the longest known solargraphs was exposed for eight years on the Bayfordbury Observatory in England, recording over 2,900 daily solar arcs.
