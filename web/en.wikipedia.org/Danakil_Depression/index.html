The Danakil Depression is the northern part of the Afar Triangle, a geological depression in the Horn of Africa where three tectonic plates are pulling apart. Lying as much as 125 metres below sea level, it is one of the lowest and hottest places on Earth, with daytime temperatures regularly exceeding 50 °C. The depression sits mainly in the Afar Region of northeastern Ethiopia, extending into Eritrea and Djibouti.

Geology

The depression formed at a triple junction of the Red Sea Rift, the Gulf of Aden Rift, and the East African Rift. As the Arabian Plate separates from the African Plate, the crust thins and sinks, and magma rises close to the surface. Geologists regard the region as an ocean in the making: eventually the Red Sea is expected to flood the depression. Active volcanoes such as Erta Ale, with its persistent lava lake, and the colourful sulphur springs of Dallol testify to the ongoing rifting.

Climate

The Danakil is among the hottest inhabited places on Earth. The settlement of Dallol recorded an average annual temperature of about 34 °C, the highest known for an inhabited location. Rainfall is minimal, and much of the landscape consists of salt flats, lava fields, and acid springs.

People and economy

The Afar people have mined and traded salt from the depression's flats for centuries, cutting salt blocks that were traditionally carried by camel caravans to markets in the Ethiopian highlands. In recent decades the area has also attracted potash mining ventures and a growing stream of tourists drawn to Erta Ale and Dallol, despite the harsh conditions and security concerns.

Palaeontology

The wider Afar region is famous for hominid fossils, including "Lucy" (Australopithecus afarensis), discovered in 1974 at Hadar, which illuminate early human evolution.
