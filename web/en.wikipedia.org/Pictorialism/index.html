Pictorialism

Period
c. 1885–1915
Medium
Photography
Key figures
Alfred Stieglitz, Edward Steichen, Julia Margaret Cameron, Peter Henry Emerson

Pictorialism was an international movement in photography, dominant from the 1880s to the First World War, that sought to establish photography as a fine art. Pictorialist photographers emphasized beauty of subject matter, tonality, and composition over sharp factual documentation, often manipulating prints to resemble paintings, etchings, or charcoal drawings.

Techniques

Soft focus, special lenses, and darkroom processes such as gum bichromate, bromoil, and platinum printing allowed photographers to control texture and tone. Works were often atmospheric and moody, favouring landscapes, portraits, and allegorical scenes.

Organizations and legacy

The movement was promoted by groups such as the Linked Ring in Britain and the Photo-Secession, founded by Alfred Stieglitz in New York in 1902, and by journals like Camera Work. After the First World War, "straight photography" — sharp, unmanipulated images championed by many former Pictorialists — displaced it, though its insistence on photography as art permanently raised the medium's status.
