David

David (Hebrew: דָּוִד, Dāwīḏ) was, according to the Hebrew Bible, the third king of the United Monarchy of Israel. In the biblical narrative he succeeds Saul and precedes Solomon. Historians of the Ancient Near East generally agree that David probably lived around 1000 BCE, although the scale of his kingdom is debated.

Biblical narrative

In the Books of Samuel, David is introduced as a young shepherd and musician from Bethlehem, secretly anointed by the prophet Samuel. He enters Saul's court as a harpist and wins fame by killing the Philistine champion Goliath with a sling stone. As Saul's jealousy grows, David flees and lives as an outlaw and mercenary until Saul's death, when he is made king — first of Judah at Hebron, then of all Israel. He captures Jerusalem and makes it his capital, brings the Ark of the Covenant there, and expands the kingdom through wars against the Philistines, Moabites, Ammonites, and Arameans.

The narrative also records his adultery with Bathsheba and arrangement of her husband Uriah's death — rebuked by the prophet Nathan — and later the rebellion of his son Absalom. Tradition credits David with composing many of the Psalms, and he is a central figure in Judaism, Christianity (where he is an ancestor of Jesus), and Islam, where he is the prophet Dawud.

Historicity

The principal archaeological evidence is the Tel Dan Stele (9th century BCE), which mentions the "House of David" (bytdwd), widely read as confirming a historical Davidic dynasty. The Mesha Stele may also refer to his house. Scholars debate whether David ruled a substantial kingdom or a small chiefdom; discoveries at Khirbet Qeiyafa and Jerusalem continue to inform the discussion.

Legacy

David is among the most depicted figures in Western art — Michelangelo's marble David (1501–1504) in Florence is the most famous representation — and the "Star of David" became a symbol of Judaism. The messianic hope of a restored "son of David" runs through Jewish and Christian theology.
