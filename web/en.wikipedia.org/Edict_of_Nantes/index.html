Edict of Nantes

Signed: 13 April 1598, Nantes

Signatory: Henry IV of France

Revoked: 1685, by the Edict of Fontainebleau

The Edict of Nantes (French: édit de Nantes) was signed in April 1598 by King Henry IV and granted the minority Calvinist Protestants of France, also known as Huguenots, substantial rights in the essentially Catholic nation. In the edict, Henry aimed primarily to promote civil unity. It effectively ended the French Wars of Religion that had torn the country apart since 1562.

Provisions

The edict granted Huguenots freedom of conscience throughout France and freedom of worship in specified places: where Protestant worship had existed in 1597, in two places in each bailliage, and in the houses of Protestant nobles. It restored their civil rights, including access to universities, schools, and hospitals, and allowed them to hold public office. As security, the crown permitted the Huguenots to retain garrisons in some 200 fortified places of surety (places de sûreté), including La Rochelle, at royal expense.

Aftermath

Under Louis XIII the military clauses were dismantled after the siege of La Rochelle (1627–28) and the Peace of Alès (1629), though religious provisions remained. Under Louis XIV, pressure on Huguenots intensified through measures such as the dragonnades, and in October 1685 the Edict of Fontainebleau revoked the Edict of Nantes entirely, prompting the emigration of an estimated 200,000 or more Huguenots to Protestant countries.

Legacy

The edict is remembered as an early, pragmatic landmark of religious toleration in Europe, and its revocation as a cautionary episode that deprived France of many skilled citizens while enriching the countries that received them.
