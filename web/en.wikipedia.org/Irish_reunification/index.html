Irish reunification

Also calledUnited Ireland
Legal frameworkGood Friday Agreement (1998): reunification by concurrent referendums
Partition1921 (Government of Ireland Act 1920)

Irish reunification is the proposition that the island of Ireland — divided since 1921 between the Republic of Ireland and Northern Ireland, part of the United Kingdom — should be united as a single sovereign state. A "United Ireland" is the central goal of Irish nationalism and republicanism, opposed by unionists who wish Northern Ireland to remain in the UK.

Background

Partition followed the Anglo-Irish Treaty of 1921. The Republic's 1937 constitution claimed the whole island until the claim was replaced, under the 1998 Good Friday Agreement, with an aspiration to unity by consent. The Agreement provides that Northern Ireland's status can change only with the consent of a majority there, expressed in a referendum ("border poll"), alongside a concurrent referendum in the Republic; the UK Secretary of State must call a poll if it appears likely a majority would favour unity.

Debate

Interest in reunification rose after Brexit, since Northern Ireland voted to remain in the EU and the post-Brexit arrangements created a distinct trading position for it. Opinion polls show a growing but still minority share in Northern Ireland favouring unity, with demographics, economics (including the UK subvention to Northern Ireland), health services, and the constitutional form of a united state all actively debated. Sinn Féin campaigns for a poll; unionist parties defend the Union; the Irish government has funded research through its Shared Island initiative.
