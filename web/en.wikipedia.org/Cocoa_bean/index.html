Cocoa bean

The cocoa bean (also cacao bean) is the dried and fully fermented seed of Theobroma cacao, the cacao tree native to the tropical regions of the Americas. Cocoa beans are the basis of chocolate, as well as of cocoa butter, cocoa solids and the traditional Mesoamerican drink known to the Aztecs as xocolatl.

Production

Cacao pods are harvested by hand, opened, and the beans and pulp are fermented for several days, during which precursor flavours develop. The beans are then dried, usually in the sun. The world's largest producers are Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana, which together account for well over half of global output; Indonesia, Nigeria, Ecuador and Cameroon are also major producers.

Processing

After drying, beans are cleaned, roasted and cracked; the shell is removed by winnowing to leave cocoa nibs. Nibs are ground into cocoa mass (chocolate liquor), which may be pressed to separate cocoa butter from cocoa solids, or combined with sugar and other ingredients to make chocolate.

History

Cacao was domesticated in the upper Amazon region and spread through Mesoamerica, where the Maya and Aztecs valued beans as food, drink and currency. After the Spanish conquest of the Americas, cocoa was introduced to Europe, where sweetened hot chocolate became fashionable, and in the 19th century Dutch processing methods and the chocolate press made solid chocolate widely available.
