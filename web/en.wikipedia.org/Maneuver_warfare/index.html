Maneuver warfare

Maneuver warfare is a military strategy emphasizing movement, speed, and surprise to dislocate and disorient an enemy, defeating it by shattering its cohesion and will to fight rather than by destroying it through attrition and firepower alone.

Principles

Key ideas include mission-type orders (Auftragstaktik), tempo faster than the opponent's decision cycle (John Boyd's OODA loop), concentration at a decisive point (Schwerpunkt), attacking command, logistics, and flanks, and combined arms. German Blitzkrieg in 1939–41 is the classic example; theorists include B. H. Liddell Hart and William Lind.

Adoption

The US Marine Corps adopted maneuver warfare doctrine in FMFM-1 Warfighting (1989). The US Army's AirLand Battle emphasized related concepts. Critics note maneuver depends on logistics, intelligence, and training, and may fail against prepared defenses without supporting fires.

History

Precursors range from Mongol mobility and Napoleon's corps system to infiltration tactics of 1918 and Soviet deep battle theory.
