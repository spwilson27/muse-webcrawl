Compliance (psychology)

Compliance in psychology is a type of social influence involving a change in behaviour in response to a direct or indirect request or suggestion from another person. Unlike persuasion, which aims to change attitudes or beliefs, compliance focuses on outward behaviour: the person complies with the request even though they may privately disagree with it.

Social psychologists have identified a number of techniques by which compliance can be increased. These techniques are widely used in everyday life, in marketing and sales, in politics, and in organisational settings.

Techniques

The foot-in-the-door technique involves first making a small, easy request that the target is likely to accept, followed by a larger, related request. Having agreed to the first request, people tend to see themselves as helpful or committed, and are more likely to agree to the second.

The door-in-the-face technique works in the opposite direction: an initially large request that is expected to be refused is followed by a smaller, more reasonable request. The refusal creates a sense of reciprocal concession, making the second request seem like a compromise worth accepting.

The low-ball technique secures agreement to a request before its hidden costs are revealed; once a person has committed, they tend to honour the commitment even when the terms become less attractive. Related approaches include the "that's-not-all" technique, in which additional bonuses are added before the target has a chance to respond, and the use of ingratiation and flattery to create a favourable mood.

Underlying principles

Robert Cialdini identified several principles that make requests more effective, including reciprocity, commitment and consistency, social proof, liking, authority, and scarcity. Compliance is more likely when the requester is perceived as an authority figure, when the request appears consistent with the target's prior commitments, and when others appear to be complying with similar requests.

Research

Classic experimental work, including the studies of Stanley Milgram on obedience and Solomon Asch on conformity, forms the backdrop against which compliance research developed, although compliance is distinguished from both: conformity involves group pressure without a direct request, while obedience involves an explicit order from an authority.
