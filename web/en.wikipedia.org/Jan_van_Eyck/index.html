Bornc. 1390, probably Maaseik, Prince-Bishopric of Liège
Died9 July 1441, Bruges, Burgundian Netherlands

Jan van Eyck (c. 1390 – 9 July 1441) was a Flemish painter active in Bruges, a leading figure of Early Netherlandish painting whose mastery of oil paint and minute naturalistic detail transformed European art. His best-known works are the Ghent Altarpiece (completed 1432) and the Arnolfini Portrait (1434).

Life

Little is known of van Eyck's youth; he probably came from Maaseik, near Maastricht. He first appears in records in 1422 working in The Hague for John of Bavaria, Count of Holland. From 1425 until his death he was court painter and valet de chambre to Philip the Good, Duke of Burgundy, who entrusted him with diplomatic missions, including journeys to Portugal in 1428–29 to negotiate the duke's marriage to Isabella of Portugal and paint her portrait. He settled in Bruges about 1430.

Works

Van Eyck signed and dated an unusual number of panels, often with his motto Als ich kan ("As I can"). With his brother Hubert — whose exact contribution is debated — he completed the Ghent Altarpiece, The Adoration of the Mystic Lamb, for St Bavo's Cathedral, Ghent, in 1432, one of the most influential paintings of the Northern Renaissance. The Arnolfini Portrait of 1434, depicting the merchant Giovanni di Nicolao Arnolfini and his wife in a Bruges interior, is famed for its convex mirror reflecting two witnesses, one of them presumably the artist, above the inscription "Jan van Eyck was here 1434". Other major works include the Madonna of Chancellor Rolin, the Lucca Madonna, the Madonna at the Fountain, and the portrait known as Portrait of a Man (Self Portrait?) of 1433, the presumed self-portrait in a red turban.

Technique and legacy

Giorgio Vasari wrongly credited van Eyck with inventing oil painting, but he did perfect the technique of building translucent coloured glazes over detailed underdrawing, achieving unprecedented effects of light, texture, and jewel-like colour. Reproductions of his methods spread quickly through Europe. The Ghent Altarpiece is frequently called the most stolen artwork in history, having been looted, dismantled, and partially stolen many times.
