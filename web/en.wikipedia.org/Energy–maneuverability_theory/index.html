Energy–maneuverability theory

Energy–maneuverability theory (E-M theory) is a quantitative method for analyzing and comparing the performance of aircraft, particularly fighter aircraft in air combat maneuvering. It was developed in the 1960s by United States Air Force pilot John Boyd together with mathematician Thomas Christie, and it strongly influenced the design of the F-15 Eagle and F-16 Fighting Falcon.

Basic concepts

E-M theory treats an aircraft's total mechanical energy — the sum of its kinetic energy (depending on speed) and potential energy (depending on altitude) — as the central quantity of air combat. Because energy can be traded between speed and height, the important figure is an aircraft's specific excess power, usually written Ps: the rate at which the aircraft can gain (or is forced to lose) energy at a given speed, altitude, and G loading. Specific excess power depends on thrust, drag, and weight; where Ps is positive the aircraft can climb, accelerate, or sustain a turn without losing energy, and where it is negative, maneuvering bleeds energy away.

Applications

By plotting Ps contours across the flight envelope, designers and pilots can compare two aircraft directly and identify the speeds and altitudes at which each holds an advantage. Boyd used the theory to argue for fighters with high thrust-to-weight ratios and low wing loading, shaping the "Lightweight Fighter" program that produced the F-16. The theory also underlies much modern teaching of basic fighter maneuvers, in which pilots are trained to manage their energy state deliberately.

History

Boyd developed the theory after combat experience in the Korean War and study of engagements in Vietnam, where American fighters designed for interception performed poorly in turning dogfights. Working with Christie at Eglin Air Force Base, he computerized the analysis, producing the first practical energy–maneuverability diagrams. The work became a cornerstone of what Boyd later called the "fighter mafia" reform movement in the Pentagon.
