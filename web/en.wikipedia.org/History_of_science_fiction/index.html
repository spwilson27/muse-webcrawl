History of science fiction


A 1920s pulp cover in the style of Amazing Stories

The history of science fiction follows the development of speculative literature imagining the impact of science and technology on society. Although fantastic voyages appear in ancient literature, science fiction as a distinct genre crystallised in the 19th century with Mary Shelley and Jules Verne, took its name from Hugo Gernsback's pulp magazines in 1926, and matured through the "Golden Age" of the 1940s–50s into a global multimedia phenomenon.

Precursors

Lucian's True History (2nd century CE) parodied travellers' tales with a voyage to the Moon. Kepler's Somnium (1634) imagined lunar astronomy; Cyrano de Bergerac wrote lunar voyages; Swift's Gulliver's Travels (1726) satirised science through Laputa's flying island. The Gothic and industrial revolutions inspired Mary Shelley's Frankenstein (1818), often called the first science fiction novel, in which a scientist creates life. Edgar Allan Poe wrote tales of mesmerism, balloon voyages and cosmology that influenced later writers.

Verne, Wells and the scientific romance

Jules Verne's Voyages extraordinaires (from 1863) — including From the Earth to the Moon (1865) and Twenty Thousand Leagues Under the Seas (1870) — grounded speculation in contemporary engineering. H. G. Wells, in The Time Machine (1895), The War of the Worlds (1898) and other "scientific romances", used speculative premises to explore evolution, class and imperialism. Writers worldwide contributed: Kurd Lasswitz in Germany, and in Japan, translations of Verne inspired early Japanese SF.

The pulp era and Gernsback

Hugo Gernsback launched Amazing Stories in 1926, coining "scientifiction", later "science fiction", and fostering a fan community through letter columns — the origin of science fiction fandom. Pulp magazines published space opera (E. E. "Doc" Smith, Edmond Hamilton), Edgar Rice Burroughs's Barsoom adventures, and early work by Jack Williamson and others. In film, Metropolis (1927) set visual standards for the cinematic future.

The Golden Age

John W. Campbell Jr., editor of Astounding from 1937, demanded greater scientific rigour and literary quality, launching the careers of Isaac Asimov (Foundation, robot stories), Robert A. Heinlein and Arthur C. Clarke — the "Big Three". The atomic bomb and the space race lent the genre urgency. Writers such as Ray Bradbury, Theodore Sturgeon and Clifford D. Simak expanded its emotional range; the Hugo Awards were established in 1953.

New Wave and diversification

In the 1960s–70s the New Wave (Michael Moorcock's New Worlds, Harlan Ellison's anthologies) embraced literary experiment and "inner space": J. G. Ballard, Ursula K. Le Guin, Samuel R. Delany and Joanna Russ brought anthropological, feminist and stylistic depth. Le Guin's The Left Hand of Darkness (1969) and Octavia Butler's work broadened the genre's treatment of gender and race. Star Trek (1966–) and Star Wars (1977) carried SF to mass audiences.

Cyberpunk to the present

William Gibson's Neuromancer (1984) crystallised cyberpunk's vision of cyberspace and corporate dystopia. Subsequent decades saw the rise of hard SF (Greg Egan, Kim Stanley Robinson), space opera revivals, military SF, climate fiction, and major contributions from non-Anglophone traditions — notably Chinese SF, whose global prominence was marked by Liu Cixin's The Three-Body Problem (2008; Hugo Award 2015). Science fiction became dominant in film, television and gaming, and its fandom and awards diversified internationally.
