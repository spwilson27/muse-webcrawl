Farinata

OriginGenoa, Liguria, Italy
TypeUnleavened chickpea flatbread / pancake
Main ingredientsChickpea flour, water, olive oil, salt
Regional variantsSocca (Nice), cecina (Tuscany), fainá (Argentina/Uruguay), karantika (Algeria)
ServedHot, often with black pepper or rosemary

Farinata (Ligurian: fainâ) is a thin, unleavened flatbread made from chickpea flour, originating in Genoa, Liguria, and now found in many forms around the Mediterranean and beyond. It is prepared from a simple batter of chickpea flour, water, salt, and olive oil, rested for several hours, then baked in a very hot oven in a wide, shallow copper pan (a testo) until golden with a crisp surface and soft, custardy interior.

History and legend

A popular Genoese legend dates farinata to the aftermath of the Battle of Meloria (1284), when Genoese ships returning with Pisan prisoners were storm-tossed and barrels of chickpea flour and olive oil spilled and mixed with seawater; the resulting paste, dried in the sun or baked, proved edible and was refined ashore. Whatever its true antiquity — chickpea-flour batters were known in the ancient world — farinata became a staple cheap street food sold by Genoese sciamadde (fry shops).

Regional varieties

In Nice it is socca, in Tuscany cecina (often tucked into a small focaccia or pizza bread), and along the French coast cade. Genoese emigrants carried it to Argentina and Uruguay, where fainá is a standard companion to pizza, eaten on top of a slice. A closely related chickpea bake, karantika (calentica), is a street food of Algeria. Variations season the batter with rosemary, onions, or young artichokes; in the Savona area a white-flour version is also made. Farinata is naturally gluten-free and vegan, suiting modern diets as well as Lenten tradition.
