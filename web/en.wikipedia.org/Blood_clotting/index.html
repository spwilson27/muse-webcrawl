Coagulation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia — "Blood clotting" redirects here

Coagulation, also known as blood clotting, is the process by which blood changes from a liquid to a gel, forming a blood clot. It potentially results in hemostasis, the cessation of blood loss from a damaged vessel, followed by repair. The mechanism of coagulation involves activation, adhesion and aggregation of platelets, as well as deposition and maturation of fibrin.

Physiology

Coagulation begins almost instantly after injury to the endothelium lining a blood vessel. Exposure of blood to the subendothelial space initiates two processes: changes in platelets and exposure of subendothelial tissue factor to plasma factor VII, which ultimately leads to cross-linked fibrin formation. Primary hemostasis forms a weak platelet plug; secondary hemostasis, the coagulation cascade, stabilises it with a fibrin mesh.

The coagulation cascade

The cascade is traditionally divided into the intrinsic pathway (contact activation), the extrinsic pathway (tissue factor), and the common pathway. In the modern cell-based model, clotting factors — mostly serine proteases synthesised in the liver, many requiring vitamin K — sequentially activate one another on the surface of activated platelets. The enzyme thrombin converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin strands, and factor XIII cross-links them. Calcium ions are essential at several steps.

Regulation and fibrinolysis

Antithrombin, the protein C/protein S system, and tissue factor pathway inhibitor limit clot growth, while plasmin dissolves fibrin during fibrinolysis as the vessel heals.

Disorders and clinical testing

Deficiencies cause bleeding disorders such as haemophilia A (factor VIII) and haemophilia B (factor IX); excessive clotting causes thrombosis, including deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism. Common laboratory tests include the prothrombin time (PT/INR) and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT). Anticoagulant drugs such as warfarin, heparin, and direct oral anticoagulants are widely used in treatment and prevention.
