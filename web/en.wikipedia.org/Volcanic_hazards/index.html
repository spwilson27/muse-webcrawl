Volcanic hazards

Volcanic hazards are the dangerous phenomena produced by volcanic activity that can cause loss of life, injury, property damage and environmental disruption. More than 500 million people live within reach of active volcanoes, so hazard assessment and monitoring are central tasks of volcanology.

Types of hazard

  • Pyroclastic density currents — fast-moving clouds of hot gas and rock fragments that sweep down a volcano's flanks; the deadliest volcanic phenomenon, responsible for the destruction of Pompeii in AD 79 and of Saint-Pierre in 1902.
  • Lava flows — streams of molten rock, usually slow enough to escape but destructive to property in their path.
  • Tephra and ash fall — airborne fragments that can collapse roofs, contaminate water, damage crops and jet engines, and disrupt aviation far downwind.
  • Lahars — volcanic mudflows of water and debris that can inundate valleys tens of kilometres away, as at Armero, Colombia, in 1985.
  • Volcanic gases — carbon dioxide, sulfur dioxide and hydrogen sulfide, which can asphyxiate, acidify rain and, in large eruptions, cool the global climate.
  • Landslides, tsunamis and sector collapse — flank failures, such as at Mount St. Helens in 1980, and caldera-forming eruptions with worldwide effects.

Mitigation

Hazards are mitigated through monitoring (seismicity, ground deformation, gas emissions), hazard maps and exclusion zones, evacuation planning, and public education. The Volcanic Explosivity Index (VEI) ranks eruptions by the volume of material ejected.
