Neural correlates of consciousness

Candidate neural systems whose activity correlates with conscious experience.

The neural correlates of consciousness (NCC) are the minimal set of neural events and mechanisms jointly sufficient for a specific conscious percept or experience. Neuroscientists use empirical approaches to discover the neural correlates of subjective phenomena; that is, neural changes which necessarily and regularly correlate with a specific experience. The concept was championed by Francis Crick and Christof Koch in the 1990s as a tractable empirical route into the science of consciousness.

Approach

A standard experimental strategy contrasts brain activity when a stimulus is consciously perceived with activity when the same stimulus is not perceived, using paradigms such as binocular rivalry, the attentional blink, masking, and anesthesia. Because report itself can confound the results, researchers have developed "no-report" paradigms that infer perception from reflexive measures such as pupil dilation or optokinetic responses.

Candidate structures

Evidence from lesions, stimulation, and neuroimaging points especially to a posterior cortical "hot zone" spanning parietal, temporal, and occipital areas, and to interactions between the cortex and the thalamus, including the claustrum and intralaminar nuclei. Rival theoretical frameworks — notably global workspace theory and integrated information theory — predict different NCCs and have been tested in adversarial collaborations.

Levels of consciousness

The NCC for the level of consciousness (wakefulness versus dreamless sleep, coma, or anesthesia) are distinguished from the NCC for specific contents of experience. Disorders of consciousness such as the vegetative state have motivated clinical measures, including perturbational complexity indices derived from EEG responses to transcranial magnetic stimulation.
