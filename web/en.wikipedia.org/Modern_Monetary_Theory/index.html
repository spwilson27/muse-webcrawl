Modern Monetary Theory

Modern Monetary Theory or Modern Money Theory (MMT) is a heterodox macroeconomic theory that describes currency as a public monopoly and unemployment as evidence that a currency monopolist is overly restricting the supply of the financial assets needed to pay taxes and satisfy savings desires. MMT challenges mainstream economic conventions about the role of government deficits, the importance of deficit spending, and related matters.

Core principles

MMT's main tenets include:

  • A government that issues its own fiat currency under a floating exchange rate cannot run out of money or default on debts denominated in its own currency; it faces constraints from real resources and inflation, not solvency.
  • Government spending logically precedes tax collection: taxes drive demand for the currency and create space for spending, rather than funding spending in an operational sense.
  • The public deficit corresponds to private-sector net financial savings; persistent surpluses by the government imply deficits elsewhere.
  • Unemployment results from insufficient aggregate demand; MMT advocates often propose a job guarantee as an automatic stabilizer and price anchor.

History and proponents

MMT developed in the 1990s from the work of Warren Mosler, building on earlier ideas from chartalism, Abba Lerner's functional finance, Hyman Minsky, and Wynne Godley's sectoral balances approach. Academic proponents have included L. Randall Wray, Stephanie Kelton, Pavlina Tcherneva, and Bill Mitchell. The theory gained wider public attention in the late 2010s through political debates over funding programs such as the Green New Deal.

Criticism

Mainstream economists have criticized MMT as overstating the safety of deficit spending, understating inflation and exchange-rate risks, and offering little that is new compared with Keynesian and post-Keynesian economics. Supporters respond that MMT is primarily a description of monetary operations in modern fiat systems.
