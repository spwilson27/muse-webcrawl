Cuneiform is a logo-syllabic writing system that was used to write several languages of the ancient Near East. The script was in active use from the early Bronze Age until the beginning of the Common Era. It is named for the characteristic wedge-shaped impressions (Latin cuneus, "wedge") which form its signs. Cuneiform was originally developed to write the Sumerian language of southern Mesopotamia (modern Iraq) and is the earliest known writing system.

History

Cuneiform emerged from a system of clay tokens and pictographic tablets used for accounting in the late fourth millennium BC. Around 3400–3300 BC, scribes in the city of Uruk began impressing stylized pictures into wet clay with a cut reed stylus. Over time the pictograms became more abstract, and the reed, cut at an angle, produced the characteristic wedge-shaped strokes.

The script was gradually adapted to write phonetic values, becoming a mixed logographic-syllabic system. It was adopted by the Akkadians, Babylonians and Assyrians for their Semitic languages, and later by the Hittites, Hurrians, Urartians and Elamites. A simplified alphabetic variant was used at Ugarit, and the Persians developed a distinct, largely syllabic Old Persian cuneiform for royal inscriptions.

The last dated cuneiform tablet, an astronomical almanac, was written in AD 75. Knowledge of the script was then lost until its decipherment in the nineteenth century, a process aided by the trilingual Behistun Inscription of Darius I and the work of scholars such as Georg Friedrich Grotefend and Henry Rawlinson.

Signs and usage

Classical cuneiform sign lists contain between 600 and 1,000 signs, many of them polyvalent — a single sign could have several logographic and syllabic readings, and a single sound could be written with several different signs. Determinatives, unpronounced signs placed before or after words, clarified categories such as gods, places, or wooden objects. Texts were written on clay tablets with a reed stylus; monumental inscriptions were also carved in stone.

Corpus

Hundreds of thousands of cuneiform tablets survive, covering administrative records, law codes such as the Code of Hammurabi, literature including the Epic of Gilgamesh, mathematics, astronomy, medicine and private letters. Major collections are held in the British Museum, the Louvre, and the Pergamon Museum.
