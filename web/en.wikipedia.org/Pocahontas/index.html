Pocahontas (born Matoaka; c. 1596 – March 1617) was a Native American woman of the Powhatan chiefdom in Virginia, notable for her association with the colonial settlement at Jamestown. The daughter of Powhatan, the paramount chief of a network of tributary tribes, she acted as an intermediary between her people and the English colonists. "Pocahontas" was a childhood nickname meaning "playful one".

Life

According to John Smith's later account, in December 1607 the young Pocahontas saved him from execution by her father by interposing her own head — a story absent from Smith's earlier writings and now widely doubted by historians, though some suggest it may garble a ritual adoption ceremony. She frequently brought food to the starving colonists and carried messages. In 1613 the English lured her aboard a ship and held her for ransom; during her captivity she converted to Christianity and was baptised Rebecca. In April 1614 she married tobacco planter John Rolfe — the first recorded marriage between an English colonist and a Native American — which helped secure several years of peace between the English and Powhatan peoples. Their son Thomas was born in 1615.

Journey to England and death

In 1616 the Rolfes travelled to England, where Rebecca Rolfe was presented at court as an example of a "civilised savage" to encourage investment in Virginia. In March 1617, as the family prepared to return, she fell ill and died at Gravesend, aged about 21, and was buried at St George's Church there. The exact cause of death is unknown.

Legacy

Pocahontas became one of the most mythologised figures of early America, reshaped by each era — as a romantic heroine in 19th-century plays and paintings, and for modern audiences in Disney's 1995 animated film Pocahontas, whose fictionalised romance with Smith historians and Native commentators have criticised. Many prominent Virginia families (the "First Families") trace descent from her through Thomas Rolfe.
