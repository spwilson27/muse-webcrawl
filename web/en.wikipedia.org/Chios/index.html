Chios

Chios (Greek: Χίος) is the fifth largest Greek island, situated in the northern Aegean Sea, close to the coast of Turkey. The island is notable for its exports of mastic gum, its medieval villages, and its maritime tradition, and its main town is also called Chios.

Geography and economy

Chios covers about 842 square kilometres, with a mountainous spine in the north and fertile plains in the south. The southern region, the Mastichochoria ("mastic villages"), is the only place in the world where the mastic tree yields commercially significant resin, long prized as a chewing gum, spice, and medicine. Shipping has historically been a major industry, and several prominent Greek shipping families originate from the island.

History

Inhabited since the Neolithic period, Chios was colonised by Ionians and became a prosperous maritime power celebrated in antiquity as a possible birthplace of Homer. It later passed through Roman, Byzantine, Genoese, and Ottoman rule. The island suffered a catastrophic massacre in 1822 during the Greek War of Independence, an event commemorated in Delacroix's painting The Massacre at Chios. Chios joined the modern Greek state in 1912.

Landmarks

The 11th-century monastery of Nea Moni, a UNESCO World Heritage Site, contains celebrated Byzantine mosaics. The fortified mastic villages of Pyrgi and Mesta preserve distinctive medieval street plans and painted geometric house decoration known as xysta.
