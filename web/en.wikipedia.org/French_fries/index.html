French fries (North American English), chips (British and most Commonwealth English), finger chips (Indian English), french-fried potatoes, or simply fries, are batonnet or allumette-cut deep-fried potatoes of disputed origin from Belgium or France. They are prepared by cutting potatoes into even strips, drying them, and frying them, usually in a deep fryer. Pre-cut, blanched, and frozen russet potatoes are widely used, and sometimes baked in a regular or convection oven instead.

French fries are served hot, either soft or crispy, and are generally eaten as part of lunch or dinner or by themselves as a snack, commonly appearing on the menus of diners, fast food restaurants, pubs, and bars. They are often salted and may be served with ketchup, vinegar, mayonnaise, tomato sauce, or other local specialities.

History

The origin of French fries is disputed between Belgium and France. Belgian lore holds that poor villagers in the Meuse valley fried small fish, and when the river froze in winter they cut potatoes into fish-like shapes and fried those instead — a practice dated by some to the late 17th century. France claims the dish was sold by street vendors on the Pont Neuf in Paris in the late 18th century, hence pommes Pont-Neuf. The name "French" may derive from the verb "to french" (to cut into strips), or from the French-speaking Belgians who popularised the dish, or from American soldiers encountering them in francophone Belgium during the First World War.

Thomas Jefferson brought a taste for "potatoes served in the French manner" back from France, and they were served at the White House in 1802. Fries became a global staple with the rise of fast-food chains such as McDonald's in the mid-20th century.

Preparation

The standard method involves cutting peeled potatoes into strips of uniform width, soaking them to remove surface starch, and frying — often twice. In the double-fry method associated with Belgian frites, the potatoes are first fried at a lower temperature to cook the interior, rested, then fried again at a higher temperature to crisp the exterior. Traditionally beef tallow was used in Belgium; vegetable oils are now common.

Variants

Variants include shoestring, curly, waffle, crinkle-cut, steak and wedge fries, as well as sweet potato fries. Loaded fries are topped with cheese, gravy (as in Canadian poutine), chilli, or sauces. In Britain, thicker-cut "chips" accompany fish and chips; in the Low Countries fries are commonly served with mayonnaise.

Health aspects

French fries are energy-dense and typically high in fat and sodium. High-temperature frying of starchy foods also produces acrylamide, a compound monitored by food-safety authorities. Baked and air-fried versions are promoted as lower-fat alternatives.
