Chain rule

FieldCalculus
Statement(f ∘ g)′(x) = f′(g(x)) · g′(x)
Related rulesProduct rule, quotient rule, inverse function rule

In calculus, the chain rule is a formula that expresses the derivative of the composition of two differentiable functions f and g in terms of the derivatives of f and g. More precisely, if h is the function such that h(x) = f(g(x)) for every x, then the chain rule states that h is differentiable and its derivative can be expressed as follows.

h′(x) = f′(g(x)) · g′(x)

Alternatively, in Leibniz's notation, if a variable z depends on the variable y, which itself depends on the variable x, then z depends on x as well, and the chain rule is written:

dz/dx = (dz/dy) · (dy/dx)

Intuition

Informally, the chain rule says that if a quantity changes at one rate relative to a second quantity, and the second changes at another rate relative to a third, then the first changes relative to the third at the product of the two rates. For example, if an airplane flies twice as fast as a car travels, and the car travels three times as fast as a person walks, the plane moves six times as fast as the walker.

Examples

To differentiate sin(x²), let the outer function be sin(u) and the inner function be u = x². The derivative of the outer function is cos(u), and the derivative of the inner function is 2x, giving 2x·cos(x²). Similar decompositions underlie techniques such as integration by substitution, which is essentially the chain rule applied in reverse.

Higher derivatives and generalizations

Faà di Bruno's formula generalizes the chain rule to higher derivatives of compositions. The chain rule also extends to functions of several variables, where derivatives become Jacobian matrices and the rule becomes matrix multiplication, and to the abstract setting of differentiable manifolds and Fréchet derivatives.

History

The chain rule seems to have first been used by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, whose 1684 work contains an error-ridden version of the rule. Guillaume de l'Hôpital used it implicitly in his 1696 Analyse des Infiniment Petits. The first clean statement in something like its modern form is often credited to Joseph-Louis Lagrange.
