Kerosene lamp

A kerosene lamp (also known as a paraffin lamp in some countries) is a type of lighting device that uses kerosene as a fuel. Kerosene lamps have a wick or mantle as light source, protected by a glass chimney or globe; they may be carried by hand or hung, and were the principal form of household and portable lighting from the 1860s until the widespread adoption of electric light.

History

The modern kerosene lamp was developed in the 1850s following the commercialisation of kerosene distillation. A landmark design was the lamp of Polish pharmacist Ignacy Łukasiewicz, built in Lviv in 1853. Subsequent improvements — the flat-wick burner with a tall glass chimney, the central-draught tubular wick, and the incandescent mantle lamps of the 1890s — made kerosene lighting steadily brighter and cleaner.

Types

Flat-wick lamps are the simplest common type, with a ribbon-like wick whose height adjusts the flame. Cold-blast and hot-blast tubular-wick "lanterns" were designed for outdoor and railway use and burn safely in wind. Pressurised lamps, such as the Petromax and Tilley lamps, vaporise the fuel and use a glowing mantle, producing a very bright white light comparable to early electric bulbs.

Use today

Kerosene lamps remain in use in regions without reliable electricity and as emergency or recreational lighting elsewhere, though cheap solar LED lamps have displaced them in many areas. They carry risks of fire and indoor air pollution, and fuel adulteration has been a persistent safety problem.
