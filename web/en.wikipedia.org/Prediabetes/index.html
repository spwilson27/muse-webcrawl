Prediabetes is a metabolic state in which blood glucose levels are higher than normal but not yet high enough to meet the diagnostic threshold for type 2 diabetes. It encompasses impaired fasting glucose and impaired glucose tolerance. Prediabetes usually has no symptoms, but it substantially increases the risk of developing type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease. It is very common: the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention estimates that more than one in three American adults has prediabetes, most of them unaware of it.

Diagnosis

Under American Diabetes Association criteria, prediabetes is indicated by any of: a glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) of 5.7–6.4%; a fasting plasma glucose of 100–125 mg/dL (5.6–6.9 mmol/L); or a two-hour glucose of 140–199 mg/dL (7.8–11.0 mmol/L) during a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test. The World Health Organization uses a higher fasting threshold (6.1–6.9 mmol/L) and does not endorse the HbA1c range, so prevalence estimates vary by definition.

Management

Lifestyle intervention is the cornerstone of treatment. In the landmark Diabetes Prevention Program trial, an intensive programme of diet, 150 minutes per week of moderate exercise and 5–7% body-weight loss reduced progression to diabetes by 58% over about three years — substantially more than metformin, which reduced it by 31%. Metformin is nonetheless recommended for some higher-risk patients. Many people with prediabetes can return their glucose levels to the normal range, and regular re-testing (at least annually) is advised.
