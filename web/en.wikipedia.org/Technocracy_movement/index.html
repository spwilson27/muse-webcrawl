Technocracy movement

Technocracy Inc. promoted rule by engineers and scientists in place of politicians and businessmen.

The technocracy movement was a popular mass movement in the United States and Canada during the Great Depression of the 1930s. It proposed replacing politicians and businesspeople with scientists and engineers — people with technical expertise — to manage the economy, and measuring economic activity in units of energy rather than money.

Origins and ideas

The movement drew on the ideas of Thorstein Veblen, who advocateded a "soviet of technicians", and on an energy survey of North America conducted by a group of engineers and scientists at Columbia University. Howard Scott emerged as the movement's leader, founding Technocracy Inc. in 1933. Technocrats proposed a continental administrative unit, the "Technate", in which production and distribution would be organized by technical criteria, citizens would receive energy certificates instead of wages or prices, and the working week would be drastically shortened because of automation.

Popular reception

Technocracy enjoyed a media vogue in 1932–1933; its members appeared in distinctive grey uniforms and drove grey cars marked with the movement's Monad symbol. The movement faded quickly amid internal disputes, the economic measures of the New Deal, and criticism of Scott's leadership, though Technocracy Inc. survived as a small organization. The word "technocracy" has since broadened to mean any governance dominated by technical experts, and the movement influenced later ideas about post-scarcity and resource-based economies.
