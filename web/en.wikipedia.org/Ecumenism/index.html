Ecumenism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ecumenical dialogue seeks visible unity among divided Christian churches.

Ecumenism is the concept and principle that Christians who belong to different Christian denominations should work together to develop closer relationships among their churches and promote Christian unity. The adjective ecumenical derives from the Greek oikoumenē, "the whole inhabited world". The modern ecumenical movement is generally dated from the 1910 World Missionary Conference in Edinburgh.

History

Christianity had split through the East–West Schism of 1054 and the Protestant Reformation of the 16th century. Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century missionary work exposed the scandal of divided churches preaching in the same fields. The movement produced the World Council of Churches, founded in Amsterdam in 1948, which today brings together hundreds of member churches from Protestant, Orthodox, Anglican and other traditions.

Roman Catholic involvement

The Second Vatican Council's decree Unitatis Redintegratio (1964) committed the Roman Catholic Church to ecumenism; the Church participates in dialogues and is a full member of some bodies, though not of the World Council of Churches itself. Major milestones include the lifting of the mutual excommunications of 1054 in 1965 and the Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification (1999) with the Lutheran World Federation.

Forms and challenges

Ecumenism takes forms from local cooperation and shared worship to formal doctrinal dialogues and organic church unions, as in the united churches of South India and elsewhere. Disagreements over ministry, sacraments, authority and moral teaching remain major obstacles.
