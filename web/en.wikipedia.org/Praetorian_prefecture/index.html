Praetorian prefecture

The praetorian prefecture (Latin: praefectura praetorio; Greek: ἐπαρχότης τῶν πραιτωρίων) was the largest administrative division of the late Roman Empire, above the mid-level dioceses and the low-level provinces.

The prefecture was the seat of the praetorian prefect, the empire's most senior civilian official, who acted as the emperor's chief administrator for finance, justice, and civil government in his assigned portion of the empire.

History

The office of praetorian prefect originated with the commanders of the Praetorian Guard under the early empire. Constantine I stripped the prefects of their military role and converted them into purely civilian administrators of large territorial circumscriptions. Over the fourth century the prefectures crystallised into four great divisions: the Gauls, Italy (later Italy and Africa), Illyricum, and the East. Dioceses governed by vicars and provinces governed by governors sat beneath them.

The four prefectures

The prefecture of the Gauls covered Britain, Gaul, Hispania and Mauretania Tingitana, with its capital at Trier and later Arles. The prefecture of Italy administered Italy, the Danubian provinces of the west and later Africa, from Milan and Ravenna. The prefecture of Illyricum, smallest of the four and long disputed between East and West, was administered from Sirmium and later Thessalonica. The prefecture of the East, the richest, covered the Balkans' eastern dioceses, Anatolia, the Levant and Egypt from Constantinople.

Decline

In the West the prefectures disappeared with the collapse of imperial government in the fifth century, though the structures survived for a time in the Ostrogothic kingdom. In the East the prefecture of the East and of Illyricum endured into the seventh century, when the invasions and administrative reforms associated with the theme system rendered them obsolete; the last praetorian prefect of the East is attested around 629.
