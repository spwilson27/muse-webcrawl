Porro prism

A Porro prism, named for its inventor Ignazio Porro, is a type of reflection prism used in optical instruments to alter the orientation of an image. It consists of a block of glass in the shape of a right geometric prism with right-angled isosceles triangles for its ends and a rectangular hypotenuse face. Porro prisms are most commonly used in pairs in binoculars to erect the image and fold the light path, making the instrument shorter than the focal length of its objective lenses would otherwise require.

Operation

Light entering the prism undergoes two internal reflections from the inclined faces, which reverse the image direction along one axis; a second identical prism rotated 90 degrees and placed after the first reverses the image along the other axis. The combined double Porro arrangement thus fully erects the inverted image formed by the objective while offsetting the light path, which also increases the separation between the objective lenses and improves depth perception by enhancing the stereoscopic effect.

Applications

Traditional Porro-prism binoculars are recognized by their offset, zig-zag barrels. Although roof prism designs have become more common in compact binoculars, Porro prism instruments remain popular for their lower manufacturing cost and excellent light transmission. Single Porro prisms are also used in some rifle sights, rangefinders, and camera viewfinders.
