In the mathematical field of graph theory, the Petersen graph is an undirected graph with 10 vertices and 15 edges. It is a small graph that serves as a useful example and counterexample for many problems in graph theory. The Petersen graph is named after Julius Petersen, who in 1898 constructed it to be the smallest bridgeless cubic graph that cannot be coloured with three colours for its edges.

Construction

The Petersen graph is most commonly drawn as a pentagon with a five-pointed star (pentagram) inside, with spokes connecting corresponding vertices. It can be constructed as the Kneser graph KG(5,2): its vertices correspond to the two-element subsets of a five-element set, and two vertices are adjacent when the subsets are disjoint. It is also the complement of the line graph of the complete graph K5, and a generalized Petersen graph GP(5,2).

Properties

The Petersen graph is cubic (3-regular) and strongly regular with parameters (10, 3, 0, 1): adjacent vertices have no common neighbours, while non-adjacent vertices have exactly one. It has girth 5 — its shortest cycles are pentagons — and is the smallest (3,5)-cage graph. Its automorphism group is the symmetric group S5, acting on the underlying five-element set.

Colouring and Hamiltonicity

Although its chromatic number is 3, the Petersen graph is the classic example of a snark: a bridgeless cubic graph whose edges cannot be coloured with only three colours. It has no Hamiltonian cycle, though deleting any single vertex leaves a Hamiltonian graph (it is hypohamiltonian). It is nonplanar but has crossing number 2.

Role in graph theory

Donald Knuth remarked that the Petersen graph is "a remarkable configuration that serves as a counterexample to many optimistic predictions about what might be true but fails for this odd beast." It appears in connection with the four colour theorem, Tait's conjectures, nowhere-zero flows, and numerous other topics.
