Weather balloon

A weather balloon, also known as a sounding balloon, is a high-altitude balloon that carries instruments aloft to send back information on atmospheric pressure, temperature, humidity, and wind speed, measured by a small expendable unit called a radiosonde. Weather balloons are a cornerstone of meteorological observation and numerical weather prediction.

Operation

The balloon is made of highly flexible latex or synthetic rubber and filled with hydrogen or helium. At launch it is about 2 metres in diameter; as it ascends into lower pressure it expands, reaching perhaps 6 metres or more before bursting at altitudes around 30–35 km. The radiosonde, a parachute, and sometimes a radar reflector or GPS receiver hang below on a line. A typical flight lasts about two hours, ascending at roughly 300 metres per minute and drifting as far as 200 km from the launch site.

The global network

About 1,300 upper-air stations worldwide launch radiosondes, most of them twice daily at 00:00 and 12:00 UTC, so that the data arrive simultaneously for assimilation into forecast models. The resulting vertical profiles of the atmosphere—temperature, dew point, and winds—are plotted on diagrams such as the skew-T log-P diagram.

History and other uses

Instrumented balloon sounding began in the 1890s with French meteorologist Léon Teisserenc de Bort, whose flights discovered the stratosphere. The modern radiosonde was developed in the late 1920s and 1930s by Pavel Molchanov and Robert Bureau. Weather balloons occasionally feature in public news, as with the 1947 Roswell incident, in which debris from a then-secret Project Mogul balloon train was initially reported as a crashed flying disc.
