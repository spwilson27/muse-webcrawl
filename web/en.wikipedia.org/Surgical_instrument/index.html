Surgical instrument

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A surgical instrument is a medical device designed to perform specific actions of carrying out desired effects during a surgery or operation, such as modifying biological tissue, or to provide access for viewing it. Over time, many different kinds of surgical instruments and tools have been invented, each designed for use in specific procedures or operations. Some surgical instruments are generic — used in many kinds of surgery — while others are designed for a specific procedure or body system, and are named accordingly.

Classification

Surgical instruments are commonly grouped by function:

  • Cutting and dissecting: scalpels, surgical scissors (such as Mayo and Metzenbaum scissors), knives, curettes, osteotomes, drills, and snares;
  • Grasping and holding: forceps (tissue, dressing, and thumb forceps), clamps, and needle holders;
  • Clamping and occluding: haemostats and vascular clamps, which control bleeding by compressing blood vessels;
  • Retracting and exposing: retractors, both hand-held (e.g. Army–Navy, Deaver) and self-retaining (e.g. Weitlaner, Balfour), which hold incisions and tissue open;
  • Suturing and stapling: needle holders, suture needles, and surgical staplers;
  • Suctioning and aspirating: suction tips such as the Yankauer and Poole sucker;
  • Accessories: dilators, probes, specula, retractors, and electrosurgical (Bovie) units for cutting and coagulation.

Materials

Most surgical instruments are made of high-grade stainless steel, which resists corrosion through repeated sterilisation in steam autoclaves. Some components use titanium (light, strong, and non-magnetic), tungsten carbide inserts in needle holders and scissors for durability, and plastics for single-use devices. Instruments are cleaned, inspected, packaged into procedure trays, and sterilised before each use, and counted before and after an operation to ensure none remains inside the patient.

History

Specialised instruments accompany surgery from antiquity: Roman excavations, most famously at Pompeii, have yielded bronze scalpels, forceps, probes, and specula resembling modern designs. The nineteenth century saw an explosion of named instruments associated with individual surgeons — Kocher's forceps, Kelly's clamps, Allis forceps — many of which remain in use. In modern practice, instrument sets are standardised for procedures, and energy-based devices (electrocautery, ultrasonic scalpels) and robotic instruments supplement the classic hand tools.
