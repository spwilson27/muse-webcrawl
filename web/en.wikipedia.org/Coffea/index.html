Coffea

Coffea is a genus of flowering plants in the family Rubiaceae. Coffea species are shrubs or small trees native to tropical and southern Africa and tropical Asia. The seeds of some species, called coffee beans, are used to flavor various beverages and products. The fruits, like the seeds, contain a large amount of caffeine, and have a distinct sweet taste. The plant ranks as one of the world's most valuable and widely traded commodity crops and is an important export product of several countries, including those in Central and South America, the Caribbean and Africa.

Description

Coffee plants are evergreen shrubs or small trees with glossy, dark-green, opposite leaves. They produce clusters of fragrant white flowers, followed by fruits called cherries that ripen from green to red or yellow. Each cherry normally contains two seeds—the coffee beans—surrounded by pulp and parchment. Plants are often pruned to a manageable height for harvest and are sensitive to frost, thriving in the "coffee belt" between the tropics.

Species

More than 120 species of Coffea have been described. Two dominate world trade: Coffea arabica, native to the highlands of Ethiopia, accounts for a majority of production and is prized for its flavour; it is a natural allotetraploid hybrid of C. canephora and C. eugenioides. Coffea canephora, known commercially as robusta, is hardier, more disease-resistant, and higher in caffeine, and is used heavily in espresso blends and instant coffee. Coffea liberica is grown locally in parts of Africa and Southeast Asia. Wild species, many endangered, are a genetic reservoir important for breeding against diseases such as coffee leaf rust and for adapting cultivation to climate change.

Cultivation and ecology

Coffee is grown in more than 70 countries on millions of small farms. Traditional shade-grown systems support rich biodiversity, while sun-grown plantations raise yields at environmental cost. Major threats include coffee leaf rust (Hemileia vastatrix), which devastated Ceylon's industry in the 19th century, and the coffee berry borer beetle. Climate change is projected to shrink suitable arabica-growing areas substantially this century, pushing cultivation to higher elevations.
