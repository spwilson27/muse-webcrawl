Anglo-Spanish War (1585–1604)

The Anglo-Spanish War (1585–1604) was an intermittent, never formally declared conflict between the kingdoms of England and Spain, fought as part of the wider European struggle between Protestant and Catholic powers and intertwined with the Dutch Revolt and the French Wars of Religion. It is best remembered for the defeat of the Spanish Armada in 1588, but the war at sea and in Ireland, the Netherlands and France dragged on for nineteen years, bankrupting neither side but exhausting both, and it ended with the Treaty of London in 1604, shortly after the deaths of Elizabeth I and Philip II.

Causes

Relations between Elizabeth I's Protestant England and Philip II's Catholic Spain decayed through disputes over English privateering against Spanish treasure fleets, Philip's support for Catholic plots against Elizabeth, and England's increasing aid to the Dutch rebels fighting Spanish rule in the Netherlands. In 1585 Philip seized English ships in Spanish ports and Elizabeth signed the Treaty of Nonsuch, openly committing English troops under the Earl of Leicester to the Dutch cause — an act Philip treated as war. Sir Francis Drake's raids on Santo Domingo, Cartagena and Cádiz (the "singeing of the King of Spain's beard", 1587) followed.

The Armada and after

In 1588 Philip launched the Spanish Armada — about 130 ships carrying some 30,000 men — to escort Parma's army from Flanders to invade England. Harried up the Channel by English gunnery, scattered by fireships at Calais and beaten at Gravelines, the Armada was driven north around Scotland and Ireland, losing perhaps a third of its ships, many to storms. England's own counter-Armada of 1589 against Lisbon failed disastrously, restoring the balance at sea. Further Spanish armadas sailed in 1596 and 1597, and Spanish troops landed at Kinsale in Ireland in 1601 to support the Nine Years' War, surrendering after an English siege in 1602. Meanwhile English privateers and expeditions — Essex's capture of Cádiz in 1596 among them — kept up a costly guerre de course against Spanish shipping, while Spain strained to fight simultaneously in France and the Netherlands.

Peace

With Philip II dead (1598) and Elizabeth dying in 1603, her successor James I moved quickly to peace. The Treaty of London (August 1604) restored the status quo ante: Spain recognised the Protestant succession and renounced intervention in England, England ended support for the Dutch revolt and its privateering, and the Channel was opened to Spanish shipping. The war had confirmed England as a naval power and foreshadowed Spain's long relative decline at sea.
