Bohr model

The Bohr model of the atom, proposed by the Danish physicist Niels Bohr in 1913, depicts the atom as a small, positively charged nucleus surrounded by electrons travelling in circular orbits, similar in structure to the Solar System but with attraction provided by electrostatic forces rather than gravity. It was a decisive improvement on the Rutherford model and an important precursor of quantum mechanics.

Postulates

Bohr's model rested on several revolutionary assumptions:

  • Electrons can occupy only certain allowed orbits, in which they do not radiate energy despite being accelerated.
  • The angular momentum of an electron in an allowed orbit is quantized, taking only values that are integer multiples of ħ (the reduced Planck constant): m v r = n ħ, where n = 1, 2, 3, …
  • Electrons emit or absorb radiation only when jumping between orbits, with photon energy equal to the difference in orbital energies, E = hν.

Successes and failures

The model triumphantly reproduced the Rydberg formula for the spectral lines of hydrogen, and allowed the Rydberg constant to be calculated from fundamental constants. It also explained the size scale of atoms and gave a physical basis for the periodic table through electron shells.

However, the Bohr model failed for atoms with more than one electron, could not explain the fine structure of spectral lines or the relative intensities of lines, and was inconsistent with the uncertainty principle. It was superseded in the 1920s by the full quantum-mechanical description of Schrödinger and Heisenberg, in which electrons are described by orbitals — probability distributions — rather than definite orbits. Nevertheless, the Bohr model remains a standard introduction to atomic structure, and its concept of discrete energy levels and "quantum jumps" transformed physics.

Legacy

Bohr's institute in Copenhagen became a centre of the new quantum theory, and Bohr received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1922. A refined version of the model (the Bohr–Sommerfeld model) allowed elliptical orbits, but the essential quantization idea survived intact into modern quantum mechanics, where angular momentum remains quantized — though the ground state of hydrogen has zero angular momentum, unlike in Bohr's picture.
