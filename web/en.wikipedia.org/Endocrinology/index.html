Endocrinology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Endocrinology (from Greek éndon, "within", and krīnō, "to separate", plus -logy) is a branch of biology and medicine dealing with the endocrine system, its diseases, and its specific secretions known as hormones. It is also concerned with the integration of developmental events — proliferation, growth, and differentiation — and the psychological or behavioural activities of metabolism, growth and development, tissue function, sleep, digestion, respiration, excretion, mood, stress, lactation, movement, reproduction, and sensory perception caused by hormones.

The endocrine system

The endocrine system consists of glands that secrete hormones directly into the blood: the pituitary, thyroid, parathyroids, adrenal glands, pancreas, ovaries and testes, together with hormone-producing tissues elsewhere, coordinated partly through the hypothalamus. Hormones regulate target organs at a distance and are themselves controlled by feedback loops, most famously the hypothalamic–pituitary axes.

Diseases

Disorders arise from hormone excess, deficiency, or resistance. Common conditions managed by endocrinologists include diabetes mellitus, thyroid disorders (hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, goitre), disorders of the pituitary and adrenal glands (Cushing's syndrome, Addison's disease), osteoporosis, disorders of growth and puberty, and lipid and reproductive disorders. Diabetes alone affects hundreds of millions of people worldwide and is the specialty's largest clinical burden.

History

The field is often dated to Thomas Addison's description of adrenal insufficiency in 1855 and the Bayliss and Starling discovery of secretin in 1902 — the first use of the word "hormone". The isolation of insulin by Banting, Best, Macleod and Collip in 1921–22 transformed type 1 diabetes from a fatal disease into a manageable one and remains the discipline's emblematic triumph.
