Close air support

Close air support (CAS) is defined as aerial warfare actions — often air interdiction and airstrikes — by military aircraft against hostile targets in close proximity to friendly forces. Because of the danger of friendly fire, CAS requires detailed integration of each air mission with the fire and movement of ground forces, usually coordinated by a forward air controller or joint terminal attack controller (JTAC) in communication with the aircraft.

History

Aircraft first supported ground troops in the First World War, strafing trenches and spotting for artillery. The Second World War saw the development of dedicated ground-attack aircraft such as the German Ju 87 Stuka and the Soviet Il-2, and of "cab rank" fighter-bomber tactics over Normandy. In Korea and Vietnam, jet aircraft, helicopters and gunships such as the AC-47 and later the AC-130 took over much of the role, while the A-10 Thunderbolt II was purpose-built for CAS during the Cold War.

Modern practice

Modern CAS uses precision-guided munitions, targeting pods, GPS coordinates and digital data links to strike targets sometimes within a few hundred metres of friendly troops ("danger close"). Fast jets, attack helicopters, armed drones and gunships all fly CAS missions; success depends on training, communications and clear rules of engagement to avoid fratricide and civilian casualties.

Debates

Air forces have sometimes resisted the CAS mission in favour of strategic bombing or air superiority, leading to recurring debates about dedicated aircraft versus multirole platforms.
