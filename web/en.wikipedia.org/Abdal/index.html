Abdal

Abdal (Arabic: أبدال, plural of badal, "substitute") is a term in Islamic, especially Sufi, theology for a class of saints believed to sustain the order of the world. The Abdal form part of an invisible hierarchy of holy men (the rijāl al-ghayb, "men of the unseen"), headed by the Qutb (pole) and including the Awtad and other ranks.

Doctrine

According to the tradition, there are always a fixed number of Abdal in the world — often given as forty, sometimes seven or seventy — concentrated particularly in Syria (al-Sham). When one dies, God immediately substitutes another in his place, hence the name "substitutes". They are hidden from ordinary recognition and travel the world performing miracles and interceding for humanity. The concept is attributed in hadith literature and was elaborated by Sufi writers such as al-Hakim al-Tirmidhi and Ibn Arabi, who integrated the Abdal into his doctrine of the hierarchy of saints.

In Sufi practice

The Abdal are invoked in prayers for rain, healing, and protection. In Levantine popular religion, shrines associated with the forty Abdal were visited by supplicants, and the number forty carries wider symbolic resonance in Islamic folklore. Some traditions also connect the Abdal with the mysterious figure al-Khidr.
