The Communist International, known as the Comintern or Third International, was an international organisation of communist parties founded in Moscow in 1919 with the aim of promoting world revolution and replacing capitalism with socialism by all available means, including armed force.

History

The Comintern was established under Vladimir Lenin after the Russian Revolution, splitting the world socialist movement from the reformist Second International. Member parties were bound by the Twenty-One Conditions, which required democratic centralism and loyalty to the Soviet model. In its early years it sought to spread revolution in Europe, supporting uprisings in Germany and Hungary that ultimately failed.

Under Joseph Stalin the organisation became increasingly an instrument of Soviet foreign policy. Its "Third Period" line denounced social democrats as "social fascists", a stance reversed in 1935 by the Popular Front strategy of alliances against fascism. Many foreign communists were caught up in the Great Purge. Stalin dissolved the Comintern in 1943 as a gesture to his Western wartime allies.

Legacy

Through its schools, emissaries, and funding, the Comintern shaped communist parties worldwide and trained a generation of leaders, while its subordination to Moscow left a lasting mark on the international left.
