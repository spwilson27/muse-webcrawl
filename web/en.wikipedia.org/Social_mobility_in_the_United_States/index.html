Social mobility in the United States

Social mobility in the United States refers to the extent to which individuals change their socioeconomic position relative to their parents, measured through intergenerational income elasticity, rank-rank correlations, occupational movement, and educational attainment. The United States has traditionally celebrated high mobility as part of the "American Dream," but empirical research since the 1990s finds U.S. relative mobility lower than in many other wealthy countries, particularly the Nordic nations and Canada.

Findings

Estimates of the U.S. intergenerational income elasticity cluster around 0.4–0.6, meaning roughly half of the earnings advantage of high-income parents is passed to their children. Research led by Raj Chetty and colleagues, using de-identified tax records for tens of millions of families, found that relative mobility has been roughly stable across recent birth cohorts, but that absolute mobility — the share of children out-earning their parents — fell from about 90 percent for the 1940 birth cohort to about 50 percent for the 1980 cohort, reflecting slower growth and its unequal distribution. The same research program documented wide variation across places: children raised in areas such as Salt Lake City and San Jose experience far more upward mobility than those raised in parts of the Southeast and industrial Midwest, with mobility correlated with less segregation, better schools, stronger social capital, and more two-parent families.

Factors

Researchers emphasize education, family structure, neighborhood quality, race, and inherited wealth. Mobility is lower for Black Americans than white Americans at the same parental income, a gap Chetty's team traced in large part to outcomes for Black men. Education remains a major channel of upward movement, though access to selective colleges is skewed toward affluent families.

Political significance

Mobility statistics figure prominently in debates over inequality, taxation, and education policy, with the "Great Gatsby curve" — the cross-country association between higher inequality and lower mobility — frequently cited. Surveys show Americans systematically overestimate actual mobility rates.
