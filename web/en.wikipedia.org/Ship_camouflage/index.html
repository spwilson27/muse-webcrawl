Ship camouflage

Ship camouflage is a form of military deception used on naval and merchant vessels, most famously in the two World Wars.

Ship camouflage is a form of military deception in which ships are painted, and sometimes structurally modified, to make them harder for an enemy to detect, identify, or aim at. It was extensively applied to naval and merchant vessels during the First and Second World Wars in response to the threat of submarines and torpedo attack.

First World War and dazzle

Early attempts simply painted ships in uniform grey or disruptive tones to reduce visibility. In 1917 the British marine artist Norman Wilkinson proposed dazzle camouflage (called "razzle dazzle" in the United States): bold, contrasting geometric patterns that did not hide the ship but distorted its outline, making it difficult for a U-boat commander using a periscope to judge the vessel's type, size, speed and course — the quantities needed to aim a torpedo. Thousands of British and American ships were painted in individually designed dazzle schemes.

Second World War and after

In the Second World War navies standardised graded camouflage "measures": light and dark greys, graded hull shading, false bow waves and painted false bows and sterns to confuse range-finding, alongside continued disruptive patterns. Effectiveness was debated, as it is hard to separate from convoying and other countermeasures. After 1945, radar and modern sensors reduced the value of visual camouflage, and most warships adopted plain haze-grey schemes, though experiments with patterned paint recur from time to time.
