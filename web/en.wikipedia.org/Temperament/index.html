Temperament

Temperament in psychology refers to consistent individual differences in behaviour that are biologically based and relatively independent of learning, values and attitudes. It describes the innate aspects of personality — such as activity level, emotional reactivity and sociability — that appear early in life and remain moderately stable over time.

Historical theories

The oldest temperament theory is the four temperaments of Hippocratic and Galenic medicine — sanguine, choleric, melancholic and phlegmatic — based on the balance of the four humours. Modern research replaced humoral theory with dimensional models: Hans Eysenck's neuroticism and extraversion, and Mary Rothbart's dimensions of surgency, negative affect and effortful control in children.

Research

Alexander Thomas and Stella Chess's New York Longitudinal Study identified nine temperament characteristics and grouped infants as "easy", "difficult" or "slow to warm up", introducing the concept of goodness of fit between a child's temperament and their environment. Jerome Kagan's work on behavioural inhibition showed that highly reactive infants are more likely to become inhibited toddlers. Temperament is considered the biological foundation upon which personality develops.
