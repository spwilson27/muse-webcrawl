In Māori society, a hapū is a division of a larger tribe, or iwi: it is the primary political unit in traditional Māori social organisation. A Māori person can belong to or have links to many hapū, but the hapū with which a person is mainly identified is usually the one where their parent or grandparent had local land rights.

Organisation

Before European colonisation, hapū were largely independent units controlling defined territory, owning land communally and acting collectively in matters of warfare, resource use and diplomacy. Each hapū consisted of several whānau (extended families) led by a rangatira (chief). Historically a hapū numbered roughly 150 to 200 people — the practical limit for subsistence farming in a defined area — though many were smaller or larger.

Name and meaning

The word hapū also means "pregnant", and members may interpret the term metaphorically: the members of a hapū were born of the same womb, suggesting the bonds of kinship that unite them. When Māori signed the Treaty of Waitangi in 1840, they generally did so at the hapū level, and much modern research into Treaty claims emphasises hapū as the group holding rangatiratanga (authority).

Modern role

Today hapū remain important social and political units; several hapū together form an iwi, and in recent decades hapū assertiveness within some iwi has grown, particularly in relation to Treaty settlement negotiations and management of traditional territories.
