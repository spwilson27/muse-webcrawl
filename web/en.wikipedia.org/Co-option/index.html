Co-option

Co-option, also spelled co-optation or coöption, is the process by which a group, committee, or organisation adds new members by invitation or appointment by the existing members, rather than by election by a wider constituency. In a broader sociological sense, co-option describes the strategy by which an established power absorbs potential challengers — individuals, movements, or ideas — into its own structures, thereby neutralising opposition while lending itself an appearance of openness and renewal.

Organisational use

Many boards, parish councils, learned societies, and foundation trustees are empowered to co-opt members to fill casual vacancies, secure particular expertise, or balance representation between elections. The Roman Senate under the emperors, the self-electing colleges of the older universities, and modern corporate boards all illustrate the principle: continuity and control rest with those already inside, who select their future colleagues. Critics note that co-option can entrench homogeneity and self-perpetuation, while defenders argue it preserves competence and institutional memory.

Co-option in social theory

The sociologist Philip Selznick gave the term analytical prominence in his 1949 study TVA and the Grass Roots, describing how the Tennessee Valley Authority deflected opposition by formally absorbing local elites into its decision-making structures — a process he called formal co-optation. Later writers applied the concept to social movements: governments and corporations may co-opt movement leaders, fund moderate factions, or adopt diluted versions of radical demands. In cultural criticism, co-option (or recuperation, in Situationist terminology) describes the mainstreaming of subcultural styles and oppositional symbols, converting them into commodities.

Related concepts

Co-option overlaps with regulatory capture, in which regulators come to serve the industries they oversee, and with Gramsci's notion of trasformismo, the absorption of opposition leaders into a governing bloc. In evolutionary biology, co-option (or exaptation) is used analogously for a trait evolved for one purpose being recruited for another.
