Random effects model

A random effects model is a statistical model in which some parameters (effects) are treated as random variables drawn from a population distribution, rather than as fixed unknown constants. Such models are a standard tool in panel data analysis, hierarchical modeling, and meta-analysis.

In panel data

In econometrics, the random effects model for panel data assumes that unobserved unit-specific effects are uncorrelated with the explanatory variables, allowing estimation by generalized least squares that combines within-unit and between-unit variation. When that assumption fails, the fixed effects model is preferred; the Hausman test is commonly used to choose between them.

In meta-analysis

A random-effects meta-analysis assumes the true effect size varies across studies according to a distribution (typically normal with between-study variance τ²), in contrast to a fixed-effect model that assumes one common effect. Estimators such as DerSimonian–Laird are used to estimate τ², and the model generally produces wider confidence intervals.

Mixed models

Models containing both fixed and random effects are called mixed models; they generalize random effects models to complex hierarchical and longitudinal data.
