Internet activism

Internet activism, also known as online activism, digital campaigning, digital activism, online organizing, electronic advocacy, e-campaigning, and e-activism, is the use of electronic communication technologies such as social media, email, and podcasts for various forms of activism to enable faster and more effective communication by citizen movements, the delivery of particular information to large and specific audiences, as well as coordination.

Researchers from the Oxford Internet Institute and other institutions have described the internet as a tool for "critical mass" public activism achieved with minimal resources.

Types

Online activism can be divided into awareness and advocacy, organization and mobilization, and action and reaction. Common techniques include online petitions, hashtag activism on social media platforms, email campaigns to officials, virtual sit-ins, and distributed denial-of-service protests by groups such as Anonymous.

Examples

Early examples include the 1994 Lotus Marketplace controversy and the Zapatista movement's use of the internet in the mid-1990s. The 1999 Seattle WTO protests used the Indymedia network for citizen journalism. Later movements such as the Arab Spring of 2010–2012, Occupy Wall Street, the Ice Bucket Challenge, #MeToo, and Fridays for Future relied heavily on online coordination.

The 2012 SOPA/PIPA protests, in which Wikipedia and other sites went dark, are widely credited with defeating the proposed US legislation.

Criticism

Critics coined the term "slacktivism" for low-effort online gestures that substitute for substantive action. Others note that internet activism can be monitored by states, manipulated by astroturfing and bots, and that digital divides limit participation.
