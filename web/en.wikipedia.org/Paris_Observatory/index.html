The Paris Observatory (French: Observatoire de Paris) is France's national astronomical observatory and one of the oldest in continuous operation in the world. Founded in 1667 under King Louis XIV and his minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert, shortly after the creation of the French Academy of Sciences, its main historic site stands in the 14th arrondissement of Paris, with additional campuses at Meudon and a radio astronomy station at Nançay. It is a constituent institution of PSL University.

The Paris Observatory building, designed by Claude Perrault

History

The observatory's first director was the Italian astronomer Giovanni Domenico Cassini, beginning a family dynasty of four generations of Cassinis who directed it for over a century. Early work included measuring the longitude and mapping France, determining the distance to Mars (and thus the scale of the Solar System), and Cassini's discovery of four moons of Saturn and the division in its rings that bears his name. The Paris meridian, running through the building, was for centuries France's prime meridian and rivalled Greenwich; the metre was originally defined from meridian measurements through Paris.

19th century onward

Under François Arago and then Urbain Le Verrier, the observatory was central to celestial mechanics: Le Verrier's calculations of anomalies in the orbit of Uranus led to the discovery of Neptune in 1846. The Meudon site, added in 1876, became a centre for solar physics and astrophotography and hosted the Carte du Ciel project. Today the institution, formally the Observatoire de Paris – PSL, employs several hundred researchers working in fields from planetary science and cosmology to high-precision timekeeping; it hosts France's official time service.
