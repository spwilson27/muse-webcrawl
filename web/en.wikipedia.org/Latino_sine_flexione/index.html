Latino sine flexione ("Latin without inflections"), also called Interlingua in its inventor's usage, is a constructed international auxiliary language devised by the Italian mathematician Giuseppe Peano in 1903. It consists of Latin vocabulary stripped of grammatical inflections: nouns and verbs appear in invariant forms (nouns typically in the form of the Latin ablative, verbs in the imperative), with grammatical relations expressed by word order and prepositions instead of case endings and conjugations.

History

Peano presented the language in his 1903 article De Latino Sine Flexione, Lingua Auxiliare Internationale, published in the Revue de Mathématiques — an article that famously begins in classical Latin and progressively sheds inflections until the final part is written in Latino sine flexione itself. Peano promoted the language through the Academia pro Interlingua, which he directed, and published journals and dictionaries in it. The language later evolved under Peano and his circle into a slightly regularized variant; after Peano's death, and later still, the name "Interlingua" was taken up by the distinct language developed by the International Auxiliary Language Association (IALA) and published in 1951, which drew heavily on Peano's pioneering idea.

Grammar

In Latino sine flexione there are no declensions or conjugations. Nouns use a single form (for most, the Latin ablative singular: e.g. patre for "father"), plural is often unmarked or shown by context or quantifiers, verbs use a single form for all persons and tenses, with auxiliaries such as vol (will) for the future and habe (has) for the perfect. Articles and pronouns are drawn from Latin. Because vocabulary is taken directly from Latin, anyone educated in Latin can read the language with minimal learning, which was Peano's chief selling point: the international scientific vocabulary already derives largely from Latin roots.

Example

The Lord's Prayer begins: "Patre nostro, qui es in celos, que tuo nomine fi sanctificato..." — a rendering in which every word retains a recognizable Latin shape while all inflection has been discarded.
