Neuromodulation

Implantable neuromodulation devices deliver electrical stimulation to targeted neural structures.

Neuromodulation is the physiological process by which a given neuron uses one or more neurotransmitters to regulate diverse populations of neurons. Neuromodulators typically act through G protein-coupled receptors on slower timescales than classical fast neurotransmission, and a single neuron can receive and integrate multiple modulatory inputs — including dopamine, serotonin, acetylcholine, norepinephrine, histamine, and neuropeptides — that tune its excitability and synaptic strength.

Medical neuromodulation

The term is also used for a family of medical therapies that alter nervous system activity through targeted delivery of electrical stimulation or pharmacological agents. Established implantable therapies include deep brain stimulation for Parkinson's disease, essential tremor, and dystonia; spinal cord stimulation for chronic neuropathic pain; vagus nerve stimulation for epilepsy and depression; sacral nerve stimulation for bladder and bowel dysfunction; and intrathecal drug delivery pumps. Non-invasive approaches include transcranial magnetic stimulation and transcranial direct-current stimulation.

Mechanisms

At the cellular level, neuromodulators alter ion channel function, change the gain of synaptic transmission, and gate forms of synaptic plasticity, effectively reconfiguring the functional state of neural circuits. Small invertebrate circuits such as the crustacean stomatogastric ganglion have been central model systems demonstrating how the same anatomical network can produce different outputs under different modulatory states.
