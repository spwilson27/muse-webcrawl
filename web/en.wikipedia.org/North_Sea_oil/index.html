North Sea oil

North Sea oil is a mixture of hydrocarbons, comprising liquid petroleum and natural gas, produced from petroleum reservoirs beneath the North Sea. In oil-industry terminology, the "North Sea" often includes areas such as the Norwegian Sea and the area east of Shetland known as the East Shetland Basin. Brent crude, one of the world's principal oil price benchmarks, takes its name from the Brent oilfield in the British sector.

History

Commercial exploration began in the 1960s after the Groningen gas discovery in the Netherlands in 1959 suggested hydrocarbons might lie offshore. The 1958 Geneva Convention and subsequent agreements divided the North Sea continental shelf among the United Kingdom, Norway, Denmark, Germany, and the Netherlands. The first major finds were the Ekofisk field (Norway, 1969) and the Forties field (UK, 1970), followed by Brent (1971) and Statfjord (1974). Production began in the mid-1970s, transforming the economies of Norway and, to a lesser extent, Scotland and the wider UK.

Production and decline

UK production peaked in 1999 at about 2.9 million barrels per day, and Norwegian production peaked in the early 2000s. Both sectors have since been in gradual decline as the largest fields mature, although new technologies and smaller-field developments have slowed the fall. Aberdeen in Scotland and Stavanger in Norway became the industry's principal service centres. In 1988 the Piper Alpha platform disaster killed 167 men, leading to sweeping reforms of the UK offshore safety regime.

Economic and political significance

Norway channelled much of its oil revenue into the Government Pension Fund Global, one of the world's largest sovereign wealth funds. In Britain, North Sea revenues were a major political issue, feeding debates about Scottish independence and the management of public finances. Decommissioning of ageing infrastructure is now a major activity, and parts of the basin are being studied for carbon capture and storage and offshore wind development.
