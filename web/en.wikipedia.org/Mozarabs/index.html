The Mozarabs (Spanish: mozárabes) were the Iberian Christians living under Muslim rule in Al-Andalus after the Umayyad conquest of Hispania in 711. Their descendants remained unconverted to Islam, but adopted elements of Arabic language and culture; the name derives from the Arabic musta'rib (مستعرب), meaning "Arabized". As dhimmis, they paid the jizya tax and faced legal restrictions but were allowed to worship and govern their internal affairs under their own bishops and counts.

Over time the Mozarabs became heavily Arabized in dress, names and language — many spoke Andalusi Arabic and the Romance vernacular known today as Mozarabic — while preserving their distinctive Visigothic liturgy, the Mozarabic rite (also called the Hispanic rite), celebrated in churches such as those of Toledo.

History

In the 9th century, a movement of voluntary martyrs in Córdoba (850s), recorded by Eulogius of Córdoba, protested against growing Arabization. Waves of Mozarabs migrated north to the Christian kingdoms, bringing agricultural, architectural and scholarly knowledge; others were deported to North Africa after revolts. After Alfonso VI conquered Toledo in 1085, its large Mozarabic community kept its rite, which Cardinal Cisneros later revived and codified in a printed missal around 1500. The Almoravid and Almohad periods brought harsher persecution and mass deportations, and the distinct Mozarabic population gradually disappeared through migration, conversion and assimilation after the Christian conquest of the south (13th century).

Legacy

Mozarabic influence survives in Iberian Romance vocabulary, in the horseshoe-arch architecture called Mozarabic art built by emigrants in the northern kingdoms (e.g. San Miguel de Escalada), and in the still-celebrated Mozarabic Mass in Toledo Cathedral.
