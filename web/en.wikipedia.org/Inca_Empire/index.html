Inca Empire

The Inca Empire (Quechua: Tawantinsuyu, "the four parts together"), also known as the Incan Empire, was the largest empire in pre-Columbian America. Its administrative, political, and military center was the city of Cusco, in present-day Peru. The empire arose from the Peruvian highlands sometime in the early 13th century and expanded rapidly after 1438 under the emperor Pachacuti, stretching at its height along the Andes from present-day Colombia to central Chile.

History

The Inca state began as a small kingdom around Cusco. Under Pachacuti Inca Yupanqui (r. 1438–1471), who repelled a Chanka invasion and then conquered widely, and his successors Topa Inca Yupanqui and Huayna Capac, the empire absorbed much of the Andean world. After Huayna Capac's death (c. 1527), his sons Atahualpa and Huáscar fought a civil war from which Atahualpa emerged victorious in 1532 — just as Francisco Pizarro's Spanish expedition arrived. Pizarro captured Atahualpa at Cajamarca in November 1532, executed him in 1533, and took Cusco. A Neo-Inca state held out at Vilcabamba until 1572.

Society and administration

The empire was divided into four quarters (suyu) meeting at Cusco. Subjects owed labor tax (mit'a) rather than tribute in goods; the state maintained storehouses, resettled populations (mitma), and recorded information with knotted cords called quipu. An extensive road network, the Qhapaq Ñan, of some 40,000 km linked the realm, with relay runners (chasqui) carrying messages. Quechua was spread as an administrative language.

Achievements

The Inca are famed for monumental dry-stone masonry with precisely fitted blocks, as at Sacsayhuamán and Machu Picchu; terraced agriculture producing potatoes, maize, and quinoa at high altitude; freeze-dried food storage (chuño); and fine textiles and metalwork — all achieved without wheeled transport, draft animals larger than llamas, iron tools, or a widely used writing system.
