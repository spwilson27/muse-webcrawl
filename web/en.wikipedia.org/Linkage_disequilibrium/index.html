Linkage disequilibrium

In population genetics, linkage disequilibrium (LD) is the non-random association of alleles at different loci in a population — that is, the tendency for particular combinations of alleles (haplotypes) to occur more or less frequently than would be expected if the loci assorted independently. When alleles are inherited together at the rate expected under independence, the loci are in linkage equilibrium.

Measures

The basic measure is the coefficient D, the difference between the observed frequency of a haplotype and the frequency expected from the product of the individual allele frequencies: D = pAB − pApB. Because the range of D depends on allele frequencies, standardised measures are usually preferred: D′ (D normalised by its theoretical maximum, introduced by Richard Lewontin) and r², the squared correlation coefficient between alleles at the two loci. An r² of 1 indicates perfect LD, in which the two loci provide identical information.

Causes and decay

LD arises through mutation (a new allele initially occurs on a single haplotype background), genetic drift, natural selection (including hitchhiking of nearby variants with a favoured allele), population admixture and structure, and non-random mating. It is broken down by recombination: under random mating, D decays by a factor of (1 − c) per generation, where c is the recombination fraction between the loci. Closely linked loci therefore retain LD for many generations, while unlinked loci reach equilibrium quickly.

Applications

Patterns of LD are fundamental to genome-wide association studies (GWAS): because a genotyped marker is correlated with nearby untyped variants, an association signal can localise a causal variant within an LD block. LD structure also informs haplotype maps (such as the HapMap project), imputation of genotypes, inference of demographic history and selection scans, and the design of association studies, where "tag SNPs" are chosen to capture variation efficiently.
