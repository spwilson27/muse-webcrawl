Good manufacturing practice

Good manufacturing practice (GMP) is a system for ensuring that products are consistently produced and controlled according to quality standards. It is designed to minimise the risks involved in any pharmaceutical production that cannot be eliminated through testing the final product. GMP covers all aspects of production, from starting materials, premises and equipment to the training and personal hygiene of staff.

Principles

GMP regulations require that manufacturing processes be clearly defined and controlled; that critical steps be validated; that instructions and procedures be written in clear, unambiguous language; that operators be trained; that records be made during manufacture demonstrating that required steps were taken; and that any significant deviations be investigated. Products must be stored and distributed under conditions that minimise risk to their quality, and a recall system must be in place.

Regulation

In the United States, current good manufacturing practice (cGMP) for pharmaceuticals is enforced by the Food and Drug Administration under regulations including 21 CFR Parts 210 and 211. The European Union enforces equivalent standards through the EudraLex rules and national inspectorates, with mutual recognition of inspections. The World Health Organization publishes GMP guidelines used by many national regulators, and the PIC/S scheme promotes international harmonisation. GMP-like regimes also apply to food, cosmetics, dietary supplements and medical devices, though with differing strictness.

Consequences of non-compliance

Failure to comply can result in regulatory warning letters, import alerts, product seizures, injunctions and closure of facilities. High-profile contamination incidents — such as the 2008 heparin crisis — have repeatedly driven tightening of GMP enforcement and supply-chain oversight.
