Levee

A levee (from the French lever, "to raise") is an embankment built to prevent the overflow of a river. Levees are constructed from earth, clay, sand, or other materials and run parallel to the course of a river or along a coastline, confining floodwaters to the channel and protecting the land behind them. Natural levees also form without human intervention: when a river floods, it deposits its coarsest sediment nearest the banks, gradually raising them above the surrounding floodplain.

History

The earliest levees were raised by the ancient civilisations of Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Indus Valley and China, where flood control was bound up with irrigation agriculture. In China, embanking of the Yellow River is recorded over more than two millennia and produced the river's famous peril: confined by its own levees, the silt-laden riverbed rose above the surrounding plain. The Dutch built extensive dike systems from the Middle Ages onward, and French and later American engineers raised levees along the Mississippi from the early 18th century; the Mississippi levee system, overseen since 1879 by the Mississippi River Commission and the U.S. Army Corps of Engineers, now runs for thousands of kilometres.

Design and failure

Engineered levees are wide-based trapezoidal embankments, often with a clay core to resist seepage, sometimes supplemented by floodwalls, relief wells, and setback alignments that give the river room. Failure modes include overtopping, erosion, internal seepage and "piping" beneath the embankment, and slope collapse. Catastrophic breaches occurred in the Great Mississippi Flood of 1927 and during Hurricane Katrina in 2005, when levee and floodwall failures inundated most of New Orleans. Because levees raise flood levels downstream and encourage development in floodplains (the "levee effect"), modern flood management increasingly combines levees with wetlands, bypasses and controlled flood storage.
