Ordination

Ordination
Practice of: Many religions, including Christianity, Buddhism and Judaism
Purpose: Consecration and appointment to religious ministry or office

Ordination is the process by which individuals are consecrated — set apart and elevated from the laity class to the clergy — and thus authorised to perform various religious rites and ceremonies. The process and ceremonies of ordination vary by religion and denomination, but ordination traditionally involves the laying on of hands by those already ordained.

Christianity

In Catholic, Orthodox and many Anglican traditions, ordination is a sacrament (holy orders) conferring ordination to the threefold ministry of bishop, priest and deacon, and is held to impart an indelible sacramental character. In most Protestant churches, ordination is the recognition by a congregation or denomination of a person's call to ministry, typically to the office of pastor or elder. The ordination of women is practised in many denominations and remains contested in others.

Other religions

In Theravada and Mahayana Buddhism, ordination admits monks (bhikkhu) and nuns (bhikkhuni) to the sangha, with higher ordination (upasampadā) following a period as a novice. In Judaism, semikhah is the formal ordination of rabbis. Similar ceremonies of appointment exist in Hindu, Sikh and other traditions.
