The Grapes of Wrath is a novel by American writer John Steinbeck, published in 1939. Set during the Great Depression, the novel focuses on the Joads, a poor family of tenant farmers driven from their Oklahoma home by drought, economic hardship, agricultural industry changes, and bank foreclosures forcing tenant farmers out of work. Due to their nearly hopeless situation, the Joads set out for California seeking jobs, land, dignity, and a future.

Plot summary

Tom Joad returns from prison to find his family preparing to leave their dust-choked farm. Joined by former preacher Jim Casy, the family travels west on Route 66 in a dilapidated truck. Along the way, the grandparents die, family members desert, and the Joads face hostility toward "Okies" in California, where migrant laborers are exploited and wages driven down. The novel ends after floods destroy the family's remaining shelter, with Rose of Sharon, having lost her baby, offering her breast milk to a starving stranger.

Writing and publication

Steinbeck researched the novel by visiting migrant camps with Tom Collins of the Farm Security Administration, and drew on his earlier journalistic series "The Harvest Gypsies." The title comes from the "Battle Hymn of the Republic" and from Revelation 14:19–20. The novel alternates narrative chapters about the Joads with short intercalary chapters describing the broader social landscape.

Reception

The novel won the National Book Award and the Pulitzer Prize for fiction and was a bestseller, but it also provoked intense controversy: it was banned and publicly burned in some places, and California growers condemned its depiction of their practices. When Steinbeck received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1962, The Grapes of Wrath was cited prominently. John Ford's 1940 film adaptation, starring Henry Fonda as Tom Joad, won two Academy Awards.
