Magnetoencephalography

Magnetoencephalography (MEG) is a functional neuroimaging technique for mapping brain activity by recording magnetic fields produced by electrical currents occurring naturally in the brain, using very sensitive magnetometers. The fields are extraordinarily weak — on the order of femtoteslas — so MEG systems use SQUID sensors cooled by liquid helium inside magnetically shielded rooms.

Principles

When populations of neurons fire in synchrony, their intracellular currents generate a measurable magnetic field perpendicular to the current flow. Because magnetic fields pass through the skull and scalp with little distortion, MEG localises sources more precisely than EEG while retaining millisecond temporal resolution. The first MEG recordings were made by physicist David Cohen in 1968 and 1972.

Applications

Clinically, MEG is used to localise epileptic foci before surgery and to map eloquent cortex (motor, sensory, and language areas) so surgeons can avoid them. In research it is used to study perception, language, memory, and disorders such as autism and schizophrenia. Newer optically pumped magnetometer systems are wearable and do not require cryogenic cooling.
