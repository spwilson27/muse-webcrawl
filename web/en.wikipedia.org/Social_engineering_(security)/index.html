Social engineering (security)

In information security, social engineering is the psychological manipulation of people into performing actions or divulging confidential information, rather than breaking in by purely technical means. The term was popularized in the security field by former computer criminal and security consultant Kevin Mitnick, who described it as exploiting trust, helpfulness, and authority — "hacking people" rather than machines.

Techniques

Common techniques include phishing (fraudulent messages soliciting credentials or payment, with targeted variants called spear phishing and whaling), pretexting (inventing a scenario to extract information), baiting (offering something enticing, such as infected USB drives), quid pro quo offers, tailgating into secured areas, and vishing (voice phishing by telephone). Attackers frequently gather background from social media and public records — open-source intelligence — to make pretexts credible, and exploit cognitive principles such as authority, urgency, scarcity, reciprocity, and liking.

Notable cases

Social engineering has featured in many major breaches, from Mitnick's own intrusions in the 1980s–90s to business email compromise frauds that cost organizations billions of dollars, the 2020 Twitter compromise achieved by phone spear phishing of employees, and casino breaches in 2023 attributed to help-desk social engineering. Industry breach investigations consistently find the "human element" involved in a majority of incidents.

Countermeasures

Defenses emphasize security-awareness training and phishing simulations, verification procedures (such as callbacks and code words for help-desk resets), least-privilege access, multi-factor authentication resistant to phishing, and an organizational culture in which employees can challenge unusual requests without penalty. Penetration testers routinely include social engineering in assessments, subject to agreed rules of engagement.
