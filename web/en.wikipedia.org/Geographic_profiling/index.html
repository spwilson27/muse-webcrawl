Geographic profiling

Geographic profiling is an investigative methodology used by law enforcement to infer the most likely area of an offender's residence or base of operations from the locations of a connected series of crimes. It is based on the principles of environmental criminology and on the observation that offenders, like other people, tend to operate within an "awareness space" shaped by their routine movements, while avoiding offending immediately adjacent to their home.

Principles

The technique rests on the distance-decay function — crime locations cluster at distances that decrease in frequency as one moves away from the offender's base — combined with a buffer zone immediately around the home, within which the offender is less likely to offend for fear of recognition. Rossmo's formula combines these effects to compute, for every point on a map, a probability score, producing a three-dimensional "jeopardy surface" that investigators use to prioritise search areas, suspect lists, and patrol deployments.

Development and use

Geographic profiling was formalised in the doctoral research of D. Kim Rossmo at Simon Fraser University and operationalised while he served with the Vancouver Police Department. The method has since been applied to serial murder, rape, arson, and robbery cases internationally, and the underlying spatial models have been adapted to fields such as epidemiology, invasive species biology, and the foraging behaviour of animals.

Criticism

Effectiveness depends on the quality and completeness of the crime series and on offenders conforming to the model's assumptions; "commuter" offenders who travel into an area to offend fit the marauder model poorly. Reviews generally regard geographic profiling as a useful decision-support tool rather than a precise locator.
