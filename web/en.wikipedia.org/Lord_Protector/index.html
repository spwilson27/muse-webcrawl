Most famous holdersOliver Cromwell (1653–1658), Richard Cromwell (1658–1659)

Lord Protector (plural Lords Protector) was a title used in British constitutional law for the head of state in two distinct periods. In late medieval and Tudor England it denoted a regent governing on behalf of a monarch who was a minor or incapacitated — for example Humphrey, Duke of Gloucester for Henry VI; Richard, Duke of Gloucester before he became Richard III; and Edward Seymour, Duke of Somerset for Edward VI.

The Commonwealth

The title is best known from the Interregnum. Under the Instrument of Government of 1653, Oliver Cromwell became Lord Protector of the Commonwealth of England, Scotland and Ireland, head of state of the republic after the civil wars and the execution of Charles I. He governed with a Council of State and successive parliaments, and in 1657 was offered — and refused — the Crown under the Humble Petition and Advice, which made the Protectorate effectively hereditary.

Succession and end

On Oliver Cromwell's death in September 1658 his son Richard Cromwell succeeded him, but Richard lacked his father's authority with the army and resigned in May 1659. The Protectorate collapsed, leading within a year to the Restoration of Charles II in 1660.
