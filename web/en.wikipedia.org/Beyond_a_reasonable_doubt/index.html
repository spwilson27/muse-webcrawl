Beyond a reasonable doubt

Beyond a reasonable doubt is a legal standard of proof required to validate a criminal conviction in most adversarial legal systems. It is the highest standard of proof used in court, reflecting the principle that it is worse to convict an innocent person than to let a guilty person go free. Prosecutors bear the burden of proving each element of the offence to this standard; if the jury or judge harbours a reasonable doubt about the defendant's guilt, they must acquit.

Definition

There is no universally fixed wording. Common formulations instruct jurors that proof beyond a reasonable doubt is proof "of such a convincing character that you would be willing to rely and act upon it without hesitation in the most important of your own affairs", or that a reasonable doubt is one for which a reason can be given, based on reason and common sense rather than speculation. In the United States the standard is constitutionally required by the Due Process Clause, as affirmed in In re Winship (1970).

Comparison with other standards

The criminal standard is higher than the standards used in civil cases: the "preponderance of the evidence" (more likely than not) and the intermediate "clear and convincing evidence". It is often described informally as requiring roughly 90–95% certainty, though courts generally resist quantifying it.

History

The standard developed in English common law in the late 18th century, partly from theological anxieties about jurors damning their souls by wrongfully convicting. It remains the standard across common-law jurisdictions, including England and Wales, Canada, Australia, and the United States.
