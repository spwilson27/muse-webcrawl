Copyright infringement

Copyright infringement (at times referred to as piracy) is the use of works protected by copyright law without permission for a usage where such permission is required, thereby infringing certain exclusive rights granted to the copyright holder, such as the right to reproduce, distribute, display or perform the protected work, or to make derivative works.

Legal framework

The copyright holder is typically the work's creator, or a publisher or other business to whom copyright has been assigned. Copyright holders routinely invoke legal and technological measures to prevent and penalize copyright infringement. Copyright infringement disputes are usually resolved through direct negotiation, a notice and take down process, or litigation in civil court. Egregious or large-scale commercial infringement, especially when it involves counterfeiting, is sometimes prosecuted via the criminal justice system.

Defences and limitations

Most jurisdictions limit copyright through doctrines such as fair use (United States) or fair dealing, which permit limited use of copyrighted material for purposes such as criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, or research. Other exceptions include first-sale doctrine, library and archival copying, and de minimis use.

Online infringement

The rise of the Internet and file-sharing technologies, from Napster through peer-to-peer networks and streaming sites, greatly increased the scale of infringement and spurred laws such as the U.S. Digital Millennium Copyright Act (1998) and notice-and-takedown systems under which platforms remove infringing material on complaint. Estimates of economic harm from infringement are widely debated.

Terminology

The term "piracy" for unauthorized copying, borrowed from maritime robbery, has been used since the 17th century for unauthorized printing, though its use is contested by those who regard it as pejorative.
