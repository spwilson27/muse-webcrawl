Carbon-14

Symbol14C
Half-life5,730 ± 40 years
Decay modeBeta decay to nitrogen-14
Natural abundance~1 part per trillion of carbon

Carbon-14 (14C) is a radioactive isotope of carbon containing six protons and eight neutrons. It is produced continuously in the upper atmosphere when cosmic-ray neutrons strike nitrogen-14 atoms, and it decays back to nitrogen-14 by beta emission with a half-life of about 5,730 years. Its predictable decay in once-living matter is the basis of radiocarbon dating.

Production and decay of carbon-14 in the carbon cycle.

Production and distribution

Cosmic rays generate thermal neutrons in the upper atmosphere; the reaction 14N + n → 14C + p creates carbon-14, which oxidizes to carbon dioxide and mixes through the atmosphere, oceans, and biosphere. Living organisms maintain the atmospheric ratio of 14C to stable carbon while alive; at death, uptake stops and the isotope decays, allowing ages to be estimated for organic material up to roughly 50,000–60,000 years old.

Radiocarbon dating

Willard Libby developed radiocarbon dating in the late 1940s, earning the 1960 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Because atmospheric 14C levels have varied (with solar activity, geomagnetic changes, fossil-fuel dilution — the Suess effect — and the mid-twentieth-century bomb pulse from nuclear testing), raw ages are converted to calendar ages using calibration curves such as IntCal, built from tree rings and other archives. Modern measurements are made chiefly by accelerator mass spectrometry.

Other uses

Carbon-14 compounds serve as tracers in biochemistry and drug metabolism studies, and the bomb pulse has been used to date modern biological tissues and forensic samples.
