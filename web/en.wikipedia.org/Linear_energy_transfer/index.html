Linear energy transfer
Dense (high-LET) and sparse (low-LET) ionisation tracks
SymbolLET, L
SI unitjoule per metre (J/m); commonly keV/µm
Related quantityStopping power

Linear energy transfer (LET) is a measure of the energy an ionising particle transfers to the material it traverses per unit distance. It describes the action of radiation on matter and is closely related to the stopping power of the material. LET is usually expressed in keV per micrometre (keV/µm) of path length in the material, frequently water or tissue.

Definition

LET is defined as the quotient of the mean energy locally imparted by a charged particle to a medium, divided by the length of its track. Strictly, the ICRU distinguishes unrestricted LET (the total stopping power) from restricted LET, which counts only energy deposited within a specified local region, excluding energetic secondary electrons (delta rays) that carry energy away from the track.

High- and low-LET radiation

Sparsely ionising radiations — X-rays, gamma rays and energetic electrons — have low LET, of the order of 0.2–3 keV/µm in water. Densely ionising particles — alpha particles, protons near the end of their range (the Bragg peak), heavy ions and fission fragments — have high LET, up to several hundred keV/µm.

Biological significance

For a given absorbed dose, high-LET radiation generally causes more severe and less repairable biological damage, because the dense ionisation produces clustered lesions in DNA. This is quantified by the relative biological effectiveness (RBE) and by the radiation weighting factors used in radiological protection (for example, 20 for alpha particles versus 1 for photons), and it is exploited in heavy-ion and proton radiotherapy.
