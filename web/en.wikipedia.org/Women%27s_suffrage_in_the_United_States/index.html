Women's suffrage in the United States

Women's suffrage in the United States was achieved gradually, first at state and local levels during the late 19th and early 20th centuries, and nationally in 1920 with the Nineteenth Amendment to the US Constitution, which prohibits the denial of the right to vote on the basis of sex.

Early movement

The movement is conventionally dated to the Seneca Falls Convention of 1848, organised by Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott, whose Declaration of Sentiments demanded the vote. Leaders such as Susan B. Anthony, who founded the National Woman Suffrage Association in 1869, campaigned for a federal amendment.

State victories and the amendment

Wyoming Territory granted women the vote in 1869 and retained it at statehood in 1890; Colorado, Idaho, and Utah followed in the 1890s, and by 1918 fifteen states had full suffrage. The "Anthony Amendment" was introduced in Congress in 1878 and finally passed in 1919; Tennessee became the 36th and deciding state to ratify on 18 August 1920.

After 1920

Many Black, Native American, Asian American, and Latina women remained disenfranchised by discriminatory state laws and practices until the Voting Rights Act of 1965 and other reforms secured their access to the ballot.
