Ergative–absolutive language

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An ergative–absolutive language, or simply ergative language, is a language in which the single argument of an intransitive verb (S) behaves grammatically like the object (patient, P) of a transitive verb, and differently from the agent (A) of a transitive verb. This contrasts with nominative–accusative languages such as English, in which S is grouped with A.

The alignment pattern

RoleErgative–absolutive caseNominative–accusative case
S (intransitive subject)AbsolutiveNominative
A (transitive agent)ErgativeNominative
P (transitive patient)AbsolutiveAccusative

In a typical ergative language such as Basque, the sentence "the man arrived" marks man in the absolutive case, and in "the man saw the woman", woman is absolutive while man takes a special ergative suffix (Basque -k).

Distribution and examples

Ergative alignment is found in languages of many families and regions: Basque in Europe; many languages of the Caucasus such as Georgian; Kurdish dialects; Australian Aboriginal languages such as Dyirbal; Inuit languages; Mayan languages; and Tibeto-Burman languages such as Tibetan. Hindi-Urdu shows "split ergativity": ergative marking appears only in the perfective aspect.

Morphological vs. syntactic ergativity

Most ergative languages are ergative only in their case marking and verb agreement (morphological ergativity), while their syntax — coordination, relativization, and so on — remains accusative-like. Dyirbal is the classic example of a language often described as syntactically ergative as well: in coordination, the shared argument must be S or P, not A.
