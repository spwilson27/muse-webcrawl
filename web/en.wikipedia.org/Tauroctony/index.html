Tauroctony

Tauroctony is a modern name given to the central cult image of the Mithraic mysteries in the Roman Empire. The image depicts the god Mithras killing a bull, an event that was the central icon of the religion. The word derives from the Greek tauroktonos, "bull-slaying". Nearly every Mithraeum (Mithraic temple) contained such an image, typically as a relief at the head of the sanctuary, though freestanding sculptures and paintings are also known.

Description

In the standard composition, Mithras wears a Phrygian cap and eastern dress, kneels on the back of a bull, pulls its head back by the nostrils, and plunges a dagger into its neck while looking away over his shoulder. A dog and a snake reach toward the wound, a scorpion grasps the bull's genitals, and a raven is often present. Ears of wheat sometimes grow from the bull's tail or blood. The twin torchbearers Cautes and Cautopates stand on either side, and the sun and moon frequently frame the scene.

Interpretation

The meaning of the tauroctony is debated, since Mithraism left no surviving theological texts. Franz Cumont, the discipline's founder, read it through Persian mythology as a cosmogonic act of creation. Later scholars, notably David Ulansey, have argued that the scene is an astronomical star map: the figures correspond to constellations around the constellation Taurus, and the image encodes the precession of the equinoxes, with Mithras representing a power beyond the celestial sphere, sometimes identified with the hero Perseus.
