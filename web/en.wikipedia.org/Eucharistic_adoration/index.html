Eucharistic adoration

Eucharistic adoration is a devotional practice in the Roman Catholic Church, and in some Anglican and Lutheran traditions, in which the consecrated host — the Blessed Sacrament — is exposed for veneration and prayer, typically in a monstrance placed on an altar. Adherents believe in the real presence of Christ in the Eucharist and spend time in silent prayer before it.

History

The practice developed in the medieval Church alongside the elevation of the host at Mass and the feast of Corpus Christi, instituted in 1264. Processions and exposition of the Sacrament became widespread in the late Middle Ages, and after the Council of Trent the devotion was strongly promoted as an affirmation of transubstantiation. In the modern period, perpetual adoration chapels — where volunteers maintain continuous prayer before the exposed Sacrament around the clock — have become a feature of many parishes and religious communities.

Practices

Related devotions include Benediction of the Blessed Sacrament, the Holy Hour (an hour of prayer recalling Christ's agony in Gethsemane), Forty Hours' Devotion, and Eucharistic processions. Popes of the modern era, including John Paul II, encouraged the practice as a school of contemplative prayer.
