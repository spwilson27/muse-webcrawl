Standard electrode potential

In electrochemistry, standard electrode potential, or E°, is the measure of an individual electrode's standard potential. It quantifies the relative ease with which a species can be oxidized or reduced under standard conditions. The basis for an electrochemical cell, such as the galvanic cell, is always a redox reaction which can be broken down into two half-reactions: oxidation at the anode (loss of electrons) and reduction at the cathode (gain of electrons).

Definition and measurement

By convention, standard electrode potentials are measured relative to the standard hydrogen electrode (SHE), which is assigned a potential of 0.00 V under standard conditions: unit activity of solutes, 1 bar pressure of gases, and usually 25 °C. The cell potential of a reaction is the difference of the potentials of the cathode and anode: E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode. A positive standard electrode potential indicates that the reduced form is favored relative to hydrogen; a negative value indicates the species is a stronger reducing agent than hydrogen gas.

Use in predicting reactions

Tables of standard electrode potentials (for example, fluorine at +2.87 V and lithium at −3.04 V) allow the calculation of the standard potential of any redox reaction and, through the relation ΔG° = −nFE°, the standard Gibbs energy change. Combined with the Nernst equation, potentials under non-standard conditions can also be predicted.
