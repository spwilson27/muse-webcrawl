Land tenure

Land tenure is the legal regime in which land "owned" by an individual or group is held by that party — that is, the set of rules and rights governing who may use land, for how long, and under what conditions. Tenure determines access to land for homes, farming, and business, and is a central institution in law, economics, and development.

Forms of tenure

Common forms include: freehold (ownership in perpetuity, as fee simple), leasehold (use for a fixed term in return for rent), customary tenure (rights allocated by community rules and chiefs, dominant in much of rural Africa and the Pacific), communal or collective tenure, state ownership with use rights (as in China and Vietnam), and tenancy/sharecropping, where cultivators farm another's land for a share of the crop. English feudal tenure, under which all land was ultimately held of the Crown in return for services, was the ancestor of common-law property systems.

Tenure security

Economists such as Hernando de Soto have argued that secure, documented tenure lets owners invest in land and use it as collateral for credit, while insecure tenure discourages improvement and leaves occupants — disproportionately women and the poor — vulnerable to eviction. Formal titling programmes, however, can also override customary rights and provoke disputes if poorly designed. The concept of a "bundle of rights" describes tenure as separable rights to access, withdraw resources, manage, exclude others, and alienate (sell) land.

Land reform

Redistributive land reforms — in Japan, Taiwan, and South Korea after 1945, and attempted in many other countries — sought to break up large estates and grant tenure to tillers. Collective tenure systems in ejidos (Mexico), kibbutzim (Israel), and Soviet collective farms represent alternative social organisations of landholding. Indigenous land rights movements seek recognition of customary tenure predating colonial states.
