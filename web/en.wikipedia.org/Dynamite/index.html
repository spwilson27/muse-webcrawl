Dynamite

Dynamite is an explosive made of nitroglycerin, sorbents (such as powdered shells or clay) and stabilizers. It was invented by the Swedish chemist and engineer Alfred Nobel in Geesthacht, Northern Germany, and patented in 1867. It rapidly gained wide-scale use as a more powerful alternative to black powder.

History

Nobel discovered that mixing nitroglycerin with a fine sand called kieselguhr (diatomaceous earth) turned the liquid into a paste that could be shaped into rods. The absorbent rendered the explosive much safer to handle and transport than nitroglycerin alone, which was notoriously shock-sensitive. Nobel named his invention dynamite, from the Greek dynamis (power), and built a network of factories across Europe and North America. The fortune he accumulated from dynamite and other inventions funded the Nobel Prizes.

Composition and use

Traditional dynamite consists of about 25% nitroglycerin absorbed in diatomaceous earth. Later formulations replaced part or all of the nitroglycerin with ammonium nitrate. Dynamite is usually sold as sticks about 20 cm long and 3.2 cm in diameter, wrapped in paper, and is detonated with a blasting cap. It was widely used in mining, quarrying, construction and demolition, and in warfare, though it has largely been replaced by cheaper and safer explosives such as ANFO and emulsion explosives.

Safety

Aged dynamite can "sweat" beads of pure nitroglycerin, making it extremely dangerous to handle. Unlike nitroglycerin, dynamite itself is only moderately sensitive to shock and friction, but it must still be stored cool and dry and used within its shelf life.
