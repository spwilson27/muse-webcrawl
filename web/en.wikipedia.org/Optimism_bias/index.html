Optimism bias

Optimism bias (also called unrealistic optimism) is a cognitive bias that causes someone to believe that they themselves are less likely to experience a negative event than others, and more likely to experience a positive event. It is one of the most robust findings in the psychology of judgement, documented across ages, cultures and domains, and is related to the planning fallacy and illusions of control and invulnerability.

Evidence and measurement

Studies typically ask people to estimate their own chances, and the chances of an average peer, of events such as divorce, illness, accidents or career success; most respondents rate their own prospects more favourably. In project planning the bias appears as systematic underestimation of time, cost and risk — the planning fallacy. Neil Weinstein's work in the 1980s established the phenomenon, and Tali Sharot's experiments and neuroimaging research have explored how the brain updates beliefs asymmetrically, learning more readily from good news than from bad.

Causes and consequences

Proposed explanations include egocentrism in comparisons, selective recall and attention, perceived controllability, and a motivational drive to protect self-esteem. Moderate optimism can support health, persistence and well-being, but unrealistic optimism may discourage precautionary behaviour — for example in health screening, safe driving and financial planning — and inflate public projects' forecasts. Debiasing methods such as reference-class forecasting and premortems are used in policy and project appraisal to counteract it.
