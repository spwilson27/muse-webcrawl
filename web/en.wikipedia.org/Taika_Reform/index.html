Taika Reform

The Taika Reform (Japanese: 大化の改新, Taika no Kaishin) was a set of doctrines established by Emperor Kōtoku in the year 645. They were written shortly after the death of Prince Shōtoku and the defeat of the Soga clan, one of Japan's ruling clans, and were intended to unify Japan under a centralized imperial government modelled on that of Tang-dynasty China. The reforms take their name from the Taika era (645–650), the first Japanese era name (nengō).

Background

In 645 the courtier Nakatomi no Kamatari and Prince Naka no Ōe (later Emperor Tenji) assassinated Soga no Iruka in the Isshi Incident, destroying the dominance of the Soga clan over the imperial court. The new Emperor Kōtoku was installed, and the reform edicts followed in early 646.

The reform edicts

The four articles of the Reform Edict of 646 abolished private ownership of land and people, declaring all land the property of the emperor and its cultivators direct subjects liable for taxation; established a new administrative system of provinces, districts and villages with appointed governors; ordered household registers, a census and a system of land allotment (handen-sei); and replaced the miscellaneous levies of the past with a uniform tax system of grain, cloth and labour service.

Significance

Although implementation was slow and uneven, the Taika Reform laid the foundations of the ritsuryō state, the system of codes and bureaucracy that structured early Japanese government. The reforms marked a decisive turn toward Chinese-style centralized rule and the principle that sovereignty resided in the emperor rather than in the great hereditary clans (uji).
