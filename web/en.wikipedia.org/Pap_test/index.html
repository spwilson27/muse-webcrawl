The Papanicolaou test (abbreviated as Pap test, also known as Pap smear or cervical smear) is a method of cervical screening used to detect potentially precancerous and cancerous processes in the cervix (opening of the uterus). Abnormal findings are often followed up by more sensitive diagnostic procedures and, if warranted, interventions aimed at preventing progression to cervical cancer.

History

The test was independently invented in the 1920s by Greek physician Georgios Papanikolaou (and, separately, Aurel Babeș). Papanikolaou's 1943 monograph Diagnosis of Uterine Cancer by the Vaginal Smear, written with Herbert Traut, established the technique, and organised screening programmes from the 1950s onward were followed by dramatic falls — of 70% or more — in cervical cancer incidence and mortality in countries that adopted them.

Procedure

During a speculum examination, a clinician collects cells from the outer opening of the cervix using a small brush or spatula. In the conventional method the cells are smeared directly onto a glass slide and fixed; in liquid-based cytology they are rinsed into a vial of preservative and processed in a laboratory. A cytotechnologist or pathologist then examines the cells microscopically for abnormalities, reported since 1988 under the Bethesda System categories (including ASC-US, LSIL and HSIL). Many screening programmes now combine cytology with testing for high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV), the cause of nearly all cervical cancers, or use HPV testing as the primary screen.

Effectiveness and guidelines

Regular Pap screening detects precancerous lesions (cervical intraepithelial neoplasia) years before invasive cancer develops, allowing treatment by excision or ablation. Guidelines in many countries recommend screening roughly every three years for cytology alone, or every five years with HPV co-testing, for people with a cervix between about ages 21 and 65, though exact recommendations vary. The test is not used to screen for other gynaecological cancers and can yield false positives and false negatives, which is why abnormal results lead to colposcopy and biopsy for confirmation.
