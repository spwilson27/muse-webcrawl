Aspergillus niger

KingdomFungi
OrderEurotiales
FamilyTrichocomaceae
Common nameBlack mould

Aspergillus niger is a filamentous fungus and one of the most common species of the genus Aspergillus. Its dark, nearly black conidia give it the name "black mould", and it is a frequent contaminant of foods such as fruits and vegetables.

Industrial importance

Selected strains of A. niger are workhorses of industrial biotechnology. The fungus is the principal source of commercial citric acid, produced by fermentation on a massive scale since the early 20th century, and is also used to manufacture gluconic acid and a range of enzymes including amylases, pectinases, and glucose oxidase. Many of these uses hold GRAS (generally recognized as safe) status in the United States.

Ecology and health

The species is ubiquitous in soil and indoor environments. It causes post-harvest spoilage of stored produce and a condition called black mould rot in onions and peanuts. In humans it most often causes otomycosis (fungal ear infection) and can contribute to aspergillosis in immunocompromised people, though it is less pathogenic than Aspergillus fumigatus. Some strains produce the mycotoxin ochratoxin A.
