Diagonal matrix

In linear algebra, a diagonal matrix is a matrix in which the entries outside the main diagonal are all zero; the term usually also requires that the matrix be square. The diagonal entries themselves may be zero or non-zero. Every square diagonal matrix is symmetric, and the identity matrix and zero matrix are examples of diagonal matrices.

Properties

Diagonal matrices are closed under addition, multiplication and transposition: the product of two diagonal matrices is diagonal, with entries given by the pairwise products of the diagonal entries. Consequently, powers of a diagonal matrix are computed by raising each diagonal entry to the power, and the inverse (when all diagonal entries are non-zero) is obtained by taking reciprocals. The determinant of a diagonal matrix is the product of its diagonal entries, and its eigenvalues are exactly those entries.

Diagonalisation

A square matrix is called diagonalisable if it is similar to a diagonal matrix, i.e. if there exists an invertible matrix P such that P−1AP is diagonal. Diagonalisable matrices are much easier to compute with, and the spectral theorem guarantees that every real symmetric matrix is diagonalisable by an orthogonal transformation.

Related forms

Generalizations include block diagonal matrices, band matrices, and triangular matrices. The operation of multiplying a matrix by a diagonal matrix from the left scales its rows, and from the right scales its columns.
