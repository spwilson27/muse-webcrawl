Xist (X-inactive specific transcript) is a long non-coding RNA gene on the X chromosome of placental mammals that acts as the master regulator of X-chromosome inactivation, the process by which one of the two X chromosomes in female cells is silenced to achieve dosage compensation with males.

Mechanism

Xist RNA is expressed only from the future inactive X chromosome. The RNA remains in the nucleus and progressively coats the chromosome in cis, recruiting chromatin-modifying complexes — including Polycomb repressive complexes PRC1 and PRC2 — through interacting proteins such as SPEN and hnRNPK. This leads to repressive histone marks (such as H3K27me3), DNA methylation and compaction of the chromosome into the Barr body.

Regulation

Xist is negatively regulated by its antisense transcript, Tsix, which is expressed from the active X and represses Xist there. Early in development, transient expression patterns and "counting" mechanisms within the X-inactivation centre ensure that exactly one X remains active per diploid genome. Transcription factors including OCT4, SOX2 and NANOG repress Xist in undifferentiated embryonic stem cells, linking inactivation to pluripotency.

Discovery

The gene was identified in 1991 by Carolyn Brown, Huntington Willard and colleagues, who showed that it was expressed exclusively from the inactive X. Targeted deletion and transgene experiments in mice in the mid-1990s demonstrated that Xist is both necessary for inactivation in cis and sufficient to induce silencing when inserted into an autosome.

Clinical relevance

Mutations in Xist or its regulatory region are rare but can cause skewed X-inactivation. Xist expression and X-inactivation status are studied in female cancers and in cellular reprogramming, where reactivation of the inactive X is a marker of successful resetting to a naive pluripotent state.
