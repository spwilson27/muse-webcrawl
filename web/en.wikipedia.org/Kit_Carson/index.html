Kit Carson

Christopher Houston Carson (December 24, 1809 – May 23, 1868), better known as Kit Carson, was an American frontiersman. He was a fur trapper, wilderness guide, Indian agent, and U.S. Army officer. He became a frontier legend in his own lifetime through biographies and dime novels, and his exploits were exaggerated in popular accounts.

Early life

Carson was born in Kentucky and raised on the Missouri frontier near Boonville. Apprenticed to a saddler, he ran away at sixteen to join a wagon train on the Santa Fe Trail, beginning a life in the West that spanned the fur trade era.

Mountain man and guide

Carson trapped beaver throughout the Rocky Mountains and the Southwest, learned several Native American languages, and married into Cheyenne and Arapaho families. His reputation as a scout led John C. Frémont to hire him as guide for his expeditions of 1842, 1843–44, and 1845. Frémont's widely read reports made Carson nationally famous.

Mexican–American War and Civil War

During the Mexican–American War, Carson carried dispatches across the continent and served in California, including the controversial episode at San Pasqual. In the Civil War he commanded the 1st New Mexico Volunteer Infantry for the Union, fighting at Valverde, and later led campaigns against Navajo, Mescalero Apache, Kiowa, and Comanche forces. His 1863–64 campaign against the Navajo, involving scorched-earth tactics and the forced "Long Walk" removal to Bosque Redondo, remains a deeply contested part of his legacy.

Later life and death

Carson served as Indian agent for the Utes and other tribes at Taos, and was brevetted brigadier general in 1865. He died at Fort Lyon, Colorado, in 1868, a month after his wife Josefa's death.

Legacy

Carson is commemorated in numerous place names, including Nevada's capital, Carson City, and counties in several states. Modern assessments are divided: he is remembered both as a skilled scout and soldier and as an agent of violent westward expansion against Native peoples.
