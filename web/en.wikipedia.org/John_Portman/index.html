John Portman

John Calvin Portman Jr. (December 4, 1924 – December 29, 2017) was an American architect and developer known for popularizing the atrium hotel. His designs — sweeping interior atriums with glass elevators — transformed hotel architecture in the 1960s and 1970s and reshaped the downtown districts of Atlanta, Los Angeles, Detroit, and other cities.

Career

Portman graduated from the Georgia Institute of Technology in 1950. Working as both architect and developer, he built the Peachtree Center complex in downtown Atlanta, including the Hyatt Regency Atlanta (1967), whose 22-story atrium was a sensation. His signature projects include the Westin Bonaventure Hotel in Los Angeles (1976), the Renaissance Center in Detroit (1977), the Marriott Marquis hotels in Atlanta and New York, and the Embarcadero Center in San Francisco.

Style and criticism

Portman's buildings feature dramatic interior spaces — atriums, hanging gardens, water features, and panoramic elevators — but were criticized for turning inward, with blank or fortress-like exteriors that disengaged from the street. Critics such as Fredric Jameson used the Bonaventure Hotel as an emblem of postmodern hyperspace. Nevertheless, the atrium hotel became a worldwide standard, and Portman's "coordinated unit" developments influenced urban renewal across the United States and Asia.
