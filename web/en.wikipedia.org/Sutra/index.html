Sutra

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sutra (Sanskrit: सूत्र, romanised: sūtra, lit. 'string, thread') in Indian literary traditions refers to an aphorism or a collection of aphorisms in the form of a manual or, more broadly, a condensed manual or text. Sutras are a genre of ancient and medieval Indian texts found in Hinduism, Buddhism and Jainism.

In Hinduism, sutras are a distinct type of literature: short, terse rules deliberately set forth with the utmost concision, concerning ritual, philosophy, grammar, law, and other fields of knowledge. The earliest sutras were composed in the later Vedic period to preserve technical knowledge accurately in oral transmission. In Buddhism, sutras (Pali: sutta) are canonical scriptures, many of which are regarded as records of the oral teachings of the Buddha. In Jainism, sutras are canonical texts authored by the ganadharas and the tradition's elders.

Etymology and style

The word sūtra derives from the verbal root siv, "to sew", and is related to sūci (needle) and sūnā (woven band) — hence "that which sews and holds things together". The sutra style prizes brevity: classical commentators define a sutra as having few syllables, being unambiguous, containing the essence of a topic, addressing all sides, and being free of fault. Because of their compression, sutras were traditionally studied with written commentaries (bhāṣya), which unpack the rules with examples and discussion.

Hindu sutras

Sutra literature is attached to each of the six auxiliary disciplines of the Veda (the Vedangas), including phonetics, ritual (Kalpa Sutras, comprising Shrauta, Grihya, and Dharma Sutras), grammar (Pāṇini's Aṣṭādhyāyī, itself composed in sutras), and etymology. The darśana (philosophical) schools each condensed their doctrine into sutras — the Brahma Sutras of Vedanta, Patañjali's Yoga Sutras, the Nyaya Sutras, the Samkhya Sutras, and others — which later commentators such as Shankara and Ramanuja interpreted in divergent directions.

Buddhist sutras

In Buddhism, the term (Pali sutta, Sanskrit sūtra) designates discourses attributed to the Buddha or his close disciples. Theravada Buddhism preserves the Sutta Piṭaka of the Pali Canon, including the long, middle-length, connected, and numerical discourses. Mahayana Buddhism produced hundreds of additional sutras in Sanskrit and translation, including the Prajñāpāramitā (Perfection of Wisdom) sutras, the Lotus Sutra, the Pure Land sutras, and the Heart Sutra and Diamond Sutra, which remain among the most widely recited texts in East Asian Buddhism.

Jain sutras

Jain canonical literature (the Agamas) includes sutras such as the Ācārāṅga Sūtra and the Kalpa Sūtra, the latter famous for its biographies of the Tirthankaras and for richly illustrated manuscripts. The Tattvārtha Sūtra, accepted by both Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions, summarises Jain doctrine in sutra form.
