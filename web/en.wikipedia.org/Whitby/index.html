Whitby

Whitby is a seaside town, port and civil parish in North Yorkshire, England, on the east coast at the mouth of the River Esk. It has a long maritime and mineral heritage and is a popular tourist destination. The ruined Gothic Whitby Abbey on the East Cliff, made famous by Bram Stoker's novel Dracula, overlooks the town.

History

The earliest settlement dates to the Bronze Age. In AD 657, St Hilda founded a monastery at Streoneshalh, the site later known as Whitby Abbey. In 664 the abbey hosted the Synod of Whitby, at which the Northumbrian church decided to adopt the Roman method of calculating Easter and the Roman tonsure. The town developed as a fishing port and, in the 18th and 19th centuries, became a centre for whaling, shipbuilding, alum production and the mining of jet.

The explorer Captain James Cook learned his trade in Whitby, serving his apprenticeship in the town; his ship HMS Endeavour was built there as the collier Earl of Pembroke. A museum in the town commemorates Cook's connection with Whitby.

Dracula

Bram Stoker holidayed in Whitby in 1890 and set several chapters of his 1897 novel Dracula there: the vampire's ship, the Demeter, runs aground at Whitby, and Dracula comes ashore in the form of a black dog. The town's abbey ruins, churchyard and 199 steps up the East Cliff feature in the novel, and Whitby now hosts popular Gothic festivals.

Economy and transport

Fishing, particularly for Whitby scampi, and tourism are the main industries. The town is served by the Esk Valley line railway to Middlesbrough, and the heritage North Yorkshire Moors Railway runs steam trains from Whitby to Pickering.
