Banking Act of 1935

The Banking Act of 1935 is a United States federal law passed during the Great Depression as part of Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal. Signed on August 23, 1935, it permanently established the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC) and reformed the Federal Reserve System, centralizing monetary policy authority in Washington.

Provisions

The Act renamed the Federal Reserve Board as the Board of Governors of the Federal Reserve System, increased its size, and gave it greater control over the regional Federal Reserve Banks. It created the Federal Open Market Committee in its modern form, removed the Secretary of the Treasury and the Comptroller of the Currency from the Board, and extended deposit insurance coverage. Much of its design reflected the influence of Marriner Eccles, the Fed chairman who advocated centralized control of monetary policy.
