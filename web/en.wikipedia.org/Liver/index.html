The liver is a major metabolic organ found in vertebrates. In humans it is located in the right upper quadrant of the abdomen, below the diaphragm, and weighs about 1.5 kg, making it the largest internal organ and the largest gland in the body. The liver performs more than 500 vital functions, including detoxification of the blood, synthesis of proteins and biochemicals necessary for digestion and growth, production of bile, and storage of glycogen, vitamins, and minerals.

Anatomy

The liver is divided anatomically into two large lobes — the right and the left — separated by the falciform ligament, and functionally into eight segments based on the Couinaud classification of its internal blood supply. It has a unique dual blood supply: the hepatic portal vein delivers nutrient-rich but oxygen-poor blood from the gut and spleen (about 75% of flow), and the hepatic artery delivers oxygenated blood. The gallbladder sits beneath the right lobe and stores bile produced by the liver, which drains through the biliary tree into the duodenum.

Functions

Hepatocytes carry out most liver functions: they produce bile (up to a litre a day) for fat emulsification; process absorbed nutrients — converting glucose to glycogen and excess amino acids to urea; synthesise plasma proteins including albumin and most clotting factors; metabolise drugs and toxins through the cytochrome P450 system; and break down old red blood cells, whose haem pigment becomes bilirubin. The liver's resident macrophages, Kupffer cells, clear bacteria and debris from portal blood. Remarkably, the liver can regenerate: after partial hepatectomy it can regrow to its original mass within months.

Diseases

Liver diseases include viral hepatitis (A–E), fatty liver disease associated with alcohol and metabolic syndrome, cirrhosis (end-stage scarring), autoimmune hepatitis, and liver cancer (hepatocellular carcinoma). Jaundice — yellowing of skin and eyes from accumulated bilirubin — is a classic sign. Advanced liver failure may be treated by liver transplantation, first successfully performed by Thomas Starzl in 1967.
