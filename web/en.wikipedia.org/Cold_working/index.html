Cold working

Cold working is a metalworking process in which metal is shaped below its recrystallization temperature, usually at room temperature. Processes such as rolling, drawing, bending, and forging performed cold increase the metal's strength through strain hardening, while producing good surface finish and dimensional accuracy.

Mechanism

Plastic deformation at low temperature multiplies dislocations in the crystal structure. As dislocations become entangled, further deformation becomes harder, so the metal gains strength and hardness but loses ductility. Heavily cold-worked metal may require annealing to restore formability before further shaping.

Processes and advantages

Common cold-working operations include cold rolling of sheet and strip, wire and tube drawing, cold heading of fasteners, extrusion, and press forming. Compared with hot working, cold working needs no heating equipment, gives tighter tolerances and smoother surfaces, and strengthens the part — but requires greater forces and is limited in the amount of deformation per step.

Applications

Cold-worked products include steel sheet for car bodies and appliances, drawn wire and cables, coins, and precision fasteners.
