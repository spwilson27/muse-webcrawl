Queen regent

A queen regent (plural: queens regent) is a queen who rules a kingdom in the name of a monarch who is unable to rule, most commonly because the monarch is a minor, absent, or incapacitated. A queen regent typically holds plenary royal powers for the duration of the regency, in contrast to a queen consort, who is merely the spouse of a reigning king, or a queen dowager, the widow of a king.

Distinctions

A queen regent should not be confused with a queen regnant, who reigns in her own right, such as Elizabeth II of the United Kingdom. Historically, queen mothers were the most common regents, ruling on behalf of underage children. Some regencies were exercised jointly with councils of nobility or ministers.

Notable queens regent

Famous examples include Catherine de' Medici and Anne of Austria in France; Maria Christina of Austria in Spain, who governed during the minority of Alfonso XIII; Blanche of Castile, regent of France for Louis IX; and Hatshepsut of ancient Egypt, who began as regent for Thutmose III before assuming kingship herself. In the modern era, Queens Wilhelmina's predecessors and other European royal widows often served similar roles, although constitutional monarchies now usually provide statutory regency arrangements.

Legal position

The legal basis of a queen's regency could rest on the late king's will, customary law, or the act of an assembly or parliament. The regency ended when the monarch reached majority or recovered capacity.
