The Kellogg–Briand Pact (or Pact of Paris, officially the General Treaty for Renunciation of War as an Instrument of National Policy) is a 1928 international agreement in which signatory states promised not to use war to resolve "disputes or conflicts of whatever nature or of whatever origin they may be". It was signed in Paris on 27 August 1928 by fifteen nations, including France, the United States, the United Kingdom, Germany, Italy and Japan, and ultimately adhered to by more than sixty states.

Origins

The pact grew out of a 1927 proposal by French foreign minister Aristide Briand for a bilateral treaty with the United States renouncing war between the two countries. U.S. Secretary of State Frank B. Kellogg, wary of a one-sided commitment, counter-proposed a multilateral treaty open to all nations, which Briand accepted. Kellogg received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1929 for his work on the agreement.

Terms and limitations

The treaty contains only two substantive articles: the parties condemn recourse to war for the solution of international controversies and renounce it as an instrument of national policy, and agree that disputes shall be settled only by pacific means. It provided no enforcement mechanism, no sanctions, and did not define aggression or limit the right of self-defence, which several signatories expressly reserved in accompanying notes.

Legacy

The pact failed to prevent the Japanese invasion of Manchuria (1931), the Italian invasion of Ethiopia (1935) or the Second World War. Nevertheless, it was hugely influential in international law: it underpinned the charges of "crimes against peace" at the Nuremberg and Tokyo war-crimes trials, inspired the prohibition of war in Article 2(4) of the United Nations Charter, and remains formally in force. Historians credit it with transforming the legal status of war from an accepted instrument of statecraft into an act presumptively illegal.
