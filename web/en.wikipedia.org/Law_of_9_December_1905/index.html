Law of 9 December 1905

The Law of 9 December 1905 concerning the Separation of the Churches and the State is the foundational text of secularism (laïcité) in France. Championed by rapporteur Aristide Briand and passed under Prime Minister Émile Combes's successor government, it ended the Napoleonic Concordat of 1801 in most of France.

Provisions

Article 1 guarantees freedom of conscience and free exercise of religion within public order; Article 2 declares the Republic neither recognises, nor salaries, nor subsidises any religion. Church buildings built before 1905 became public property made available to worshippers. The law provoked fierce conflict — inventories of church property sparked riots and the Vatican condemned it — but Briand's relatively liberal application eased its acceptance.

Legacy

The law does not apply in Alsace-Moselle (German territory in 1905), where the Concordat survives. It remains the legal bedrock of French secularism, invoked in debates over religious symbols, Islam and public institutions.
