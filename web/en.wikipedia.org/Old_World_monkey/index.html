Old World monkey

FamilyCercopithecidae
OrderPrimates
SubfamiliesCercopithecinae, Colobinae
RangeAfrica and Asia

Old World monkeys are primates of the family Cercopithecidae, native to Africa and Asia. Together with the apes they form the parvorder Catarrhini, the "Old World anthropoids", as distinct from the New World monkeys (Platyrrhini) of Central and South America. The family includes baboons, macaques, guenons, langurs, colobus monkeys and proboscis monkeys — about 160 living species in more than twenty genera.

Characteristics

Old World monkeys have narrow, downward-pointing nostrils set close together, non-prehensile tails (or no visible tail in a few species such as the Barbary macaque), and opposable thumbs. Many possess tough sitting pads (ischial callosities) and cheek pouches for storing food. Their dental formula is 2.1.2.3 in each quadrant, the same as in apes and humans. Sizes range from the tiny talapoin, about 1 kg, to the male mandrill at over 35 kg.

Classification and evolution

The family divides into two subfamilies. The Cercopithecinae — baboons, macaques, guenons and their relatives — are mostly omnivorous with cheek pouches and relatively simple stomachs. The Colobinae — langurs, colobuses and odd-nosed monkeys — are leaf-eaters with complex, multi-chambered stomachs for fermenting foliage, and lack cheek pouches. Fossil evidence places the split between Old World monkeys and apes roughly 25–30 million years ago.

Behaviour and ecology

Most species are social, living in troops with dominance hierarchies, and many are partly terrestrial, unlike the almost wholly arboreal New World monkeys. Several macaque species have adapted closely to human environments across Asia. The gelada of the Ethiopian highlands is a specialised grass-eater, while the proboscis monkey of Borneo is a strong swimmer with partly webbed feet.
