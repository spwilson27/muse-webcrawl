The British Board of Film Classification (BBFC) is a non-governmental organisation founded by the British film industry in 1912 and responsible for the national classification and censorship of films exhibited at cinemas and video works (such as films and television programmes) released on physical media and streaming services within the United Kingdom.

History

Founded as the British Board of Film Censors in the wake of the Cinematograph Act 1909, the Board initially issued two certificates: U (universal) and A (adult). Categories multiplied over the decades — H for horror in the 1930s, X in 1951 — and in 1982 the Board took its present name, signalling a shift from censorship to classification. Under the Video Recordings Act 1984 its decisions became legally binding for video releases, giving it a statutory role alongside its advisory cinema function.

Ratings

The current age ratings are:

  • U — suitable for all audiences aged four and over.
  • PG — parental guidance; some scenes may be unsuitable for young children.
  • 12A — cinema only; children under 12 must be accompanied by an adult.
  • 12 — video/streaming; not to be supplied to anyone below 12.
  • 15 and 18 — restricted to those ages and above.
  • R18 — sexually explicit works, restricted to licensed premises.

Examiners classify content against published guidelines, periodically revised after large public consultations, considering themes such as violence, language, sex, drugs and discrimination. The BBFC can require cuts or refuse classification, effectively banning a work.

Notable decisions

Historically controversial bans and cuts included The Wild One, A Clockwork Orange (withdrawn by Kubrick himself), and the "video nasties" era of the 1980s. In recent decades the Board has become markedly more permissive, passing works at 18 that would once have been cut.
