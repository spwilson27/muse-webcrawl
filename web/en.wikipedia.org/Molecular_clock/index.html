Molecular clock

A molecular clock converts genetic distance into estimates of divergence time

The molecular clock is a technique in molecular evolution that uses the mutation rate of biomolecules to deduce the time in prehistory when two or more life forms diverged. The molecular data used are usually nucleotide sequences for DNA, RNA, or amino acid sequences for proteins. The idea rests on the observation that mutations accumulate in many genes at a roughly constant average rate over long timescales, so the number of differences between two species' sequences is approximately proportional to the time since they shared a common ancestor.

History

Emile Zuckerkandl and Linus Pauling proposed the concept in 1962–65 after noticing that differences in haemoglobin sequences between species scaled with fossil-based divergence estimates. The hypothesis gained support from Motoo Kimura's neutral theory of molecular evolution (1968), which held that most sequence change is selectively neutral and accumulates at a rate governed by mutation rather than selection.

Calibration and use

To convert genetic distance into absolute years, clocks are calibrated against known events — fossil first appearances or dated geological splits. Molecular clocks have been used to date the divergence of humans and chimpanzees (roughly 5–7 million years ago), the radiation of modern bird and mammal orders, and the spread of viruses such as HIV and influenza. Modern "relaxed clock" Bayesian methods allow the rate to vary among branches, accommodating the fact that the clock is only approximately constant — generation time, population size, and selection all cause rate variation among lineages.

Criticism

Detractors have pointed to rate heterogeneity and calibration uncertainty, and molecular estimates have sometimes conflicted with the fossil record, generally pushing divergences earlier. The debate has driven improvements in both palaeontology and modelling.
