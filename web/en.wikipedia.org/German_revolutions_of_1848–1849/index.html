The German revolutions of 1848–1849 were a series of loosely coordinated protests and uprisings across the states of the German Confederation, part of the wave of revolutions that swept Europe in 1848. Inspired by liberal and nationalist ideas, the revolutionaries demanded constitutional government, freedom of the press and assembly, and the unification of Germany into a single nation-state.

Outbreak

News of the February revolution in Paris triggered the "March Days" of 1848. Rulers across Germany conceded the so-called March demands, appointing liberal ministries and promising constitutions. In Berlin, street fighting on March 18–19 forced Frederick William IV of Prussia to pay homage to the fallen revolutionaries, and in Vienna Metternich fled into exile.

The Frankfurt Parliament

In May 1848 the first freely elected all-German assembly, the Frankfurt Parliament, convened in the Paulskirche to draft a constitution for a united Germany. After long debate it adopted the Frankfurt Constitution in March 1849 and offered the imperial crown to the Prussian king — who refused it as a "crown from the gutter." The parliament's lack of an army left it powerless against the recovered monarchies.

Defeat and aftermath

Prussian and federal troops crushed the remaining revolutionary strongholds in the Palatinate and Baden by the summer of 1849. Thousands of defeated revolutionaries — the "Forty-Eighters," including Carl Schurz — emigrated to the United States. Although the revolution failed, it abolished many feudal burdens in the countryside, and the goal of unification it articulated was achieved a generation later, albeit "from above" under Bismarck.
