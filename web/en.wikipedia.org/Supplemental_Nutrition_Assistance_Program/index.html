Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP), known historically as the Food Stamp Program, is a United States federal programme that provides food-purchasing assistance to low- and no-income people. It is administered by the Department of Agriculture's Food and Nutrition Service and distributed through state agencies.

Operation

Eligibility is based mainly on household income and assets, with gross income limits generally at 130% of the federal poverty line. Benefits are delivered monthly on an Electronic Benefit Transfer (EBT) card that works like a debit card at authorised retailers. Benefits may be used for most foods for home consumption, but not alcohol, tobacco, hot prepared foods, or non-food items. Participation fluctuates with the economy, peaking at about 47 million people after the Great Recession and standing at roughly 42 million in the early 2020s; average benefits were on the order of US$180–190 per person per month.

History

A temporary food stamp plan ran from 1939 to 1943; a permanent programme was created by the Food Stamp Act of 1964 and expanded nationwide in the 1970s. Paper coupons were replaced by EBT nationwide by 2004, and the 2008 Farm Bill renamed the programme SNAP. Periodic farm bills set funding and rules, and work requirements — including time limits for "able-bodied adults without dependents" — have been a recurring subject of political debate.

Effects

SNAP is the largest federal nutrition assistance programme and a major automatic stabiliser: research links it to reduced food insecurity and poverty, improved child health outcomes, and local economic stimulus, while debates continue over benefit levels, eligibility, and work rules.
