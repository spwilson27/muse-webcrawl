Interferometry
PrincipleSuperposition of waves
Key instrumentsMichelson, Fabry–Pérot, Mach–Zehnder interferometers
ApplicationsAstronomy, metrology, gravitational-wave detection, fibre optics

Interferometry is a technique which uses the interference of superimposed waves to extract information. Interferometry typically uses electromagnetic waves and is an important investigative technique in astronomy, fibre optics, engineering metrology, optical metrology, oceanography, seismology, spectroscopy, quantum mechanics, nuclear and particle physics, plasma physics, remote sensing, biomolecular interactions, surface profiling, microfluidics, mechanical stress/strain measurement, velocimetry, and optometry.

Principles

Interferometry makes use of the principle of superposition: when two or more waves meet, the resulting pattern depends on their phase difference. By splitting a coherent beam of light and recombining it after the parts have travelled different paths, extremely small differences in path length — fractions of a wavelength — can be measured from the resulting interference fringes.

History

Thomas Young's double-slit experiment (1801) demonstrated the wave nature of light. In the 1880s Albert Michelson developed the interferometer bearing his name; the Michelson–Morley experiment of 1887 famously failed to detect the luminiferous aether, a result that helped pave the way for special relativity.

Applications

In astronomy, aperture synthesis combines signals from separated telescopes — as in very-long-baseline interferometry — to achieve the resolution of a much larger instrument; the Event Horizon Telescope used this method to image a black hole in 2019. Giant laser interferometers such as LIGO and Virgo detected gravitational waves for the first time in 2015. In industry, interferometers measure surface flatness, refractive indices and tiny displacements with nanometre precision.
