Specific phobia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A specific phobia is an anxiety disorder characterized by a marked, persistent, and excessive fear of a specific object or situation — such as animals, heights, blood, flying, or enclosed spaces — that poses little or no actual danger. Exposure to the feared stimulus almost invariably provokes an immediate anxiety response, and the person typically avoids the stimulus or endures it with intense distress. Specific phobias are among the most common anxiety disorders.

Classification

The DSM-5 groups specific phobias into types: animal type (e.g., arachnophobia, fear of spiders), natural environment type (e.g., acrophobia, fear of heights; astraphobia, fear of storms), blood-injection-injury type, situational type (e.g., claustrophobia, fear of flying), and other type. The blood-injection-injury type is distinctive because it often triggers a vasovagal response and fainting rather than the usual fight-or-flight reaction.

Symptoms

Symptoms include rapid heartbeat, shortness of breath, trembling, sweating, nausea, and an overwhelming urge to escape. In children, the fear may be expressed by crying, tantrums, freezing, or clinging. For a diagnosis, the fear must be out of proportion to the actual danger and must interfere with normal functioning or cause clinically significant distress.

Causes

Specific phobias are thought to arise from a combination of genetic vulnerability, temperament, and learning. Many phobias develop after a traumatic experience with the feared object, through observing others' fear, or through information transmission; some may reflect evolved predispositions to fear ancestrally dangerous stimuli such as snakes and spiders.

Treatment

The most effective treatment is exposure therapy, a form of cognitive behavioral therapy in which the person is gradually and repeatedly exposed to the feared stimulus in a controlled way until the fear diminishes. Techniques include systematic desensitization, in vivo exposure, and virtual reality exposure. Medication is generally not the primary treatment, though it may be used for situational symptoms.

Epidemiology

Lifetime prevalence estimates for specific phobia are roughly 7–11% in Western countries, making it one of the most common mental disorders. Onset is often in childhood, and women are affected about twice as often as men for most types.
