Serapis (Greek: Σέραπις, later Σάραπις) was a Greco-Egyptian god whose cult was promoted in the 3rd century BC on the orders of the pharaoh Ptolemy I Soter, the Hellenistic ruler of Egypt. Serapis fused the Egyptian gods Osiris and Apis (the sacred bull of Memphis) with Greek deities including Zeus, Hades, Dionysus and Asclepius, in an effort to unite Ptolemy's Greek and Egyptian subjects under a common religion.

Origins and cult

According to legend retold by Plutarch and Tacitus, Ptolemy dreamed of a colossal statue and had it brought from Sinope on the Black Sea to Alexandria, where priests identified it as Serapis. His great temple, the Serapeum of Alexandria, became one of the wonders of the ancient world and housed a famous library. Depictions show Serapis as a bearded, Greek-looking god wearing a modius (grain measure) on his head, symbolising abundance, often with the three-headed dog Cerberus at his feet.

Spread and decline

The cult of Serapis, closely paired with that of Isis, spread throughout the Roman Empire, reaching Rome, Pompeii, Delos and even Londinium, where a temple of Serapis is attested. Serapis was associated with healing, the afterlife and the bounty of the Nile flood. The cult declined with the Christianisation of the empire; the Serapeum at Alexandria was destroyed by a Christian mob under Patriarch Theophilus in 391 AD, an event often treated as a symbol of the end of pagan antiquity.
