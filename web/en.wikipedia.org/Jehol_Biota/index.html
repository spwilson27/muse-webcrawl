Jehol Biota

AgeEarly Cretaceous, ~133–120 million years ago
LocationLiaoning, Hebei, and Inner Mongolia, China
Key formationsYixian Formation, Jiufotang Formation

The Jehol Biota is the name given to the diverse fossil assemblage of organisms preserved in Early Cretaceous rocks of northeastern China, principally in the Yixian and Jiufotang formations of Liaoning Province and neighbouring regions. The deposits, dated to roughly 133 to 120 million years ago, preserve an extraordinary range of terrestrial and freshwater life, including feathered dinosaurs, early birds, mammals, pterosaurs, fish, amphibians, insects, and flowering plants, often with soft tissues, feathers, and even stomach contents intact.

Geology and preservation

The fossils occur in fine-grained lacustrine sediments interleaved with volcanic ash layers. Periodic volcanic eruptions killed and rapidly buried organisms in lake bottoms, and the fine sediment and low-oxygen conditions allowed exceptional preservation, with details such as feather barbs, skin, and internal organs recorded. The name "Jehol" derives from an older romanisation of Rehe, the historical province (Jehol Province) that once covered much of the region.

Significance

The Jehol Biota transformed understanding of dinosaur evolution and the origin of birds. Discoveries such as Sinosauropteryx, the first non-avian dinosaur found with feather-like structures, Microraptor, a four-winged dromaeosaur, and early birds such as Confuciusornis provided direct evidence of the dinosaur–bird transition and of feather evolution. Mammals such as the gliding Volaticotherium and the badger-sized Repenomamus—one specimen of which preserves a baby dinosaur in its stomach—show that Mesozoic mammals were more ecologically diverse than once thought.

Life of the Jehol ecosystem

The Jehol lakes and forests supported fish such as the peipiaosteid Lycoptera, abundant insects, conchostracans, and the frog-like amphibians and choristoderes of the lake margins. Volcanic landscapes surrounded by conifer forests provided the setting for one of the most completely documented terrestrial ecosystems of the Mesozoic.
