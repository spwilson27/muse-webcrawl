Jabir ibn Hayyan

Bornc. 721, Tus, Khorasan (traditionally)
Diedc. 815, Kufa, Iraq
Known forAlchemy; early chemistry; the Jabirian corpus
Latin nameGeber

Abū Mūsā Jābir ibn Ḥayyān (Arabic: أبو موسى جابر بن حيّان; c. 721 – c. 815), known in Latin as Geber, was a polymath of the early Islamic Golden Age, traditionally regarded as the father of early chemistry. A vast body of Arabic writings on alchemy, chemistry, medicine, philosophy, astronomy and magic — the Jabirian corpus — is attributed to him, though historians debate whether all of it came from one man or from a school of authors writing under his name in the ninth and tenth centuries.

Life

According to tradition, Jabir was born in Tus in Khorasan, the son of an apothecary, and later lived in Kufa, where he is said to have been a protégé of the Shia imam Ja'far al-Sadiq and to have enjoyed the patronage of the Barmakid viziers of the Abbasid caliph Harun al-Rashid. The details of his biography are heavily embellished by legend.

The Jabirian corpus

The writings attributed to Jabir number in the hundreds (later bibliographers credited him with as many as 3,000 treatises). They include the Book of the Balance, the Seventy Books and the Book of Stones. The corpus presents alchemy as a systematic, quasi-quantitative science governed by a "science of the balance" (ʿilm al-mīzān), and sets out the sulphur–mercury theory of metals, according to which all metals are composed of sulphur and mercury in different proportions and purities.

Contributions to chemistry

The Jabirian texts describe many chemical processes and substances with unprecedented clarity: distillation, crystallisation, calcination, sublimation and filtration; the preparation of mineral acids; and the refinement of techniques for making steel, dyes, varnishes and artificial pearls. Latin works attributed to "Geber" — probably by a later, separate Latin author sometimes called pseudo-Geber — transmitted descriptions of strong acids such as nitric and sulphuric acid to medieval Europe and strongly influenced the development of chemistry there.

Legacy

Jabir's insistence on experiment and on classifying substances laid foundations built upon by later figures such as al-Razi. Modern scholarship, following Paul Kraus, treats the corpus as the work of a circle rather than a single author, but its historical importance in the transition from alchemy to chemistry is undisputed.
