Bufadienolide

Bufadienolides are a class of naturally occurring chemical compounds consisting of a steroid structure with a six-membered α-pyrone (doubly unsaturated lactone) ring at the C-17 position. The name derives from the genus Bufo, the true toads, whose skin secretions are a major natural source.

Occurrence

Bufadienolides occur in toad venom — compounds such as bufalin, cinobufagin, marinobufagenin and resibufogenin are components of traditional preparations like the Chinese medicine Chan Su. They also appear in plants, notably the squills (Drimia, formerly Scilla) and Kalanchoe species, and even in fireflies, where lucibufagins serve a defensive role.

Pharmacology

Like the related cardenolides of foxglove, bufadienolides inhibit the sodium-potassium ATPase pump of cell membranes. In cardiac tissue this raises intracellular calcium and increases the force of contraction, which explains their historical medicinal use for heart failure — and their narrow margin between therapy and poisoning. Livestock poisoning by Kalanchoe and related plants (krimpsiekte in South Africa) is a significant veterinary problem.

Research

Certain bufadienolides show cytotoxic activity against cancer cell lines in vitro, and endogenous mammalian bufadienolides such as marinobufagenin have been investigated for roles in salt-sensitive hypertension and pre-eclampsia, though their physiological status remains debated.
