Pāramitā

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pāramitā (Sanskrit: पारमिता; Pali: pāramī) is a Sanskrit term often translated as "perfection" and used in Buddhism to describe the virtues perfected by a bodhisattva on the path to Buddhahood. The word is commonly explained as deriving from parama, "supreme", or from pāra, "the further shore" — crossing from the shore of samsara to the shore of nirvana.

The six perfections

Mahayana Buddhism classically lists six pāramitās:

  1. Dāna — generosity, giving
  2. Śīla — virtue, morality, discipline
  3. Kṣānti — patience, forbearance
  4. Vīrya — energy, diligence, vigour
  5. Dhyāna — meditation, concentration
  6. Prajñā — wisdom, insight into emptiness

Some Mahayana sutras, such as the Daśabhūmika Sūtra, expand the list to ten, adding skilful means (upāya), aspiration or vow (praṇidhāna), power (bala) and knowledge (jñāna), correlated with the ten stages (bhūmi) of the bodhisattva path.

Theravada Buddhism

In Theravada Buddhism the pāramīs are ten qualities cultivated by a bodhisattva (bodhisatta) over many lifetimes, recounted in texts such as the Buddhavaṁsa and the Jātaka tales: generosity, virtue, renunciation, wisdom, energy, patience, truthfulness, determination, loving-kindness and equanimity.

Practice

The pāramitās structure bodhisattva training: the first five perfections concern method and merit, and are perfected when guided by the sixth, wisdom realising the emptiness of giver, gift and recipient. The Perfection of Wisdom (Prajñāpāramitā) sutras, which take their name from the sixth perfection, are among the most influential texts of Mahayana Buddhism.
