The domestic rabbit (Oryctolagus cuniculus domesticus) is the domesticated form of the European rabbit, kept as livestock for meat and fur, as a laboratory animal, and increasingly as a house pet. All domestic rabbits descend from wild rabbits of southwestern Europe and northwest Africa; full domestication occurred gradually, with rabbits kept in walled enclosures and monastic hutches in the early Middle Ages and true selective breeding developing from the sixteenth century onward.

Breeds and uses

More than 300 breeds are recognised in various countries, ranging from the tiny Netherland Dwarf (under 1 kg) to the Flemish Giant (over 8 kg). Meat breeds include the New Zealand and Californian; the Angora is shorn for its long wool; Rex rabbits are valued for their velvety fur. China is the world's largest producer of rabbit meat. Rabbits are also historically important laboratory animals, used in antibody production and in the development of the pregnancy test and the polio vaccine era research.

As pets

Rabbits are among the most popular small pets. They are social, litter-trainable, and live 8–12 years, but have delicate digestive systems requiring unlimited hay, fragile spines, and continually growing teeth. The Easter association of rabbits with eggs derives from German Lutheran tradition of the Osterhase (Easter hare).

Biology

Domestic rabbits retain the wild species' crepuscular activity, burrowing instinct, and practice of caecotrophy — re-ingesting soft night faeces to absorb nutrients. Females (does) are induced ovulators with a gestation of about 30 days; young (kits) are born blind and hairless in a nest lined with the mother's fur.
