Endymion (mythology)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Endymion (Ancient Greek: Ἐνδυμίων) is a figure of Greek mythology, a beautiful youth — in various accounts a shepherd, a hunter, or a king of Elis — who was loved by Selene, the goddess of the Moon. He was granted eternal sleep, in some versions at his own request so that he might remain forever young, in others as a gift or condition imposed by Zeus.

The myth

According to the most familiar version, Selene fell in love with Endymion's beauty as he slept in a cave on Mount Latmus in Caria. She asked Zeus to grant him eternal youth, and Endymion was placed in everlasting sleep; each night Selene descended to visit him. By him she was said to have borne fifty daughters. Other traditions made Endymion a king who introduced the Olympic games, or an astronomer-king whose devotion to observing the moon explained the tale.

In art and literature

The sleeping Endymion was a favourite subject of ancient sarcophagi, symbolising serene death, and of later painting: artists including Cima da Conegliano, Poussin, Girodet and Guérin depicted Selene's moonlit visits. In English literature, John Keats's long poem Endymion (1818) opens with the famous line "A thing of beauty is a joy for ever". The myth has been read as an allegory of the soul, of death, and of the union of earthly beauty with the divine.
