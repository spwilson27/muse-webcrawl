A tea ceremony is a ritualized preparation and presentation of tea practiced in several East Asian cultures, most famously the Japanese tea ceremony (chanoyu, "hot water for tea"; also sadō or chadō, "the way of tea"), in which powdered green tea, matcha, is whisked with hot water and served to guests according to prescribed forms. Chinese gongfu tea and the Korean tea ceremony are related but distinct traditions.

History

Tea reached Japan from China in the early medieval period, and Zen Buddhist monks used it to stay awake during meditation. Under tea masters Murata Jukō, Takeno Jōō, and above all Sen no Rikyū, the practice was refined in the sixteenth century into an aesthetic and spiritual discipline emphasizing simplicity and rusticity (wabi), practiced in small tatami tea houses with a tokonoma alcove and seasonal scroll and flowers.

The gathering

A formal gathering (chaji) may last four hours, including a kaiseki meal, sweets, and both thick tea (koicha) and thin tea (usucha). Every movement — handling the tea bowl, whisk, scoop, and ladle — follows codified temae procedures taught by schools such as Urasenke, Omotesenke, and Mushakōjisenke, descended from Rikyū's heirs.

Philosophy

The ceremony embodies the principles of harmony (wa), respect (kei), purity (sei), and tranquility (jaku), and the Zen-inflected ideal of ichi-go ichi-e — "one time, one meeting" — treasuring the unrepeatable nature of each encounter.
