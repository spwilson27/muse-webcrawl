Cinematography

A cinematographer at work; the discipline covers camera, lighting and photographic choices.

Cinematography is the art of motion picture (and more recently, electronic video) photography. The cinematographer, or director of photography (DP), is the member of a film crew responsible for the photographic images of a production: choices of camera, lenses, framing, camera movement, lighting, exposure, film stock or digital sensor settings, and colour.

The word derives from the Greek kinema (movement) and graphein (to write or record). Working from the director's vision, the cinematographer designs the visual language of a film — whether naturalistic, stylised, high-key or low-key — and supervises the camera and lighting crews, including camera operators, gaffers and grips.

Technique

Key elements include aspect ratio and shot scale (from extreme wide shots to close-ups), depth of field and focus pulling, camera movement by dolly, crane, Steadicam or handheld work, and lighting design with key, fill and back lights. Frame rate choices (24 frames per second for cinema, slow motion at higher rates) and filtration further shape the image. In the digital era, cinematographers also govern on-set colour pipelines, LUTs and digital intermediate grading.

History

The craft developed from the earliest cameramen of the 1890s through the studio-era masters and the expressive lighting of film noir, the location shooting of the French New Wave, and the mobile "available light" styles of the 1970s New Hollywood. Professional recognition includes the Academy Award for Best Cinematography, first awarded in 1929.
