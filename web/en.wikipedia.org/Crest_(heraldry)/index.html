Crest (heraldry)

A crest is a component of a heraldic display, consisting of the device borne on top of the helm. Its origins lie in the sculpted ornaments worn by knights in tournaments and, to a lesser extent, battle as a means of identification, and also as decoration. The crest is often described as the "third part" of a coat of arms, after the shield and the helm, and it became a standard element of heraldic achievement from the later Middle Ages onwards.

History and use

Crests began to be used in the second half of the 13th century, initially as fan-like or sculpted structures attached to the helmet for the tournament. In Germany and Scandinavia crests were particularly elaborate and took a wide variety of forms, including animal heads, wings, horns, and whole figures. In British heraldry, crests are granted as part of arms and are inherited in the male line; the same crest may be borne by all legitimate male-line descendants of the grantee.

Forms

Crests may take the form of animals (real or fabulous), parts of animals such as heads or demi-lions, human figures or parts of figures, wings, horns, feathers, weapons, tools, ships, buildings, or abstract devices. The crest is normally shown resting on a wreath (torse) of twisted silk, a crest coronet, or a chapeau, which sits atop the helmet.

The crest as "coat of arms"

Because crests were often used alone as badges and seals, the word "crest" came in popular usage to denote the whole coat of arms or any heraldic emblem — a usage heraldists regard as incorrect, since the crest is properly only the device above the helmet.

Crests in other traditions

In Scottish heraldry, the crest has a special role within clan insignia: clansmen wear a crest badge consisting of the chief's crest surrounded by a strap and buckle bearing the clan motto. In Japanese heraldry, the kamon serve an analogous identifying function, though without the helmet element.
