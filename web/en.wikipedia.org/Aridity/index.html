Aridity

Aridity is a measure of the dryness of the climate at a particular location. It is typically expressed through an aridity index, which compares the amount of precipitation a region receives with the amount of water that could potentially be lost through evaporation and transpiration.

Aridity index

The aridity index (AI) is defined as the ratio of mean annual precipitation (P) to mean annual potential evapotranspiration (PET): AI = P/PET. The United Nations Environment Programme classifies lands by this index: hyper-arid (AI < 0.05), arid (0.05–0.20), semi-arid (0.20–0.50), and dry sub-humid (0.50–0.65).

Drylands

Drylands — the arid, semi-arid and dry sub-humid zones combined — cover roughly 41 percent of Earth's land surface and are home to more than two billion people. They occur on every continent except Antarctica, with major expanses in North Africa, the Arabian Peninsula, Central Asia, Australia, and southwestern North America.

Causes

Aridity results from combinations of subtropical high-pressure belts, rain shadows, cold ocean currents, distance from moisture sources, and high temperatures that increase evaporative demand.
