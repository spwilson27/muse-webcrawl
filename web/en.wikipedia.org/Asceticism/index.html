Asceticism

Asceticism is a lifestyle characterized by abstinence from worldly pleasures, often for the purpose of pursuing spiritual goals. Ascetics may withdraw from the world or remain within society, practising self-discipline and voluntary renunciation — of possessions, comfort, sex, rich food or sleep — in pursuit of contemplation, purity, or liberation. The term derives from the Greek áskēsis, "exercise" or "training".

In world religions

Indian religions

Asceticism is central to Jainism, Buddhism and Hinduism. Jain monks practise extreme non-violence and austerity; the Buddha, after years of severe asceticism, taught a "middle way" between indulgence and mortification. Hindu sannyasa is a formal renunciant stage of life.

Christianity

Christian asceticism grew out of the Desert Fathers of Egypt and Syria, hermits such as Anthony the Great and stylites who lived atop pillars. Monastic orders later institutionalized fasting, celibacy, poverty and prayer.

Islam and Judaism

While mainstream Islam discouraged monasticism, Sufi traditions embraced ascetic discipline (zuhd). Judaism has known ascetic movements such as the Essenes and Nazirites, though rabbinic tradition generally affirms worldly life.

Philosophical asceticism

Ancient Cynics and Stoics practised ascetic exercises to achieve self-mastery; philosophers such as Schopenhauer and Nietzsche later debated the meaning and value of the "ascetic ideal".
