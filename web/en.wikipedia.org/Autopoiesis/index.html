Autopoiesis

An autopoietic system, such as a cell, continually produces the components that produce it.

Autopoiesis (from Greek auto-, "self", and poiēsis, "creation, production") refers to a system capable of producing and maintaining itself by creating its own parts. The concept was introduced in the early 1970s by the Chilean biologists Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela as a definition of the minimal organisation of living systems.

Definition

Maturana and Varela defined an autopoietic machine as "a machine organized ... as a network of processes of production (transformation and destruction) of components which: (i) through their interactions and transformations continuously regenerate and realize the network of processes (relations) that produced them; and (ii) constitute it ... as a concrete unity in space in which they exist by specifying the topological domain of its realization as such a network." A living cell is the paradigm case: its metabolic network produces the membrane that bounds it and the enzymes that run the metabolism. Systems that produce something other than themselves are termed allopoietic.

Related ideas

The theory holds that living systems are operationally closed — their behaviour is determined by their internal organisation rather than instructed by inputs — while remaining structurally coupled to their environment. Cognition, in this view, is coextensive with life itself: "living systems are cognitive systems, and living as a process is a process of cognition."

Applications and criticism

Niklas Luhmann controversially extended autopoiesis to social systems, treating communication rather than life as the self-producing process; the idea has also been used in law, management theory, architecture and artificial life. Critics dispute whether the definition is empirically testable, whether social applications stretch the concept beyond usefulness, and how it relates to concepts of self-organisation and homeostasis developed in cybernetics and systems theory.
