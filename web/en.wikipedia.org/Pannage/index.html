Pannage

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pannage is the practice of releasing domestic pigs into a forest so that they can feed on fallen tree mast — particularly acorns, beechnuts and chestnuts — during autumn. Historically it was a common right in medieval England and elsewhere in Europe: commoners were entitled to turn their pigs out into woodland, usually in return for a payment to the landowner, and the term could refer both to the practice and to the payment itself.

History

Pannage rights are recorded as early as the Domesday Book (1086), which assessed many estates partly by the number of swine their woods could support. The season typically ran from late September (around Michaelmas) for about two months. Eating acorns and beech mast fattened pigs before winter slaughter, and the pigs' rooting was also thought to benefit the woodland by turning the soil. Unchecked, however, swine could damage young trees, and forest law regulated the practice.

Survival in the New Forest

The practice survives most famously in the New Forest in Hampshire, England, where commoners still exercise the "right of mast": each autumn, for a minimum period set by the Verderers (usually around 60 days, and extended in heavy mast years), several hundred pigs are released to eat acorns, which are mildly poisonous to the free-roaming ponies and cattle. A rare breed often associated with pannage is the British Saddleback. A similar tradition, the montanera, persists in Spain, where Iberian pigs are fattened on acorns in the dehesa to produce jamón ibérico de bellota.
