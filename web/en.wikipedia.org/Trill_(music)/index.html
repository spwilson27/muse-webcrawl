Trill (music)

Notation of a trill with its realisation

A trill (or shake, as it was known from the 16th until the early 20th century) is a musical ornament consisting of a rapid alternation between two adjacent notes, usually a semitone or whole tone apart (the interval is sometimes more precisely called a tremolo when it concerns a wider interval; the term shake also includes this). It is notated with a tr or a wavy line above the staff.

Notation

In most modern notation, a trill is generally indicated by the letters tr (or sometimes simply t) above the note, often followed by a wavy line, or by the wavy line alone. In Baroque and early Classical music, the sign ⁓ (a horizontal squiggle) or a cross (+) above the note could also indicate a trill or a related ornament.

Performance practice

The manner of performing a trill changed over the centuries. In Baroque music, the trill conventionally began on the upper auxiliary note and was played "on the beat", with the alternation accelerating toward the end and often closing with a termination (a turn or two-note resolution). From the Classical era onward, trills increasingly began on the main note itself, a practice standard in modern performance.

The speed and rhythmic regularity of the alternation depend on the instrument and the musical context. On the piano, trills involving certain finger pairs are notoriously demanding; on string and wind instruments they are produced by rapid finger motion, and singers execute them with controlled oscillation of the larynx.

In specific traditions

The trill is a stock ornament in Western classical music from the Renaissance onward, appearing in the works of composers from Giulio Caccini to Frédéric Chopin, whose piano writing exploits long, brilliant trills. Related ornaments include the mordent, the inverted mordent (or pralltriller), and the turn. Rapid alternation also features in other traditions, such as the gamaka of Indian classical music.
