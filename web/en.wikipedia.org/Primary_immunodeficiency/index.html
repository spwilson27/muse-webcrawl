Primary immunodeficiency

Primary immunodeficiencies (PIDs), also called inborn errors of immunity, are a group of more than 450 distinct genetic disorders in which part of the body's immune system is missing or functions improperly. Most are caused by inherited genetic mutations. People with primary immunodeficiency suffer recurrent, severe, or unusual infections, and may also have autoimmune disease, allergies, or an increased risk of malignancy. The conditions range from mild antibody deficiencies that may go undiagnosed until adulthood to severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID), which is fatal in infancy without treatment.

Primary immunodeficiencies can affect antibodies, T cells, phagocytes, or complement.

Classification

The International Union of Immunological Societies classifies inborn errors of immunity into categories including combined immunodeficiencies (such as SCID), predominantly antibody deficiencies (such as common variable immunodeficiency and X-linked agammaglobulinaemia), diseases of immune dysregulation, defects of phagocyte number or function (such as chronic granulomatous disease), defects of intrinsic and innate immunity, autoinflammatory disorders, and complement deficiencies.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on clinical suspicion — typically a history of recurrent infections — followed by laboratory tests of immunoglobulin levels, lymphocyte subsets, and vaccine responses, and increasingly by genetic sequencing. Newborn screening for SCID using T-cell receptor excision circles (TRECs) is now routine in many countries. Treatments include immunoglobulin replacement therapy, prophylactic antimicrobials, haematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and, increasingly, gene therapy for specific defects.

Epidemiology

Individually rare, primary immunodeficiencies collectively may affect as many as 1 in 1,000 to 1 in 2,000 people when milder forms are included. Selective IgA deficiency is the most common. Awareness campaigns use the "10 warning signs" of primary immunodeficiency to promote earlier diagnosis.
