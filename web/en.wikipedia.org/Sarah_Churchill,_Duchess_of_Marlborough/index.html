Sarah Churchill, Duchess of Marlborough

BornSarah Jennings, 15 June 1660, Sandridge, Hertfordshire, England
Died18 October 1744 (aged 84), London, England

RolesMistress of the Robes; Keeper of the Privy Purse; Lady of the Bedchamber

Sarah Churchill, Duchess of Marlborough (née Jennings; 15 June 1660 – 18 October 1744), was one of the most influential women of her time through her close friendship with Queen Anne of Great Britain. Rising from modest beginnings at court, she became Mistress of the Robes, the most senior office in the Queen's household, and effectively governed much of the early politics of the Queen's reign through her influence.

Early life

Sarah Jennings was born at Holywell House in Sandridge, Hertfordshire, the daughter of Richard Jennings, a member of parliament, and Frances Thornhurst. In about 1673 she came to court as a maid of honour to Mary of Modena, Duchess of York. There she met John Churchill, an ambitious army officer, whom she married in secret in the winter of 1677–78.

Friendship with Princess Anne

Sarah formed an intense friendship with the young Princess Anne, the future queen. To guard against eavesdroppers the two corresponded under code names — Anne was "Mrs Morley" and Sarah "Mrs Freeman." When Anne came into conflict with William III and Mary II, Sarah remained loyal, and after Anne ascended the throne in 1702 Sarah received a shower of offices: Mistress of the Robes, Keeper of the Privy Purse, and Ranger of Windsor Great Park.

Political influence and fall

Through Anne, Sarah promoted the interests of her husband, the great general of the War of the Spanish Succession, and of the Whig party. Gradually the friendship cooled: Sarah's domineering manner, her increasingly radical Whig sympathies, and the rise in Anne's favour of Abigail Masham — Sarah's own cousin — caused a celebrated rupture in 1710–11, when Sarah was dismissed from all her offices.

Later life and legacy

After the Duke's death in 1722, Sarah became one of the richest women in Europe. She supervised the completion of Blenheim Palace, fought numerous lawsuits, and wrote a famously self-justifying memoir, An Account of the Conduct of the Dowager Duchess of Marlborough (1742). She died at Marlborough House, London, in 1744.
