Nine-rank system

The nine-rank system (Chinese: 九品中正制), also known as the jiupin zhongzheng system, was a method of selecting officials in imperial China during the Three Kingdoms, Jin dynasty, and Northern and Southern dynasties periods. It was created in 220 CE by Chen Qun, a minister of Cao Wei, and remained the chief mechanism for bureaucratic recruitment until it was displaced by the imperial examination system under the Sui dynasty.

Under the system, officials called Rectifiers (zhongzheng) were appointed for each commandery and province to grade local men of talent into nine ranks, from first (highest) to ninth, taking into account ability, virtue, and family background. The central government then appointed candidates to office in light of their ranks, in principle matching grade to post.

[ Image: A Jin dynasty mural depicting officials of ranked grades ]

Degeneration and criticism

In practice, family background quickly overwhelmed merit in the Rectifiers' judgments. By the Western Jin, the system had entrenched a hereditary aristocracy of great clans: a contemporary lament held that "in the upper ranks there are no poor families; in the lower ranks, no powerful houses" (上品無寒門,下品無勢族). Ranking registers became a formal certification of pedigree, and the great families (menfa) monopolised high office for centuries.

Abolition

The rise of the imperial examinations under the Sui (from 605) and their expansion under the Tang gradually made competitive written testing the principal path to office, and the nine-rank machinery was abandoned, though the terminology of nine grades survived in the later ranking of officials themselves (the nine grades of the mandarinate).
