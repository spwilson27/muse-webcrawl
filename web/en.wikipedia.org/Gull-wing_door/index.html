Gull-wing door

A gull-wing door, also known in French as a porte papillon (butterfly door, though the terms are sometimes distinguished), is a car door that is hinged at the roof rather than the side, so that it opens upward like a seagull's wing. The design is most famously associated with the Mercedes-Benz 300 SL of 1954, and later with the DeLorean DMC-12.

History

The design was pioneered on the 1952 Mercedes-Benz 300 SL (W194) racing car: its tubular space frame had high, deep sills that made conventional doors impractical, so the doors were cut into the roof and hinged there. The feature carried over to the 1954 production 300 SL coupé and became its signature. In the DeLorean DMC-12 (1981–1983), gull-wing doors with cryogenically preset torsion bars and gas struts became a defining feature of an iconic, if commercially unsuccessful, car.

Advantages and drawbacks

Gull-wing doors allow access in tight parking spaces and over high sills, but they complicate sealing against weather, require strong roof structures and gas struts, and raise concerns about opening the doors if the car rolls onto its roof — a problem addressed in the Mercedes-Benz SLS AMG by explosive bolts that blow the doors off. The Tesla Model X uses a double-hinged "falcon wing" variant for its rear doors.
