Phloem is the living tissue in vascular plants that transports soluble organic compounds made during photosynthesis — particularly the sugar sucrose — to parts of the plant where they are needed. This transport process is called translocation. Together with the xylem, which conducts water and minerals upward from the roots, the phloem forms the plant's vascular system.

Unlike xylem vessels, which are dead at maturity, phloem conducting cells remain alive, though they lose their nuclei and depend on neighbouring companion cells for metabolic support.

Structure

Phloem tissue consists of several cell types. The main conducting elements in flowering plants are sieve tube elements, stacked end to end into sieve tubes. Their end walls are perforated by sieve plates, allowing sap to flow between cells. Each sieve tube element is paired with one or more companion cells, connected by plasmodesmata, which load and unload sugars and supply proteins and ATP. In gymnosperms the conducting cells are simpler sieve cells associated with albuminous cells. Phloem also contains parenchyma cells for storage and fibres (bast fibres) for support.

Function

Phloem sap is rich in sucrose (often 10–30% by mass), amino acids, hormones, and other organic molecules, along with potassium and trace elements. It moves from sources — organs that produce or release sugars, such as mature leaves — to sinks — organs that consume or store them, such as roots, fruits, seeds, and young growing leaves. Because different parts of the plant act as sources or sinks at different times, flow within the phloem can be bidirectional, though within a single sieve tube it runs one way at a time.

Pressure-flow hypothesis

The widely accepted pressure-flow (or mass-flow) hypothesis, proposed by Ernst Münch in 1930, explains translocation as an osmotically driven bulk flow. Sugar loading at the source raises solute concentration, drawing water in from the xylem and generating high turgor pressure. At the sink, sugar is unloaded, water leaves, and pressure falls. Sap flows passively down this pressure gradient. The model accounts for observed sap velocities, typically 0.3–1.5 metres per hour.

Phloem and bark

In woody stems, the phloem produced by the vascular cambium toward the outside forms the inner bark. As new phloem is added each season and older tissue is crushed, much of the outer bark is dead, sloughed-off material. Some species' phloem fibres are economically important: flax, hemp, jute, and ramie fibres all come from phloem.
