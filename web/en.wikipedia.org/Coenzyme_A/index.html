Coenzyme A

Coenzyme A
Formula: C21H36N7O16P3S
Role: Acyl group carrier
Key derivative: Acetyl-CoA
Vitamin source: Pantothenic acid (B5)

Coenzyme A (CoA, CoASH, or HSCoA) is a coenzyme notable for its role in the synthesis and oxidation of fatty acids and the oxidation of pyruvate in the citric acid cycle. It carries acyl groups, most importantly in the form of acetyl-CoA, one of the central molecules of metabolism.

Structure and discovery

Coenzyme A consists of a pantothenic acid (vitamin B5) unit linked to adenosine diphosphate and a terminal cysteamine group whose thiol (–SH) forms thioester bonds with acyl groups. It was identified in 1947 by Fritz Lipmann, who later received the Nobel Prize in part for this discovery; its structure was worked out in the early 1950s.

Function

The thioester bond in acyl-CoA compounds is energy-rich, making CoA derivatives activated carriers for two-carbon and longer acyl units. Acetyl-CoA feeds the citric acid cycle, supplies carbon for fatty acid and cholesterol synthesis, and provides acetyl groups for protein acetylation. Coenzyme A participates in roughly 4% of known enzymatic reactions.

Biosynthesis

Organisms synthesize CoA from pantothenate in a five-step pathway requiring ATP and cysteine. Humans must obtain pantothenic acid from the diet; it is widespread in foods, so deficiency is rare.
