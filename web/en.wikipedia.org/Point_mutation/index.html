Point mutation

A point mutation is a genetic mutation where a single nucleotide base is changed, inserted or deleted from a DNA or RNA sequence of an organism's genome. Point mutations have a variety of effects on the downstream protein product—consequences that are moderately predictable based upon the specifics of the mutation. These consequences can range from no effect (e.g. synonymous mutations) to deleterious effects (e.g. frameshift mutations), on a product's production, composition, and function.

Classification

Substitutions of one base for another are classified as transitions, in which a purine is replaced by another purine (A ↔ G) or a pyrimidine by another pyrimidine (C ↔ T), or transversions, in which a purine is replaced by a pyrimidine or vice versa. Within protein-coding regions, substitutions may be synonymous (silent), missense (changing one amino acid), or nonsense (creating a premature stop codon). Insertions or deletions of a single base (indels) generally shift the reading frame unless they occur in multiples of three.

Causes

Point mutations arise from errors during DNA replication, spontaneous chemical changes such as depurination and deamination, and exposure to mutagens including ultraviolet radiation, ionizing radiation, and certain chemicals.

Examples and significance

The classic example of a point mutation is the single A→T transversion in the β-globin gene that causes sickle cell disease, changing a glutamic acid codon to valine. Point mutations in oncogenes and tumor suppressor genes drive many cancers, and accumulated somatic point mutations are a hallmark of aging genomes. Most point mutations are neutral, and their steady accumulation over generations underlies the concept of the molecular clock in evolution.
