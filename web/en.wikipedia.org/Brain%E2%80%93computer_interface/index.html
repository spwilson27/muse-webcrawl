Brain–computer interface

Also calledBCI, brain–machine interface
ApproachesInvasive, partially invasive, non-invasive
SignalsEEG, ECoG, single-unit activity

A brain–computer interface (BCI) is a direct communication pathway between the brain and an external device, translating neural activity into commands that control computers, prosthetic limbs, or communication aids, without relying on peripheral nerves and muscles.

Approaches

Non-invasive systems record from the scalp, most commonly with electroencephalography (EEG). Partially invasive devices such as electrocorticography (ECoG) grids rest on the brain's surface, while invasive systems implant microelectrode arrays into the cortex, capturing single-neuron activity with the highest signal quality but requiring surgery. Research animal work dates to the 1970s, and human trials have enabled paralysed users to move cursors, type, and operate robotic arms.

Applications and challenges

Principal applications are assistive: restoring communication for people with locked-in syndrome or ALS, and controlling neuroprostheses. Companies and academic groups have demonstrated implanted systems allowing users to generate text and speech at increasing speeds. Challenges include signal stability over years, surgical risk, bandwidth, and ethical questions about neural data and agency.
