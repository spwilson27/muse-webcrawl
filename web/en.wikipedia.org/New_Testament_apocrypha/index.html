New Testament apocrypha

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A fragment of early Christian writing

The New Testament apocrypha (singular: apocryphon) are a number of writings by early Christians that give accounts of Jesus and his teachings, the nature of God, or the teachings of his apostles and of their lives. Some of these writings were cited as scripture by early Christians, but since the fifth century a widespread consensus has emerged limiting the New Testament to the 27 books of the modern canon. Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, and Protestant churches generally do not view these writings as part of the Bible.

Definition and history

The word apocrypha means "things put away" or "things hidden". The Greek adjective apokryphos originally meant "hidden" or "esoteric", and came to be applied to writings kept secret because they were the vehicles of esoteric knowledge considered too profound or sacred to be disclosed to anyone other than the initiated. By extension, later church writers applied the term to writings whose usefulness or authority was disputed.

The New Testament apocrypha include dozens of gospels, acts, epistles, and apocalypses composed from the second century onwards. Many were written under the names of apostles or other prominent figures of the earliest Church — a practice known as pseudepigraphy — in order to gain authority for their contents.

Principal works

Notable examples include the Gospel of Thomas, a sayings gospel rediscovered in the Nag Hammadi library in 1945; the Infancy Gospel of Thomas; the Protoevangelium of James, which tells of the birth and childhood of Mary; the Gospel of Peter; the Acts of Paul and Thecla; and the Apocalypse of Peter. These texts influenced Christian art, liturgy, and popular piety for centuries, even when excluded from the canon.
