Free will

Choice, the everyday face of the free will problem

Free will is the capacity or ability of agents to choose between different possible courses of action. It is closely linked to moral responsibility, praise and blame, deliberation, and the sense of personal agency, and it stands in long-standing tension with determinism — the thesis that every event, including human action, is necessitated by prior events and the laws of nature.

Positions

  • Libertarianism (in metaphysics): free will exists and is incompatible with determinism, so determinism is false.
  • Hard determinism: determinism is true and free will does not exist.
  • Compatibilism: free will and determinism can coexist; free action is action flowing from the agent's own desires and character, uncoerced.
  • Hard incompatibilism: free will is impossible whether or not determinism is true.

Key debates

The consequence argument (associated with Peter van Inwagen) holds that if determinism is true, our acts are consequences of the distant past and the laws, over which we have no control. Harry Frankfurt's counterexamples challenge the idea that responsibility requires alternative possibilities. Theologians have wrestled with reconciling free will with divine foreknowledge and predestination.

Science

Neuroscience entered the debate with Benjamin Libet's experiments in the 1980s suggesting that brain activity preceding a voluntary movement begins before subjects report conscious awareness of deciding. Later studies refined or disputed the interpretation of these readiness potentials, and no scientific consensus is generally taken to have settled the philosophical question.
