Gullah

The Gullah are African Americans who live in the Lowcountry region of the US states of Georgia, South Carolina, Florida and North Carolina, including the coastal plain and the Sea Islands. They are known for preserving more of their African linguistic and cultural heritage than other African American communities, including the Gullah language, an English-based creole with strong West African influences.

History

Gullah culture developed among enslaved Africans brought to work on rice, indigo and cotton plantations. Because the Sea Islands were relatively isolated, and because malaria made white planters absent for much of the year, enslaved people retained African languages, farming knowledge (including rice-growing techniques from West Africa's Rice Coast), foodways, music, spiritual practices and crafts such as sweetgrass basket coiling. After the Civil War, the Gullah remained largely isolated until bridges and resort development reached the islands in the twentieth century.

Culture today

Today land loss to development and the dispersal of younger generations threaten Gullah communities. The US Congress established the Gullah/Geechee Cultural Heritage Corridor in 2006 to support preservation. A Gullah translation of the New Testament was completed in 2005, and the language and culture have attracted growing scholarly and public interest.
