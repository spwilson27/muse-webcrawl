Crimes against humanity

Crimes against humanity
Legal fieldInternational criminal law
First prosecutedNuremberg trials (1945–46)
Current definitionRome Statute, Article 7 (1998)
ForumInternational Criminal Court; ad hoc tribunals; national courts

Crimes against humanity are certain serious offences intentionally committed as part of a widespread or systematic attack directed against any civilian population, with knowledge of the attack. Unlike war crimes, they may be committed in peacetime as well as during armed conflict, and unlike genocide they do not require intent to destroy a particular group. They are among the core crimes of international criminal law.

History

The phrase entered international diplomacy in 1915, when Britain, France and Russia condemned the massacres of Armenians in the Ottoman Empire as "crimes against humanity and civilisation". The concept was first given legal form in the 1945 London Charter of the International Military Tribunal at Nuremberg, under which Nazi leaders were prosecuted for murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation and persecution. The Nuremberg principles were later affirmed by the UN General Assembly, and the definition was developed by the ad hoc tribunals for Yugoslavia (1993) and Rwanda (1994) before being codified in Article 7 of the 1998 Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court.

Definition

Article 7 lists acts including murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation or forcible transfer of population, imprisonment in violation of fundamental rules of international law, torture, rape and other grave sexual violence, persecution on political, racial, national, ethnic, cultural, religious or gender grounds, enforced disappearance, the crime of apartheid, and other inhumane acts. The acts must form part of a widespread or systematic attack pursuant to a state or organisational policy.

Prosecution

Crimes against humanity are subject to universal jurisdiction in many states and carry no statute of limitations under the 1968 UN Convention. Prosecutions have occurred before the ICC (including cases arising from situations in Kenya, Libya, Côte d'Ivoire and Mali), the Yugoslav and Rwanda tribunals, hybrid courts such as the Extraordinary Chambers in the Courts of Cambodia, and national courts.
