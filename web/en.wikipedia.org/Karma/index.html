Karma

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sanskritकर्म (karma, "action, deed")
TraditionsHinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism
Related conceptsSamsara, rebirth, dharma, moksha

Karma (Sanskrit: कर्म, from the root kṛ, "to do, make") is the principle of cause and effect in the Indian religions in which intentional actions — of body, speech, and mind — shape the agent's future experiences, including the circumstances of rebirth within the cycle of samsara. Good deeds produce merit and fortunate results; harmful deeds produce demerit and suffering, whether in this life or a later one.

Development

The concept emerged in Vedic religion, where karma originally referred to ritual action and its efficacy, and was transformed in the Upanishads into a universal moral law governing rebirth. The Buddha made intention (cetana) the essence of karma, while Jainism conceived karma as a subtle material substance that adheres to the soul through action and passion. Hindu schools debated how karma's fruits are dispensed — by God, as in theistic Vedanta, or by an impersonal cosmic order — and how liberation (moksha) from its bondage is achieved.

Interpretations

Traditions classify karma by when it ripens and by whether it is newly created, stored, or already bearing fruit. Karma is not fate: present choices remain free, and spiritual practice, devotion, and knowledge can exhaust or transcend accumulated karma. In modern popular usage, especially in the West, "karma" has become a general shorthand for moral cause and effect — "what goes around comes around."
