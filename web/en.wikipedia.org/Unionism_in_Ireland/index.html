Unionism in Ireland

Belfast City Hall, a focal point of unionist civic identity.

Unionism in Ireland is a political tradition that favours the continuation of the political union between Northern Ireland and Great Britain, and opposes a united Ireland independent of the United Kingdom. Though unionism is strongest among the largely Protestant population of Northern Ireland, it historically had support across the island, and unionists in the south played a significant role before partition.

History

The union itself dates from the Acts of Union 1800, which merged the Kingdom of Ireland with Great Britain. Organized unionism emerged in the 1880s in opposition to Irish Home Rule, uniting the Irish Unionist Alliance in the south and west with the Ulster unionists of the north-east. Under Edward Carson, Ulster unionists resisted the Third Home Rule Bill by signing the Ulster Covenant (1912) and forming the Ulster Volunteer Force.

Partition and Northern Ireland

The Government of Ireland Act 1920 partitioned Ireland; six of the nine counties of Ulster formed Northern Ireland, which remained in the UK, while the south became the Irish Free State. The Ulster Unionist Party governed Northern Ireland from 1921 to 1972. During the Troubles, unionism fragmented among parties including the UUP and the Democratic Unionist Party. Unionists supported the Good Friday Agreement's principle of consent — that Northern Ireland's status cannot change without majority support — though the DUP initially opposed the agreement before entering government under it in 2007.

Ideology

Unionism combines British national identity, loyalty to the Crown, and (for many) Protestant religious heritage, though it also includes secular and Catholic unionists. Loyalism is a more militant working-class variant of the tradition.
