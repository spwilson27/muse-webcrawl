Scouting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Scouts at camp

Scouting, also known as the Scout Movement, is a worldwide youth social movement employing the Scout method, a programme of informal education with an emphasis on practical outdoor activities, including camping, woodcraft, aquatics, hiking, backpacking and sports. The movement is recognised by its members' distinctive uniform, intended to hide social differences and signify equality.

Scouting was founded in 1907 by Robert Baden-Powell, a British Army officer who held an experimental camp on Brownsea Island and published Scouting for Boys in 1908. A parallel movement for girls, Guiding, followed soon after. Today Scouting counts tens of millions of members in most countries of the world, coordinated internationally by the World Organization of the Scout Movement (founded 1922).

The Scout method

The Scout method comprises elements including the Scout Promise and Law, learning by doing, the patrol system of small peer groups, personal progression through badges and awards, and an emphasis on nature and the outdoors. Scouts progress through age-based sections, and adult volunteers provide leadership and training.

History

Baden-Powell's ideas drew on his military experience, including the siege of Mafeking, as well as on Ernest Thompson Seton's Woodcraft Indians and other youth movements. The movement grew rapidly: the first World Scout Jamboree was held in London in 1920, and Scouting spread through the British Empire and beyond. During the World Wars, Scouts contributed to the war effort at home, and the movement adapted after 1945 to coeducation and modernised programmes in many countries.

Activities and traditions

Typical activities include camping, hiking, orienteering, pioneering (building structures with poles and lashings), first aid, community service and jamborees gathering Scouts internationally. Common symbols include the fleur-de-lis emblem and the three-fingered Scout sign and salute.
