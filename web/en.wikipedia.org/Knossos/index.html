Knossos

Location: Crete, Greece (near Heraklion)
Period: Neolithic to Roman; palace flourished c. 2000–1350 BC
Excavated by: Arthur Evans (from 1900)

Knossos (Greek: Κνωσός) is the largest Bronze Age archaeological site on Crete and has been called Europe's oldest city. Settled as early as the Neolithic period, it became the ceremonial and political centre of the Minoan civilization, famous in Greek myth as the seat of King Minos and the Labyrinth of the Minotaur.

The palace

The great palace of Knossos, covering some 14,000 square metres, was built and rebuilt between about 2000 and 1350 BC. Its maze of storerooms, workshops, shrines, and royal apartments around a central court inspired the legend of the Labyrinth. Highlights include the Throne Room with its gypsum throne and griffin frescoes, the Grand Staircase of the royal apartments, extensive magazines holding giant storage jars (pithoi), and sophisticated drainage and water systems. Frescoes depict bull-leaping, processions, dolphins, and lilies.

Excavation and script

Sir Arthur Evans purchased the site and excavated from 1900, controversially reconstructing parts of the palace in reinforced concrete painted in vivid colours. Tablets inscribed in the undeciphered Linear A script were found, along with later Linear B tablets — written in an early form of Greek — which Michael Ventris deciphered in 1952, showing a Greek-speaking administration in the palace's final phase, probably after a Mycenaean takeover.

Decline

The palace was destroyed by fire around 1375–1350 BC, though the town continued into the Iron Age and Roman period. Today Knossos is Crete's most visited archaeological site and a tentative UNESCO World Heritage listing.
