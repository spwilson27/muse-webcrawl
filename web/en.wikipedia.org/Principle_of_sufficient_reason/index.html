Principle of sufficient reason

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, chief proponent of the principle.

The principle of sufficient reason (PSR) states that everything must have a reason or cause. In the formulation of Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, who made the principle one of the two great principles of his philosophy (alongside the law of noncontradiction): no fact can be true or existent, and no statement correct, unless there is a sufficient reason why it should be thus and not otherwise — even if that reason is known only to God.

History

Precursors of the principle appear in Parmenides, Anaximander, Aquinas, and Spinoza, whose Ethics asserts that for each thing a cause or reason must be assigned for its existence or non-existence. Arthur Schopenhauer devoted his doctoral dissertation, On the Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason (1813), to distinguishing four forms of the principle: of becoming (causality), of knowing (grounds of judgement), of being (mathematical and spatial relations), and of acting (motivation).

Uses

Leibniz deployed the principle in his cosmological argument for God's existence — the universe's existence requires a sufficient reason lying outside the series of contingent things — and in arguments about the identity of indiscernibles and the nature of space. It also underlies his doctrine that God chose the best of all possible worlds, since God could not act without a reason for preferring one world to another.

Criticism

David Hume argued that we can conceive of things beginning to exist without a cause, and Immanuel Kant treated the principle as valid within experience but not beyond it. In the twentieth century, some philosophers argued that quantum mechanics, with its apparently uncaused events such as radioactive decay, challenges the principle, while defenders reply that probabilistic explanations still supply sufficient reasons of a statistical kind.
