Greco-Persian Wars

Date499–449 BC
LocationMainland Greece, Thrace, Aegean Sea, Asia Minor, Cyprus, Egypt
ResultGreek victory; Peace of Callias (c. 449 BC)
BelligerentsGreek city-states (Athens, Sparta, allies) vs. Achaemenid Empire

The Greco-Persian Wars (also often called the Persian Wars) were a series of conflicts between the Achaemenid Empire and Greek city-states that started in 499 BC and lasted until 449 BC. The collision between the fractious political world of the Greeks and the enormous empire of the Persians began when Cyrus the Great conquered the Greek-inhabited region of Ionia in 547 BC.

Course of the wars

The Ionian Revolt (499–493 BC), in which Athens and Eretria aided rebellious Greek cities in Asia Minor, provoked Darius I to punish them. His first expedition was wrecked by storms; the second was defeated by the Athenians at the Battle of Marathon (490 BC). Darius's son Xerxes I led a vast invasion in 480 BC. The Greeks were defeated at Thermopylae — where Leonidas and his Spartans died in a famous last stand — and Athens was evacuated and burned, but the Persian fleet was destroyed at the Battle of Salamis. In 479 BC the Persian army was crushed at Plataea and its fleet at Mycale, ending the invasion.

Thereafter Athens led the Delian League in offensive operations across the Aegean and eastern Mediterranean. Fighting continued until the Athenian disaster in Egypt (454 BC) and further campaigns in Cyprus led to a negotiated settlement, the Peace of Callias (c. 449 BC), by which Persia accepted the independence of the Ionian Greeks.

Significance

The preservation of Greek independence allowed classical Greek culture to flourish, and the wars became foundational to Greek — and later Western — identity. Our principal source is Herodotus, whose Histories was written to record the conflict. The wars also transformed Athens, whose wartime naval confederacy became an empire, setting the stage for the Peloponnesian War with Sparta.
