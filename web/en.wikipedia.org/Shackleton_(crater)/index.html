Shackleton (crater)

Shackleton crater lies almost exactly at the Moon's south pole.

Shackleton is an impact crater at the lunar south pole, named after the Antarctic explorer Ernest Shackleton. It is about 21 kilometres in diameter and 4.2 kilometres deep, and the Moon's axis of rotation passes within a few kilometres of its rim, giving the site extreme patterns of light and shadow.

Illumination and ice

Because the lunar axis is tilted only slightly, points along Shackleton's rim are illuminated for most of the lunar year — among the highest illumination fractions anywhere on the Moon — while the crater floor and inner walls lie in permanent shadow. These permanently shadowed regions are cold traps, colder than the surface of Pluto, where water ice delivered by comets or produced by solar-wind chemistry can persist for billions of years. Radar and neutron measurements, and data from orbiters including Lunar Prospector, Chandrayaan-1 and LRO, have indicated hydrogen and likely water ice in the south polar region, including in and around Shackleton, although estimates of its abundance and purity vary.

Exploration interest

The combination of near-constant sunlight for power and nearby shadowed ice makes the Shackleton area one of the most attractive sites for future lunar bases and resource extraction. The crater and its rim have featured in landing-site studies for NASA's Artemis programme and in proposals by other space agencies, and a communications and navigation relay role has also been proposed for its rim.
