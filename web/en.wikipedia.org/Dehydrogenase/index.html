Dehydrogenase

A dehydrogenase is an enzyme belonging to the group of oxidoreductases that oxidizes a substrate by reducing an electron acceptor, usually NAD+/NADP+ or a flavin coenzyme such as FAD or FMN. In the reaction, the dehydrogenase removes hydrogen atoms (or their equivalents, protons and electrons) from the substrate and transfers them to the acceptor, thereby converting it to NADH or NADPH. Dehydrogenases play central roles in cellular respiration, fermentation, and many biosynthetic pathways.

Examples

Well-known dehydrogenases include alcohol dehydrogenase, which oxidizes alcohols to aldehydes or ketones; lactate dehydrogenase, which interconverts pyruvate and lactate and is important in anaerobic metabolism; glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, the first enzyme of the pentose phosphate pathway, whose deficiency is the most common human enzyme defect and can cause haemolytic anaemia; and glutamate dehydrogenase, which links amino acid metabolism with the citric acid cycle. In the citric acid cycle itself, isocitrate dehydrogenase, α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, and malate dehydrogenase generate the reduced coenzymes that feed the electron transport chain.

Clinical and industrial significance

Serum levels of certain dehydrogenases are used as diagnostic markers: elevated lactate dehydrogenase, for instance, is a nonspecific indicator of tissue damage. Dehydrogenases are also used in biotechnology and diagnostics, for example in enzymatic assays for blood glucose and alcohol, and they are being developed as biocatalysts for the production of chiral alcohols and other fine chemicals.
