Ezra Cornell

Ezra Cornell (January 11, 1807 – December 9, 1874) was an American businessman, politician, and philanthropist. He was a founder of the Western Union Telegraph Company and co-founder, with Andrew Dickson White, of Cornell University in Ithaca, New York.

Ezra Cornell, founder of Cornell University

Telegraph career

Born in Westchester County, New York, and raised in De Ruyter, Cornell began as a carpenter and mechanic. In 1843 he helped Samuel Morse build the experimental telegraph line between Washington, D.C., and Baltimore, devising a machine to lay the cable underground. He became deeply involved in the rapidly expanding telegraph industry, building lines throughout the northeastern United States. In 1855 his companies merged with rivals to form the Western Union Telegraph Company, of which he was a principal shareholder, making him a wealthy man.

Cornell University

As a New York state senator, Cornell chaired the agriculture committee and supported the use of New York's allocation under the Morrill Land-Grant Act of 1862 to found a new university. In 1865 the state chartered Cornell University, with Cornell contributing $500,000 and land in Ithaca. His stated ideal was to found "an institution where any person can find instruction in any study," and the university opened in 1868. Cornell also founded Ithaca's public library and served on the university's board of trustees until his death.
