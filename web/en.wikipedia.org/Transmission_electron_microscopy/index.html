Transmission electron microscopy

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) is a microscopy technique in which a beam of electrons is transmitted through an extremely thin specimen, interacting with it as it passes through. An image is formed from the interaction of the electrons with the sample, and is magnified and focused onto a detector. TEMs are capable of imaging at resolutions far higher than light microscopes, reaching atomic resolution in modern instruments, because of the small de Broglie wavelength of electrons.

History

The first electron microscope was built in 1931 by Ernst Ruska and Max Knoll in Berlin; Ruska later received the Nobel Prize in Physics for this work. Commercial instruments appeared by the end of the 1930s, and the technique became a central tool of cell biology after methods for ultrathin sectioning and heavy-metal staining were developed in the 1950s.

Operation

An electron gun, commonly a heated tungsten filament, a lanthanum hexaboride crystal or a field-emission source, produces a beam accelerated through 100,000 to 300,000 volts. Electromagnetic lenses condense the beam onto the specimen, which must typically be less than about 100 nanometres thick. Objective and projector lenses form and magnify the image, which is recorded on a CCD camera or direct electron detector. The whole column is kept under high vacuum.

Applications

TEM is used in materials science to study crystal defects, nanoparticles and interfaces; in biology to image viruses, organelles and macromolecular complexes; and in cryo-electron microscopy, frozen-hydrated specimens are imaged to determine protein structures at near-atomic resolution, a development recognised by the 2017 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. Related techniques include scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM), electron diffraction and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy.
