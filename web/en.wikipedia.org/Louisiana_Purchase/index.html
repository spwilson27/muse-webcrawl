The Louisiana Purchase (Vente de la Louisiane) was the acquisition of the territory of Louisiana by the United States of America from the French Republic in 1803. In return for fifteen million dollars, or approximately eighteen dollars per square mile, the United States acquired some 828,000 square miles (2,140,000 km²) of land west of the Mississippi River — roughly 23% of the present territory of the United States — nearly doubling the size of the young republic at a stroke.

The Louisiana Purchase, 1803

Background

France had ceded Louisiana to Spain in 1763; by the secret Treaty of San Ildefonso (1800), Spain returned it to Napoleon's France. When the Spanish intendant at New Orleans withdrew American settlers' right of deposit in 1802, President Thomas Jefferson feared a powerful France would close the Mississippi — the outlet for western produce — and block American expansion. Jefferson sent James Monroe to join envoy Robert Livingston in Paris, authorised to buy New Orleans and the Floridas for up to $10 million.

The treaty

Napoleon, his American ambitions ruined by the failure to reconquer Saint-Domingue (Haiti) and needing funds for renewed war with Britain, unexpectedly offered the whole of Louisiana. Livingston and Monroe exceeded their instructions and agreed a price of $15 million (60 million francs, plus $3.75 million in assumed claims) on 30 April 1803. The Senate ratified the treaty in October; formal transfers took place at St. Louis in March 1804.

Consequences

The Purchase raised constitutional questions — Jefferson doubted the federal power to acquire territory but proceeded — and transformed the United States into a continental power. Jefferson commissioned the Lewis and Clark Expedition (1804–1806) to explore the new lands. All or part of fifteen present states were formed from the territory, from Louisiana and Missouri to Montana and Wyoming. The acquisition also dispossessed Native American nations whose lands were sold without their consent, a consequence with lasting effects.
