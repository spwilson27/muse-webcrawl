Breadfruit

Breadfruit (Artocarpus altilis) is a species of flowering tree in the mulberry and jackfruit family (Moraceae) believed to be a domesticated descendant of the breadnut (Artocarpus camansi) originating in New Guinea, the Maluku Islands and the Philippines. It was spread throughout Oceania by Austronesian voyagers and, in the late 18th century, transplanted to the Caribbean — the destination of HMS Bounty's ill-fated 1789 voyage under William Bligh. Breadfruit is a staple food in many tropical regions and was named for the texture of its cooked flesh, said to resemble freshly baked bread.

Description

Breadfruit trees grow to 26 metres (85 ft) tall, with large, thick, deeply pinnatifid leaves; all parts produce a milky latex. The plants are monoecious, bearing male and female flowers on the same tree. The fruit develops from the entire inflorescence into a multiple fruit weighing 0.25–6 kg, green to yellow-green, with a bumpy skin. Polynesian varieties are largely seedless and propagated from root shoots; trees begin bearing within 3–5 years and may produce 150–200 fruits per season for decades, yielding more food per unit area than rice, wheat or maize in comparable climates.

History and dispersal

Austronesian peoples carried breadfruit (one of the "canoe plants") across the Pacific, reaching Hawaii by around 1300 CE, where as ʻulu it became central to agriculture and mythology. In 1769 Joseph Banks observed it in Tahiti with Captain Cook and promoted it as cheap food for enslaved people in the British West Indies. Lieutenant William Bligh's first breadfruit expedition ended with the mutiny on the Bounty (1789); his second voyage (1791–1793) successfully delivered plants to St Vincent and Jamaica. Breadfruit was initially rejected by those it was meant to feed, but over generations it was absorbed into Caribbean cuisines, as in oil down, Grenada's national dish.

Culinary use

Breadfruit is eaten at all ripeness stages: mature-but-firm fruit is roasted, baked, boiled, fried or fermented (as in the Marquesan mā), tasting of potato and fresh bread; ripe fruit is sweeter and used in desserts. Nutritionally it is rich in complex carbohydrates and a complete source of essential amino acids for a fruit, gluten-free, and increasingly studied for food security under climate change, since established trees need little input and withstand tropical storms reasonably well. The wood is light and termite-resistant, used for canoes and houses; the latex served as caulk and birdlime.
