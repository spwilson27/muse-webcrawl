In mathematics and signal processing, the Z-transform converts a discrete-time signal — a sequence of real or complex numbers — into a complex frequency-domain representation. It is the discrete-time counterpart of the Laplace transform and is a standard tool in digital signal processing and discrete control theory.

Definition

The bilateral Z-transform of a sequence x[n] is

X(z) = Σn=−∞∞ x[n] z−n

where z is a complex number. The set of z for which the sum converges is the region of convergence (ROC), which is essential to specifying the transform uniquely. The unilateral Z-transform sums from n = 0 and is used for causal systems and difference equations with initial conditions.

Properties and use

Key properties include linearity, time shifting (a delay of k samples multiplies by z−k), and the convolution property: convolution of sequences becomes multiplication of their transforms. For a linear time-invariant system, the ratio H(z) = Y(z)/X(z) is the transfer function; its poles determine stability — a causal system is stable exactly when all poles lie inside the unit circle. Evaluating X(z) on the unit circle gives the discrete-time Fourier transform.

Inverse transform

The inverse Z-transform is defined by a contour integral around a path within the ROC encircling the origin; in practice, transforms are usually inverted by inspection using tables and partial-fraction expansion.
