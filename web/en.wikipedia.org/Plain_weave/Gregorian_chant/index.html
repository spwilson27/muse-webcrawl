Gregorian chant

Gregorian chant is the central tradition of Western plainchant, a form of monophonic, unaccompanied sacred song sung in Latin in the Roman Catholic Church. Named by tradition after Pope Gregory I (r. 590–604), the repertory was in fact assembled and notated mainly in the eighth and ninth centuries as Roman and Gallican chant traditions merged under Carolingian sponsorship.

Characteristics

Chant melodies move in free rhythm governed by the text, within the eight church modes. Early notation used neumes written above the text; staff notation developed from the eleventh century. The repertory includes the chants of the Mass — the fixed Ordinary (Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus, Agnus Dei) and the changing Proper for each feast — and the chants of the Divine Office, notably psalm antiphons.

History and revival

After the Council of Trent, edited chant books standardized the repertory; the Medicean edition of 1614–15 long dominated. In the nineteenth century the monks of Solesmes Abbey, led by Dom Prosper Guéranger and later Dom Joseph Pothier and Dom André Mocquereau, restored earlier readings from medieval manuscripts, producing the chant books adopted by Rome in the early twentieth century. The Second Vatican Council affirmed chant's "pride of place" in the Roman liturgy, though vernacular music largely displaced it in parish practice.
