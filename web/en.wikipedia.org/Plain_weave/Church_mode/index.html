Church mode

The church modes (also called ecclesiastical modes or Gregorian modes) are the eight scales used to classify and organize Gregorian chant in medieval and Renaissance music theory. Each mode is defined by its final (finalis), its range (ambitus), and characteristic melodic formulas, especially the reciting tone or tenor used in psalmody.

The eight modes

The system pairs authentic and plagal modes sharing a final: modes I and II end on D (Dorian and Hypodorian), III and IV on E (Phrygian and Hypophrygian), V and VI on F (Lydian and Hypolydian), and VII and VIII on G (Mixolydian and Hypomixolydian). Authentic modes range roughly an octave above the final; their plagal counterparts range below and above it. Chant melodies were assigned to modes partly so that psalm tones — the formulas used to sing psalms — would join smoothly to antiphons.

History

The modal system developed in the Carolingian era, drawing in part on Byzantine oktōēchos theory, and was codified in treatises such as the Musica enchiriadis. Glarean's Dodecachordon (1547) expanded the system to twelve modes, adding modes on A and C that foreshadow the later major and minor scales. Modal thinking persisted in folk music and was revived by twentieth-century composers and jazz musicians.
