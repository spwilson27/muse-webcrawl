Neume

A neume (from Greek pneuma, "breath") is the basic element of the earliest systems of musical notation for Gregorian chant, used in the West from roughly the ninth century. Neumes are signs written above the sung text indicating a single pitch or, more commonly, a group of two to four pitches sung to one syllable, along with hints of melodic gesture and expression.

Types

Basic neumes include the punctum (single low or neutral note), virga (single higher note), podatus (two notes ascending), clivis (two descending), torculus (low–high–low), porrectus (high–low–high), and compound forms such as the scandicus and climacus. Special neumes — the quilisma, with its trembling effect, and liquescent neumes used on diphthongs — added nuance. Early "adiastematic" neumes showed contour but not exact pitch; "heightened" and staff notation later fixed pitches precisely, culminating in the square notation still printed in modern chant books.

Origins

Scholars debate whether neumes derived from ekphonetic signs for scriptural reading, from prosodic accent marks, or from cheironomic conducting gestures. Regional families — St. Gall, Breton, Beneventan, Mozarabic — developed distinct shapes before staff notation standardized the tradition.
