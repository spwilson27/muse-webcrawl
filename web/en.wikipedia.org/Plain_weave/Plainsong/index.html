Plainsong

Plainsong (also plainchant; Latin: cantus planus) is a body of chants used in the liturgies of the Western Church. Plainsong is monophonic — consisting of a single, unaccompanied melodic line — and is sung in free rhythm, following the natural accents of the Latin words rather than a regular metre. Its best-known repertory is Gregorian chant, the chant of the Roman rite.

Characteristics

Plainsong melodies are organised by the system of eight church modes. Notation originally used neumes written above the text to indicate melodic gestures; later these were placed on a four-line staff. Melodies may be syllabic (one note per syllable), neumatic (a few notes per syllable), or melismatic (long runs of notes on a single syllable, as in the Alleluia).

History

The repertory developed during the early Middle Ages, drawing on Roman and Gallican traditions, and was codified and orally transmitted in monasteries before being written down from about the ninth century. Regional chant dialects included Ambrosian chant in Milan, Mozarabic chant in Spain, and Old Roman chant. In the nineteenth century the monks of Solesmes Abbey led a restoration of Gregorian chant from medieval manuscripts, shaping modern editions and performance practice.

Uses

Plainsong forms the sung core of the Mass and the Divine Office in the Roman Catholic tradition, and adapted chant is also used in Anglican and Lutheran worship, often in vernacular translation. Composers from the medieval organum pioneers through Renaissance polyphonists used plainsong melodies as the structural basis (cantus firmus) of their works, a practice echoed by some modern composers.
