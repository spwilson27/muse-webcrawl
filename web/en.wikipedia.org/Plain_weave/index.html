Plain weave

Plain weave (also called tabby weave, linen weave, or taffeta weave) is the most basic of the three fundamental types of textile weaves, alongside twill and satin. In plain weave, the warp and weft threads cross at right angles, with each weft yarn passing alternately over and under successive warp yarns, and each row offset from the one before. The result is a strong, flat, checkerboard-like interlacing with the maximum number of binding points, identical on both sides of the fabric.

Characteristics

Because the threads interlace more frequently than in any other weave, plain weave produces fabrics that are firm, durable, and resistant to slipping, though less smooth and lustrous than satin and less flexible than twill. The balanced structure makes printing and finishing straightforward. If the warp and weft yarns differ in colour, simple patterns such as checks and stripes result; if yarn thicknesses differ, ribbed effects such as grosgrain and poplin are produced.

Fabrics made with plain weave

Many familiar fabrics are plain weaves, including muslin, cambric, lawn, voile, chiffon, organza, taffeta, canvas (in lighter forms), buckram, and much shirting. Plaid and tartan cloth is commonly woven as twill, but plain-weave versions exist; balanced plain weave with two colours in both directions gives the classic gingham check.

Derivative weaves

Variations include basket weave (two or more yarns grouped together in each direction), rib weaves in which warp or weft predominates, and leno weave, a related structure in which warp yarns twist around the weft.
