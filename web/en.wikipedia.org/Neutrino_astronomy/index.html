Neutrino astronomy

Optical modules of the IceCube Neutrino Observatory at the South Pole.

Neutrino astronomy is the branch of astronomy that observes astronomical objects with neutrino detectors in special observatories. Neutrinos are created as a result of certain types of radioactive decay, nuclear reactions such as those that take place in the Sun, in nuclear reactors, or when cosmic rays hit atoms. Because neutrinos interact only very weakly with matter, they travel nearly unimpeded from their sources across the universe, carrying information about the most violent and distant astrophysical environments — but they are correspondingly difficult to detect.

Detection

Neutrino telescopes instrument huge volumes of transparent natural media — Antarctic ice (IceCube), Mediterranean seawater (KM3NeT, ANTARES), or Siberian lake water (Baikal-GVD) — with arrays of photomultipliers that record Cherenkov light from secondary particles produced in rare neutrino interactions. Underground liquid-scintillator and water detectors such as Super-Kamiokande and the Sudbury Neutrino Observatory observe lower-energy neutrinos from the Sun and supernovae.

Discoveries

Raymond Davis Jr.'s Homestake experiment first detected solar neutrinos in the 1960s, and the resulting "solar neutrino problem" was resolved by the discovery of neutrino oscillation, recognized by the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physics. In 1987 detectors worldwide recorded a burst of neutrinos from SN 1987A, the first (and still only) confirmed extrasolar neutrino source beyond the Sun. In 2013 IceCube announced the discovery of a diffuse flux of high-energy astrophysical neutrinos, and in 2017–2018 the blazar TXS 0506+056 was identified as the first likely source of an individual high-energy neutrino, in a landmark multimessenger observation with gamma-ray telescopes.
