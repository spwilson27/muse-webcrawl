Reform Act 1832

The Representation of the People Act 1832 (also known as the Reform Act 1832, Great Reform Act or First Reform Act) was an Act of Parliament of the United Kingdom that introduced major changes to the electoral system of England and Wales. It abolished many "rotten boroughs", redistributed seats toward the growing industrial cities, and modestly widened the franchise.

Background

The unreformed system had changed little since the 17th century. Seats were distributed without regard to population: Old Sarum, with seven voters, elected two MPs, while Manchester, Birmingham and Leeds elected none directly. Voting qualifications varied wildly by borough, and many seats were controlled by aristocratic patrons.

The reform crisis

Earl Grey's Whig government introduced reform bills from 1831. The Lords' rejection of the second bill in October 1831 provoked riots in Derby, Nottingham and Bristol. When the third bill stalled in committee in May 1832, Grey resigned; the "Days of May" followed, with mass agitation, a threatened run on the banks ("To stop the Duke, go for gold"), and pressure for revolution. William IV recalled Grey and promised to create enough new peers to pass the bill; the Lords backed down, and the Act received royal assent on 7 June 1832. Separate Acts covered Scotland and Ireland.

Provisions

The Act disenfranchised 56 boroughs entirely and reduced representation of 31 more. It created 22 new two-member borough seats and 20 single-member seats for industrial towns, and added county seats. In the boroughs, the franchise was standardised for men occupying property worth £10 a year ("ten-pound householders"); in counties, £10 copyholders and £50 tenants-at-will were added. The electorate rose from about 400,000 to about 650,000 — roughly one in five adult males. The Act explicitly defined voters as "male persons", formally excluding women for the first time.

Consequences

The Act did not create democracy, but it broke the aristocratic monopoly on the Commons, forced parties to organise nationally with registration societies, and set the precedent for the further Reform Acts of 1867, 1884 and 1918.
