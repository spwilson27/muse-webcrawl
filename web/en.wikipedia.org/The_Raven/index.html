The Raven

"The Raven" is a narrative poem by American writer Edgar Allan Poe. First published in January 1845 in the New York Evening Mirror, the poem is often noted for its musicality, stylized language and supernatural atmosphere.

It tells of a distraught lover who, at midnight in December, receives a mysterious visit from a talking raven. The bird speaks only one word — "Nevermore" — which the narrator interprets with growing despair over the lost Lenore.

Synopsis

Weak and weary over volumes of forgotten lore, the narrator is startled by a tapping at his chamber door. Admitting a stately raven, he asks its name and a series of increasingly anguished questions, each answered "Nevermore", until he concludes his soul shall be lifted nevermore.

Composition and themes

Poe claimed in his essay "The Philosophy of Composition" (1846) to have written the poem by a logical, methodical process, choosing the melancholy topic of the death of a beautiful woman. Themes include grief, devotion, and the confrontation with death and memory.

Publication and influence

The poem made Poe popular in his lifetime, was widely reprinted and parodied, and inspired illustrations by Gustave Doré and Édouard Manet. Its refrain remains among the most quoted lines in American poetry.
