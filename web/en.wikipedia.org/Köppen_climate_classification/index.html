Köppen climate classification

The Köppen climate classification is one of the most widely used climate classification systems. It was first published by German-Russian climatologist Wladimir Köppen in 1884 and later modified by Köppen and by Rudolf Geiger. The system divides climates into five main groups based on seasonal precipitation and temperature patterns, each denoted by a capital letter.

Main groups

The five groups are: A (tropical), B (arid), C (temperate), D (continental), and E (polar). Second and third letters describe precipitation seasonality and temperature characteristics respectively — for example, Cfb denotes an oceanic climate, BWh a hot desert, and Dfc a subarctic climate.

Criteria

Thresholds are empirical, chosen so that zones correspond roughly with vegetation distributions. Tropical climates have every month averaging above 18 °C; polar climates have no month averaging above 10 °C. Aridity thresholds depend on temperature and on the seasonal distribution of rainfall.

Use and criticism

The Köppen system is used throughout geography, ecology, and climate-change research; updated maps by Peel, Finlayson and McMahon (2007) and later authors apply it to modern data. Critics note its arbitrary thresholds and poor handling of highlands and transitional zones, and alternatives such as the Trewartha classification modify its treatment of temperate and continental climates.
