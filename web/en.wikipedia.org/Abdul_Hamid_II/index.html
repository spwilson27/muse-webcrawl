Abdul Hamid II

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Abdul Hamid II (Ottoman Turkish: عبد الحميد ثانی; 21 September 1842 – 10 February 1918) was the 34th sultan of the Ottoman Empire, reigning from 31 August 1876 until his deposition on 27 April 1909. The last Ottoman sultan to exercise effective personal control over the empire, his reign—the "Hamidian era"—is remembered for autocracy, pan-Islamic policy, extensive modernisation projects, and the Hamidian massacres of Armenians in the 1890s.

Early reign and constitution

Abdul Hamid came to the throne after the deposition of his brother Murad V, having promised the reformist statesman Midhat Pasha that he would proclaim a constitution. The first Ottoman constitution (Kanun-i Esasi) was promulgated on 23 December 1876 and a parliament elected. However, following the disastrous Russo-Turkish War of 1877–78 and the Treaty of Berlin, the sultan suspended parliament in February 1878 and ruled autocratically for the next thirty years from Yıldız Palace, governing through an extensive palace bureaucracy and intelligence network.

Rule and reforms

Despite his authoritarianism, Abdul Hamid pressed ahead with modernisation: the state debt was renegotiated through the Ottoman Public Debt Administration; railways—including the Hejaz Railway to Medina and the Anatolian and Baghdad lines—were built; telegraph networks, schools, hospitals and museums were expanded; and the civil service was professionalised. He promoted Islam as a unifying ideology across the empire's remaining territories and cultivated his claim to the caliphate, alarming European powers with large Muslim populations.

Armenian massacres and opposition

Armenian revolutionary activity and demands for reform in the eastern provinces led to repression. In 1894–96, massacres by regular troops and Kurdish Hamidiye regiments killed tens of thousands—estimates range from 80,000 to 300,000—of Armenians, events known as the Hamidian massacres. Opposition coalesced in the Committee of Union and Progress (the Young Turks), whose 1908 revolution forced Abdul Hamid to restore the constitution. After the counter-revolutionary 31 March Incident in 1909, he was deposed and exiled to Salonica, later returning to confinement in Istanbul, where he died in 1918.
