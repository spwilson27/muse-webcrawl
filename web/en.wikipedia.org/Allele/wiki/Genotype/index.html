Genotype

The genotype of an organism is its complete set of genetic material, or, in a narrower sense, the particular alleles it carries at one or more loci of interest. Together with environmental influences, the genotype determines the phenotype — the organism's observable characteristics. The terms genotype and phenotype were coined by the Danish botanist Wilhelm Johannsen in 1909.

Three genotypes at a locus (AA, Aa, aa) can produce two or three phenotypes depending on dominance.

Genotype and phenotype

Individuals with the same genotype may differ in phenotype because of environmental effects and developmental variation, while individuals with different genotypes (such as AA and Aa under complete dominance) may share a phenotype. Genotyping — determining an individual's genotype — is done by DNA sequencing, PCR-based assays, and DNA microarrays, and is used in medicine, forensics, agriculture, and ancestry testing.

Inheritance

An organism's genotype at a locus is homozygous if its two alleles are identical and heterozygous if they differ. Punnett squares model the expected proportions of genotypes among offspring of a cross. Modern genomics studies genotypes genome-wide, for example in genome-wide association studies that correlate genetic variants with diseases and traits.
