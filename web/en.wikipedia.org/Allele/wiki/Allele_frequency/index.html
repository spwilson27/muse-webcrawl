Allele frequency

Allele frequency, or gene frequency, is the relative frequency of an allele (a variant of a gene) at a particular locus in a population, expressed as a fraction or percentage of all copies of that gene in the gene pool. Allele frequencies are the fundamental quantities of population genetics: microevolution is defined as a change in allele frequencies over generations.

Changes in allele frequency over generations are the basic measure of evolution.

Calculation

For a diploid locus with two alleles A and a, the frequency of A is the number of A copies divided by the total number of copies of the gene in the population. Under random mating with no selection, mutation, migration, or drift, genotype frequencies settle at the Hardy–Weinberg proportions p², 2pq, and q², where p and q are the two allele frequencies.

Forces that change allele frequencies

Four processes alter allele frequencies: natural selection, genetic drift, mutation, and gene flow. Selection systematically favours alleles that increase fitness; drift — random sampling error — is strongest in small populations and can fix or eliminate alleles by chance; mutation introduces new alleles at low rates; and migration homogenises frequencies between populations.
