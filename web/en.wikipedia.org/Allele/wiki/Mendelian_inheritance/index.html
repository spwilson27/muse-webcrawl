Mendelian inheritance

Mendelian inheritance (also known as Mendelism) is a type of biological inheritance following the principles proposed by Gregor Mendel in 1865 and 1866, based on his experiments with pea plants, and rediscovered in 1900 by Hugo de Vries and Carl Correns. Mendel's laws describe how discrete hereditary units — genes — are passed from parents to offspring.

Mendel's crosses of pea plants revealed the laws of segregation and independent assortment.

The laws

The Law of Segregation states that the two alleles for a trait separate during gamete formation, so each gamete carries only one. The Law of Independent Assortment states that genes for different traits are inherited independently — true for genes on different chromosomes or far apart on the same one. The Law of Dominance states that in a heterozygote, one allele (the dominant) may mask the other (the recessive).

Mendelian traits and disorders

Traits governed mainly by a single locus, such as Mendel's pea characters, are Mendelian traits. In humans, single-gene disorders following Mendelian patterns include cystic fibrosis (autosomal recessive), Huntington's disease (autosomal dominant), and haemophilia (X-linked recessive). Many real traits depart from simple Mendelism through incomplete dominance, codominance, linkage, multiple alleles, and polygenic inheritance.
