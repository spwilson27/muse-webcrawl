Allele


Two alleles for a gene located at the same position (locus) on homologous chromosomes.

An allele is one of two or more alternative forms of a gene found at the same position, or locus, on a chromosome. Alleles can result in different observable characteristics, or phenotypes, in an organism. In diploid organisms such as humans, each individual carries two copies of most genes — one inherited from each parent — and the two copies may be the same allele or different alleles.

The word "allele" is a short form of allelomorph ("other form"), a term coined in the early days of genetics. The combination of alleles an individual possesses at a locus is its genotype for that gene; if the two alleles are identical, the organism is homozygous at that locus, and if they differ, it is heterozygous.

Dominant and recessive alleles

Where two different alleles are present, one may mask the expression of the other. An allele that is expressed in the heterozygote is described as dominant, while the allele whose effect is hidden is recessive; a recessive allele is expressed only when the organism is homozygous for it. In Gregor Mendel's classic experiments with pea plants, the allele for purple flowers was dominant over the allele for white flowers, so heterozygous plants had purple flowers even though they carried the white allele.

Not all inheritance follows this simple pattern. In incomplete dominance, the heterozygote shows an intermediate phenotype — pink flowers in snapdragons crossed from red and white parents — while in codominance both alleles are fully expressed, as in the AB blood type in humans, where both A and B alleles produce their respective antigens.

Multiple alleles and allele frequency

Although any one diploid individual carries at most two alleles of a gene, a population may contain many different alleles of the same gene — a situation called multiple allelism. The human ABO blood group system, with its A, B and O alleles, is a standard example. Population geneticists track the allele frequency — the proportion of all copies of a gene in a population that are a particular allele — and study how frequencies change under natural selection, mutation, genetic drift and gene flow.

Molecular basis

At the molecular level, alleles differ in their DNA sequence. The differences may be as small as a single nucleotide (a single-nucleotide polymorphism, or SNP) or as large as insertions, deletions or rearrangements of long stretches of DNA. Some sequence differences change the amino acid sequence of the encoded protein and alter its function; others affect gene regulation; still others are "silent" and have no detectable effect. An allele that produces no functional product is called a null allele, and alleles that differ from the usual form found in nature are often contrasted with the wild type allele.

Alleles and disease

Many genetic disorders are caused by particular alleles. Recessive disease alleles, such as the ΔF508 allele of the CFTR gene that causes most cases of cystic fibrosis, produce disease only in homozygotes; carriers with one copy are typically healthy. Dominant disease alleles, such as the expanded-repeat allele of the HTT gene responsible for Huntington's disease, cause disease in heterozygotes. Other alleles, such as certain variants of APOE, modify the risk of common diseases like Alzheimer's disease without being directly determinative.
