Bowen's reaction series

FieldPetrology / geochemistry
Developed byNorman L. Bowen
Published1922–1928

Bowen's reaction series is a framework in igneous petrology describing the order in which minerals crystallize from a cooling magma, developed by Canadian geologist Norman Levi Bowen in the early 20th century. It explains why certain minerals are commonly found together in igneous rocks.

The two branches

The discontinuous series follows ferromagnesian silicates through progressively more silica-rich structures as temperature falls: olivine, pyroxene, amphibole, and biotite mica. The continuous series tracks plagioclase feldspar, which changes continuously from calcium-rich (anorthite) to sodium-rich (albite) compositions. At low temperatures the branches converge on potassium feldspar, muscovite, and finally quartz.

Significance

The series underpins the concept of fractional crystallization: early-formed crystals removed from a melt leave residual magma enriched in silica and alkalis, so a single parent magma can yield rocks from gabbro to granite. Rocks crystallizing early are described as ultramafic or mafic; late-crystallizing assemblages are felsic.
