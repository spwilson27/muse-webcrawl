The Tlingit (Tlingit: Lingít, pronounced [ɬɪŋ.kɪ́t], lit. "People of the Tides") are an Indigenous people of the Pacific Northwest Coast of North America. Their homeland is the temperate rainforest archipelago of Southeast Alaska — the present-day Alaska Panhandle — with interior groups extending into British Columbia and the Yukon. They are a matrilineal culture with a complex system of clans, crests, and ceremonial feasting.

Society and culture

Tlingit society is divided into two moieties — Raven (Yeil) and Eagle/Wolf (Chʼaakʼ / Gooch) — each composed of numerous clans (naa), which in turn hold rights to crests (at.óow, sacred clan property), names, stories, fishing sites, and ceremonial regalia. Descent and inheritance pass through the mother. The potlatch (ku.éexʼ), a memorial feast hosted by one moiety for the other, validates marriages, deaths, name-giving, and the raising of totem poles (kootéeyaa). Tlingit art is a leading expression of Northwest Coast formline design, seen in carved house posts, masks, bentwood boxes, and Chilkat blankets woven from mountain-goat wool and cedar bark. Traditional subsistence centers on salmon, halibut, herring, shellfish, sea mammals, berries, and deer.

Language

The Tlingit language, a member of the Na-Dene family (related distantly to Eyak and the Athabaskan languages), is known for its complex phonology, including ejective consonants and a near-absence of labial consonants. Once widely spoken, it became severely endangered through 20th-century boarding-school policies; today fluent first-language speakers number in the hundreds, but active revitalization programs, immersion nests, and university courses are under way.

History

The Tlingit resisted Russian colonization — destroying the Russian post at Sitka in 1802, though the Russians re-established it after the Battle of Sitka in 1804 — and maintained a vigorous trade with European and American ships, obtaining firearms and goods in exchange for sea-otter pelts. After the United States purchased Alaska in 1867, the Tlingit pressed land claims that were partially addressed by the Alaska Native Claims Settlement Act of 1971; the Tlingit are largely represented regionally by Sealaska Corporation and by the Central Council of Tlingit and Haida Indian Tribes of Alaska.
