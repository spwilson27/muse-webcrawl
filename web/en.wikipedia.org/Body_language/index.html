Body language

Posture, gesture and facial expression are all channels of body language.

Body language is a type of nonverbal communication in which physical behaviours, as opposed to words, are used to express or convey information. Such behaviour includes facial expressions, body posture, gestures, eye movement, touch and the use of space. The study of body movement is known as kinesics. Body language exists in both animals and humans, and although much of it in humans is subconscious, it forms a large part of how people interpret one another's attitudes and emotions.

Components

Facial expressions are a principal channel: combinations of movements of the eyes, brows, lips and other features convey emotions such as happiness, sadness, anger, fear, surprise and disgust, some of which researcher Paul Ekman argued are recognised across cultures. Gestures range from "emblems" with direct verbal equivalents (such as a thumbs-up) to illustrative movements accompanying speech. Posture and orientation of the body can signal openness, defensiveness, dominance or submission, while proxemics concerns the use of interpersonal space, and haptics the communication of touch. Eye contact and gaze direction regulate conversation and signal attention and interest.

Interpretation and criticism

Popular accounts often exaggerate how reliably body language can be read; the frequently cited claim that 93 per cent of communication is nonverbal misapplies Albert Mehrabian's narrow experiments on feelings and attitudes. Psychologists caution that cues must be read in clusters and in context, that individuals differ considerably, and that interpretation varies between cultures — gestures, personal space and eye contact norms are not universal. Claims that body language can reliably detect deception have weak empirical support, and such methods are treated with scepticism by researchers, even though they are widely taught in business and law-enforcement settings.

Applications

Body language is used in acting, public speaking, negotiation, teaching, clinical psychology and job interviews, and is an active area of research in human–computer interaction, where systems attempt to recognise gestures and affect automatically. Sign languages, it should be noted, are full languages with grammar and are not merely "body language".
