Chantilly porcelain is a soft-paste porcelain made at Chantilly, France, between about 1730 and 1800. Founded under the patronage of Louis Henri, Prince of Condé, an avid collector of East Asian ceramics, the factory was among the earliest and finest of the French porcelain manufactories.

Production

The factory was established by the financier Ciquaire Cirou, who obtained a royal patent in 1735. Chantilly's body used a tin-opacified glaze in imitation of Chinese porcelain, giving a creamy white surface well suited to enamel painting. Early wares closely copied Japanese Kakiemon designs — quails, prunus, and the "red dragon" pattern — before developing French floral styles (à la branche) and the sprigged chantilly pattern of small blue flowers.

Later history

The Prince of Condé's death in 1740 removed the factory's patron, and competition from Vincennes and Sèvres, which enjoyed royal privileges over coloured grounds and gilding, pushed Chantilly towards simpler tablewares. Production declined through the later 18th century and the factory closed around 1800.
