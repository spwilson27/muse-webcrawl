Gradient


The gradient of a scalar field points in the direction of steepest increase.

In vector calculus, the gradient of a scalar-valued differentiable function f of several variables is the vector field whose value at a point p is the "direction and rate of fastest increase" of the function at p. It is denoted ∇f or grad f, where ∇ is the nabla (del) operator. If the function increases most rapidly in a given direction, the gradient points that way, and its magnitude equals the rate of increase in that direction.

Definition

In three-dimensional Cartesian coordinates, the gradient of f(x, y, z) is ∇f = (∂f/∂x)i + (∂f/∂y)j + (∂f/∂z)k. The gradient is normal (perpendicular) to the level surfaces of f, and the directional derivative in any direction is the dot product of the gradient with the unit vector in that direction. Formally it is characterized by the property that df = ∇f · dr for infinitesimal displacements.

Interpretation and uses

For a height function over terrain, the gradient at a point indicates the direction and steepness of the steepest slope. Physical examples include the temperature gradient driving heat conduction, the pressure gradient force in meteorology, and the electric field as the negative gradient of electric potential. In machine learning, gradient descent and its stochastic variants minimize loss functions by stepping opposite to the gradient, the direction of steepest descent; backpropagation computes gradients through large models efficiently. Conservative vector fields are precisely those that are gradients of a scalar potential.

Generalizations

The concept generalizes through the derivative (Jacobian) of multivariable maps, and on Riemannian manifolds the gradient is defined via the metric so that df(v) = ⟨grad f, v⟩. Related operators built from ∇ include divergence and curl.
