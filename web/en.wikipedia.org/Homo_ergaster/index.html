Homo ergaster

Homo ergaster is an extinct species or subspecies of archaic humans who lived in Africa in the Early Pleistocene. Whether H. ergaster constitutes a species of its own or should be subsumed into H. erectus is an ongoing and unresolved dispute within palaeoanthropology. Proponents of synonymisation typically designate the early African populations of H. erectus as H. erectus sensu stricto and other populations as H. erectus sensu lato; by contrast, others use H. ergaster for the African form. The name means "working man" in Latin (ergaster from Greek ergastḗr), chosen in reference to the hand axes produced by the species.

Discovery and fossils

The species was named by Colin Groves and Vratislav Mazák in 1975, based on the mandible KNM-ER 992 found at Koobi Fora, Kenya. The most complete fossil is KNM-WT 15000, the "Turkana Boy" (or Nariokotome Boy), an almost complete skeleton of a juvenile discovered in 1984 near Lake Turkana by Kamoya Kimeu and described by Richard Leakey's team; it dates to about 1.6 million years ago. Material has been found in Kenya, Ethiopia, Tanzania and South Africa (including Swartkrans).

Anatomy

H. ergaster had a cranial capacity ranging from about 700 to 900 cm³ in earlier specimens and up to around 1,000 cm³ in later ones, with a long, low skull, prominent brow ridges (supraorbital torus) and a reduced chin. The Turkana Boy, estimated to have been between 7 and 11 years old at death, stood about 1.6 m (5 ft 3 in) tall and, had he reached adulthood, would likely have been around 1.8 m (5 ft 11 in). The body proportions were tall and slender, adapted for efficient walking in hot, open environments, and the species is inferred to have had relatively hairless dark skin aiding heat loss through sweating.

Behaviour and technology

H. ergaster is associated with the Acheulean stone tool industry, characterised by large bifacial handaxes, which appears about 1.76 million years ago. It may have been among the first hominins to use fire regularly, although evidence remains debated. Its reduced gut and tooth size relative to earlier hominins have been taken to suggest a diet richer in meat and cooked food. Some researchers have proposed that H. ergaster possessed a form of proto-language, though the narrow spinal cord of the Turkana Boy initially suggested limited breath control for speech.
