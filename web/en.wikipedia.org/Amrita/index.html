Amrita

[Image placeholder]

Amrita (Sanskrit: अमृत, IAST: amṛta), Amrit or Amata in Pali, (also called Sudha, Amiy, Ami) is a Sanskrit word that means "immortality". It is a central concept within Indian religions and is often referred to in ancient Indian texts as an elixir. Amrita is cognate to and shares many similarities with ambrosia, the food or drink of the Greek gods that also confers longevity or immortality.

Mythology

In Hindu tradition, amrita is the drink of the devas (gods), which grants them immortality. It arose from the Samudra Manthana, the churning of the Ocean of Milk by the devas and asuras working together with Mount Mandara as the churning rod and the serpent Vasuki as the rope. Among the treasures that emerged from the ocean was the pot (kumbha) of amrita, borne by Dhanvantari, physician of the gods. The asuras seized the pot, but Vishnu, taking the enchantress form of Mohini, distracted them and distributed the nectar to the devas. The demon Svarbhanu, disguised as a god, drank some of the amrita; the Sun and Moon alerted Vishnu, who beheaded him — but the head, having tasted immortality, lived on as Rahu, and the body as Ketu.

In other traditions

In Buddhism, amata ("deathless") is an epithet of nirvana. In Jainism, amrita appears in cosmological and ritual contexts. In Sikhism, Amrit is the name of the holy water used in the Amrit Sanchar initiation ceremony, prepared by stirring water and sugar crystals in an iron bowl with a double-edged sword while reciting hymns. Amrit is also a common given name in South Asia, particularly among Sikhs and Hindus.
