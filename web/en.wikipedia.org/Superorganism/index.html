Superorganism

A superorganism is an organism composed of many individual organisms. The concept is most commonly applied to colonies of social insects — ants, bees, wasps and termites — in which division of labour is so specialised, and individuals so interdependent, that the colony can usefully be analysed as a single unit of natural selection, analogous to the cells of a body.

Concept and history

The term was coined in the 19th century and popularised for insect societies by entomologist William Morton Wheeler in the early 20th century. E. O. Wilson and Bert Hölldobler revived and developed the concept in works including The Superorganism (2008), arguing that colony-level traits — nest architecture, thermoregulation, defence and foraging — emerge from the interactions of individuals much as physiology emerges from cells.

Features

Superorganisms typically show reproductive division of labour (a queen caste reproduces while workers are sterile or near-sterile), cooperative brood care, overlapping generations within the colony, and extensive communication, for example through pheromones in ants and bees or the waggle dance of honey bees. Colonies can regulate their internal environment, as in the precise temperature control of bee hives and termite mounds.

Extensions and criticism

The concept has been extended metaphorically to human societies, ecosystems, and James Lovelock's Gaia hypothesis of the Earth as a self-regulating system. Critics caution that the analogy can obscure real conflicts of interest within colonies and that colony-level selection claims require rigorous evidence; modern multilevel selection theory treats the superorganism as one end of a spectrum of biological individuality.
