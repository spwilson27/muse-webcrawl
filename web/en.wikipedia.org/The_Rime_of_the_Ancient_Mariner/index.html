The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (originally The Rime of the Ancyent Marinere) is the longest major poem by the English poet Samuel Taylor Coleridge, written in 1797–98 and published in 1798 in the first edition of Lyrical Ballads, the volume Coleridge produced with William Wordsworth that launched English Romanticism. It relates the experiences of a sailor who, having shot an albatross, suffers supernatural punishment and is condemned to wander the earth retelling his tale.

Summary

At a wedding feast, an aged mariner detains one of three guests and holds him with his "glittering eye" while he tells his story. His ship was driven by storms toward the South Pole, where an albatross appeared through the fog and was welcomed as a good omen; the Mariner inexplicably shot it with his crossbow. His shipmates first condemned, then approved the act, and hung the dead bird around his neck. Becalmed under a burning sun — "Water, water, every where, / Nor any drop to drink" — the crew died one by one, cursing him with their eyes, while the spectre-woman Life-in-Death claimed his soul. Watching water snakes in the moonlight, the Mariner blessed them unawares, the albatross fell from his neck, and he was gradually redeemed: rain fell, angels animated the crew's bodies to sail the ship home, and he sank into a saving sleep. Rescued, he is left with a penance of wandering and teaching love and reverence for all creatures: "He prayeth best, who loveth best / All things both great and small."

Composition and publication

The poem was planned during a walking tour of the Quantocks with Wordsworth, who suggested the shooting of the albatross (drawn from a voyage narrative) and contributed a few lines. Coleridge revised the poem repeatedly; the famous marginal gloss, written in a faux-antique style, was added in the 1817 collection Sibylline Leaves. Gustave Doré's engravings for an 1876 edition became the poem's canonical illustrations.

Interpretation and legacy

Readings of the poem range from Christian allegory of sin and redemption to ecological parable and psychological study of guilt. Its phrases have entered the language: "an albatross around one's neck," "a sadder and a wiser man," and "water, water, every where." It has inspired works from Moby-Dick to Iron Maiden's epic metal setting of 1984.
