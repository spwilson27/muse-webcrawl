Oboe
ClassificationWoodwind; double-reed aerophone
RangeFrom B♭3 to about A6
RelatedCor anglais, oboe d'amore, bass oboe, shawm

The oboe is a type of double-reed woodwind instrument. Oboes are usually made of wood, but may also be made of synthetic materials, such as plastic or resin composites. The oboe, commonly used in orchestras, concert bands, chamber music, and film music, has a distinctive penetrating tone often described as bright and reedy.

Because its pitch is steady and penetrating, the oboe traditionally gives the tuning note (concert A) to the orchestra before a performance.

Construction

The oboe consists of a conical bore tube about 62 cm (24½ in) long, in three sections (top joint, bottom joint, and bell), sounded by a double reed made of two pieces of cane bound to a small metal tube (staple). Most modern oboes are made of grenadilla (African blackwood) and have elaborate keywork of the Conservatoire system.

History

The oboe developed in mid-17th-century France from the shawm, refined by instrument makers such as the Hotteterre and Philidor families for the court of Louis XIV; its French name, hautbois ("high wood"), is the source of the English name. Baroque oboes had few keys; the instrument gained its modern key system in the 19th century through the work of the Triebert family and others.

Repertoire

Major oboe concertos include those by Albinoni, Vivaldi, Marcello, Mozart, Richard Strauss, and Vaughan Williams. The instrument features prominently in orchestral works, including famous solos in music by Bach, Beethoven, Brahms, Tchaikovsky, and Prokofiev — whose Peter and the Wolf assigns the oboe the character of the duck.

Family

Related instruments include the smaller oboe d'amore in A, the cor anglais (English horn) in F, the bass oboe, and the rare heckelphone.
