Spanish Road

Spanish Road
Spanish: Camino Español
Route: Milan → Lorraine/Luxembourg → Flanders
Active: 1567 – mid-17th century
Length: c. 1,000 km

The Spanish Road (Spanish: Camino Español) was a military supply and troop route used by the Spanish Monarchy between 1567 and the 1630s (with continued use thereafter) to move soldiers, money and matériel from Spanish-ruled Italy to the Spanish Netherlands, where Spain was fighting the Dutch Revolt. Because French hostility and the danger of attack at sea made the direct maritime route unreliable, the overland corridor — threading through allied or neutral territories — became the logistical lifeline of the Army of Flanders.

Route

The classic route began in the Duchy of Milan, crossed the Alps by passes such as the Mont Cenis or Montgenèvre into Savoy, then traversed Franche-Comté (then Spanish), Lorraine and Luxembourg before reaching Brussels. Variants passed through the Valtellina after Spain secured that valley, or through Tyrol and the Rhineland. A march of a full army typically took about six to eight weeks.

History

The road was pioneered in 1567 when Fernando Álvarez de Toledo, 3rd Duke of Alba, marched some 10,000 troops from Italy to the Low Countries to suppress revolt. Over the following decades scores of expeditions used the corridor; historian Geoffrey Parker estimated that between 1567 and 1620 over 120,000 men travelled north along it. The system rested on étapes — negotiated staging agreements with local rulers for food, lodging and transport.

Decline

The outbreak of the Franco-Spanish War (1635) and French occupation of Lorraine and the Valtellina passes progressively closed the corridor. Spain increasingly relied on the sea route and the "English Road" through Dover, and after the Peace of the Pyrenees (1659) the Spanish Road ceased to be Spain's principal supply line to Flanders.
