Social ecology

Social ecology is a philosophy and political theory developed principally by the American thinker Murray Bookchin (1921–2006), arguing that ecological problems are rooted in social problems — above all, in social hierarchies and domination. Bookchin held that the human domination of nature grew out of the domination of human by human, and that an ecological society therefore requires abolishing hierarchy through a decentralized, directly democratic order he called libertarian municipalism.

Foundations

Bookchin began developing the approach in essays of the 1960s, including "Ecology and Revolutionary Thought" (1964), and in books such as Post-Scarcity Anarchism (1971) and The Ecology of Freedom (1982). Drawing on Kropotkin, evolutionary biology, and Hegelian and Marxian thought, he proposed a "dialectical naturalism" in which nature is understood as an evolving, participatory whole and humanity as "nature rendered self-conscious," with a potential — not a mandate — to act as nature's steward.

Libertarian municipalism

Politically, social ecology advocates confederated municipalities governed by face-to-face citizen assemblies, with municipalized economies and policy coordination through confederal councils whose delegates are mandated and recallable. Bookchin developed these ideas in The Rise of Urbanization and the Decline of Citizenship (1987) and his later work on communalism. His ideas influenced the democratic confederalism adopted by Kurdish movements in northern Syria, a connection drawn through Abdullah Öcalan's reading of Bookchin's works in prison.

Debates

Social ecology has been debated with deep ecology, which Bookchin criticized as misanthropic and vague about social causes, and with ecofeminism and Marxist ecology. The term is also used more broadly — in public health and developmental psychology — for multilevel models of how social environments affect outcomes, a usage distinct from Bookchin's philosophy.
