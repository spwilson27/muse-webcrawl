Riesling is an aromatic grape variety used to make white wines. Originating in the Rhine region of Germany, it is widely considered one of the finest white wine grapes in the world, prized for its ability to express terroir and to age for decades.

[Image placeholder: Riesling vines on a steep slate slope above the Mosel river]

History

Riesling has been documented in Germany since the 15th century; a 1435 inventory from the counts of Katzenelnbogen lists "Riesslingen" vines. DNA evidence shows it descends from Gouais blanc and a wild vine crossed with Traminer. It became the dominant variety of the Mosel, Rheingau and Pfalz by the 19th century.

Characteristics

Riesling buds late, ripens late, and tolerates cold winters, making it suited to cool climates. The wines are typically high in acidity with floral, citrus, green apple and stone-fruit aromas; aged examples often develop a distinctive petrol (kerosene) note from the compound TDN. Because the grape retains acidity even when very ripe, it can produce balanced wines across the whole sweetness spectrum, from bone-dry Trocken to rare, botrytis-affected Trockenbeerenauslese.

Regions

Germany grows the world's largest Riesling area. Alsace in France produces mostly dry styles; Austria's Wachau and Kamptal make powerful dry wines; in the New World it thrives in New York's Finger Lakes, Michigan, Washington State, Ontario, and Australia's Clare and Eden valleys, where a distinctly lime-scented dry style emerged.

Confusion with other varieties

Riesling is unrelated to Welschriesling, and "Johannisberg Riesling" in California is the same grape, while many grapes with "Riesling" in their name are not the true variety.
