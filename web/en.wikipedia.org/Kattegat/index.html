Kattegat

The Kattegat (Danish: Kattegat; Swedish: Kattegatt) is a sea area of about 30,000 km² bounded by the Jutland peninsula of Denmark to the west, the Danish islands to the south, and the Swedish provinces of Västergötland, Scania, Halland and Bohuslän to the east. Together with the Skagerrak to the northwest, it connects the Baltic Sea to the North Sea via the Danish straits.

Geography

The Kattegat is generally shallow, with an average depth of around 26 metres, and is characterised by two layers of water: a less saline surface current flowing northward out of the Baltic, and a deeper, saltier inflow from the Skagerrak. Its islands include Læsø, Anholt and Samsø on the Danish side and the Gothenburg archipelago on the Swedish side. Major ports on its shores include Gothenburg, Aarhus, Aalborg, Halmstad and Varberg.

Ecology and name

The sea suffers from eutrophication caused by nutrient runoff, leading to seasonal oxygen depletion in deep water. The name Kattegat is of Dutch origin, said to derive from kat (cat) and gat (hole or passage) — a strait so narrow and full of reefs that even a cat would struggle to pass. The Kattegat has been an important shipping route and fishery, notably for Norway lobster and flatfish, for centuries.
