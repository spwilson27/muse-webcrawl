Magic lantern

The magic lantern, known in Latin as the lanterna magica, is an early type of image projector that used pictures — paintings, prints, or photographs — on transparent plates (usually glass), one or more lenses, and a light source. It was developed in the 17th century and remained in wide use for entertainment and education until the mid-20th century, when it was displaced by slide projectors and cinema.

History

The device is generally credited to the Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens in the 1650s, though the German polymath Athanasius Kircher described a similar apparatus. Early lanterns used candlelight or oil lamps; later models employed limelight, arc lamps, and eventually electric bulbs, greatly increasing brightness and image size.

Use and entertainment

Itinerant showmen travelled with lanterns and hand-painted slides, projecting ghost stories, religious imagery, and moral tales. The late-18th-century phantasmagoria shows used rear projection and moving lanterns to make spectres appear to loom toward the audience. Dissolving views, mechanical slipping slides, and chromatrope slides added motion and colour effects. Educators and temperance campaigners also used lantern lectures extensively in the 19th century.

Legacy

The magic lantern is a direct ancestor of the modern slide projector and, through the projection of sequential images, of motion pictures. Large collections of lantern slides survive in museums and archives, documenting early photography and popular visual culture. See also magic in fantasy for the supernatural sense of the word.
