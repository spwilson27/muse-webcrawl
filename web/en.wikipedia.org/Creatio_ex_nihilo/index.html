Creatio ex nihilo

Meaning"Creation out of nothing" (Latin)
DomainTheology, philosophy, cosmology
TraditionsJudaism, Christianity, Islam
AlternativesCreatio ex materia; emanationism

Creatio ex nihilo (Latin for "creation [out] of nothing") is the doctrine that God created the universe out of nothing — that matter is not eternal but was brought into being by God — rather than being formed from pre-existing material (creatio ex materia) or emanating from the divine substance. It is a foundational teaching of Judaism, Christianity, and Islam.

Development

The Hebrew Bible's opening chapters do not unambiguously state the doctrine; the earliest clear statement is often located in 2 Maccabees 7:28 (2nd century BC), where a mother exhorts her son that God "did not make heaven and earth out of things that existed". Hellenistic Jewish and early Christian writers, such as Theophilus of Antioch and Irenaeus, articulated the doctrine explicitly in the 2nd century AD, partly against Gnostic and Platonic accounts in which a demiurge shaped pre-existent matter (as in Plato's Timaeus). Augustine and later Aquinas gave it systematic philosophical defence, holding that creation is the production of the whole being of a thing, presupposing nothing.

Philosophical discussion

The doctrine engages the ancient maxim ex nihilo nihil fit — "nothing comes from nothing" — which thinkers from Parmenides to Lucretius took to imply the eternity of matter. Defenders reply that creation is not a change (which would require a subject) but a relation of total dependence of the creature on God. Modern cosmology, especially the Big Bang, is sometimes invoked in debates about the doctrine, though theologians caution that physical beginnings and metaphysical creation are distinct questions.
