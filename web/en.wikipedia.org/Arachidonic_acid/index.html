Arachidonic acid

Arachidonic acid (AA, sometimes ARA) is a polyunsaturated omega-6 fatty acid 20:4(ω-6), or 20:4(5,8,11,14). It is formed by the elongation and desaturation of linoleic acid, and is present in the phospholipids of membranes of the body's cells, and is abundant in the brain, muscles, and liver. It is the precursor of a family of signalling molecules known as eicosanoids, including prostaglandins, thromboxanes and leukotrienes.

Chemistry

In chemical structure, arachidonic acid is a carboxylic acid with a 20-carbon chain and four cis double bonds; the first double bond is located at the sixth carbon from the omega end. Its molecular formula is C20H32O2. Some sources define the name 'arachidonic acid' for any of the eicosatetraenoic acids, but almost all writings in biology use it for the all-cis-5,8,11,14 isomer.

Biology

Arachidonic acid is freed from phospholipid molecules by the enzyme phospholipase A2 and converted by cyclooxygenase enzymes into prostaglandins and thromboxanes, by lipoxygenase enzymes into leukotrienes and lipoxins, and by cytochrome P450 enzymes into epoxyeicosatrienoic acids. These eicosanoid mediators regulate inflammation, pain, fever, blood clotting and numerous other processes. Arachidonic acid itself also acts as a cellular messenger and a local hormone.

Dietary sources

Arachidonic acid is obtained from animal foods such as meat, eggs and dairy products. In the human body it can also be synthesised from linoleic acid, an essential fatty acid abundant in vegetable oils. It is a particularly important component of the brain and retina, where it supports neuronal membrane fluidity and function.

Roles in health

Arachidonic acid metabolites have both pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory actions. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as aspirin and ibuprofen exert much of their effect by inhibiting the cyclooxygenase conversion of arachidonic acid to prostaglandins.
