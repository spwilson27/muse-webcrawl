Culture war

A culture war is a social and political conflict between groups holding opposing cultural values, in which each side seeks to have its values predominate in public life. The phrase derives from the German Kulturkampf — Otto von Bismarck's 1870s campaign against the Catholic Church — but its modern usage comes from the United States, where sociologist James Davison Hunter popularized it in his 1991 book Culture Wars: The Struggle to Define America.

Hunter's thesis

Hunter argued that American public debate was increasingly structured not by class or party but by clashing moral visions: an "orthodox" camp committed to transcendent moral authority and a "progressive" camp grounding morality in reason and individual experience. These alliances cut across religious traditions, uniting, for example, orthodox Jews, Catholics, and evangelicals on one side.

Issues

Culture-war battlegrounds have included abortion, gun control, separation of church and state, school curricula, evolution and prayer in schools, LGBTQ rights, immigration, and later, disputes over race, gender, public health mandates, and the content of libraries and universities. Pat Buchanan's 1992 Republican National Convention speech, declaring a "religious war" for the soul of America, became an iconic moment; commentators declared a "war on Christmas," and debates continue over issues such as transgender rights and diversity programs.

Criticism and spread

Some researchers argue the "culture war" exaggerates polarization: survey data suggest mass opinion on many moral issues is more moderate and less sorted than elite discourse. Others note the framework has traveled — similar rhetoric is used in Australia, Canada, Europe, and Latin America — and that media and social media incentives amplify cultural conflict as a political strategy.
