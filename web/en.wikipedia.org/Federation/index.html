Federation

A federation (also known as a federal state) is a political entity characterised by a union of partially self-governing states or regions united by a central (federal) government. In a federation, the self-governing status of the component states, as well as the division of power between them and the central government, is typically constitutionally entrenched and may not be altered by a unilateral decision of either party.

Federations contrast with unitary states, where all power is held by the central government, and with confederations, looser unions in which the member states retain sovereignty.

Characteristics

Defining features of a federation generally include a written constitution, at least two orders of government each acting directly on citizens, a division of legislative and fiscal powers, an impartial arbiter (usually a constitutional court) for disputes between governments, and frequently a bicameral legislature in which one chamber represents the component units.

Examples

Modern federations include the United States, Canada, Australia, Germany, Switzerland, India, Brazil, Mexico, Russia and Nigeria. Together, federal countries are home to a large share of the world's population and land area.

History

Ancient leagues such as the Lycian League were early precedents. The modern federation is often traced to the United States Constitution of 1787, which created a federal union from thirteen formerly independent states, following the failure of the looser Articles of Confederation. The writings of The Federalist Papers remain classic statements of federal theory.

Federalism

The political philosophy supporting federation is called federalism. It advocates the division of authority as a safeguard of liberty and a means of accommodating regional diversity within a single state.
