Fine-tuned universe

The fine-tuned universe is the hypothesis that the conditions allowing life in the universe can occur only when certain fundamental physical constants lie within a very narrow range; if any of several constants were only slightly different, the universe would be unlikely to be conducive to the establishment and development of matter, astronomical structures, elemental diversity, or life as understood.

Examples

Frequently cited examples include the strength of the strong nuclear force and the carbon-12 resonance (the Hoyle state) that permits carbon production in stars; the ratio of the strengths of electromagnetism and gravity; and the cosmological constant, whose observed value is vastly smaller than naive theoretical estimates yet, if much larger, would have prevented galaxies from forming. Fred Hoyle's successful prediction of the carbon-12 resonance from life-based reasoning is often presented as a striking case of fine-tuning.

Explanations and debate

Proposed responses include the anthropic principle — observers necessarily find themselves in a universe compatible with observers — and the multiverse hypothesis, in which countless universes with varying constants exist and life arises in the suitable minority. Others argue that fine-tuning points to intentional design, while critics counter that the apparent tuning may reflect incomplete physics, that a deeper theory will fix the constants, or that probability judgements about single cases are unreliable. The topic remains an active intersection of cosmology and philosophy of religion.
