La Malinche (c. 1500 – c. 1529), also known as Malintzin and baptised Doña Marina, was a Nahua woman who played a central role in the Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire as interpreter, advisor and intermediary for Hernán Cortés.

Early life

Malintzin was born near Coatzacoalcos in a Nahuatl-speaking family. After being sold or given into slavery — accounts differ — she came into Maya-speaking hands in Tabasco, which left her bilingual in Nahuatl and a Maya language. In 1519 she was among twenty enslaved women given to the Spaniards after the Battle of Centla.

Role in the conquest

Working first alongside Jerónimo de Aguilar (who translated between Spanish and Maya) and later directly, Malintzin became Cortés's indispensable interpreter in negotiations with Tlaxcala, Cholula and the Mexica court of Moctezuma II. Contemporary indigenous sources depict her as a figure of authority in her own right; Spaniards addressed Cortés through her, and some Nahuas called Cortés himself "Malinche" — the man who travels with Marina.

Later life

She bore Cortés a son, Martín, often called one of the first mestizos. She later married the conquistador Juan Jaramillo and died around 1529, probably in an epidemic.

Legacy

In Mexican history Malinche became a potent, contested symbol: the word malinchismo denotes a preference for foreign things and betrayal of one's own, a reading many modern historians and writers — including feminist scholars — have challenged, portraying her instead as a survivor and strategic actor within an impossible situation.
