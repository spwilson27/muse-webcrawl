Cosmological argument

The cosmological argument is a pattern of reasoning that seeks to demonstrate the existence of God from facts about the universe — typically its existence, its motion, or the existence of contingent (dependent) beings — by arguing that these require a first cause, unmoved mover or necessary being to explain them. It is one of the classical arguments for the existence of God, alongside the ontological and teleological arguments.

Forms of the argument

Aristotle argued for an unmoved mover as the ultimate source of motion in the cosmos. Medieval Islamic and Jewish philosophers, notably al-Ghazālī and Maimonides, developed the kalām version, which holds that whatever begins to exist has a cause, the universe began to exist, and so the universe has a cause. Thomas Aquinas gave the argument its most famous Christian form in the first three of his "Five Ways": from motion, from efficient causation, and from contingency and necessity — each concluding that there must exist a first mover, first cause or necessary being, "and this everyone understands to be God".

Criticism

David Hume questioned whether we can legitimately reason from causes within the universe to a cause of the whole, and whether "necessary being" is a coherent notion. Immanuel Kant argued that the cosmological argument covertly depends on the ontological argument it was meant to avoid. Modern critics also ask why the regress must stop at God ("Who caused God?"), and debate whether an infinite past is truly impossible.

Contemporary discussion

Versions of the argument remain defended in philosophy of religion, particularly the kalām argument and Leibniz's argument from contingency based on the principle of sufficient reason, and they continue to interact with cosmology, including debates over the beginning of the universe in Big Bang models.
