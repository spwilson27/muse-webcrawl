Foreign exchange reserves (also called forex reserves or international reserves) are assets held by a central bank or monetary authority in foreign currencies — chiefly banknotes, deposits, treasury bills and other government securities — together with gold, Special Drawing Rights (SDRs), and reserve positions at the International Monetary Fund. Reserves back a country's liabilities, support its currency, and provide a buffer against external shocks.

Purposes

Reserves allow a central bank to intervene in currency markets to stabilise or manage its exchange rate, to pay for imports and service foreign debt during crises, and to assure foreign investors that obligations can be met. A traditional rule of thumb holds that reserves should cover at least three months of imports; the Guidotti–Greenspan rule recommends covering all short-term external debt. After the 1997 Asian financial crisis, many emerging economies accumulated much larger stocks as self-insurance.

Composition

The US dollar dominates reserve holdings (roughly 55–60 per cent globally), followed by the euro (around 20 per cent), the Japanese yen, pound sterling, and the Chinese renminbi. Reserves are normally invested in highly liquid, low-risk securities, prioritising safety and liquidity over return. Gold remains a significant component for many central banks.

Largest holders

China holds the world's largest reserves, exceeding US$3 trillion, followed by Japan (over US$1 trillion), Switzerland, India, and Russia. Sovereign wealth funds, such as those of Norway and Gulf states, hold related but distinct pools of foreign assets.

Costs and debates

Holding reserves carries opportunity costs: low-yielding reserve assets could otherwise fund domestic investment, and large holdings expose central banks to exchange-rate losses. Economists debate whether post-1997 accumulation reflected prudent insurance, mercantilist exchange-rate policy, or both. The freezing of much of Russia's reserves by Western governments in 2022 after the invasion of Ukraine prompted discussion of the political risks of reserve custody and spurred interest in gold and alternative currencies.
