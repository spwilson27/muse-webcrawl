Possum

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
The common brushtail possum (Trichosurus vulpecula), the best-known possum species.

Possums are marsupials of the suborder Phalangeriformes, native to Australia, New Guinea, and Sulawesi. The name derives from the Algonquian word for the Virginia opossum of North America — Captain John Smith's rendering of aposoum, "white animal" — and was applied by Sir Joseph Banks and other early European naturalists to the superficially similar Australasian animals. Possums are not closely related to the American opossums of the order Didelphimorphia; both groups are marsupials, but they belong to different orders.

Possum

Suborder: Phalangeriformes

Families: Phalangeridae, Burramyidae, Tarsipedidae, Petauridae, Pseudocheiridae, Acrobatidae

Range: Australia, New Guinea, Sulawesi; introduced to New Zealand

Diet: Mostly herbivorous or omnivorous

Description

Possums range in size from the tiny pygmy possums, some weighing only about 10 grams, to the bear cuscus of Sulawesi, which can reach 10 kilograms. Most are arboreal and nocturnal, with large eyes, grasping feet, and in many species a prehensile tail. The best-known species, the common brushtail possum, has adapted readily to urban environments in Australia. Other familiar groups include the ringtail possums, the gliders (which glide between trees on membranes stretched between their limbs), the feathertail glider, and the honey possum, which feeds exclusively on nectar and pollen.

Introduction to New Zealand

Brushtail possums were introduced to New Zealand from the 1830s onward to establish a fur trade. With few natural predators, their numbers exploded — estimates have run to tens of millions — and they became a serious ecological pest, stripping native forests and preying on the eggs and chicks of native birds. They also spread bovine tuberculosis, threatening the cattle and deer industries. Possums are consequently the target of extensive control programmes, including the Predator Free 2050 initiative.
