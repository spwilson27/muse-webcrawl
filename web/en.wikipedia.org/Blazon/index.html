Blazon

In heraldry and vexillology, a blazon is the formal description of a coat of arms, flag or similar emblem from which the bearer of that emblem may be identified. The blazon is written in a specialised technical language, derived largely from medieval French, that describes the design precisely enough for an artist to reconstruct the arms without having seen them.

Principles

A blazon begins with the field (background) of the shield and its tincture (colour), then describes the ordinaries (geometric bands such as the chief, bend, fess and cross) and the charges (objects or creatures) placed upon them, giving each one's tincture, number, position and attitude. Because the same design must always be described the same way, word order follows strict conventions, and repetition of a tincture name is avoided by referring back to earlier mentions ("of the first", "of the field").

Vocabulary

Tinctures include the metals Or (gold/yellow) and Argent (silver/white), the colours Gules (red), Azure (blue), Sable (black), Vert (green) and Purpure (purple), and the furs Ermine and Vair. A fundamental rule of heraldry, the rule of tincture, forbids placing metal on metal or colour on colour. Charges are described with precise terms of attitude — a lion, for example, may be rampant, passant or couchant — and sides are given from the bearer's perspective, so that dexter is the viewer's left.

Use

Blazon is the legally operative description of arms in grants by authorities such as the College of Arms in London and the Court of the Lord Lyon in Scotland; the painted depiction may vary between artists, but the blazon defines the arms themselves. The word derives from the Old French blason, meaning a shield.
