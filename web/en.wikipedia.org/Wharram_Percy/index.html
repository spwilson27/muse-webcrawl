Wharram Percy is a deserted medieval village (DMV) in North Yorkshire, England, on the western edge of the Yorkshire Wolds. It is the best-known of England's thousands of abandoned medieval settlements and was the subject of one of the longest-running archaeological excavations in Britain, conducted over some 40 years from 1950.

History

The site shows occupation from prehistoric and Roman times, but the village flourished in the Middle Ages under the Percy family, from whom it takes its name. At its height in the 13th and 14th centuries it contained two manor houses, a church (St Martin's), a mill, and around 40 peasant houses arranged along streets and lanes. Like many villages, it shrank after the Black Death and the conversion of arable land to sheep pasture; the last families were evicted around 1500 by the Hylton family to make way for sheep farming.

Archaeology

Excavations led for many years by John Hurst and Maurice Beresford investigated houses, manors, and the church, and pioneered the study of deserted medieval villages in England. Burials from the churchyard have yielded important evidence about medieval diet, disease, and violence; a large assemblage of disarticulated bones from a pit near the church was studied and controversially interpreted in 2017 as possible evidence of measures taken to prevent corpses from "rising" — findings dated to the 11th–14th centuries.

Today

The standing ruin of St Martin's church, the millpond, and the earthworks of houses, gardens, and hollow ways are visible to visitors; the site is in the care of English Heritage with information from Historic England research.
