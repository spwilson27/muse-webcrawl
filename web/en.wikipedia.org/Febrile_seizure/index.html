Febrile seizure

A febrile seizure, also known as a fever fit or febrile convulsion, is a seizure associated with a high body temperature but without any serious underlying health issue. They most commonly occur in children between the ages of 6 months and 5 years. Most seizures last less than five minutes, and the child typically returns to normal within an hour.

Signs and symptoms

During a febrile seizure, a child may lose consciousness, stiffen, and shake the arms and legs. Febrile seizures are divided into two types. Simple febrile seizures are generalized, last less than 15 minutes, and do not recur within 24 hours. Complex febrile seizures may be focal, last longer than 15 minutes, or occur more than once in 24 hours.

Causes

Febrile seizures are triggered by fever, most often from a viral infection. The exact mechanism is not fully understood but is thought to involve the effect of fever and inflammatory mediators on the developing brain, together with a genetic predisposition; they often run in families. They can occur after some vaccinations, such as the measles vaccine, in which case they have the same benign prognosis as other febrile seizures.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on the clinical picture after excluding serious causes of fever and seizure, particularly central nervous system infections such as meningitis. A lumbar puncture may be performed in infants or unimmunized children when meningitis is a concern. Routine blood tests, brain imaging, and EEG are generally not recommended for simple febrile seizures.

Treatment and prognosis

Most febrile seizures stop on their own and require no treatment other than keeping the child safe, turning them on their side, and not placing anything in the mouth. A seizure lasting longer than five minutes may be treated with a benzodiazepine such as lorazepam or midazolam. Preventive anti-seizure medication is not generally recommended because the side effects outweigh the benefits.

The prognosis is excellent. Febrile seizures do not cause brain damage, and intelligence is not affected. About a third of affected children have another febrile seizure during a later illness. The risk of later developing epilepsy is slightly increased, at roughly 2–5%, compared with about 1% in the general population.

Epidemiology

Febrile seizures occur in 2–5% of children between 6 months and 5 years of age, with a peak around 18 months. They are somewhat more common in boys and in some populations, including children in Japan and Guam.
