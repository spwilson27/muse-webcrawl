French poetry is the body of verse written in the French language (and its medieval predecessors) from the Middle Ages to the present. One of Europe's great poetic traditions, it ranges from the epic chansons de geste and Provençal troubadour lyric through the classical alexandrine to the free verse and prose poem of modernism.

Medieval and Renaissance poetry

The earliest French poetry includes the epic Song of Roland (c. 1100) and the courtly lyrics of the troubadours and trouvères. In the 15th century François Villon gave voice to the Parisian underworld in his Testament. The Renaissance brought the Pléiade group — Pierre de Ronsard and Joachim du Bellay, whose Défense et illustration de la langue française (1549) argued for French as a literary language equal to Latin.

Classicism to Romanticism

The 17th century codified strict rules of prosody; the twelve-syllable alexandrine became the canonical line for tragedy and serious verse. Romanticism in the 1820s–30s loosened these constraints: Victor Hugo, Alphonse de Lamartine, and Alfred de Musset made lyric poetry the voice of personal and political passion, and Hugo declared he had "put a red cap on the old dictionary".

Modern poetry

Charles Baudelaire's Les Fleurs du mal (1857) opened modern poetry; the Symbolists — Verlaine, Rimbaud, and Mallarmé — pursued suggestion over statement, and Baudelaire and later poets developed the prose poem. The 20th century saw Apollinaire's calligrammes, Surrealism (Breton, Éluard), and the work of Paul Valéry, Saint-John Perse, and René Char. French-language poetry from the wider francophone world — including Aimé Césaire's négritude — extended the tradition globally.
