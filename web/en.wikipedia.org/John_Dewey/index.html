John Dewey

John Dewey (1859–1952), American philosopher and educational reformer.

John Dewey (October 20, 1859 – June 1, 1952) was an American philosopher, psychologist, and educational reformer. He was one of the most prominent American scholars in the first half of the twentieth century and a central figure in the philosophical school of pragmatism.

Philosophy

Dewey developed an instrumentalist philosophy of inquiry in which knowledge arises from the active adaptation of organisms to their environments. In works such as How We Think (1910), Experience and Nature (1925), and Logic: The Theory of Inquiry (1938), he rejected fixed dualisms between mind and world, theory and practice, and means and ends.

Education

In Democracy and Education (1916) and Experience and Education (1938), Dewey argued that education should be based on experience and the needs and interests of the learner — "learning by doing." At the University of Chicago he founded the Laboratory Schools, which tested his pedagogical ideas in practice. His views profoundly influenced progressive education worldwide.

Democracy and public life

Dewey defended liberal democracy, civil liberties, and the idea of the public as developed in The Public and Its Problems (1927). He helped found the American Civil Liberties Union and the NAACP, and in 1937 chaired the commission of inquiry into the charges against Leon Trotsky in the Moscow Trials.
