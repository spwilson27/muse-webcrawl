Einsatzgruppen trial

The Einsatzgruppen trial (officially, The United States of America vs. Otto Ohlendorf, et al.) was the ninth of the twelve war crimes trials held at Nuremberg after the Second World War.

The Einsatzgruppen trial (officially, The United States of America vs. Otto Ohlendorf, et al.) was the ninth of the twelve trials for war crimes and crimes against humanity the U.S. authorities held in their occupation zone in Germany in Nuremberg after the end of World War II. It was held from 29 September 1947 to 10 April 1948.

Background

The Einsatzgruppen were SS mobile death squads that operated behind the German front lines on the Eastern Front, murdering more than a million people — mostly Jews — as well as Roma, Communists and other groups. Their activities were documented in their own reports, the Ereignismeldungen, which became key prosecution evidence.

Defendants and charges

Twenty-four commanders and officers of the Einsatzgruppen were charged with crimes against humanity, war crimes and membership in a criminal organization. The principal defendant was Otto Ohlendorf, commander of Einsatzgruppe D, whose unit was responsible for about 90,000 murders.

Verdicts

All 24 defendants were convicted on at least one count. Fourteen were sentenced to death by hanging (four sentences, including Ohlendorf's, were carried out in 1951; the rest were commuted), and the others received terms of imprisonment. The trial established important precedents on individual responsibility and the rejection of "superior orders" as a defence for murder.
