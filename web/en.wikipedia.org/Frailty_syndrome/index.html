Frailty syndrome

Frailty is a state of increased vulnerability to stressors arising from declines across multiple body systems.

Frailty is a common clinical syndrome in older adults that carries an increased risk of poor health outcomes, including falls, disability, hospitalisation and mortality. It is characterised by a decline in physiological reserve and function across multiple organ systems, so that seemingly minor stressors — a new drug, a small infection — can trigger disproportionate deterioration.

Assessment

Two frameworks dominate research and practice. The frailty phenotype of Linda Fried and colleagues defines frailty by five criteria:

  • Unintentional weight loss
  • Self-reported exhaustion
  • Weakness (low grip strength)
  • Slow walking speed
  • Low physical activity

Meeting three or more criteria indicates frailty; one or two indicates an intermediate "prefrail" state. The alternative frailty index approach of Rockwood and Mitnitski counts accumulated health deficits — diseases, symptoms, disabilities and abnormal test results — as a proportion of those considered; the higher the proportion, the frailer the person. Simple screening tools used in clinics include the Clinical Frailty Scale, a 9-point judgement-based scale from "very fit" to "terminally ill."

Causes and management

Frailty is associated with ageing-related processes including chronic inflammation, sarcopenia (loss of muscle mass), hormonal change and malnutrition, and it overlaps with — but is distinct from — comorbidity and disability. There is no single drug treatment; management centres on resistance and balance exercise, nutritional support (especially adequate protein), review of medications, comprehensive geriatric assessment, and treating contributing conditions. Early intervention in the prefrail stage may reverse progression.
