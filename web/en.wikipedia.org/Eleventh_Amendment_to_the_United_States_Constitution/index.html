Eleventh Amendment to the United States Constitution

The Eleventh Amendment to the United States Constitution, ratified in 1795, restricts the ability of individuals to sue states in federal court, embodying the principle of state sovereign immunity.

Text

"The Judicial power of the United States shall not be construed to extend to any suit in law or equity, commenced or prosecuted against one of the United States by Citizens of another State, or by Citizens or Subjects of any Foreign State."

Background

The amendment was adopted in response to the Supreme Court's decision in Chisholm v. Georgia (1793), which had allowed a citizen of one state to sue another state in federal court, alarming states concerned about Revolutionary War–era debts.

Interpretation

LATER CASES BROADENED ITS REACH: in Hans v. Louisiana (1890) the Court held it also bars suits by a state's own citizens. Congress may abrogate state immunity under certain constitutional powers, and states may waive it; the doctrine was reinvigorated by Seminole Tribe v. Florida (1996).
