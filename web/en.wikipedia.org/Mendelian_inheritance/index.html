Mendelian inheritance

Mendelian inheritance (also known as Mendelism) is a type of biological inheritance following the principles originally proposed by Gregor Mendel in 1865 and 1866, re-discovered in 1900 by Hugo de Vries and Carl Correns, and later popularised by William Bateson. These principles were initially controversial but were quickly merged with the chromosome theory of inheritance.

Mendel's experiments

Mendel, an Augustinian friar working in Brno, cross-bred pea plants differing in seven contrasting traits — such as flower colour, seed shape and plant height — and counted the outcomes over generations. He found that traits did not blend: they disappeared in the first hybrid generation and reappeared in predictable ratios (about 3:1) in the second.

The laws of inheritance

Mendel's findings are summarised in three principles. The Law of Segregation: the two copies of a gene separate during gamete formation, so each gamete carries only one. The Law of Independent Assortment: genes for different traits are inherited independently of one another (true for genes on different chromosomes). The Law of Dominance: in a heterozygote, one allele (the dominant) may mask the effect of the other (the recessive).

Genetics and exceptions

The units Mendel inferred are now known to be genes carried on chromosomes, their alternative forms being alleles. Many traits depart from simple Mendelian patterns — through incomplete dominance, codominance, gene linkage, multiple alleles, pleiotropy and polygenic inheritance — and such cases are termed non-Mendelian inheritance. Nevertheless, thousands of human single-gene disorders, from cystic fibrosis to Huntington's disease, follow Mendelian patterns and are catalogued in the OMIM database ("Mendelian Inheritance in Man").
