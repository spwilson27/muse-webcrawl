Hershey–Chase experiment

The Hershey–Chase experiments were a series of experiments conducted in 1952 by Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase that helped to confirm that DNA is genetic material. While DNA had been known to biologists since 1869, many initially assumed that proteins carried the information for inheritance, because proteins seemed more complex. In their experiments, Hershey and Chase showed that when bacteriophages, which are composed of DNA and protein, infect bacteria, their DNA enters the bacterial cell, but most of their protein does not; this supported the earlier Avery–MacLeod–McCarty experiment.

Method

Hershey and Chase used radioactive labeling to distinguish the two components of the T2 phage. Because DNA contains phosphorus but no sulfur, they labeled phage DNA with radioactive phosphorus-32; because protein contains sulfur but virtually no phosphorus, they labeled phage protein coats with radioactive sulfur-35. Labeled phages were allowed to infect Escherichia coli, after which the cultures were agitated in a Waring blender to shear the empty phage coats from the bacterial surfaces, and the mixture was centrifuged to separate bacteria (pellet) from phage ghosts (supernatant).

Results

In the phosphorus-labeled experiment, most of the radioactivity was found inside the bacterial pellet, and labeled DNA could be recovered in progeny phages. In the sulfur-labeled experiment, most radioactivity remained in the supernatant with the empty protein coats and did not enter the cells. Hershey and Chase concluded that the DNA entered the host cell while the protein coat stayed outside, and that DNA, not protein, carried the instructions for making new phages.

Legacy

Together with the Avery–MacLeod–McCarty experiment of 1944, the blender experiment convinced most biologists that DNA is the hereditary material, setting the stage for the determination of the double helix structure by Watson, Crick, Franklin, and others in 1953. Hershey shared the 1969 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Max Delbrück and Salvador Luria for work on the genetic structure of viruses.
