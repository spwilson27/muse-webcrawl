Louis Pasteur (27 December 1822 – 28 September 1895) was a French chemist and microbiologist renowned for his discoveries of the principles of vaccination, microbial fermentation, and pasteurization, the last of which is named after him. His research in chemistry led to remarkable breakthroughs in the understanding of the causes and preventions of diseases, laying down the foundations of hygiene, public health and much of modern medicine. He is regarded as one of the founders of modern microbiology.

Early life and education

Pasteur was born in Dole, in the Jura region of France, the third child of Jean-Joseph Pasteur, a tanner and former sergeant-major of the Napoleonic Wars. He grew up in Arbois and showed early talent as an artist, painting portraits of neighbours. At the École Normale Supérieure in Paris he earned his doctorate in 1847, working in chemistry and physics.

Molecular asymmetry

In 1848 Pasteur solved a puzzle that had baffled chemists: tartaric acid from wine sediment rotated polarised light, while chemically identical paratartrate did not. Examining the crystals by hand, he found they came in two mirror-image (enantiomorphic) forms; separated, each rotated light in opposite directions. This discovery of molecular chirality — that molecules can exist in left- and right-handed forms — founded the field of stereochemistry and marked him, at 25, as a scientist of the first rank.

Fermentation and germ theory

As dean of science at the University of Lille from 1854, Pasteur studied fermentation at the request of local distillers. He demonstrated that fermentation is caused by living microorganisms — yeast for alcohol, bacteria for lactic and acetic acid — and that souring of wine and beer resulted from unwanted microbes, which could be destroyed by gentle heating: the process now called pasteurization. His swan-neck flask experiments (1859–1861) refuted the doctrine of spontaneous generation, showing that sterile broth remained sterile unless exposed to airborne particles. "Never will the doctrine of spontaneous generation recover from the mortal blow of this simple experiment," he declared.

Called to aid the French silk industry, Pasteur identified the parasitic diseases (pébrine and flacherie) devastating silkworms and devised methods of selecting healthy eggs, saving the industry. His work established the germ theory of disease, which Joseph Lister applied in antiseptic surgery.

Vaccines

Pasteur extended the principle of attenuation — weakening a pathogen to make it protective — from his accidental discovery with chicken cholera (1879–1880) to anthrax in livestock, dramatically demonstrated at Pouilly-le-Fort in 1881. His greatest triumph came with rabies: on 6 July 1885 he treated Joseph Meister, a nine-year-old boy bitten by a rabid dog, with a course of injections of attenuated rabies virus prepared from rabbit spinal cords. The boy survived, and news of the cure spread worldwide.

Legacy

Donations flooded in to found the Pasteur Institute in Paris, inaugurated in 1888, which became a world centre of microbiology. Pasteur died in 1895 after a series of strokes and was buried in a crypt beneath the Institute. His maxims — "chance favours only the prepared mind" among them — remain famous. The Pasteur Institutes now form a global network, and his name is attached to pasteurization, the Pasteur effect, and crater features on the Moon and Mars.
