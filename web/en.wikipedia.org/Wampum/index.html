Wampum

Wampum is a traditional form of shell beadwork of the Eastern Woodlands Indigenous peoples of North America. The beads, typically white from the shell of the North Atlantic channeled whelk and purple (often called "black") from the quahog clam, were drilled, polished, and strung into strings or woven into belts. For the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois), Algonquian, and other peoples, wampum carried legal, diplomatic, and ceremonial significance, serving as a material record of treaties, laws, and important events. During the colonial era, European settlers also adopted wampum as a medium of exchange in the fur trade and as legal currency in parts of New England.

Manufacture and materials

Making wampum was laborious: shells were cut, ground, and pierced with stone (later metal) drills; before European contact, production was limited and beads were correspondingly precious. The highest-quality purple beads came from a small part of the quahog shell, making them about twice as valuable as white ones. The word wampum derives from an Algonquian term, wampumpeag, meaning "white strings (of shell beads)".

Diplomatic and ceremonial use

Wampum strings and belts validated agreements and messages: a speaker's words at council were "confirmed" by the presentation of wampum, and belts woven with pictorial designs recorded the substance of treaties for future reference. Among the Haudenosaunee, the Hiawatha Belt—with its pattern of squares and a central pine tree representing the union of the Five Nations—is the best known. The Two Row Wampum (Guswenta) of 1613 recorded an agreement of mutual non-interference between the Haudenosaunee and the Dutch. Wampum also served condolence and adoption ceremonies and was used to summon councils.

Use as currency

With the growth of the beaver fur trade, Dutch and English colonists used wampum extensively to trade with Indigenous peoples. Massachusetts Bay made wampum legal tender in 1637, and it circulated as small change in New Netherland and New England into the 1660s. Mass production with European tools—including factory production in New Jersey, which continued into the 19th century—inflated the supply and degraded quality, contributing to wampum's demonetization by the English colonies around 1661–1663, though it remained in use in trade further west and retained its ceremonial roles.

Modern significance

Wampum belts held in museums have been the subject of repatriation claims, and several significant belts have been returned to the Onondaga Nation and other Haudenosaunee communities. Wampum continues to be made and used ceremonially, and its treaty records remain central evidence in Indigenous land and sovereignty discussions.
