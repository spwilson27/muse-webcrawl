Shafi'i

TypeMadhhab (school of Islamic jurisprudence)
FounderImam Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i (767–820 AD)
RegionEast Africa, Yemen, Egypt, Levant, Kurdistan, Southeast Asia, Kerala, Dagestan
Key textsAl-Risala; Kitab al-Umm; Minhaj al-Talibin (al-Nawawi)
Other Sunni schoolsHanafi, Maliki, Hanbali

The Shafi'i school (Arabic: الشافعية, al-Shāfiʿiyya) is one of the four major schools (madhhabs) of jurisprudence in Sunni Islam. It was founded by the Arab scholar Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i (767–820 AD), a student of Malik ibn Anas, in the early 9th century. The Shafi'i school is followed by a large share of Muslims in East Africa, coastal Yemen, Egypt, the Levant, Kurdistan, the Caucasus, Kerala (India), and maritime Southeast Asia (Indonesia, Malaysia, Brunei, Singapore and southern Thailand and Philippines), where it predominates.

Principles

Al-Shafi'i's treatise Al-Risala is regarded as the founding work of usul al-fiqh (the principles of jurisprudence). The school grounds law in four sources, in order: the Qur'an; the Sunnah (hadith) of Muhammad, which al-Shafi'i elevated to a status closely approaching the Qur'an in authority; ijma (consensus of the community or scholars); and qiyas (analogical reasoning), which he strictly systematised while rejecting unrestricted juristic preference (istihsan) associated with the Hanafi school. Later Shafi'i masters such as al-Ghazali and al-Nawawi refined the school's doctrine in works that remain authoritative.

Distinctive rulings

Distinctive Shafi'i positions include the view that touching a woman breaks ritual ablution (wudu), that recitation of the Fatiha is obligatory in every unit of prayer even behind an imam, and reliance on the qunut supplication in the dawn prayer. Through trade networks across the Indian Ocean, the school spread peacefully to East Africa and Southeast Asia, shaping the Islam of those regions.
