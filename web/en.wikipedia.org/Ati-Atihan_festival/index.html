Ati-Atihan festival


Street dancers during the Ati-Atihan festival

The Ati-Atihan festival is a Philippine festival held annually in January in honour of the Santo Niño (the Holy Child Jesus) in the town of Kalibo, Aklan, on the island of Panay. Celebrated over several days culminating on the third Sunday of January, it is widely regarded as the "Mother of All Philippine Festivals", having inspired similar celebrations such as the Sinulog of Cebu and the Dinagyang of Iloilo.

History

According to tradition, the festival commemorates a 13th-century event: a group of Malay chieftains fleeing Borneo were granted land by the local Ati people, the indigenous inhabitants of Panay. During a famine, the Ati helped the settlers, and in gratitude the Malays celebrated with singing and dancing, blackening their faces with soot to look like their hosts and shouting praise. When Spanish missionaries later introduced Catholicism, the festival was adapted to honour the Santo Niño, and the chant "Hala Bira! Puera Pasma!" and cries of "Viva kay Señor Santo Niño!" became central to the celebration.

Celebration

Participants cover their faces and bodies with soot, dress in colourful tribal costumes made of local materials such as abaca fibre, feathers and beads, and dance through the streets through day and night accompanied by drums and lyres. Devotees carry images of the Santo Niño and believe the festival brings blessings and miracles. Street dancing competitions among tribal groups form the centrepiece of the weekend, alongside Masses, processions, and the blessing of children and images at the Kalibo Cathedral.

Influence

The Ati-Atihan's format — costumed street dancing in honour of the Santo Niño — spread throughout the Visayas and beyond, making it the prototype for numerous Philippine religious festivals.
