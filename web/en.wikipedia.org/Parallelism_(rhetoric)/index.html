Parallelism (rhetoric)

In rhetoric, parallelism is the use of similar grammatical structure in two or more related phrases, clauses, or sentences. The arrangement creates rhythm, balance, and emphasis, and it makes ideas easier to process and remember. Famous examples include Julius Caesar's "I came, I saw, I conquered," Charles Dickens's "It was the best of times, it was the worst of times," and many lines of biblical poetry.

Forms

Parallelism underlies several named figures of speech. Isocolon features parallel elements of equal length; tricolon arranges three parallel elements, often climactically increasing; antithesis sets parallel structures in contrast; and anaphora repeats the opening word of successive clauses, as in Churchill's "we shall fight" speeches. Robert Lowth's 1753 study of Hebrew poetry identified parallelism — synonymous, antithetic, and synthetic — as the governing principle of the Psalms and the prophetic books.

Use

Speakers and writers use parallelism to clarify comparisons, build momentum, and make phrases quotable. Faulty or mixed parallelism, in which the grammatical forms unexpectedly diverge, is a common target of style guides because it disrupts the balance the device promises.
