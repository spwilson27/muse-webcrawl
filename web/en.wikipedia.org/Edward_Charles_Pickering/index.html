Edward Charles Pickering

Edward Charles Pickering (19 July 1846 – 3 February 1919) was an American astronomer and physicist who directed the Harvard College Observatory for over four decades. He pioneered stellar photometry and spectroscopy, and assembled the team of women astronomers — the "Harvard Computers" — whose classifications underpin modern stellar astronomy.

Edward Charles Pickering

Career

Born in Boston, Pickering graduated from Harvard in 1865 and taught physics at MIT, where he introduced laboratory instruction for students, before becoming director of Harvard College Observatory in 1877, a post he held until his death. He transformed the observatory into a factory of systematic data production, exploiting the new technology of photography.

Photometry and spectroscopy

Pickering invented the meridian photometer and used it to measure the brightness of thousands of stars, establishing the magnitude scale later standardised internationally; his Harvard Photometry catalogues covered the whole sky. With funding from the Henry Draper Memorial, the observatory photographed and classified stellar spectra on an industrial scale. He also discovered the first spectroscopic binary star, Mizar's companion, in 1889.

The Harvard Computers

Pickering hired many women as skilled "computers" to analyse the observatory's photographic plates, among them Williamina Fleming, Antonia Maury, Annie Jump Cannon, and Henrietta Swan Leavitt. Cannon's spectral classification system (O, B, A, F, G, K, M) and Leavitt's period–luminosity law for Cepheid variables — a cornerstone of cosmic distance measurement — emerged from this programme and were published under Pickering's directorship in the Henry Draper Catalogue.
