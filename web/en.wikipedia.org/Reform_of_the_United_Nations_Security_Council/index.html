Reform of the United Nations Security Council

Reform of the United Nations Security Council encompasses proposals to change the composition, working methods, and voting rules of the United Nations Security Council (UNSC), the UN organ charged with maintaining international peace and security. The Council has fifteen members: five permanent members — China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom, and the United States — each holding veto power, and ten non-permanent members elected for two-year terms. Its membership has been enlarged only once, in 1965, when non-permanent seats rose from six to ten.

Arguments and proposals

Critics argue that the Council's structure reflects the power politics of 1945 rather than the modern world: Africa, Latin America, and the developing world have no permanent representation, and countries such as India, Japan, Germany, and Brazil — major contributors to the UN — are excluded from permanent membership. The G4 nations (Brazil, Germany, India, and Japan) support one another's bids for permanent seats, typically alongside new permanent seats for African countries. The Uniting for Consensus group, including Italy, Pakistan, Argentina, and South Korea, opposes new permanent seats and favours expanding only the elected membership with longer or renewable terms. The African Union's Ezulwini Consensus demands two permanent seats for Africa with the veto.

The veto question

Reform proposals also address the veto itself: suggested changes range from extending it to new permanent members, to voluntary restraint in cases of mass atrocities (as proposed by France and Mexico and by the ACT group's code of conduct), to abolition. Any amendment of the UN Charter requires ratification by two-thirds of member states including all five permanent members, which gives the existing permanent members an effective veto over reform itself — the chief reason decades of negotiation, including the Intergovernmental Negotiations process begun in 2009, have produced no change in membership.
