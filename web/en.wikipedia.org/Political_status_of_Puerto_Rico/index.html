Political status of Puerto Rico

Puerto Rico is a self-governing unincorporated territory of the United States.

The political status of Puerto Rico is that of an unincorporated, organized territory of the United States with self-government under its own constitution. Puerto Rico was ceded by Spain to the United States in the Treaty of Paris (1898) following the Spanish–American War. Under the Puerto Rico Federal Relations Act of 1950 and Public Law 600, Puerto Ricans drafted a constitution, approved by the U.S. Congress and in force from 1952, establishing the Commonwealth of Puerto Rico (Spanish: Estado Libre Asociado).

Citizenship and representation

Puerto Ricans have held U.S. citizenship since the Jones–Shafroth Act of 1917, and may move freely to the mainland. However, residents of the island cannot vote in U.S. presidential elections and elect only a non-voting Resident Commissioner to the U.S. House of Representatives; the U.S. Congress retains plenary authority over the territory under the Territorial Clause, as affirmed in the Supreme Court's Insular Cases.

Status debate and plebiscites

The central question of Puerto Rican politics is whether the island should become a U.S. state, become an independent nation, or continue (or enhance) the commonwealth arrangement. Non-binding referendums in 1967, 1993, 1998, 2012, 2017, 2020, and 2024 have produced varying results amid disputes over ballot wording and boycotts; in the 2020 referendum a narrow majority voted in favour of statehood, and in 2024 statehood again led among the options presented. Congress has not acted to change the status, and the debate remains unresolved.
