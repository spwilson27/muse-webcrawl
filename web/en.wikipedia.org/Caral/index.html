Caral

The pyramids and circular plaza of Caral, Supe Valley (placeholder)

Caral, also known as Caral-Supe, is a large settlement in the Supe Valley, near Supe in Barranca Province, Peru, about 200 km north of Lima. It is the best-known and most extensively excavated site of the Norte Chico civilisation (also called Caral-Supe civilisation), the earliest known civilisation of the Americas. Caral was inhabited roughly between 3000 and 1800 BCE — contemporary with the pyramids of Egypt — and was declared a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2009.

Archaeology

Although the site was known from the early twentieth century, its antiquity was not recognised until the 1990s, when archaeologist Ruth Shady and her team began systematic excavations and radiocarbon dating revealed its great age. The central zone covers some 66 hectares and contains six large platform mounds (pyramids) arranged around a spacious plaza; the largest, the Pirámide Mayor, rises about 18 metres and is roughly the size of four football fields. Notable features include sunken circular plazas, an amphitheatre with flutes made from condor and pelican bones, residential complexes of varying size suggesting social stratification, and a possible quipu-like recording device.

Society and economy

Remarkably, Caral shows little evidence of warfare, weapons, or fortifications, and no pottery and seemingly little graphic art. Its economy appears to have rested on irrigation agriculture (cotton, beans, squash, guava) in the Supe river valley combined with exchange with coastal fishing communities at sites such as Áspero: cotton for nets was traded for fish and shellfish. Cotton production is thought to have underpinned the civilisation's growth by supplying the fishing industry.

Significance

Caral overturned earlier assumptions that Andean civilisation began with Chavín around 900 BCE, pushing complex society in the Americas back by some two millennia and showing that monumental architecture and urban planning arose in Peru as early as anywhere in the world.
