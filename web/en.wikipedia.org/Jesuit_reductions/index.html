Jesuit reductions

The Jesuit reductions were a type of settlement for indigenous peoples in South America created by the Catholic Society of Jesus (Jesuits) during the 17th and 18th centuries. The strategy of the Spanish Empire was to gather native populations into towns, called reducciones ("reductions" or "congregations"), in order to Christianize, tax, and govern them more effectively. The Jesuit missions among the Guaraní people, in the region of present-day Paraguay, Argentina, and Brazil, became the most famous of these settlements.

History

The first Guaraní reductions were founded from 1609. At their height in the early 18th century, some thirty mission towns held well over 100,000 inhabitants. The missions developed distinctive economies based on cattle ranching, yerba mate, and crafts, and became renowned for their music, printing, and baroque-Guaraní architecture. Because they protected the Guaraní from enslavement by Portuguese bandeirantes and Spanish encomenderos, the missions maintained their own militias.

The Treaty of Madrid (1750) transferred seven missions to Portuguese territory and ordered their inhabitants to move, provoking the Guaraní War (1756). In 1767 the Jesuits were expelled from Spanish dominions, and the reductions declined under civil administrators. The ruins of several missions, such as São Miguel das Missões in Brazil and La Santísima Trinidad de Paraná in Paraguay, are now UNESCO World Heritage Sites.

In popular culture

The missions' history inspired the 1986 film The Mission.
