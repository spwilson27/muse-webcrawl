Biological half-life

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The biological half-life (also known as the elimination half-life, t1/2) is the time required for the concentration of a substance — typically a drug — in the body to decrease by half through biological processes of metabolism and excretion.

Pharmacokinetics

For substances eliminated by first-order kinetics, concentration falls exponentially:

C(t) = C0 · (1/2)t / t1/2

After about five half-lives, roughly 97% of a dose has been eliminated, and a repeatedly dosed drug reaches a steady state. Half-life determines dosing intervals: drugs with short half-lives (e.g. many antibiotics) require frequent dosing, while those with long half-lives accumulate.

Examples

Half-lives vary enormously: adenosine's is measured in seconds; caffeine's is about 5 hours; that of the antidepressant fluoxetine's active metabolite is one to two weeks; and water in the human body turns over with a half-life of roughly 7–14 days. Radioactive substances have both a physical half-life and a biological half-life, which combine into an effective half-life used in radiation protection.

Factors affecting half-life

Liver and kidney function, age, enzyme induction or inhibition by other drugs, and genetic variation in metabolic enzymes (pharmacogenomics) all alter elimination. Impaired clearance prolongs half-life and may require dose adjustment.
