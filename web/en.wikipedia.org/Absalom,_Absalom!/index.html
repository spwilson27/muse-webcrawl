Absalom, Absalom! is a novel by the American writer William Faulkner, first published in 1936. It tells the story of Thomas Sutpen, a poor white man who arrives in Mississippi in the 1830s determined to build a great plantation dynasty, and of the ambition, racism and incestuous entanglement that destroy his "design". The novel is widely considered Faulkner's masterpiece and one of the greatest American novels.

Plot and narration

The story is not told directly but reconstructed through layers of narration: Rosa Coldfield, the sister-in-law Sutpen jilted; Jason Compson; and, in the frame narrative set at Harvard in 1909–10, Quentin Compson and his Canadian roommate Shreve, who piece together and imaginatively extend the story. Sutpen, humiliated as a boy when a Black servant turned him away from the front door of a plantation house, repudiates his first wife in Haiti on discovering her partial African ancestry, marries Ellen Coldfield, and fathers Henry and Judith. Henry befriends Charles Bon — revealed, in the narrators' reconstruction, to be Sutpen's discarded son by his first marriage — and ultimately murders Bon at the gate of Sutpen's Hundred to prevent his marriage to Judith. The dynasty collapses in violence, madness and fire; only Jim Bond, Bon's part-Black grandson, survives among the line.

Title and themes

The title alludes to King David's lament for his rebellious son Absalom in 2 Samuel ("O my son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom!"). The novel explores the founding sins of the American South — slavery, racial violence and the myth of the self-made dynasty — and, formally, the impossibility of ever fully knowing the past: each narrator remakes Sutpen's story from fragments, prejudice and desire.

Reception

Initially met with mixed reviews, the novel's reputation rose with Faulkner's Nobel Prize (1949) and the growth of Faulkner scholarship. Its 1940 corrected text, along with Faulkner's appended chronology, genealogy and map of Yoknapatawpha County, remains standard. Some editions note that the novel once held a Guinness record for the longest sentence in literature (the 1,288-word sentence in Chapter 6).
