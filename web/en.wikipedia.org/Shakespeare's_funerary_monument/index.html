Shakespeare's funerary monument

Shakespeare's funerary monument is a memorial to the playwright and poet William Shakespeare (1564–1616), located inside Holy Trinity Church in Stratford-upon-Avon, Warwickshire, England. The monument, attributed to the Flemish-born sculptor Gerard Johnson (also known as Geraert Janssen), was installed in the chancel of the church, above Shakespeare's grave, sometime before 1623, when it was mentioned in the First Folio of Shakespeare's plays.

Description

The monument consists of a half-length effigy of Shakespeare set within an architectural frame of Corinthian columns supporting an entablature and coat of arms. The figure is depicted holding a quill in his right hand, with a sheet of paper beneath his left hand resting on a cushion — a conventional pose identifying the subject as a writer. The effigy originally wore a scarlet doublet beneath a black, sleeveless gown, with painted flesh tones and brown hair and beard.

Beneath the effigy is an inscription plaque with two epitaphs: a Latin elegiac couplet and an English quatrain traditionally attributed to Shakespeare himself, which curses anyone who moves his bones:

Good friend, for Jesus' sake forbear, / To dig the dust enclosed here. / Blessed be the man that spares these stones, / And cursed be he that moves my bones.

History

The monument was in place by 1623, as Leonard Digges referred to "thy Stratford Moniment" in his commendatory poem in the First Folio. The earliest depiction is an engraving in William Dugdale's Antiquities of Warwickshire (1656), which shows the figure holding a sack or cushion with both hands rather than a quill; whether this records an earlier state, a repair, or mere inaccuracy by the engraver remains debated.

Over the centuries the monument was repeatedly repainted. In 1748–49 a restoration funded by proceeds from a benefit performance restored its original polychromy. In 1793, however, the Shakespeare scholar Edmond Malone persuaded the vicar to have the effigy painted white, in keeping with neoclassical taste; the original colours were restored in 1861. The monument was also the model for later memorials, including the statue in Poets' Corner, Westminster Abbey (1740).

Significance

Along with the Droeshout portrait in the First Folio, the monument is one of only two likenesses of Shakespeare generally accepted as having been made with knowledge of his appearance and approved by those who knew him. It is a Grade I listed structure as part of the church, and remains one of the most visited literary shrines in England.
