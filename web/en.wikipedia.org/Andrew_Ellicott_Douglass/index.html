Andrew Ellicott Douglass

Andrew Ellicott Douglass
Born: 5 July 1867, Windsor, Vermont, US
Died: 20 March 1962 (aged 94), Tucson, Arizona
Known for: Founding dendrochronology

Andrew Ellicott Douglass (July 5, 1867 – March 20, 1962) was an American astronomer who founded the science of dendrochronology—the dating of past events and climate through the pattern of tree growth rings. Working mainly at the University of Arizona, he built chronologies that fixed the construction dates of the great prehistoric pueblos of the US Southwest.

Astronomy and sunspots

Douglass began as an assistant at Harvard College Observatory and in 1894 joined Percival Lowell in Flagstaff, Arizona, helping site the Lowell Observatory. Seeking a record of solar variation, he reasoned that sunspot cycles should leave matching cycles in tree growth, and from 1904 he measured rings in ponderosa pines around Flagstaff, developing the technique of crossdating.

Crossdating and archaeology

By matching overlapping ring patterns between living trees and old beams, Douglass constructed a continuous chronology reaching back centuries. In 1929 a beam from Show Low, Arizona, bridged the gap between his floating prehistoric sequence and the modern record, instantly dating sites such as Pueblo Bonito in Chaco Canyon. He founded the Laboratory of Tree-Ring Research at the University of Arizona in 1937, the first institution of its kind.

Legacy

Beyond chronology, Douglass used rings to reconstruct past rainfall and drought, laying groundwork for dendroclimatology. Craters on the Moon and Mars are named for him, and his methods remain fundamental to archaeology, ecology, and climate science.
