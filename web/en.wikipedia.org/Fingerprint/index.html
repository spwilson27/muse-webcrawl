A fingerprint is an impression left by the friction ridges of a human finger. The recovery of partial fingerprints from a crime scene is an important method of forensic science. Moisture and grease on a finger result in fingerprints on surfaces such as glass or metal. Deliberate impressions of entire fingerprints can be obtained by ink or other substances transferred from the peaks of friction ridges on the skin to a smooth surface such as paper.

Fingerprint records normally contain impressions from the pad on the last joint of fingers and thumbs, though fingerprint cards also typically record portions of the lower joint areas of the fingers. Human fingerprints are detailed, nearly unique, difficult to alter, and durable over the life of an individual, making them suitable as long-term markers of human identity.

Biology

Fingerprints form before birth, during the first trimester of fetal development, as a result of the interaction between the growing volar pads on the fingertips and mechanical stresses in the developing skin. The general pattern type is influenced by genetics, while the fine detail (minutiae) is shaped by local conditions in the womb, which is why even identical twins have distinguishable fingerprints. The ridge patterns persist throughout life, changing only with scarring or certain skin conditions.

Classification

Fingerprint patterns are conventionally grouped into three classes: loops (about 60–65% of patterns), in which ridges enter from one side, curve around, and exit the same side; whorls (about 30–35%), in which ridges form circular or spiral patterns; and arches (about 5%), in which ridges enter from one side and exit the other, rising in the middle. Identification relies on minutiae — ridge endings, bifurcations, and other local features — a system of analysis first systematised by Francis Galton and Edward Henry.

History of identification

Fingerprints were used as marks of identity in ancient Babylon and China. In the nineteenth century, William James Herschel used fingerprints on contracts in India, and Henry Faulds published an early proposal for forensic use in 1880. Juan Vucetich established the first fingerprint bureau in Argentina in 1892, and the Henry classification system was adopted in British India and then by Scotland Yard's Fingerprint Branch in 1901. Automated fingerprint identification systems (AFIS) now store and search hundreds of millions of prints.

Forensic use and criticism

Latent prints recovered at crime scenes are compared with suspect prints by examiners using the ACE-V method (analysis, comparison, evaluation, verification). Although fingerprint evidence has a long record of courtroom acceptance, scientific reviews — including a 2009 National Academy of Sciences report and a 2016 PCAST report — have criticised the lack of quantified error rates, and cases of erroneous identification, such as that of Brandon Mayfield after the 2004 Madrid train bombings, have driven reforms including blind verification and proficiency testing.

Biometric applications

Capacitive and optical fingerprint sensors are widely used to unlock smartphones and authenticate payments, and fingerprints are recorded in passports, national identity programmes such as India's Aadhaar, and border-control systems. Critics note privacy risks: unlike passwords, compromised fingerprints cannot be changed.
