Bootlegging

[Image placeholder: Prohibition agents destroying liquor]

Bootlegging is the illegal manufacture, transport, distribution, or sale of goods — most famously alcoholic beverages in the United States during Prohibition (1920–1933). The term is said to derive from the practice of concealing a bottle in the leg of a boot, and it became synonymous with the vast black market that supplied liquor after the Eighteenth Amendment and the Volstead Act banned its production and sale nationwide. A related term, rum-running, refers to the smuggling of alcohol over water.

Bootlegging during Prohibition

When Prohibition began in January 1920, illegal supply networks grew rapidly. Liquor was smuggled from Canada, Mexico, and the Caribbean by ship and vehicle; domestic sources included illicit distilleries ("moonshine"), diverted industrial alcohol, and sacramental and medicinal supplies. Speakeasies — illegal drinking establishments — numbered in the tens of thousands in cities such as New York and Chicago. The trade generated enormous profits for organized crime, enriching figures such as Al Capone in Chicago, and it was associated with widespread bribery of police and officials, gang violence such as the Saint Valentine's Day Massacre of 1929, and corruption of the enforcement agencies charged with suppressing it.

Enforcement and repeal

Federal enforcement fell to the Bureau of Prohibition and agents such as Eliot Ness in Chicago and the duo Izzy Einstein and Moe Smith in New York, but with only a few thousand agents for the entire country, enforcement was uneven and often futile. Public disillusionment, lost tax revenue, and the burdens of the Great Depression led to repeal: the Twenty-first Amendment, ratified in December 1933, remains the only amendment to repeal another.

Modern usage

Today "bootlegging" is applied more generally to unauthorized distribution of restricted or protected goods, including untaxed cigarettes, counterfeit products, and pirated media — as in "bootleg recordings" of concerts and films.
