Kashrut is the body of Jewish dietary law: the set of rules determining which foods are "fit" (kosher) for consumption and how they must be prepared. Its foundations lie in the Torah — principally Leviticus 11 and Deuteronomy 14 — as elaborated by rabbinic tradition over two millennia.

Principal rules

Land animals must both chew the cud and have fully split hooves (excluding pigs, camels, and hares); aquatic creatures must have fins and scales (excluding shellfish); most birds traditionally identified as non-predatory are permitted, while birds of prey are not. Permitted animals must be slaughtered by shechita, a swift ritual cut, and the blood drained or salted out, blood itself being forbidden. A central feature is the separation of meat and milk, derived from the thrice-repeated command not to boil a kid in its mother's milk: the two may not be cooked or eaten together, and observant households keep separate utensils and observe waiting periods between them. Additional rules cover Passover (excluding leavened grain), wine, insect inspection of produce, and the tithing and blessing of food.

Practice and certification

Observance varies across Jewish movements, from strict Orthodox adherence to more selective practice. A large kosher-certification industry, using symbols such as Ⓤ and ⓚ, supervises commercial food production worldwide.
