Local ring

In mathematics, more specifically in ring theory, a local ring is a ring that has a unique maximal ideal. Equivalently, a (nonzero) ring R is local if the set of its non-units forms an ideal — in which case that ideal is the unique maximal ideal m, and the quotient R/m is the residue field. The name reflects their origin: local rings capture the behavior of functions near a single point.

Definition and basic properties

A commutative ring with identity is local if it possesses exactly one maximal ideal. In a local ring, an element is a unit if and only if it lies outside the maximal ideal; the sum of two non-units is a non-unit. Nakayama's lemma, a cornerstone result about finitely generated modules over local rings, underlies much of local algebra.

Examples

  • All fields, and all discrete valuation rings, are local.
  • The ring of formal power series k[[x1, …, xn]] over a field is local, with maximal ideal generated by the variables.
  • The localization of ℤ at a prime p — fractions whose denominators are not divisible by p — is local with residue field 𝔽p.
  • The ring of germs of smooth functions at a point of a manifold is local.

Localization

Given any commutative ring and a prime ideal, the localization at that prime is a local ring whose maximal ideal corresponds to the chosen prime. This construction is what lets algebraic geometers study a variety "locally" near a point: the local ring at a point of a scheme records the functions defined near that point.

Important classes

Local rings are classified by properties such as being Noetherian, regular (the maximal ideal is generated by exactly dim R elements), complete (with respect to the m-adic topology), or Cohen–Macaulay. The Krull–Remak–Schmidt and structure theorems for complete local rings make the local case a central testing ground of commutative algebra. Noncommutative generalizations define local rings by the same "unique maximal (left) ideal" condition.
