Islamic Cairo

The minarets of historic Cairo
Location: Cairo, Egypt
Founded: 969 AD
Status: UNESCO World Heritage Site (since 1979)

Islamic Cairo is the name commonly given to the historic core of Cairo, Egypt, encompassing the districts built between the founding of the Fatimid city in 969 AD and the modern era. Distinguished from the newer sections of the city, it contains one of the largest and densest concentrations of historic Islamic architecture in the world, including hundreds of mosques, tombs, madrassas, hammams, caravanserais and fountains.

History

Cairo was founded in 969 by the Fatimid general Jawhar al-Siqilli as the new capital of the Fatimid Caliphate, north of the earlier settlements of Fustat, al-Askar and al-Qata'i. The Fatimids built the great palaces of the caliphs, the mosque of al-Azhar (founded 972), and the city's walls. Under the Ayyubids, Saladin began the Citadel in 1176 and extended the fortifications. The Mamluk era (1250–1517) saw an explosion of monumental construction, as sultans and emirs endowed vast religious and funerary complexes. After the Ottoman conquest of 1517, Cairo became a provincial capital but continued to grow, adding mosques, sabils and wikala commercial buildings.

Major monuments

Notable monuments include the Mosque of Ibn Tulun (876–879, in the earlier al-Qata'i district), the Mosque-Madrasa of Sultan Hasan (1356–1363), the Citadel of Saladin with the Mosque of Muhammad Ali (1830–1848), the fortified gates Bab Zuweila, Bab al-Futuh and Bab al-Nasr, the necropolis known as the City of the Dead (Qarafa), and the market district of Khan el-Khalili.

Preservation

Historic Cairo was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1979. The area faces challenges from groundwater, pollution, earthquakes (notably the 1992 earthquake) and urban pressure, and has been the focus of major restoration campaigns, including the Historic Cairo Restoration Project.
