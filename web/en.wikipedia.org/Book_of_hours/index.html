A book of hours is a Christian devotional book popular in the Middle Ages. It is the most common type of surviving medieval illuminated manuscript. Each manuscript book is unique in some way, but most contain a similar collection of texts, prayers and psalms, often with appropriate decorations, for Christian devotion. Books of hours were typically written in Latin and centred on the Little Office of the Blessed Virgin Mary, a cycle of prayers to be recited at the canonical hours of the day.

Contents

A typical book of hours includes a calendar of church feasts (often illustrated with labours of the months and signs of the zodiac), extracts from the Gospels, the Hours of the Virgin, the Hours of the Cross and of the Holy Spirit, the penitential psalms, the litany of the saints, the Office of the Dead, and suffrages (short prayers) to individual saints. Owners could have books customized with family portraits, coats of arms, and prayers for their particular patrons.

History

The book of hours developed from the psalter in the 13th century and by the 15th century had become a luxury status symbol for royalty and wealthy merchants as well as a practical prayer book for the literate laity. Workshops in Paris, Flanders, and the Netherlands produced them in large numbers. The most famous example is the Très Riches Heures du Duc de Berry, illuminated by the Limbourg brothers around 1412–1416 and supplemented by Jean Colombe. The arrival of printing in the late 15th century produced large numbers of printed books of hours, often hand-coloured, before the genre declined after the Reformation and the Counter-Reformation's standardization of devotion.

Legacy

Because so many were made and so lovingly decorated, books of hours are among the richest sources for late medieval art, costume, landscape, and daily life. Thousands survive in libraries and museums, and they remain staples of the antiquarian book market.
