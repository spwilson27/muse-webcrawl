Tubulin in molecular biology can refer either to the tubulin protein superfamily of globular proteins, or just a member of the superfamily. α- and β-tubulins polymerize into microtubules, a major component of the eukaryotic cytoskeleton. Microtubules function in many cellular processes, including mitosis.

Tubulin-binding drugs kill cancerous cells by inhibiting microtubule dynamics, which are required for DNA segregation and therefore cell division.

Characterization

Tubulin is characterized by the evolutionarily conserved Tubulin/FtsZ family, GTPase protein domain. This GTPase protein domain is found in all eukaryotic tubulin chains, as well as the bacterial proteins FtsZ and TubZ. Tubulin-like proteins are also found in viruses.

Types

α- and β-tubulins form heterodimers that are the structural building blocks of microtubules. The α/β heterodimer assembles head-to-tail into protofilaments, typically 13 of which associate laterally to form a hollow microtubule about 25 nm in diameter. γ-Tubulin forms the γ-tubulin ring complex (γTuRC), which nucleates microtubule assembly at centrosomes. δ-, ε-, and ζ-tubulins are found in organisms with triplet microtubules in basal bodies and centrioles.

Dynamic instability

Microtubules assembled from tubulin exhibit dynamic instability — stochastic switching between phases of growth and shrinkage ("catastrophe" and "rescue"). Both α- and β-tubulin bind GTP; GTP hydrolysis in β-tubulin after incorporation destabilizes the lattice and drives depolymerization.

Drugs targeting tubulin

The taxanes (paclitaxel, docetaxel) stabilize microtubules, while the vinca alkaloids (vincristine, vinblastine) and colchicine destabilize them. Both classes are widely used as chemotherapy agents because rapidly dividing cancer cells are highly sensitive to disruption of the mitotic spindle.
