Perspective (graphical)

Perspective in the graphic arts is an approximate representation, generally on a flat surface such as paper or canvas, of an image as it is seen by the eye. Its two most characteristic features, in the linear perspective developed in Renaissance Italy, are that objects appear smaller as their distance from the observer increases, and that parallel lines converge at vanishing points on the horizon.

History

Artists had long used intuitive systems for suggesting depth, but mathematically consistent linear perspective was demonstrated in Florence in the early 15th century by Filippo Brunelleschi. Leon Battista Alberti gave the first written account in his treatise De pictura (1435), and artists such as Masaccio, Paolo Uccello and Piero della Francesca made perspective a foundation of Renaissance painting. Leonardo da Vinci further described aerial (atmospheric) perspective, in which distant objects appear paler and bluer because of the intervening air.

Types

  • One-point perspective — a single vanishing point; used for interiors and roads receding directly away from the viewer;
  • Two-point perspective — two vanishing points, for objects viewed at an angle;
  • Three-point perspective — adds a vanishing point above or below, for views looking up or down at tall structures;
  • Curvilinear perspective — represents the curvature of very wide fields of view.

Mathematical basis

Linear perspective is a projective mapping: points in three-dimensional space are projected along sight lines from a single viewpoint onto a picture plane. The geometry underlies modern computer graphics, where perspective projection matrices perform the same construction, and photography, where lens design determines the perspective rendering of a scene.
