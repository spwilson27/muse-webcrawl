Ewe music

Ewe music is the music of the Ewe people of Togo, Ghana, and Benin. It is most famous for its highly developed drumming traditions, featuring polyrhythmic ensembles in which interlocking patterns played on drums, bells, and rattles combine into complex rhythmic structures. Ewe drumming has been extensively studied by ethnomusicologists and has influenced musicians and composers worldwide.

Instruments and ensemble

A typical Ewe drum ensemble includes a double bell (gankeke) that plays the time line, a rattle (axatse), and a set of drums of graduated sizes: support drums such as the kagan, kidi, and sogo, and a master drum (atsimevu), a tall drum played with sticks and hands whose calls direct the ensemble and the dancers. The bell pattern in pieces such as Agbadza and Kpanouhoun is often in 12/8, and cross-rhythms arise between the bell, support drums, handclaps, and song.

Genres and social functions

Ewe music serves many social functions: funeral and memorial ceremonies, festivals, religious worship (including music for the Yewe and Afa traditions), recreation, and historical praise-singing. War dances such as Atamga and recreational dances such as Gahu — itself adapted in the twentieth century from Nigerian and Afro-Cuban influences — remain widely performed. Song texts may carry proverbs, histories, and social commentary.

The ethnomusicologist A. M. Jones documented Ewe rhythms in the 1950s, and the Ghanaian master drummer and scholar Kofi Ladzekpo and others have taught Ewe drumming internationally, making it one of the best-known African drumming traditions in world-music education.
