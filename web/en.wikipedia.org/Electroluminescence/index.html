Electroluminescence

Electroluminescence (EL) is an optical and electrical phenomenon in which a material emits light in response to the passage of an electric current or to a strong electric field. It differs from black-body radiation and from incandescence because the emission arises from the recombination of electrons and holes in the material rather than from heat.

Mechanisms

In semiconductor electroluminescence, charge carriers injected across a p–n junction recombine and release energy as photons — the operating principle of the light-emitting diode (LED). In powder and thin-film EL devices, a phosphor such as zinc sulfide doped with copper or manganese is excited by a strong alternating electric field, which accelerates electrons into luminescent centres. Organic electroluminescence in carbon-based materials underpins OLED displays.

History

Electroluminescence was first observed in 1907 by H. J. Round of Marconi Labs, who noticed light emission from a silicon carbide crystal detector. Oleg Losev studied and patented the effect in the 1920s and 1930s. Georges Destriau discovered powder electroluminescence in 1936, and practical thin-film devices followed in the 1960s and 1970s.

Applications

Electroluminescent devices include LEDs, OLED displays, EL panels and wire used for backlighting and decorative lighting, night lights, and flat-panel displays. EL lamps produce soft, uniform illumination without glare or appreciable heat, though at lower brightness than LED lighting.
