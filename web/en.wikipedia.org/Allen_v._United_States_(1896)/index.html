Allen v. United States (1896)

Allen v. United States, 164 U.S. 492 (1896), was a United States Supreme Court case that approved the use of a special jury instruction — now commonly called an Allen charge or "dynamite charge" — given to a deadlocked jury in a criminal trial to encourage jurors in the minority to reconsider their views in light of the majority's opinion.

Background

Alexander Allen was tried for murder in federal court in the Indian Territory. After the jury reported that it was unable to agree, the trial judge delivered a supplemental instruction summarising an approach earlier approved in Massachusetts practice, urging jurors holding minority views to consider whether their doubt was reasonable given that a majority of their fellow jurors had reached the opposite conclusion, while reminding them not to surrender their honest convictions. Allen was convicted and appealed, arguing that the instruction coerced the jury.

Decision

The Supreme Court affirmed the conviction, holding that the instruction was a permissible statement of the jurors' duty to deliberate with a disposition to be convinced by the arguments of one another. The Court stressed that the charge did not direct any verdict and left each juror free to maintain an honestly held view.

Legacy

Allen charges remain controversial. Critics argue they exert pressure on minority jurors and can undermine the requirement of unanimity; several states have banned or restricted them in state courts, and many judges prefer modified versions, while federal courts continue to permit them within limits. The case is the leading authority for the practice in the United States federal system.
