Bank robbery

Bank robbery is the criminal act of stealing from a bank, specifically while bank employees and customers are subjected to force, violence, or the threat of violence. It is distinguished from burglary, which involves entering a bank when it is closed.

History

Notable early American bank robberies include the 1866 Liberty, Missouri robbery attributed to the James–Younger Gang, and the exploits of Depression-era robbers such as John Dillinger and Bonnie and Clyde in the 1930s, which led to expanded federal jurisdiction in the United States through the Federal Bank Robbery Act of 1934.

Methods and prevention

Most bank robberies are committed by individuals or small groups using demand notes or weapons; take-over robberies involve greater violence and risk. Banks employ countermeasures including dye packs, bait money, surveillance cameras, time locks, bandit barriers, and silent alarms. Statistically, clearance rates for bank robbery are among the highest of serious crimes, while average takings are modest — a fact often attributed in criminology to the publicity surrounding notorious robbers such as Willie Sutton, to whom the apocryphal "Sutton's law" is attached.
