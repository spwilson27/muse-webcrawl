A chemical plant is an industrial processing plant that manufactures (or otherwise processes) chemicals, usually on a large scale. The general objective of a chemical plant is to create new material wealth via the chemical or biological transformation and/or separation of materials. Chemical plants use specialized equipment, units, and technology in the manufacturing process. Other kinds of plants, such as polymer, pharmaceutical, food, and some beverage production facilities, power plants, oil refineries or other refineries, natural gas processing and biochemical plants, water and wastewater treatment, and pollution control equipment use many technologies that have similarities to chemical plant technology such as fluid systems and chemical reactor systems.

History

The earliest large-scale chemical plants grew out of the Leblanc process for soda ash in the late 18th and early 19th centuries, which supplied alkali for soap, glass, and textile industries. The Solvay process later displaced it. The 20th century saw the rise of the petrochemical industry, with steam cracking of hydrocarbons, catalytic reforming, and large-scale ammonia synthesis via the Haber–Bosch process transforming plant design toward continuous, highly integrated complexes.

Plant design

The design of a chemical plant involves process synthesis, simulation, equipment sizing, piping and instrumentation diagrams (P&IDs), and economic evaluation. Plants are typically designed by chemical engineers and built by engineering, procurement, and construction (EPC) firms. Key design considerations include:

  • Process flow and mass/energy balances
  • Reaction kinetics and reactor selection
  • Separation operations such as distillation, absorption, and extraction
  • Heat integration and utility systems (steam, cooling water, refrigeration)
  • Process safety, relief systems, and hazard studies (e.g., HAZOP)
  • Environmental controls and waste treatment

Unit operations and unit processes

Plants are assembled from unit operations — physical steps such as fluid flow, heat transfer, distillation, filtration, and crushing — and unit processes, which involve chemical change, such as oxidation, nitration, polymerization, and fermentation. Modern plants combine these into continuous trains controlled from a central control room using distributed control systems (DCS) and programmable logic controllers.

Safety

Because plants handle flammable, toxic, and reactive materials at scale, process safety management is a central discipline. Major accidents such as the Bhopal disaster (1984) and the Texas City refinery explosion (2005) led to stricter regulation, including OSHA's Process Safety Management standard in the United States and the Seveso Directives in Europe.
