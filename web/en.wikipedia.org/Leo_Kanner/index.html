Leo Kanner

Leo Kanner (pronounced "conner"; June 13, 1894 – April 3, 1981) was an Austrian-American psychiatrist, physician, and social activist best known for his work related to infantile autism. Before working at the Henry Phipps Psychiatric Clinic at the Johns Hopkins Hospital in Baltimore, Maryland, Kanner practised medicine in Germany and South Dakota. In 1943, Kanner published his landmark paper Autistic Disturbances of Affective Contact, describing 11 children who displayed common traits of what Kanner called early infantile autism. He is considered to be the founder of the field of child psychiatry.

Early life and career

Kanner was born in Klekotów, Austria-Hungary (now Klekotiv, Ukraine), to a Jewish family. He studied medicine at the University of Berlin, interrupted by service in the Austro-Hungarian army during the First World War, and received his medical degree in 1921. In 1924 he emigrated to the United States, taking a position at the state hospital in Yankton, South Dakota. In 1930 he was selected to develop the first child psychiatry service in a paediatric hospital, at Johns Hopkins, where he later became professor of child psychiatry.

Child psychiatry

Kanner's 1935 textbook Child Psychiatry was the first English-language textbook on the subject. At Johns Hopkins he established the first academic department of child psychiatry in the United States, training a generation of specialists.

Autism research

In his 1943 paper, Kanner described 11 children (eight boys and three girls) who shared a pattern of profound social aloofness, insistence on sameness, and language peculiarities present from the beginning of life. He coined the term "early infantile autism", borrowing "autism" from Eugen Bleuler's use of it for schizophrenia. Kanner initially suggested an innate disturbance of affective contact, though he later also wrote of parental coldness, an association that was taken up and distorted into the harmful "refrigerator mother" hypothesis promoted by others, notably Bruno Bettelheim. Kanner himself ultimately exonerated parents, famously telling a parents' group in 1969: "I herewith especially acquit you people as parents."

Legacy

Kanner's description founded the modern study of autism spectrum conditions; "Kanner's syndrome" was an early synonym for classic autism. Later scholarship, including Lorna Wing's, broadened his narrow criteria into the concept of an autism spectrum. His work also shaped diagnostic manuals and child psychiatry services worldwide.
