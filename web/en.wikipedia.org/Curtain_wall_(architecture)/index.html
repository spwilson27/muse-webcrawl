Curtain wall (architecture)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A curtain wall is an outer covering of a building in which the outer walls are non-structural, designed only to keep the weather out and the occupants in. Because the curtain wall is not structural, it can be made of lightweight materials, such as glass, thereby reducing construction costs.

Whereas load-bearing walls carry the weight of floors and roof, a curtain wall "hangs" from the building's structural frame like a curtain. It transfers only its own dead load and wind loads back to the structure through connections at floors or columns.

History

Curtain walls became common with the rise of steel-frame and reinforced-concrete construction. Early landmarks include the Boley Building in Kansas City (1909) and Willis Polk's Hallidie Building in San Francisco (1918), one of the first glass curtain walls. Aluminium framing became standard after the Second World War, exemplified by the United Nations Secretariat Building (1950) and Lever House (1952) in New York.

Systems

  • Stick systems — assembled piece by piece on site.
  • Unitised systems — pre-assembled panels installed storey by storey, offering faster, more controlled installation.

Curtain walls must resist water penetration, air leakage, wind, and thermal movement, and they often incorporate pressure-equalised rainscreen principles to manage moisture.
