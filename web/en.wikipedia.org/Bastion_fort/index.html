Bastion fort

TypeFortification
EraEarly modern (c. 1500–1800s)
Also known asStar fort; trace italienne
Key featureAngular bastions allowing flanking fire along the walls

A bastion fort, also known as a star fort or by the French term trace italienne, is a fortification in a style that evolved during the early modern period of gunpowder warfare, when cannon came to dominate the battlefield. It was first seen in the mid-15th century in Italy. Its distinctive feature is the use of angular, arrowhead-shaped bastions projecting from the curtain walls, arranged so that every face of the fortification could be swept by defensive fire from a neighbouring bastion, eliminating the dead zones that had plagued medieval castles.

Design

Compared with medieval fortifications, bastion forts are much lower and spread over a larger area. Thick earthen ramparts, often faced with masonry, absorbed the impact of cannonballs far better than tall, thin stone walls. A wide ditch, a sloped glacis, and outworks such as ravelins, hornworks and crownworks slowed an attacker's approach and kept siege artillery at a distance. From above, the interlocking bastions give the fortress its characteristic star shape.

History

The style emerged in Italy during the Italian Wars, when French invasions demonstrated that medieval walls could not withstand modern artillery; the designs of engineers such as the Sangallo family spread the new system across Europe. In the 17th century the French engineer Sébastien Le Prestre de Vauban perfected both the construction and the systematic siege of bastion fortresses, building a ring of fortifications around France. Famous examples include the Citadel of Lille, the fortified towns of Palmanova in Italy and Naarden in the Netherlands, and Fort McHenry in Baltimore. The spread of bastion forts dramatically increased the cost of warfare and the size of armies needed for siege operations.

Decline

By the mid-19th century, rifled artillery and explosive shells had made masonry bastion forts obsolete, and fortification design moved toward detached polygonal forts and eventually concrete and earthwork systems. Many star forts survive today as historic sites.
