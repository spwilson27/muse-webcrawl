The Celts (pronounced kelts) were a diverse collection of tribal societies in Iron Age and Roman-era Europe who spoke Celtic languages and shared material culture, religious practices, and artistic traditions. The term derives from the Greek Keltoi, used by writers such as Herodotus and Hecataeus for peoples of the upper Danube and southern Gaul.

Origins and expansion

Celtic culture is generally traced to the Hallstatt culture of Central Europe (c. 1200–450 BC), centred on salt-rich regions of present-day Austria, and its successor, the La Tène culture, which from around 450 BC spread across much of Europe. Celtic-speaking peoples settled Iberia (the Celtiberians), Gaul, Britain, and Ireland; in the 4th century BC they sacked Rome (Battle of the Allia, 390 BC) and invaded the Balkans, and in 278 BC Celtic tribes crossed into Anatolia, where the Galatians established a lasting presence. Celtic mercenaries served in armies across the Mediterranean world.

Society and culture

Celtic societies were organized into tribes under kings and aristocratic warrior elites, with druids serving as priests, judges, and keepers of oral learning. Despite lacking a shared written literature, the Celts produced celebrated metalwork — torcs, brooches, and shields in gold and bronze — and fortified settlements including hillforts in Britain and the large urbanized oppida of late Iron Age Gaul.

Decline and survival

Between the 1st century BC and the 5th century AD, most Celtic peoples were absorbed by the Roman Empire, then by Germanic and Slavic expansion, and their languages receded to Europe's western fringe. Celtic languages survive today — Welsh, Irish, Scottish Gaelic, Breton, Cornish, and Manx — several of them revitalized in modern times. The six territories recognized as modern "Celtic nations" are Brittany, Cornwall, Ireland, the Isle of Man, Scotland, and Wales, whose shared heritage is celebrated by the Celtic Congress and the Pan Celtic Festival.
