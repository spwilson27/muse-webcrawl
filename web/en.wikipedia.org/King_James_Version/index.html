King James Version

Published: 1611
Commissioned by: James I of England
Translators: 47 scholars in six companies
Full name: The Holy Bible, Conteyning the Old Testament, and the New

The King James Version (KJV), also the King James Bible and the Authorised Version, is an Early Modern English translation of the Christian Bible for the Church of England, commissioned in 1604 and published in 1611 by the sponsorship of King James I of England. Noted for its majesty of style, the KJV had a profound impact on English literature and the English language.

History

James commissioned the translation at the Hampton Court Conference of 1604, partly in response to Puritan objections to the Bishops' Bible. Fifty-four scholars were initially appointed, of whom 47 are recorded as having worked in six companies at Oxford, Cambridge, and Westminster. They worked from the Hebrew Masoretic Text, the Greek Textus Receptus, and earlier English versions — above all William Tyndale's, whose phrasing supplied much of the New Testament text. The Apocrypha was included in the 1611 edition, placed between the Testaments.

Style and influence

The translators aimed for a dignified, somewhat formal rendering following strict rules of consistency. Phrases such as "the salt of the earth," "a thorn in the flesh," "the powers that be," and "let there be light" entered common English largely through the KJV. It remained the standard English Bible for over three centuries and is still widely read, printed, and cherished; revised editions include the Revised Version (1885) and, in its textual lineage, the New King James Version (1982).
