Rùm (Scottish Gaelic: Rùm) is one of the Small Isles of the Inner Hebrides, in the Lochaber area of Highland, Scotland. The largest of the Small Isles, it is a mountainous island dominated by the jagged peaks of the Rùm Cuillin, formed from the eroded remains of an ancient volcano. Almost the entire island is a national nature reserve managed by NatureScot, and its resident population is only a few dozen people, mostly at the village of Kinloch.

History

Rùm has been inhabited since the Mesolithic; bloodstone tools found at Kinloch represent some of the earliest evidence of human settlement in Scotland. The island passed through Norse and clan hands — it belonged to Clan Macdonald of Clanranald — and in the 1820s its population of about 450 was cleared to make way for sheep farming. In the 19th and 20th centuries it became a private sporting estate; Sir George Bullough built the extravagant red-sandstone Kinloch Castle, completed in 1900. The island was sold to the Nature Conservancy in 1957 and has been managed for conservation ever since.

Wildlife and geology

Rùm is famous for its long-running red deer research project, one of the longest studies of a large mammal population in the world, and for its herd of Highland cattle, feral goats, and Rùm ponies. Around 60,000 pairs of Manx shearwaters — one of the largest colonies on Earth — nest in burrows high on the Cuillin. White-tailed (sea) eagles were reintroduced here beginning in 1975, the start of their successful return to Scotland. The island's layered igneous geology, formed about 60 million years ago as the North Atlantic opened, is internationally important for the study of magma chambers.

Access

There are no public roads for visitors' vehicles; a ferry from Mallaig serves Kinloch, and accommodation ranges from Kinloch Castle hostel rooms to campsites and bothies.
