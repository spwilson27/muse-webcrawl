Constant-velocity joint

A constant-velocity joint (also called a homokinetic joint or CV joint) is a mechanical coupling that allows a drive shaft to transmit power through a variable angle at a constant rotational speed, without significant friction or play. CV joints are best known from the drive shafts of front-wheel-drive cars.

Principle

Unlike a simple universal joint, whose output speed fluctuates when operating at an angle, a CV joint keeps the input and output shafts at the same instantaneous velocity. Designs achieve this by arranging the contact points of the joint on the plane that bisects the angle between the shafts.

Types

The most common automotive types are the Rzeppa joint, which uses six balls running in spherical races and is used at the wheel end of drive shafts, and the tripod joint, which uses three rollers on needle bearings and accommodates the plunging movement at the gearbox end. Other historic designs include the Weiss, Tracta and double Cardan joints.

Applications and maintenance

CV joints are used wherever powered axles must also steer or move with suspension, in front- and all-wheel-drive vehicles and in independent rear suspensions. They are packed with grease and sealed by rubber boots; a torn boot admits grit and is the usual cause of failure, announced by clicking on full steering lock.
