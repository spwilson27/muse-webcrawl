IBM Z

IBM Z is a family of mainframe computers produced by IBM since 2000, the direct descendants of the System/360 line introduced in 1964. The "Z" stands for "zero downtime," reflecting the platform's emphasis on continuous availability for mission-critical transaction processing in banking, insurance, retail and government.

Generations

The first machine, the z900, launched the 64-bit z/Architecture in 2000. Successive generations include the z990, System z9, System z10, zEnterprise 196, z13, z14, z15 and z16 (2022), the latter adding an integrated AI accelerator (the Telum processor) for real-time inference such as fraud detection on every transaction. Each generation preserves backward compatibility with software written decades earlier.

Characteristics

IBM Z systems are noted for very high I/O bandwidth, hardware cryptographic acceleration, and logical partitioning (LPARs) that can divide one machine into many isolated systems, alongside the z/VM hypervisor. They run operating systems including z/OS, z/TPF and Linux on IBM Z. A single system can process billions of transactions daily; IBM has long claimed that the platform handles a large share of the world's credit card transactions.

Relation to earlier systems

The mainframe lineage began with minicomputers' larger ancestors — the mainframes of the 1950s — and contrasts with the commodity servers that dominate modern data centers; IBM Z remains distinct for its centralized architecture and extreme reliability, availability and serviceability (RAS) design.
