A minicomputer, or colloquially mini, is a class of multi-user computers that lies in the middle range of the computing spectrum, between the largest multi-user systems (mainframe computers such as IBM Z) and the smallest single-user systems (microcomputers and personal computers).

The term emerged in the 1960s to describe smaller machines, typically cabinet- or rack-sized, that used transistors and core memory and cost far less than a mainframe. Digital Equipment Corporation's PDP-8 (1965), priced under $20,000, is often credited with establishing the category. Machines such as the PDP-11 and VAX, Data General's Nova and Eclipse, and Hewlett-Packard's HP 3000 brought interactive computing to laboratories, universities, and departments within companies that could never have afforded dedicated mainframe time.

Relationship to mainframes

Although mainframe vendors also sold smaller systems — IBM's System/34, System/36, System/38, and AS/400 lines (now IBM i on Power Systems) were frequently called minicomputers — the traditional distinction held: mainframes like IBM Z emphasized massive I/O throughput and transaction processing for an entire enterprise, while minicomputers served a single department or workgroup. The rise of the microprocessor-based server erased the category by the 1990s.
