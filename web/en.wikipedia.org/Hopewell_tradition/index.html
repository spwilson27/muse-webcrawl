Hopewell tradition

The Hopewell tradition, also called the Hopewell culture or Hopewellian exchange, describes the Native American archaeological culture that flourished along rivers in the northeastern and midwestern Eastern Woodlands of North America during the Middle Woodland period, from approximately 100 BCE to 500 CE. The tradition is named after the Hopewell farm in Ross County, Ohio, where a major site was excavated.

Characteristics

Hopewell peoples are best known for their monumental geometric earthworks — circles, squares, and other enclosures, some covering many hectares — and large conical burial mounds. Major complexes include the Newark Earthworks and the sites now preserved in Hopewell Culture National Historical Park in Ohio, several of which were inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2023.

Exchange network

The Hopewell tradition was not a single unified society but a network of distinct local cultures connected by trade and shared ceremonial practices. Raw materials and finished goods travelled enormous distances: obsidian from the Rocky Mountains, copper from the Great Lakes, mica from the southern Appalachians, marine shells from the Gulf Coast, and shark teeth and alligator teeth from the Atlantic coast have all been found in Hopewell burials.

Society and decline

Hopewell communities farmed local plants of the Eastern Agricultural Complex, hunted, and fished, living in small settlements while gathering periodically at the great ceremonial centres. Burials within the mounds contain elaborate grave goods — copper earspools, platform pipes, cut mica silhouettes, and effigy objects — suggesting ranked social organisation and skilled craft specialists. By about 500 CE the construction of large earthworks had ceased and the exchange network had broken down, for reasons that remain debated.
