Rotor machine

In cryptography, a rotor machine is an electro-mechanical stream cipher device used for encrypting and decrypting messages. Rotor machines were the primary cryptographic devices of the interwar and World War II eras. Their core components are wired wheels called rotors, each implementing a substitution alphabet; because the rotors step after each key press, the substitution changes continuously, producing a polyalphabetic cipher with an enormous number of possible settings. The best-known example is the German Enigma machine.

Principle of operation

Each rotor is a disc with electrical contacts on both faces, internally wired so that a signal entering at one letter exits at another. A signal from the keyboard passes through a stack of rotors, in Enigma through a reflector, and back through the rotors by a different path, lighting a lamp showing the enciphered letter. After each character, at least one rotor advances by one position — like the digits of an odometer — so the same plaintext letter rarely encrypts the same way twice. The daily key consisted of the choice and order of rotors, their ring settings, the plugboard connections, and the starting positions.

History

Rotor machines were invented independently by several people around the end of World War I, including Edward Hebern in the United States, Arvid Gerhard Damm in Sweden, Hugo Koch in the Netherlands, and Arthur Scherbius in Germany, who built the Enigma. Other notable machines include the American SIGABA, the British Typex, the Swiss NEMA, and the Japanese PURPLE machine. The German military adopted Enigma in the 1920s and 1930s, believing it unbreakable.

Cryptanalysis

Polish mathematicians — Marian Rejewski, Jerzy Różycki, and Henryk Zygalski — first broke Enigma in the 1930s, and their work was handed to Britain and France in 1939. At Bletchley Park, Alan Turing, Gordon Welchman, and many others developed techniques and electromechanical "bombes" to recover daily keys, producing intelligence codenamed Ultra. Weaknesses exploited included the reflector's property that no letter could encrypt to itself and operator errors.

Legacy

Rotor machines disappeared after the war, replaced by electronic cipher systems and eventually computer algorithms, but they remain iconic objects in the history of cryptography and computing.
