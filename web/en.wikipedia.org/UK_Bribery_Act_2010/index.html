The Bribery Act 2010 is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that covers criminal law relating to bribery. Introduced to Parliament in response to long-standing criticism of the UK's fragmented and outdated anti-corruption law, and to international pressure including from the OECD, it received royal assent on 8 April 2010 and came into force on 1 July 2011.

The Act is frequently described as one of the strictest anti-bribery regimes in the world, in some respects broader than the United States Foreign Corrupt Practices Act, because it covers bribery in both the public and private sectors, contains no exception for facilitation payments, and creates a corporate offence of failing to prevent bribery.

Provisions

The Act creates four principal offences:

  • Active bribery (section 1) — offering, promising or giving a financial or other advantage intending to induce improper performance of a relevant function or activity.
  • Passive bribery (section 2) — requesting, agreeing to receive or accepting such an advantage.
  • Bribery of foreign public officials (section 6) — bribing a foreign public official with the intention of influencing them and obtaining or retaining business or a business advantage.
  • Failure of commercial organisations to prevent bribery (section 7) — a strict-liability corporate offence committed where a person associated with the organisation bribes another person intending to obtain or retain business or a business advantage for the organisation. The organisation has a defence if it can show it had "adequate procedures" in place designed to prevent bribery.

Senior officers of a company can also be personally liable where an offence by the company was committed with their consent or connivance (section 14).

Jurisdiction and penalties

The Act has extensive extraterritorial reach: offences committed abroad may be prosecuted in the UK if the person has a "close connection" with the United Kingdom, and the section 7 offence can apply to any organisation that carries on part of its business in the UK, wherever the bribery occurred. Individuals convicted face up to ten years' imprisonment and unlimited fines; companies face unlimited fines.

Enforcement

Enforcement is led by the Serious Fraud Office in England, Wales and Northern Ireland. Notable outcomes include the first section 7 conviction, of Sweett Group in 2016, and deferred prosecution agreements with companies including Standard Bank, Rolls-Royce and Airbus, the latter involving a record penalty as part of a global settlement in 2020.

Reception

Business groups initially expressed concern about the breadth of the Act, particularly regarding corporate hospitality and the lack of an exception for facilitation (or "grease") payments, which remain illegal under the Act. The Ministry of Justice published statutory guidance organised around six principles: proportionate procedures, top-level commitment, risk assessment, due diligence, communication and training, and monitoring and review.
