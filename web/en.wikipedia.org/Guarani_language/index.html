Guarani language

Guarani (avañeʼẽ, "the people's language") is a Tupian language of South America, belonging to the Tupí–Guaraní branch of the Tupian family. It is one of the official languages of Paraguay (alongside Spanish), where it is spoken by the majority of the population, and of Bolivia (Plurinational State of), and it is also spoken in north-eastern Argentina and parts of Brazil. With several million speakers, Guarani is unusual among indigenous American languages in being widely spoken by non-indigenous people.

History

Guarani was the language of the Guaraní people and was spread and standardised in the 17th and 18th centuries by Jesuit missions (reducciones), whose priests — notably Antonio Ruiz de Montoya, author of a grammar and dictionary (1639–40) — wrote and printed in it. After Paraguay's independence, Guarani remained the everyday language of most Paraguayans across social classes. The 1992 Paraguayan constitution made it co-official with Spanish and mandated bilingual education. Colloquial speech in Paraguay often mixes the two languages in a variety called jopará.

Features

Guarani is an agglutinative, highly inflecting language with nasal harmony, no grammatical gender, and a distinction between inclusive and exclusive "we". It has twelve vowels (six oral and six nasal) written with a Latin-based alphabet that includes the tilde for nasalisation and the glottal stop marker (puso). Words borrowed into other languages from Guarani and related Tupian languages include jaguar, tapir, toucan, cashew, and piranha. Guarani has its own Wikipedia edition and a growing presence in media and literature.
