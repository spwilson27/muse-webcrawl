Marine terrace

A marine terrace, also called a wave-cut terrace or raised beach platform, is a relatively flat, gently sloping surface of marine origin, usually an old abrasion (wave-cut) platform, that has been lifted out of the sphere of wave activity. Marine terraces occur along coasts where tectonic uplift, falling sea level, or both have raised former shorelines above the present sea.

Formation

Wave action erodes a platform at sea level, bounded inland by a sea cliff. If relative sea level falls — through land uplift or glacio-eustatic change — the platform is abandoned as a terrace, and a new platform forms at the lower level. Repeated cycles create staircase-like flights of terraces, famously developed on the Huon Peninsula of Papua New Guinea, the California coast (e.g. Palos Verdes and Santa Cruz Island) and in New Zealand.

Scientific significance

Because each terrace records a former high sea level, dating its fossils, corals or sediments (by radiocarbon, uranium-series or luminescence methods) lets geologists reconstruct past sea levels and measure rates of coastal uplift and earthquake deformation. On coral coasts, terraces of fossil reef limestone document interglacial highstands such as the last interglacial about 125,000 years ago.
