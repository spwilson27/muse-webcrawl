Jean-Baptiste Jourdan

Jean-Baptiste Jourdan (29 April 1762 – 23 November 1833) was a French soldier and politician who became a Marshal of the Empire under Napoleon. A veteran of the American Revolutionary War as a young man, he rose to prominence commanding the armies of the French Republic, winning the decisive Battle of Fleurus (1794). As a deputy he authored the Jourdan Law of 1798, which established the system of conscription — the levée en masse's statutory successor — that underpinned French armies for a generation. A republican by conviction, he served Napoleon in senior posts despite friction, most notably as chief of staff to Joseph Bonaparte in Spain, and ended his career as a peer and governor of Les Invalides under the July Monarchy.

Jean-Baptiste Jourdan, Marshal of the Empire.

Early life and revolutionary career

Jourdan was born in Limoges, the son of a surgeon. He enlisted as a private soldier in 1778 and served in the French expeditionary forces in the American Revolutionary War, including at the siege of Savannah. Returning to France, he left the army and became a draper in Limoges, marrying and settling into trade. The Revolution revived his military career: in 1790 he was elected an officer of the National Guard, and in 1791 lieutenant colonel of volunteers. Distinguishing himself in the Army of the North at Jemappes (1792) and Wattignies (1793), he rose to divisional general under the patronage of Lazare Carnot.

Fleurus and the Army of Sambre-et-Meuse

In 1794 Jourdan received command of the newly formed Army of Sambre-et-Meuse, some 70,000–100,000 men. On 26 June 1794 he defeated the Austrian army of the Prince of Coburg at Fleurus — the battle famous for the French observation balloon l'Entreprenant — a victory that secured France's frontier and opened the Austrian Netherlands to conquest. Advancing to the Rhine, he took Cologne and Düsseldorf, though his 1795 campaign ended in retreat. In 1796, advancing into Germany in coordination with Moreau, he was checked by Archduke Charles at Amberg and Würzburg and forced back across the Rhine, a failure that damaged his reputation. In 1799 he was defeated by the Archduke at Stockach.

The Jourdan Law

Elected to the Council of Five Hundred, Jourdan sponsored the conscription law of 5 September 1798 (19 Fructidor Year VI), known as the Jourdan Law or Jourdan-Delbrel Law. It declared every Frenchman a soldier liable for service and instituted annual levies of men aged 20 to 25 by lottery — the institutional foundation of mass conscription in France and the model later exploited by Napoleon. Jourdan opposed the coup of 18 Brumaire and was briefly excluded from public life by Napoleon, who nonetheless recognised his ability.

Under Napoleon and later life

Reconciled with the regime, Jourdan served as inspector general and administrator in Piedmont, and on 19 May 1804 was created a Marshal of the Empire — though never given one of the great independent field commands. From 1808 he was chief of staff to Joseph Bonaparte in Spain, a thankless role coordinating proud subordinates; he was present at Talavera (1809) and, disastrously, at Vitoria (1813), where the loss of Joseph's army — and Jourdan's marshal's baton, captured by the British — ended French Spain. After the Restoration he rallied successively to the Bourbons, was made a peer of France, supported the July Revolution of 1830, and served as governor of Les Invalides, where he was buried on his death in 1833. His name is inscribed on the Arc de Triomphe.
