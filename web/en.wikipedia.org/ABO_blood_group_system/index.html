ABO blood group system

The ABO blood group system is used to denote the presence of one, both, or neither of the A and B antigens on erythrocytes (red blood cells). It is the most important of the human blood group systems for transfusion medicine, because mismatched ABO transfusions can cause potentially fatal immune reactions.

Classification

Individuals are classified as type A (A antigens, anti-B antibodies), type B (B antigens, anti-A antibodies), type AB (both antigens, neither antibody), or type O (neither antigen, both antibodies). People with type O red cells are "universal donors" for red-cell transfusion, while type AB individuals are "universal recipients."

Genetics and discovery

The system was discovered by Karl Landsteiner in 1901, work for which he received the Nobel Prize in 1930. The ABO gene, located on chromosome 9, encodes a glycosyltransferase: the A and B alleles differ by a few amino acid substitutions that change the sugar added to the H antigen, while the common O allele carries a frameshift deletion that inactivates the enzyme.

Clinical and other associations

ABO type must be matched in transfusion and organ transplantation, and maternal-fetal ABO incompatibility can cause mild hemolytic disease of the newborn. Blood group frequencies vary widely among populations — type O predominates among Indigenous peoples of the Americas, while type B is most common in parts of Asia. Studies have associated ABO groups with differing risks of thrombosis, certain infections, and other diseases.
