Reconstructive surgery

Reconstructive surgery restores form and function to body parts damaged by disease, trauma or congenital conditions.

Reconstructive surgery is a branch of surgery concerned with restoring the form and function of parts of the body that are abnormal because of congenital defects, developmental abnormalities, trauma, infection, tumours or other disease. It forms one of the two main divisions of plastic surgery, the other being cosmetic (aesthetic) surgery, which alters normal structures primarily to improve appearance.

Techniques

Reconstructive surgeons use a conceptual framework sometimes called the "reconstructive ladder", progressing from the simplest reliable method of closing a defect to the most complex: healing by secondary intention, direct closure, skin grafts, local and regional tissue flaps, and finally free tissue transfer, in which skin, muscle or bone is moved with its blood vessels and reconnected under the operating microscope by microsurgery — a technique pioneered in the 1960s and 1970s. Tissue expansion, prosthetics, and increasingly tissue engineering supplement these methods.

Common procedures

The specialty includes repair of cleft lip and palate and other craniofacial anomalies; breast reconstruction after mastectomy; reconstruction after removal of skin and other cancers; treatment of burns; hand surgery and replantation of severed limbs and digits; repair of pressure ulcers and complex wounds; and reconstruction of the head, neck, trunk and lower limb after trauma. Practitioners may be plastic surgeons or surgeons of other specialties — maxillofacial, ENT, orthopaedic and others — working within their anatomical domains.

Distinction from cosmetic surgery

The boundary matters practically as well as conceptually: reconstructive procedures are generally regarded as medically necessary and covered by health insurance or public health systems, whereas cosmetic procedures usually are not. Some operations, such as breast reduction for symptomatic macromastia or rhinoplasty after injury, straddle the distinction.

History

Reconstructive techniques are ancient — the Sushruta Samhita describes forehead-flap nasal reconstruction in ancient India — but the modern specialty was forged in the world wars, when surgeons such as Harold Gillies and Archibald McIndoe developed methods for treating the severe facial injuries of servicemen.
