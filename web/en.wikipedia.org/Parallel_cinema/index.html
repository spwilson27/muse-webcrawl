Parallel cinema, also known as the New Indian Cinema or Indian New Wave, is a film movement in Indian cinema that originated in the state of West Bengal in the 1950s as an alternative to the mainstream commercial Hindi cinema (Bollywood). Inspired by Italian neorealism, it is known for its serious content, naturalism, realism and depiction of social issues, often with state support and low budgets, in contrast to the songs, dances and melodrama of popular films.

Origins

The movement began in Bengali cinema with Satyajit Ray's Pather Panchali (1955), an international success that demonstrated the viability of realistic, humanist filmmaking in India. Ray's contemporaries Ritwik Ghatak and Mrinal Sen developed their own, more overtly political styles. Early precursors included Chetan Anand's Neecha Nagar (1946) and Bimal Roy's Do Bigha Zamin (1953), both influenced by neorealism.

Growth and state support

From the late 1960s, the state Film Finance Corporation (later the National Film Development Corporation) funded low-budget art films, enabling directors such as Mani Kaul, Kumar Shahani and Shyam Benegal — whose Ankur (1974) launched the movement's Hindi phase. Strong regional cinemas in Kerala (Adoor Gopalakrishnan, G. Aravindan) and Karnataka (Girish Kasaravalli) flourished. Actors including Shabana Azmi, Smita Patil, Naseeruddin Shah and Om Puri became the movement's faces while also crossing into commercial cinema.

Characteristics and legacy

Parallel films tackled feudalism, caste, women's oppression, unemployment and political corruption, often using unknown actors, location shooting and open endings. The movement waned in the 1990s as television and market liberalisation changed funding, but its aesthetic fed into the "middle cinema" of directors like Hrishikesh Mukherjee and the independent and multiplex films of the 2000s.
