Contrabassoon

The contrabassoon sounds one octave below the bassoon.

The contrabassoon, also known as the double bassoon, is a double-reed woodwind instrument, a larger version of the bassoon, sounding an octave lower. Its tube, over 5.5 metres (18 ft) long, is folded several times within a body that stands about 1.4 metres high; it is usually made of maple, often with metal bells and fittings. Its lowest written note is typically B♭1 (sounding B♭0), near the bottom of the piano's range.

History and use

Precursors such as the Quartfagott existed from the 17th century, but the modern contrabassoon was developed in the 19th century, notably by the Heckel firm in Germany. It joined the standard symphony orchestra for large late-Romantic and modern works: Beethoven's Ninth Symphony (third movement), Brahms, Mahler, Strauss's Salome, Ravel's Boléro and Mother Goose, and Stravinsky's The Rite of Spring prominently feature it. Usually a member of the bassoon section switches to the instrument for specific passages.

Related instruments

The still rarer subcontrabassoon sounds an octave below the contrabassoon. In 2010 a purpose-built subcontrabassoon was completed in Germany, capable of notes down to C0.
