Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents

Bivens v. Six Unknown Named Agents, 403 U.S. 388 (1971), is a case in which the Supreme Court of the United States held that an individual whose Fourth Amendment rights are violated by federal agents may sue those agents for monetary damages in federal court, even though no statute expressly authorizes such a suit. Claims of this kind are now known as Bivens actions or Bivens claims.

Background

In 1965, agents of the Federal Bureau of Narcotics entered Webster Bivens's Brooklyn apartment without a warrant, arrested him, and searched his home; he was interrogated and strip-searched at the federal courthouse. Bivens sued the unnamed agents for damages, alleging a violation of his Fourth Amendment protection against unreasonable searches and seizures. The district court dismissed the complaint, and the Second Circuit affirmed, holding that the Fourth Amendment did not create a private right of action.

Decision

The Supreme Court reversed, 6–3. Writing for the majority, Justice William J. Brennan reasoned that the Fourth Amendment guarantees a right enforceable by damages, stating that where federally protected rights have been invaded, courts will be alert to adjust their remedies so as to grant necessary relief. Justice Harlan concurred, emphasizing that damages were the only effective remedy for such violations.

Legacy

The Court later extended Bivens in Davis v. Passman (1979, Fifth Amendment) and Carlson v. Green (1980, Eighth Amendment), but more recent decisions, including Ziglar v. Abbasi (2017) and Egbert v. Boule (2022), have sharply limited its extension to new contexts, describing expanding Bivens as a disfavored judicial activity.
