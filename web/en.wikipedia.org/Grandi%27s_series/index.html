Grandi's series

In mathematics, the infinite series 1 − 1 + 1 − 1 + …, also written

Σn=0∞ (−1)n,

is sometimes called Grandi's series, after the Italian mathematician, philosopher and priest Guido Grandi, who gave a memorable treatment of the series in 1703. The series is divergent: its sequence of partial sums (1, 0, 1, 0, …) does not converge to any limit. Nevertheless, by methods of summation for divergent series, such as Cesàro summation, it can be assigned the value 1/2.

History

Grandi considered the series in his 1703 book Quadratura circula et hyperbolae per infinitas hyperbolas geometrice exhibita. Grouping the terms in two ways appears to give contradictory results: (1 − 1) + (1 − 1) + … = 0, while 1 + (−1 + 1) + (−1 + 1) + … = 1. Grandi drew theological conclusions from the series, regarding it as a mathematical illustration of creation out of nothing (creatio ex nihilo). The series was debated by Leibniz, who defended the value 1/2 on probabilistic and geometric grounds, and it remained a standard example in discussions of divergent series through the work of Euler and into the nineteenth century.

Summation methods

Under Cesàro summation, the arithmetic means of the partial sums approach 1/2, so the Cesàro sum of Grandi's series is 1/2. The same value is given by Abel summation, since the power series 1 − x + x² − x³ + … = 1/(1 + x) tends to 1/2 as x approaches 1 from below. These generalised methods, which agree with ordinary summation on convergent series, were a major motivation for the modern theory of summability.

In education

Grandi's series is frequently used in teaching to show why infinite series cannot always be manipulated with the same algebraic freedom as finite sums, and to motivate the precise definition of convergence.
