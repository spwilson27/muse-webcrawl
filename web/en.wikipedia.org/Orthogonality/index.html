Orthogonality

In mathematics, orthogonality is the generalization of the geometric notion of perpendicularity to the linear algebra of bilinear forms. Two elements u and v of a vector space with bilinear form B are orthogonal when B(u, v) = 0. Depending on the bilinear form, the vector space may contain nonzero self-orthogonal vectors. In the case of function spaces, families of orthogonal functions are used to form a basis. The word comes from the Ancient Greek orthogonios, "right-angled".

In Euclidean space

In two and three dimensions, two vectors are orthogonal if their dot product is zero, which corresponds to meeting at a right angle. In n-dimensional Euclidean space, a set of vectors is orthogonal if every pair is orthogonal, and orthonormal if additionally each vector has unit length. An orthogonal basis can be built from any basis using the Gram–Schmidt process. A square matrix whose rows and columns form orthonormal sets is an orthogonal matrix, which represents rotations and reflections.

Orthogonal functions and generalizations

On an interval, functions are orthogonal when the integral of their product (possibly with a weight function) vanishes; classical families include orthogonal polynomials such as Legendre and Chebyshev polynomials, and the sine and cosine functions underlying Fourier series. In statistics, variables with zero correlation are informally described as orthogonal, and in computer science "orthogonality" describes design features that can be combined independently without side effects.
