Key signature

In Western musical notation, a key signature is a set of sharp (♯), flat (♭), or rarely natural (♮) symbols placed on the staff at the beginning of a section, indicating that certain lines and spaces are to be consistently raised or lowered from their natural pitches. It defines the key of the music by specifying the diatonic scale in use.

Conventions

Sharps are added in the order F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯, A♯, E♯, B♯; flats in the reverse order B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭, G♭, C♭, F♭ — following the circle of fifths. Each major key signature is shared by its relative minor, a minor third below. Standard notation uses at most seven sharps or flats; key signatures are normally not mixed, and a change of key within a piece is marked by a new signature, often preceded by naturals cancelling the old one.

Purpose

Key signatures reduce the need for accidentals, simplify reading, and signal the tonal centre. They apply for the whole piece or section unless cancelled, and in modern notation accidentals within a bar do not alter the signature for later bars.

History

Key signatures emerged in the 15th–16th centuries as the routine flattening of B in certain modes was written at the start of the staff; the modern system of signatures for all keys crystallized with the adoption of equal temperament and the major–minor system in the Baroque era.
