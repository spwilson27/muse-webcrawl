Polio eradication

Oral polio vaccine administered during a national immunization day.

Polio eradication, the global effort to permanently eliminate poliomyelitis (polio), is coordinated by the Global Polio Eradication Initiative (GPEI), launched in 1988 by the World Health Assembly. At that time polio was endemic in 125 countries and paralysed an estimated 350,000 children each year.

The vaccines

Two vaccines underpin the campaign: the inactivated polio vaccine (IPV), developed by Jonas Salk and first licensed in 1955, and the oral polio vaccine (OPV), developed by Albert Sabin and licensed in the early 1960s. OPV is cheaper, easy to administer as drops, and induces intestinal immunity, but can very rarely cause vaccine-associated paralytic polio or seed circulating vaccine-derived polioviruses, so eradication requires eventually phasing it out in favour of IPV.

Progress

Mass immunization campaigns, surveillance for acute flaccid paralysis, and environmental (sewage) sampling have reduced wild poliovirus cases by more than 99.9%. Two of the three wild serotypes have been certified eradicated: type 2 in 2015 and type 3 in 2019. Wild poliovirus type 1 remains endemic only in Afghanistan and Pakistan, though imported cases and vaccine-derived outbreaks continue to occur elsewhere, including periodic detections in countries long free of the disease.

If completed, polio would become only the second human disease eradicated, after smallpox (certified in 1980).

Challenges

The final phase has been hampered by conflict, inaccessible areas, vaccine misinformation, and attacks on vaccination workers, particularly in the remaining endemic regions.
