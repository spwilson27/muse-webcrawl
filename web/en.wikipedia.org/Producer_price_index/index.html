Producer price index

A producer price index (PPI) is a price index that measures the average change over time in the selling prices received by domestic producers for their output. In the United States, the PPI is published monthly by the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) and is one of the oldest continuous statistical series published by the federal government, with data extending back to 1902.

History

The index began as the Wholesale Price Index, one of the first economic indicators produced by the U.S. government. It was renamed the Producer Price Index in 1978 to emphasize that it measures prices from the perspective of the seller rather than the buyer, and to reflect a shift in weighting from commodity toward industry-based classification.

Coverage

The PPI family of indexes includes measures for final demand goods and services, intermediate demand, and stage-of-processing categories. The BLS surveys prices for thousands of items from establishments across mining, manufacturing, agriculture, forestry, fishing, and an expanding range of service industries. Prices are collected for the Tuesday of the week containing the 13th day of the month.

Uses

The PPI is used as an economic indicator of inflation at the producer level, often seen as a leading indicator of consumer price changes, since increases in producers' costs may be passed on to consumers. It is also used for contract escalation, deflating other economic series, and comparing industry price trends. It differs from the consumer price index (CPI), which measures prices paid by households for goods and services.

Core PPI

Analysts often focus on the "core" PPI, which excludes food and energy prices because of their volatility, to identify underlying inflation trends.
