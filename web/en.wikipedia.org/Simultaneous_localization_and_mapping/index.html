Simultaneous localization and mapping (SLAM) is the computational problem of constructing or updating a map of an unknown environment while simultaneously keeping track of an agent's location within it. It is central to autonomous robotics: a robot dropped into an unfamiliar place has no map and does not know where it is, yet must solve for both at once — a chicken-and-egg problem resolved by probabilistic estimation.

Approaches

Early solutions used extended Kalman filters (EKF-SLAM) to jointly estimate robot pose and landmark positions. Particle-filter methods such as FastSLAM factored the problem differently. Modern systems often formulate SLAM as a graph optimization: poses and landmarks are nodes whose constraints are minimized together, and revisiting a known place ("loop closure") corrects accumulated drift.

Visual SLAM

Visual SLAM uses cameras — monocular, stereo, or RGB-D — instead of lidar. Systems such as ORB-SLAM track distinctive image features to estimate camera motion and build sparse 3D maps in real time. Visual-inertial odometry, fusing camera data with inertial sensors, underlies the tracking in smartphones and AR headsets.

Applications

SLAM powers self-driving cars, warehouse and domestic robots (including robot vacuum cleaners that map homes), drones, underwater vehicles, augmented-reality devices, and planetary rovers.
