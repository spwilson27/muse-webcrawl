Cursive script (East Asian)

Cursive script (Chinese: 草書, cǎoshū; Japanese: 草書体, sōshotai), often translated as "grass script," is a style of Chinese calligraphy and handwriting used in East Asia. It is characterized by rapidly executed, abbreviated, and highly connected strokes in which characters are simplified — sometimes to the point of being difficult to read for the untrained — and is used both for rapid note-taking and as an expressive art form.

History

Cursive forms arose during the Han dynasty (206 BC – AD 220), as clerks sought to write the clerical script more quickly, and developed through the work of masters such as Zhang Zhi of the Eastern Han and, later, the Tang dynasty calligraphers Zhang Xu and Huaisu, famed for their wild "crazy cursive" (kuangcao). Cursive is conventionally divided into stages: draft cursive (zhangcao), in which characters remain separate, and modern cursive (jincao), in which characters may be linked in a continuous flow.

Characteristics

In cursive script, strokes are merged or omitted, dots and hooks substitute for longer movements, and the brush is lifted as little as possible. Individual characters may have several accepted cursive forms. Because legibility depends on familiarity with these conventions, cursive was historically the mark of the educated elite and a prized medium for calligraphy as fine art.

Influence

Cursive Chinese characters strongly influenced the development of Japanese writing: the hiragana syllabary derives from cursive simplifications of whole characters (via man'yōgana), and cursive styles remain important in Japanese calligraphy (shodō). Semi-cursive script (xingshu) occupies an intermediate position between the standard and fully cursive styles.
