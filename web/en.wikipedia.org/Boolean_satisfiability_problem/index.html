Boolean satisfiability problem

In logic and computer science, the Boolean satisfiability problem (sometimes called the propositional satisfiability problem and abbreviated SAT or B-SAT) is the problem of determining whether there exists an assignment of truth values to the variables of a given Boolean formula that makes the formula evaluate to true. A formula is then called satisfiable; otherwise it is unsatisfiable.

Complexity

SAT was the first problem shown to be NP-complete, in the Cook–Levin theorem (1971). This means that every problem in the complexity class NP can be reduced to SAT in polynomial time, and that no polynomial-time algorithm for SAT is known — finding one would prove P = NP. The special case 2-SAT, in which every clause has at most two literals, is solvable in polynomial time, while 3-SAT remains NP-complete.

Formulations

SAT is commonly studied for formulas in conjunctive normal form (CNF): a conjunction of clauses, each a disjunction of literals. Instances are written in the standard DIMACS format and may range from dozens to millions of variables and clauses.

SAT solvers and applications

Despite its worst-case complexity, modern SAT solvers based on the DPLL algorithm and conflict-driven clause learning (CDCL), enhanced with watched literals, clause minimisation and random restarts, routinely solve huge industrial instances. SAT technology underpins hardware and software verification, formal equivalence checking, planning in artificial intelligence, cryptanalysis, bioinformatics and automated theorem proving. Annual SAT competitions track progress in solver performance.
