Nuclear fusion

Nuclear fusion is a reaction in which two or more atomic nuclei combine to form one or more different atomic nuclei and subatomic particles (neutrons or protons). The difference in mass between the reactants and products is manifested as either the release or absorption of energy. Fusion is the process that powers the Sun and other stars: in stellar cores, hydrogen nuclei fuse into helium at temperatures of millions of kelvin, releasing energy described by Einstein's relation E = mc².

Process

Because atomic nuclei are positively charged, they repel each other electrostatically (the Coulomb barrier). Fusion requires temperatures or pressures high enough for nuclei to approach closely, aided by quantum tunnelling. In the Sun, the proton–proton chain dominates; in more massive stars the CNO cycle prevails. Iron-56 and nickel-62 have the highest binding energy per nucleon, so fusion of elements heavier than iron is generally endothermic.

Fusion for energy

The most accessible fuel cycle for terrestrial power is deuterium–tritium (D–T) fusion, which releases 17.6 MeV per reaction and a high-energy neutron. Approaches to controlled fusion include magnetic confinement—tokamaks such as ITER and stellarators—and inertial confinement using lasers, as at the US National Ignition Facility, which in December 2022 first achieved scientific breakeven (more fusion energy out than laser energy in). Sustained net-electric power generation remains an unsolved engineering goal pursued by public programmes and private ventures alike.

Applications and history

Uncontrolled fusion was first demonstrated in thermonuclear weapons in 1952. Fusion reactions are also used in neutron sources and in the production of certain isotopes. Key milestones include Mark Oliphant's 1932 laboratory fusion of hydrogen isotopes and Hans Bethe's 1939 explanation of stellar nucleosynthesis.
