Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) is a functional gastrointestinal disorder characterized by a group of symptoms that commonly include abdominal pain, bloating and changes in bowel habits — diarrhoea, constipation, or both — in the absence of detectable structural disease. It is a chronic, relapsing condition that substantially affects quality of life but does not increase the risk of colorectal cancer.

Signs and symptoms

The primary symptoms are recurrent abdominal pain associated with defecation or a change in stool frequency or form. Subtypes are classified by predominant bowel habit: IBS with constipation (IBS-C), IBS with diarrhoea (IBS-D), mixed (IBS-M), and unclassified (IBS-U). Symptoms often worsen after meals and with stress. Many patients also report fatigue, nausea, and comorbid anxiety or depression.

Causes and pathophysiology

The cause is not fully understood. Proposed mechanisms include visceral hypersensitivity, altered gut motility, low-grade intestinal inflammation, changes in the gut microbiome, bile acid malabsorption, and dysfunction of the gut–brain axis. Onset may follow an episode of gastroenteritis (post-infectious IBS). Genetic and psychosocial factors also contribute.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on symptom criteria (the Rome IV criteria) and the exclusion of alarm features such as rectal bleeding, weight loss, anaemia, or family history of colorectal cancer or inflammatory bowel disease. Coeliac disease and lactose intolerance should be considered and excluded where appropriate.

Management

Treatment is symptom-focused: dietary modification (including a low-FODMAP diet under dietitian supervision), soluble fibre, antispasmodics, loperamide or laxatives as appropriate, and, for some patients, low-dose tricyclic antidepressants, gut-directed hypnotherapy, or cognitive behavioural therapy. Education and reassurance are central to care.
