Polymorphism (materials science)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Diamond and graphite: two polymorphs of carbon

In materials science, polymorphism is the ability of a solid material to exist in more than one form or crystal structure. The different structures of a polymorphic substance are called polymorphs. Polymorphism can be found in any crystalline material, including polymers, minerals and metals; when it occurs in elemental solids it is traditionally called allotropy.

Examples

The classic example is carbon, which occurs as diamond (cubic), graphite (hexagonal layers), fullerenes and graphene — allotropes with dramatically different hardness, conductivity and appearance. Calcium carbonate crystallises as both calcite and aragonite. Silica (SiO2) has several polymorphs, including quartz, tridymite and cristobalite. Iron changes crystal structure with temperature (ferrite and austenite), a transformation central to steel heat treatment.

Thermodynamics and stability

Polymorphs differ in free energy, so at a given temperature and pressure usually only one is thermodynamically stable; others are metastable and may persist because conversion is slow. Pairs of polymorphs are enantiotropic if they interconvert reversibly at a transition temperature, and monotropic if one form is always more stable. Ostwald's rule of stages observes that a system often crystallises into the least stable form first and then transforms stepwise toward the stable one.

Importance

Polymorphism is of major practical importance in pharmaceuticals, where different polymorphs of a drug can differ in solubility, bioavailability and shelf life — as famously occurred when the HIV drug ritonavir developed an unexpected, less soluble polymorph in 1998. It also matters in pigments, explosives, foodstuffs (such as the crystal forms of cocoa butter in chocolate) and semiconductor science.
