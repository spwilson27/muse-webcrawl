SOSUS

SOSUS (Sound Surveillance System) was a passive sonar system developed by the United States Navy during the Cold War to track Soviet submarines. It consisted of chains of hydrophones laid on the ocean floor at strategic choke points — notably the GIUK gap between Greenland, Iceland and the United Kingdom — connected by undersea cable to shore stations called Naval Facilities (NAVFACs), where low-frequency recordings were analysed on paper spectrograms known as lofargrams.

SOSUS arrays guarded the Atlantic choke points used by Soviet submarines.

Development

SOSUS grew out of research begun in 1949 into low-frequency sound propagation in the deep sound channel (the SOFAR channel). The first experimental array was installed off Eleuthera in the Bahamas in 1951, and the first production arrays followed in 1954. The system proved capable of detecting submarines at ranges of hundreds of kilometres: in 1962 a SOSUS station detected a Soviet diesel-electric submarine near the Norwegian coast, the system's first detection of a Soviet boat in the Atlantic.

Operations and declassification

SOSUS data cued patrol aircraft and attack submarines, and helped the US Navy maintain tracks on Soviet ballistic-missile submarines throughout the Cold War. The system's very name remained classified until 1991, when the mission was publicly acknowledged near the end of the Cold War. Since then SOSUS arrays have been partly decommissioned and partly repurposed; their data have been used by scientists to study whale migration and vocalisation, undersea earthquakes and ocean temperature change. SOSUS, together with mobile towed arrays, was folded into the Integrated Undersea Surveillance System (IUSS).
