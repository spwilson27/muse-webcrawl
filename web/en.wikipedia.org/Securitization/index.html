Securitization

Securitization is the financial practice of pooling various types of contractual debt — such as residential mortgages, commercial mortgages, auto loans, or credit card debt — and selling their related cash flows to third-party investors as securities, which may be described as bonds, pass-through securities, or collateralized debt obligations (CDOs). Investors are repaid from the principal and interest cash flows collected from the underlying debt, redistributed through the structure created by the securitization.

Structure

In a typical transaction, an originator (such as a bank) transfers a pool of loans to a special purpose vehicle (SPV), a separate legal entity that issues securities backed by the pool. The securities are usually divided into tranches with different levels of risk and return; senior tranches are paid first and receive higher credit ratings, while junior or "equity" tranches absorb losses first. Credit ratings from rating agencies and sometimes credit enhancement support the marketability of the securities.

Benefits and risks

Securitization lets lenders remove assets from their balance sheets, freeing capital and transferring credit risk to investors; it also creates investment products matched to different risk appetites. However, separating origination from risk-bearing weakened lending standards in the United States housing market in the 2000s: complex, opaque chains of mortgage-backed securities and CDOs, combined with inflated ratings, were a central mechanism of the 2007–2008 financial crisis.

Other meanings

In international relations, "securitization" also names a theory of the Copenhagen School: the process by which an issue is presented as an existential threat, allowing extraordinary measures beyond normal politics.
