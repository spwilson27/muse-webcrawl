Measurement in quantum mechanics

In quantum physics, a measurement is the testing or manipulation of a physical system to yield a numerical result. The predictions that quantum physics makes about measurements are in general probabilistic. The mathematical tools for making predictions about what measurement outcomes may occur were developed during the 20th century and make use of linear algebra and functional analysis.

The Stern–Gerlach experiment is a canonical example of a quantum measurement.

Mathematical framework

Quantum systems are described by state vectors (wave functions) in a Hilbert space. Physical quantities are represented by Hermitian operators called observables. According to the Born rule, measuring an observable yields one of its eigenvalues, with probability given by the squared magnitude of the projection of the state onto the corresponding eigenspace. After the measurement, the state is taken to be the corresponding eigenstate — the so-called collapse of the wave function.

A more general formalism uses positive operator-valued measures (POVMs), which describe statistics of measurements without requiring an immediate repeat measurement to give the same result. Density operators extend the description to statistical mixtures and subsystems of entangled systems.

The measurement problem

The measurement problem is the problem of how (or whether) wave function collapse occurs. The unitary evolution of a closed system described by the Schrödinger equation appears to conflict with the definite, single outcomes observed in experiments. Different interpretations of quantum mechanics — Copenhagen, many-worlds, de Broglie–Bohm, objective-collapse theories, and QBism among them — answer this question in different ways, while agreeing on experimental predictions.

Weak and protective measurements

Weak measurements extract only partial information about a system, disturbing it only slightly; repeating many weak measurements can reveal average values, including "weak values" that may lie outside an observable's eigenvalue range. Protective measurements, by contrast, aim to measure the expectation value of an observable on a single system while protecting its state from disturbance.
