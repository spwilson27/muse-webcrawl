Substitution reaction

A substitution reaction (also known as a single displacement reaction or single substitution reaction) is a chemical reaction during which one functional group or atom in a chemical compound is replaced by another functional group or atom. Substitution reactions are fundamental in organic chemistry, where they are classified by the character of the attacking reagent.

Types

Nucleophilic substitution occurs when the incoming group is a nucleophile; the classic mechanisms are SN1 (unimolecular, proceeding through a carbocation intermediate with a rate-determining ionization step) and SN2 (bimolecular, a concerted backside attack that inverts stereochemistry — the Walden inversion). Electrophilic substitution, such as electrophilic aromatic substitution (nitration, halogenation, Friedel–Crafts reactions), involves attack by an electrophile. Radical substitution proceeds via free radicals, as in the chlorination of alkanes.

Factors

Reactivity depends on the substrate structure, the strength of the nucleophile or electrophile, the quality of the leaving group, solvent polarity, and temperature. Substitution competes with elimination reactions, especially for tertiary substrates and strong bases.

Inorganic chemistry

In coordination chemistry, ligand substitution reactions replace ligands around a metal centre, proceeding by associative or dissociative mechanisms.
