Phasor

RepresentsA sinusoidal function of time
EncodesAmplitude and phase
Introduced byCharles Proteus Steinmetz (1893)

In physics and engineering, a phasor (a portmanteau of phase vector) is a complex number representing a sinusoidal function whose amplitude and phase are time-invariant and whose angular frequency is shared with the other quantities under analysis. Phasors convert problems involving sinusoids into algebraic problems in the complex plane, greatly simplifying the analysis of alternating-current circuits and of wave phenomena.

A phasor diagram: arrows of length equal to amplitude, rotating at the common frequency

A sinusoid A cos(ωt + φ) is represented by the phasor A ejφ. Differentiation in the time domain corresponds to multiplying the phasor by jω, and integration to dividing by jω, so that the impedance of an inductor becomes jωL and that of a capacitor 1/(jωC); resistor, inductor and capacitor voltages can then be combined using ordinary complex arithmetic under Kirchhoff's laws. The technique was formalised by Charles Proteus Steinmetz in 1893 and became fundamental to electrical engineering. Phasors are drawn as rotating arrows in a phasor diagram; the real signal is the projection of the arrow onto one axis. Related transforms — the Laplace and Fourier transforms — generalise the same idea.

Applications

Beyond AC circuit theory, phasors are used in optics and wave mechanics to add waves of the same frequency, in signal processing, and in the analysis of three-phase power systems, where balanced phase voltages appear as three phasors separated by 120 degrees.
