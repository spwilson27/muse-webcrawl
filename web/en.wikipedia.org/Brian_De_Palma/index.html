Brian Russell De Palma (born September 11, 1940) is an American film director and screenwriter. A leading figure of the New Hollywood generation, he is known for psychological and suspense thrillers marked by virtuoso visual set pieces, split-screen and split-diopter shots, long tracking shots and slow motion, and by frequent homages to Alfred Hitchcock. With a career spanning over 60 years, he has directed films that have become both commercial hits and cult classics.

Early career

De Palma was born in Newark, New Jersey, and studied physics before turning to film. His early independent features, including Greetings (1968) and Hi, Mom! (1970) starring Robert De Niro, were countercultural satires. He gained critical attention with the horror-thriller Sisters (1972), beginning his long association with Hitchcockian suspense and the composer Bernard Herrmann.

Breakthrough and major works

His adaptation of Stephen King's Carrie (1976) was a major critical and commercial success. He followed it with the thrillers Obsession (1976), The Fury (1978), Dressed to Kill (1980), Blow Out (1981) — now widely regarded as one of his masterpieces — and Body Double (1984). He also directed the crime epic Scarface (1983), written by Oliver Stone and starring Al Pacino, which grew from mixed initial reviews into a defining cultural phenomenon, and the blockbuster The Untouchables (1987), which won Sean Connery an Academy Award. Later films include Carlito's Way (1993), the first Mission: Impossible (1996), a major box-office success, Snake Eyes (1998) and Femme Fatale (2002).

Style and reception

De Palma's work has long divided critics: detractors accuse him of derivative pastiche and sensationalism, while admirers and later filmmakers hail his formal virtuosity and his themes of voyeurism, guilt and doubles. His films are frequently cited as influences by directors such as Quentin Tarantino. The 2015 documentary De Palma, directed by Noah Baumbach and Jake Paltrow, surveyed his career in his own words.
