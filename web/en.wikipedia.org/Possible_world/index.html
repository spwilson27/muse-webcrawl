Possible world

A possible world is a complete and consistent way things could have been. Possible worlds are central to modal logic and the philosophy of language, where they provide a semantics for statements about necessity and possibility: a proposition is necessarily true if it is true in every possible world, and possibly true if it is true in at least one. Among all possible worlds, one is the actual world — the way things in fact are.

History

The idea descends from Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, who held that God contemplated infinitely many possible worlds and actualised the best of them. The modern technical notion emerged in the 20th century with the development of modal logic by Rudolf Carnap, Saul Kripke, and others. Kripke's possible-worlds semantics (from 1959 onward) defined validity in terms of accessibility relations between worlds and underpinned his influential account of naming and necessity in Naming and Necessity (1972).

Uses

  • Modal logic: worlds serve as points of evaluation for modal operators (□ and ◇).
  • Counterfactuals: following David Lewis and Robert Stalnaker, a counterfactual "if A were the case, C would follow" is true when C holds in the closest possible worlds in which A holds.
  • Conditionals and intensional semantics: propositions are modelled as sets of worlds — those in which the proposition is true.
  • Philosophy of mind and language: two-dimensional semantics uses worlds to analyse meaning and a priori truth.

Ontological status

Philosophers dispute what possible worlds are. David Lewis defended modal realism: all possible worlds exist as concrete universes, spatiotemporally isolated from ours, and "actual" is indexical, like "here." Most philosophers prefer ersatz or actualist accounts, treating worlds as abstract objects — maximal consistent sets of propositions, states of affairs, or properties the world could instantiate. Others, in the tradition of linguistic ersatzism, treat them as constructions out of language. The debate remains a major topic in metaphysics.
