The New Zealand Company, chartered in the United Kingdom, was a company that played a central role in the systematic colonisation of New Zealand. It was formed in 1839 from the merging of the New Zealand Association (established 1837) and another scheme, received a royal charter in 1841, and was driven by the ideas of Edward Gibbon Wakefield, who advocated "systematic colonisation": emigrants would buy land at a "sufficient price", generating funds to carry labourers to the colony, so that settlement would reproduce a balanced cross-section of British society.

Settlements

Racing to establish facts on the ground ahead of formal annexation, the company's ship Tory sailed for New Zealand in May 1839, and settlers began arriving at Port Nicholson—Wellington—in January 1840. The company went on to found Nelson (1841), New Plymouth (1841), and Whanganui, and later sponsored the Otago settlement at Dunedin (1848) and the Canterbury settlement at Christchurch (1850). Through its land sales it dispatched around 13,500 emigrants to New Zealand.

Land purchases and conflict

The company's land "purchases" from Māori were hurried, poorly translated, and disputed, and many transactions were later found invalid or grossly under-compensated. Quarrels over the company's claims contributed to violence, notably the Wairau Affray of 1843, and to wider tensions surrounding the Treaty of Waitangi (1840), by which Britain assumed sovereignty. Financial difficulties, criticism of its dealings, and conflict with colonial governors plagued the company throughout its existence.

Dissolution

Deep in debt, the company surrendered its charter in 1850 in exchange for a government loan and a guaranteed return on money it had spent; it lingered in a caretaker role until final dissolution in 1858. Its planned towns and emigration schemes nonetheless left a permanent imprint on New Zealand's demography and urban geography.
