Euripides (Greek: Εὐριπίδης; c. 480 – c. 406 BC) was a Greek tragedian of classical Athens. Along with Aeschylus and Sophocles, he is one of the three ancient Greek tragedians for whom any plays have survived in full. Some ancient scholars attributed 95 plays to him, but modern scholarship gives him at most 90; of these, 18 or 19 have survived nearly complete (one of them, Rhesus, is probably spurious).

Life

Traditional accounts place his birth on Salamis around 480 BC, possibly on the day of the great naval battle. He competed in the City Dionysia from 455 BC, winning first prize only four times in his lifetime (plus once posthumously, with the tetralogy containing The Bacchae and Iphigenia at Aulis). In old age he accepted the invitation of King Archelaus of Macedon, where he died in 406 BC.

Drama

Euripides is known for the psychological depth and unsettling realism of his characters — particularly his strong, suffering heroines (Medea, Electra, Helen, Andromache) — and for his sceptical, often critical treatment of the gods and of Athenian war policy (notably in The Trojan Women, produced during the Peloponnesian War). He made frequent use of the chorus as a commentator on contemporary concerns and employed the deus ex machina (a god lowered by crane to resolve the plot) more than his predecessors. His plays influenced New Comedy, Seneca, and through them the Renaissance and modern drama.
