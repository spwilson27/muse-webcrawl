Non-small-cell lung cancer

Non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), or non-small-cell lung carcinoma, is any type of epithelial lung cancer other than small-cell lung cancer (SCLC). NSCLC accounts for about 85% of all lung cancers. As a class, NSCLCs are relatively insensitive to chemotherapy, compared to small-cell carcinoma.

Types

The largest subgroups are adenocarcinoma (about 40% of lung cancers), squamous-cell carcinoma, and large-cell carcinoma. Adenocarcinoma is the most common type in non-smokers and is often found in the outer regions of the lung; squamous-cell carcinoma arises more centrally and is strongly linked to smoking.

Causes and diagnosis

Smoking is the dominant risk factor, alongside radon exposure, asbestos and air pollution. Diagnosis rests on imaging and tissue biopsy, with staging by CT and PET scans. Molecular testing for mutations in genes such as EGFR, ALK and KRAS, and for PD-L1 expression, guides treatment.

Treatment

Early-stage disease may be cured by surgical resection, sometimes with adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Advanced disease is treated with chemotherapy, targeted therapies matched to tumour mutations (such as EGFR inhibitors), and immunotherapy with checkpoint inhibitors, which have substantially improved survival for many patients.
