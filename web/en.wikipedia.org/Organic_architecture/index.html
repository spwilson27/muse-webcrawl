Organic architecture

Organic architecture is a philosophy of architecture which promotes harmony between human habitation and the natural world. This is achieved through design approaches that aim to be sympathetic and well-integrated with a site, so buildings, furnishings, and surroundings become part of a unified, interrelated composition.

Origin of the term

The term "organic architecture" was coined by Frank Lloyd Wright (1867–1959). Wright adapted the concept of the "organic" from his mentor Louis Sullivan, who spoke of an architecture in which form follows function. For Wright, however, "organic" meant something broader: a building should grow from its site and materials, and the whole should be greater than, and express, the sum of its parts. He demonstrated the philosophy in works such as Fallingwater (1935), built over a waterfall in Pennsylvania, and in his Usonian houses.

Principles

Wright summarized organic architecture as design in which the building and site are one, materials are used according to their nature, space within is the reality of the building, and ornament is integral rather than applied. In his 1908 essay "In the Cause of Architecture" and later writings he argued that a building should appear to grow naturally from its environment, as a tree grows from the soil.

Other practitioners

Architects associated with related ideas include Antoni Gaudí, Alvar Aalto, Louis Sullivan, Bruce Goff, and later figures such as Javier Senosiain, whose "bio-architecture" explores zoomorphic forms, and Kendrick Bangs Kellogg. The Finnish architect Alvar Aalto's Villa Mairea (1939) is often cited for its sensitivity to materials and landscape. In the 21st century, the term is sometimes applied to buildings that use biomimicry or responsive environmental design.
