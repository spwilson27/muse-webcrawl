Abrikosov vortex

An Abrikosov vortex (also called a fluxon) is a vortex of supercurrent in a type-II superconductor. Each vortex carries a single quantum of magnetic flux, Φ0 = h/2e, and consists of a normal (non-superconducting) core of radius roughly the coherence length ξ, around which superconducting currents circulate over a distance of roughly the penetration depth λ.

Formation

In a type-II superconductor subjected to a magnetic field between the lower and upper critical fields, it is energetically favourable for magnetic flux to penetrate the material in the form of quantised vortices rather than being expelled entirely (the Meissner effect of type-I superconductors). Alexei Abrikosov predicted this behaviour in 1957 using Ginzburg–Landau theory, showing that the vortices arrange themselves into a regular triangular lattice — the Abrikosov lattice. The work contributed to his share of the 2003 Nobel Prize in Physics.

Properties and applications

Vortices interact repulsively with one another and are pinned by material defects; if a transport current exerts a Lorentz force exceeding the pinning force, vortices move, producing resistance and destroying the zero-resistance state — a key limitation in superconducting magnet and cable engineering. Controlling vortex pinning, for example by introducing nanoscale defects, is essential to high-field superconducting applications such as MRI magnets and fusion reactors. Vortices can be directly imaged by magnetic decoration, scanning tunnelling microscopy, and neutron scattering.
