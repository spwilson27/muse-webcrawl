Coumarin

Coumarin
Chemical formulaC9H6O2
Molar mass146.14 g/mol
AppearanceColourless to white crystals
OdourSweet, like new-mown hay or vanilla
Melting point71 °C (160 °F)

Coumarin is a fragrant organic chemical compound in the benzopyrone chemical class, found in many plants, notably in high concentration in the tonka bean, vanilla grass, sweet woodruff, and cassia cinnamon. It has a sweet odour, readily recognised as the scent of new-mown hay.

History and synthesis

Coumarin was first isolated from tonka beans in 1820 by A. Vogel and independently by Nicolas-Jean-Baptiste-Gaston Guibourt, who named it after coumarou, the French word for the tonka bean. It was first synthesised in 1868 by William Henry Perkin, and became a mainstay of perfumery after its use in the landmark fragrance Fougère Royale in 1882.

Uses

Coumarin is widely used in perfumes, scented products, and as a flavouring in some contexts, though its use in food is restricted in many jurisdictions because of moderate toxicity to the liver and kidneys at high doses. It is also the parent compound of the coumarin family of chemicals, which includes the anticoagulant drug warfarin and related rodenticides.

Safety

The European Food Safety Authority has set a tolerable daily intake of 0.1 mg of coumarin per kilogram of body weight. High-coumarin cassia cinnamon is the main dietary source for most consumers.
