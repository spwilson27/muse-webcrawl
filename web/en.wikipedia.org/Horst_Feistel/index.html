Horst Feistel (30 January 1915 – 14 November 1990) was a German-American cryptographer who worked at IBM and pioneered the design of modern block ciphers. His name is attached to the Feistel network, the structural design underlying the Data Encryption Standard (DES) and many other ciphers.

Life

Born in Berlin, Feistel emigrated to the United States in 1934 and became a US citizen in 1944. He worked on identification friend-or-foe systems and radar countermeasures during the war, then at the Air Force Cambridge Research Center and MIT Lincoln Laboratory, before joining IBM's Thomas J. Watson Research Center in 1968.

Cryptography work

At IBM, Feistel led the project that produced the Lucifer cipher in the early 1970s — one of the first civilian block ciphers. A refined descendant of Lucifer was adopted in 1977 as DES, the US federal standard. The Feistel structure splits a block into halves and applies a round function alternately, making encryption and decryption nearly identical in implementation.

Legacy

Feistel networks remain a foundational construction in symmetric cryptography, used in ciphers such as DES, Blowfish and Camellia, and analysed extensively in the Luby–Rackoff results on pseudorandom permutations.
