The Athenaeum was a British literary magazine published in London from 1828 to 1921. Founded by James Silk Buckingham, its full early title was The Athenaeum, Journal of Literature, Science, and the Fine Arts. Under its second proprietor-editor Charles Wentworth Dilke (editor from 1830), it became one of the most influential literary periodicals of the Victorian age — a weekly review covering books, science, art, music and drama, noted for its unsigned reviews and its insistence on paying contributors well.

History

The paper was quick to recognise new talent: it reviewed the young John Keats's circle, championed early work by many Victorian authors, and counted among its contributors and reviewers figures such as Thomas Carlyle, John Stuart Mill, and later Gerald Gould and others. In the mid-19th century, under editors including John Doran, the Athenaeum was at the height of its authority; its columns carried important archaeological and scientific news, and its review pages influenced the fortunes of books throughout the century. Its scientific coverage published contributions from figures such as Charles Darwin's correspondents and early reports on photography and evolution.

In the early 20th century the journal was edited from 1919 by John Middleton Murry, publishing the modern generation — Katherine Mansfield, T. S. Eliot, D. H. Lawrence and Virginia Woolf contributed during its last years. In 1921 the Athenaeum was bought by J. C. Squire interests and merged into The Nation, forming The Nation and Athenaeum, which itself merged into the New Statesman in 1931 (hence the latter's historic subtitle). The magazine is not connected with the London club of the same name, the Athenaeum Club.
