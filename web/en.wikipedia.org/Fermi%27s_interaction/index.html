Fermi's interaction

Fermi's interaction, proposed by Enrico Fermi in 1933–34, was the first successful theoretical description of the weak interaction, formulated to explain beta decay. In Fermi's theory, four fermions interact directly at a single point: a neutron transforms into a proton while creating an electron and an antineutrino.

Theory

Fermi introduced a coupling constant, now called the Fermi constant GF, characterizing the strength of the interaction. His golden rule for transition rates successfully predicted electron energy spectra and lifetimes of beta emitters. Fermi modeled the interaction on electromagnetism, treating it as a vector current coupling, and included the then-hypothetical neutrino proposed by Wolfgang Pauli.

Development

Fermi's theory was a four-fermion contact interaction and is non-renormalizable, valid only at low energies. It was refined into the V−A theory by George Sudarshan, Robert Marshak, Richard Feynman, and Murray Gell-Mann in the 1950s, incorporating parity violation discovered in 1956–57. The Fermi interaction was ultimately superseded by electroweak theory, in which the weak force is mediated by the massive W and Z bosons; at energies far below the W mass, the boson-exchange picture reduces approximately to Fermi's point interaction.
