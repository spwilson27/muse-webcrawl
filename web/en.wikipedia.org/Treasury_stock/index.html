Treasury stock

Issued shares consist of shares outstanding plus treasury shares held by the company itself.

Treasury stock (also known as treasury shares) is stock that was previously issued and outstanding but has been repurchased by the issuing company from shareholders. The shares are held in the company's own treasury: they remain legally issued but are no longer outstanding, carry no voting rights, and receive no dividends. They may later be resold into the market, used for employee share schemes or acquisition consideration, or formally retired (cancelled).

Accounting treatment

On the balance sheet, treasury stock is presented as a contra-equity account, reducing total shareholders' equity by the amount paid to repurchase the shares. Under the cost method, the shares are recorded at repurchase cost; no gain or loss is recognised in the income statement when the shares are reissued — differences are adjusted through share premium (additional paid-in capital) or retained earnings. Because treasury shares are excluded from the count of shares outstanding, buybacks mechanically increase earnings per share.

Reasons for buybacks

Companies repurchase shares to return cash to shareholders as an alternative to dividends, to signal management's view that the stock is undervalued, to offset dilution from employee option plans, to support the share price, or to deter hostile takeovers by reducing the free float. The legality and limits of buybacks vary by jurisdiction: in the United States they are broadly permitted, while many other countries restrict them by capital-maintenance rules, shareholder approval requirements, or caps on the proportion of capital that may be held in treasury.
