Mass (music)

The Mass (Latin: missa), in music, is a choral composition that sets the invariable portions of the Eucharistic liturgy (chiefly that of the Catholic Church, but also Anglican and Lutheran traditions) to music. Musical Masses take their name from the Catholic liturgy called the Mass, and most set the Latin texts of the Ordinary: Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus (with Benedictus), and Agnus Dei.

The polyphonic Mass was the central genre of Renaissance sacred music.

Structure: the Ordinary

Because their texts never change, the five sections of the Ordinary can be composed as a unified musical cycle:

  • Kyrie eleison — a Greek prayer for mercy, usually in threefold form.
  • Gloria — a hymn of praise ("Glory to God in the highest").
  • Credo — the Nicene Creed, the longest text of the Ordinary.
  • Sanctus and Benedictus — the acclamation "Holy, holy, holy" and "Blessed is he who comes".
  • Agnus Dei — "Lamb of God", concluding with a plea for peace.

Related forms include the Requiem Mass (Mass for the dead, which substitutes other texts such as the Dies irae) and the short Mass (missa brevis), which compresses the setting for practical use.

History

Monophonic settings of the Mass texts survive in Gregorian chant. Polyphonic settings developed in the fourteenth century; Guillaume de Machaut's Messe de Nostre Dame (c. 1365) is the earliest complete Mass cycle by a single composer. In the Renaissance the cyclic Mass — unified by a borrowed melody (cantus firmus) or a parody of an existing polyphonic work — became the prestige genre of composers such as Josquin des Prez, Palestrina, Lassus, and Byrd. The Baroque era added instruments and concertante styles, culminating in Bach's Mass in B minor. Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven (Missa solemnis), and Schubert wrote large-scale orchestral Masses, and the tradition continued through Bruckner, Stravinsky, Britten, and many contemporary composers, often as concert works rather than liturgical music.
