Federal Trade Commission

The Federal Trade Commission (FTC) is an independent agency of the United States government whose principal missions are promoting consumer protection and eliminating anti-competitive business practices. It was established in 1914 by the Federal Trade Commission Act, signed by President Woodrow Wilson. The commission is headed by five commissioners, appointed by the president with Senate confirmation for seven-year terms; no more than three may belong to the same party.

Mandate

Section 5 of the FTC Act prohibits "unfair or deceptive acts or practices." The Bureau of Consumer Protection enforces this against false advertising, identity theft, telemarketing fraud (including the Do Not Call Registry) and privacy violations, while the Bureau of Competition enforces the antitrust laws — sharing jurisdiction with the Department of Justice — and reviews mergers under the Hart–Scott–Rodino Act. The Bureau of Economics supports both missions with research.

History

The FTC was a Progressive Era reform, created amid concern about trusts following the dissolution of Standard Oil. Its powers were significantly expanded by the Wheeler–Lea Act of 1938, which added the prohibition on unfair or deceptive practices and gave it authority over false advertising of food, drugs and cosmetics. In the 21st century the commission has become a leading privacy regulator, bringing enforcement actions over data security and dark patterns, and has contested major technology mergers.
