The Tunnel of Eupalinos or Eupalinian aqueduct (Greek: Ευπαλίνειον όρυγμα) is a tunnel of 1,036 m (3,399 ft) length running through Mount Kastro in Samos, Greece, built in the 6th century BC to serve as an aqueduct. It is the second known tunnel in history excavated simultaneously from both ends, and the first with a geometry-based approach. Today it is a popular tourist attraction and was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1992 together with the Pythagoreion and the Heraion of Samos.

History

The tunnel was commissioned under the tyrant Polycrates (reigned c. 538–522 BC) and designed by the engineer Eupalinos of Megara. The historian Herodotus described it as one of the three greatest engineering feats of the Greeks, alongside the harbour mole and the temple of Hera at Samos. It carried water from a spring on the far side of Mount Kastro to the ancient city of Pythagoreion through a channel dug below the main tunnel's floor.

Construction

Two teams dug from opposite ends of the mountain, meeting near the middle with a lateral offset of only about 5 metres and a vertical difference of under a metre — a remarkable achievement of surveying using geometry, right-angle constructions, and dog-leg corrections near the junction. The main tunnel is roughly 1.8 m by 1.8 m in cross-section; the water channel below deepens from about 4 m at the north end to about 8.5 m at the south end to maintain a steady gradient. The aqueduct remained in use for about a thousand years before silting up in the Byzantine era.

Rediscovery

The tunnel was rediscovered in 1882–1884, after the French scholar Victor Guérin had located the spring in 1853. It was cleared and studied by the German Archaeological Institute in the 1970s and restored for visitors in the 2010s.
