Constitutional court

Constitutional court
PurposeJudicial review of legislation and protection of the constitution
First exampleAustria (1920), designed by Hans Kelsen
Notable examplesFederal Constitutional Court of Germany; Constitutional Council of France; Constitutional Court of South Africa

A constitutional court is a high court that deals primarily with constitutional law. Its main authority is to rule on whether laws that are challenged are in fact unconstitutional, i.e. whether they conflict with constitutionally established rules, rights and freedoms, and to annul such laws with general effect.

The centralised model

The constitutional court model is often called the European or Kelsenian model, after the Austrian jurist Hans Kelsen, who designed the Austrian Constitutional Court of 1920 — the first specialised constitutional court. In this centralised system, ordinary courts cannot themselves strike down statutes; instead, a single specialised court holds a monopoly on constitutional review. Questions may reach it through referrals from ordinary courts, petitions from political institutions, or — as in Germany and Spain — individual constitutional complaints.

The decentralised model

By contrast, in the United States and other common-law jurisdictions, judicial review is decentralised: any court may hold a law unconstitutional in a concrete case, with a supreme court as the final arbiter. Some countries mix the two, for example by combining a supreme court with the power to give abstract rulings.

Powers and composition

Constitutional courts typically decide disputes between organs of state, review elections and referendums, rule on the constitutionality of political parties and impeachment, and protect fundamental rights. Their judges are usually appointed by political bodies (parliaments, heads of state) for long, non-renewable terms, a design intended to balance democratic legitimacy with judicial independence — and one that periodically draws the courts into political controversy.
