Fertility rate

The total fertility rate (TFR) of a population is the average number of children that would be born to a woman over her lifetime if she experienced the exact current age-specific fertility rates through her lifetime and lived from birth until the end of her reproductive life. It is one of the most widely used indicators in demography.

Replacement level

A total fertility rate of about 2.1 children per woman is called the replacement level in developed countries: at this level, each generation exactly replaces itself, ignoring migration and mortality differences. The figure exceeds 2.0 because slightly more boys than girls are born and some children die before reaching reproductive age. In countries with high mortality, replacement fertility can be considerably higher.

Global trends

The world total fertility rate fell from around 5 children per woman in the 1960s to roughly 2.3 in the early 2020s. Many countries in Europe and East Asia have rates well below replacement — South Korea's fell below 0.8, among the lowest recorded — while much of sub-Saharan Africa retains rates above 3 or 4. The demographic transition model associates fertility decline with urbanization, education (especially of women), contraception availability, and falling child mortality.

Measurement

TFR is a synthetic period measure: it sums the age-specific fertility rates observed in a single year. It can be distorted by changes in the timing of childbearing (tempo effects), so demographers also study cohort fertility — the completed family size of real generations of women.

Consequences of low fertility

Sustained below-replacement fertility leads eventually to population decline and ageing, raising policy concerns about pensions, labor supply, and elder care; high fertility raises concerns about education, employment, and resource pressure. Governments in a number of countries have adopted pro-natalist policies with mixed results.
