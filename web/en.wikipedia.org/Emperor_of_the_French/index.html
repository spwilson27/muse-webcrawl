Emperor of the French (French: Empereur des Français) was the title of the monarch and supreme ruler of the First French Empire (1804–1814, 1815) and the Second French Empire (1852–1870). Only two men held the title as reigning emperors: Napoleon I (Napoleon Bonaparte) and his nephew Napoleon III. Napoleon's son, Napoleon II, was proclaimed emperor in 1815 but never actually ruled.

Origins of the title

The title was deliberately framed as "Emperor of the French" rather than "Emperor of France", on the model of "King of the French" adopted in 1791: it implied that the monarch ruled by the will of the people rather than owning the territory. Napoleon, then First Consul, was proclaimed "Napoleon, by the grace of God and the constitutions of the Republic, Emperor of the French" by a senatus-consulte of 18 May 1804, ratified by plebiscite, and crowned on 2 December 1804 at Notre-Dame de Paris, famously taking the crown from Pope Pius VII and placing it on his own head.

First Empire

Napoleon I reigned from 1804 until his abdication on 6 April 1814, and again during the Hundred Days in 1815, ending with the defeat at Waterloo and his second abdication on 22 June 1815. His son was proclaimed Napoleon II but the title passed in practice to Louis XVIII of the restored monarchy.

Second Empire

Louis-Napoleon Bonaparte, elected President of the Second Republic in 1848, seized power in the coup of 2 December 1851 and proclaimed himself Napoleon III, Emperor of the French, on 2 December 1852. He reigned until his capture by Prussian forces at the Battle of Sedan on 2 September 1870; the Third Republic was proclaimed two days later, and the title was never revived.
