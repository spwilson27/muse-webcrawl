Siege of Ascalon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The siege of Ascalon took place in 1153 and resulted in the capture of the Fatimid Egyptian fortress of Ascalon (Ashkelon) by the Kingdom of Jerusalem. It was one of the major Crusader victories of the mid-twelfth century and removed the last Fatimid stronghold on the Palestinian coast.

Background

Ascalon was a heavily fortified port city held by Fatimid Egypt, from which raiding parties threatened the southern frontier of the Crusader kingdom. King Baldwin III of Jerusalem, having consolidated his authority after a dispute with his mother Melisende, determined to take the city, encouraged by the Knights Templar and Hospitallers and by the weakness of the Fatimid regime in Cairo.

The siege

The Crusader army, accompanied by a fleet, invested Ascalon in January 1153. The siege lasted about seven months. The attackers built siege towers and battered the walls; a great tower was burned by the defenders in a sortie, and when it collapsed against the wall it opened a breach that the Templars tried — disastrously and controversially — to exploit alone. A Fatimid relief fleet reached the city but could not lift the blockade. Exhausted and without hope of relief, the garrison negotiated surrender.

Aftermath

Ascalon surrendered on 19 August 1153 (the city was evacuated by 22 August), with the inhabitants allowed to depart safely for Egypt. The city was incorporated into the County of Jaffa and Ascalon. Its fall completed the Crusader conquest of the Levantine coast and increased pressure on Fatimid Egypt, though it also pushed the Crusader states toward the fateful Egyptian campaigns of the 1160s.
