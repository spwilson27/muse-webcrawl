Scheduled Castes and Scheduled Tribes

The Scheduled Castes (SCs) and Scheduled Tribes (STs) are officially designated groups of people in India, recognized in the Constitution of India as historically disadvantaged and entitled to certain protective and affirmative-action measures. The terms appear in the Constitution (Scheduled Castes) Order, 1950, and the Constitution (Scheduled Tribes) Order, 1950, issued under Articles 341 and 342, which list the specific castes and tribes so designated state by state. Together the two groups comprise roughly a quarter of India's population: Scheduled Castes were 16.6% and Scheduled Tribes 8.6% of the population in the 2011 census.

Historical background

The Scheduled Castes correspond broadly to communities formerly stigmatized as "untouchable" within the Hindu caste order—groups such as those who call themselves Dalit—while the Scheduled Tribes correspond to Adivasi, Indigenous tribal communities largely outside the caste system and often geographically isolated. The word "Scheduled" derives from the lists ("schedules") of castes drawn up under British rule, notably the Government of India Act 1935. B. R. Ambedkar, himself born into a Scheduled Caste and the principal architect of the Constitution, was the leading political voice for these communities in the independence era.

Constitutional provisions and policy

The Constitution abolishes untouchability (Article 17), prohibits discrimination, and provides for: reserved seats for SCs and STs in the Lok Sabha and state legislative assemblies (Articles 330 and 332), broadly in proportion to population; reservation in government employment and public education (Article 16 and related provisions); and special administrative arrangements for tribal areas under the Fifth and Sixth Schedules. Parliament has enacted protective legislation including the Protection of Civil Rights Act 1955 and the Scheduled Castes and the Scheduled Tribes (Prevention of Atrocities) Act 1989, which criminalizes caste-based violence and humiliation. National Commissions for Scheduled Castes and for Scheduled Tribes (Articles 338 and 338A) monitor safeguards. For Scheduled Tribes, the Forest Rights Act 2006 additionally recognized individual and community rights over forest land.

Contemporary issues

Sub-classification within the lists, the extension of SC status to Dalit converts to Christianity and Islam (the 1950 Order originally confined it to Hindus, later extended to Sikhs and Buddhists), and debates over a "creamy layer" exclusion are live political questions. Despite legal protections, caste atrocities, manual scavenging, land alienation of tribal communities, and large gaps in literacy and income persist, and both groups remain central to Indian electoral politics and debates over social justice.
