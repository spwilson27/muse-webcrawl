Amphibious warfare

Amphibious warfare is a type of offensive military operation that today uses naval ships to project ground and air power onto a hostile or potentially hostile shore at a designated landing beach. Through history the operations were conducted using ship's boats as the primary method of delivering troops to shore.

History

Seaborne invasions date to antiquity — the Persian landings in Greece and Roman invasions of Britain — but modern amphibious doctrine emerged in the interwar period, notably in the United States Marine Corps' Tentative Manual for Landing Operations (1934).

Second World War

The Second World War saw amphibious operations on an unprecedented scale: the Allied landings in North Africa, Sicily, Italy, and Normandy (Operation Overlord, 6 June 1944), and the island-hopping campaign in the Pacific, including Guadalcanal, Tarawa, Iwo Jima, and Okinawa. Specialized landing craft such as the LCVP ("Higgins boat") and amphibious vehicles were developed for these assaults.

Modern operations

Postwar examples include the Inchon landing in the Korean War and the British landings in the Falklands War of 1982. Modern doctrine emphasizes over-the-horizon assault, ship-to-shore connectors, and amphibious ready groups centered on large-deck amphibious assault ships.
