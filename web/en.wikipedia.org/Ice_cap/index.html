Ice cap

An ice cap is a mass of ice that covers less than 50,000 km² of land area (larger bodies are called ice sheets). Dome-shaped, ice caps spread out in all directions from their highest point, are not constrained by topographical features (unlike ice fields), and are typically found in polar and subpolar regions and on high plateaus and mountains. Their margins feed outlet glaciers that may end in calving fronts.

Examples

The world's largest ice caps include Vatnajökull (about 7,700 km²) and Langjökull in Iceland; the Devon Ice Cap and Barnes Ice Cap in the Canadian Arctic; the Austfonna ice cap on Nordaustlandet, Svalbard, one of the largest in Eurasia; and the ice caps of Severnaya Zemlya and Franz Josef Land in the Russian Arctic. Iceland's ice caps often overlie active volcanoes, so eruptions beneath them can generate catastrophic glacial outburst floods called jökulhlaups. Smaller mountain ice caps survive as remnants in Norway, the Alps, the Andes, and on sub-Antarctic islands.

Dynamics and climate role

Ice caps are thinner and more responsive to climate change than the great ice sheets, gaining mass by snow accumulation in their interiors and losing it by melting and outlet-glacier discharge. Many have thinned and retreated substantially since the late 20th century; their meltwater contributes measurably to global sea-level rise and, where caps feed an ice shelf, can destabilize it. During the Last Glacial Maximum, ice caps were far more extensive — for example over Scotland, Scandinavia's highlands, and Patagonia — and their isostatic depression and rebound still shape those landscapes.
