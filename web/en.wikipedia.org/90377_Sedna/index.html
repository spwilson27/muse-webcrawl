90377 Sedna

90377 Sedna (provisional designation 2003 VB12) is a dwarf planet in the outermost reaches of the Solar System, discovered in 2003. It follows an extremely elongated orbit with a period of about 11,400 years, ranging from a perihelion of 76 AU — beyond the Kuiper belt — to an aphelion estimated around 937 AU.

Discovery

Sedna was discovered on 14 November 2003 by Michael Brown, Chad Trujillo, and David Rabinowitz at Palomar Observatory. It is named for Sedna, the Inuit goddess of the sea and marine animals. At discovery it was the most distant observed object in the Solar System, and its existence suggested a previously unknown population of detached objects.

Physical characteristics

Sedna is about 1,000 km in diameter and has a distinctly red surface, rich in tholins, with spectra indicating water and methane ices. Its rotation period is roughly 10 hours. Despite searches, no satellite has been found.

Orbit and origin

Sedna's perihelion is too distant for it to have been scattered by Neptune, prompting hypotheses involving a passing star in the Sun's birth cluster, capture from another star, or the gravitational influence of an undiscovered distant planet such as the hypothesized Planet Nine. It is the prototype of the "sednoids."
