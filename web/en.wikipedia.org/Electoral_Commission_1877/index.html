Electoral Commission (1877)

The Electoral Commission was a temporary body created by the United States Congress on 29 January 1877 to resolve the disputed presidential election of 1876 between Republican Rutherford B. Hayes and Democrat Samuel J. Tilden.

The fifteen-member commission settled the disputed election of 1876.

Background

Tilden won the popular vote and appeared to have won the Electoral College, but the results from Florida, Louisiana, and South Carolina were contested by both parties, and one elector from Oregon was disputed—leaving 20 electoral votes in question and both sides claiming victory. Fearing a constitutional crisis or renewed civil conflict, Congress created a commission of fifteen: five senators, five representatives, and five Supreme Court justices.

Decisions

The commission was designed to be evenly balanced with one independent justice, David Davis, as the swing vote; when Davis left for the Senate, he was replaced by Republican Joseph P. Bradley. The commission then voted 8–7 along party lines on every question, awarding all 20 disputed votes to Hayes, who won the presidency by a single electoral vote, 185 to 184.

Compromise of 1877

Democrats accepted the result in the informal Compromise of 1877, in exchange for the withdrawal of the remaining federal troops from the South, effectively ending Reconstruction. The crisis later led to the Electoral Count Act of 1887, which laid down procedures for counting electoral votes.
