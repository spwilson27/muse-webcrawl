Memory B cell

Memory B cells are a type of B lymphocyte of the adaptive immune system that form after a primary infection (or vaccination) and persist in the body for years or decades. On re-encounter with the same antigen, they mount a faster, stronger and higher-affinity antibody response than naïve B cells, providing the basis of long-term humoral immunity.

Formation

During a primary immune response, activated B cells proliferate in germinal centres of lymph nodes and spleen, where they undergo somatic hypermutation and class-switch recombination. Most become short-lived plasma cells or long-lived plasma cells that migrate to the bone marrow; a subset differentiate into memory B cells, which recirculate through blood and lymphoid tissues in a quiescent state.

Reactivation

When a memory B cell's surface immunoglobulin binds its antigen again, it rapidly divides and differentiates into antibody-secreting plasma cells producing large amounts of IgG (or other switched isotypes) within days — the secondary response exploited by booster vaccinations. Memory B cells can be identified by markers such as CD27 in humans.
