Anglo-Zulu War

The Anglo-Zulu War was fought in 1879 between the British Empire and the Zulu Kingdom in what is now KwaZulu-Natal, South Africa. Provoked by an ultimatum from the British high commissioner Sir Bartle Frere that King Cetshwayo could not accept, the war opened with a humiliating British catastrophe at Isandlwana (22 January 1879) — one of the worst defeats ever inflicted on a modern European army by an indigenous force — and an immortalised defence at Rorke's Drift the same day. Reinforced, the British invaded again, destroyed the Zulu army at Ulundi on 4 July 1879, captured Cetshwayo, and dismantled the Zulu kingdom into thirteen chiefdoms, a settlement that led to civil war and the kingdom's absorption into Natal in 1897.

Background

British officials in southern Africa, notably the Colonial Secretary Lord Carnarvon and Frere in Cape Town, wanted a confederation of British colonies, Boer republics and African kingdoms under British control; the independent, militarily strong Zulu kingdom under Cetshwayo stood in the way. Exploiting a boundary dispute and minor border incidents, Frere delivered an ultimatum on 11 December 1878 demanding, among other things, the disbanding of the Zulu army — terms calculated to be rejected. When they expired unmet, British forces under Lieutenant-General Lord Chelmsford invaded on 11 January 1879 without formal sanction from London.

Course of the war

Chelmsford's central column camped beneath the hill of Isandlwana without fortifying. On 22 January a Zulu army of some 20,000 overwhelmed the camp, killing over 1,300 British and allied troops. That evening a small garrison at the mission station of Rorke's Drift — about 150 men — held off some 3,000–4,000 Zulu attackers, winning eleven Victoria Crosses. Other early actions included the British defeat at Hlobane and victory at Kambula (29 March), and the costly relief of Eshowe. Prince Napoléon, the only son of Napoleon III, was killed on patrol on 1 June, causing a sensation in Europe. With large reinforcements, Chelmsford advanced again, carefully forming square at Ulundi on 4 July and crushing the Zulu army with rifle, artillery and Gatling fire. Cetshwayo was captured on 28 August and exiled to Cape Town and London.

Aftermath

Sir Garnet Wolseley divided Zululand among thirteen chiefs, a recipe for the civil war that followed; Cetshwayo was restored in 1883 but died the next year, and Zululand was annexed to Natal in 1897. The war destroyed Zulu independence and opened the region to settler land claims, but Isandlwana remains a landmark in the history of colonial warfare and Zulu military achievement.
