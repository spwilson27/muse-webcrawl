Hogback (geology)

In geology, a hogback is a long, narrow ridge or a series of hills with a narrow crest and steep slopes of nearly equal inclination on both flanks, formed by the erosion of steeply dipping resistant rock strata. Hogbacks typically have slope angles exceeding 30 degrees, distinguishing them from cuestas, which have one gentle dip slope and one steep escarpment.

Formation

Hogbacks develop where alternating hard and soft sedimentary layers have been tilted nearly vertical by tectonic forces. Erosion removes the softer strata, leaving the resistant beds — often sandstone or limestone — standing as sharp ridges. Classic examples occur along the flanks of the Rocky Mountains in Colorado, where sandstone beds of the Dakota Group form prominent hogbacks, and in the Ridge-and-Valley Appalachians.
