The Münchhausen trilemma is an argument in epistemology that it is impossible to provide a complete justification for any proposition. The term was coined by the German philosopher Hans Albert, after Baron Münchhausen, who legendarily pulled himself out of a swamp by his own hair.

The three horns

Any attempt to justify a claim by appeal to further claims must end in one of three unsatisfying outcomes: (1) an infinite regress, in which each justification requires a further one without end; (2) circular reasoning, in which the chain of justification loops back on itself; or (3) an axiomatic stopping point, in which some claim is simply asserted as self-evident or dogmatically accepted.

Background and responses

The problem restates the trilemma attributed in antiquity to Agrippa the Skeptic, reported by Diogenes Laërtius. Responses include foundationalism, which accepts basic beliefs as properly justified; coherentism, which embraces a web-like rather than linear structure of justification; infinitism; and Karl Popper's critical rationalism, which abandons the demand for ultimate justification in favour of criticism. Albert himself recommended a form of critical rationalism that treats all claims as open to revision.
