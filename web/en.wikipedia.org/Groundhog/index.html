The groundhog (Marmota monax), also known as the woodchuck, is a rodent of the family Sciuridae, belonging to the group of large ground squirrels known as marmots. It is found through much of the eastern and central United States and across Canada into Alaska — the only marmot with such a lowland, wide-ranging distribution.

Description and behaviour

Adults measure 40–65 cm in length and typically weigh 2–6 kg, gaining substantial weight before winter. Groundhogs are excellent diggers, excavating burrow systems with multiple entrances and chambers that are also used by other animals. They are true hibernators: body temperature can fall to around 5 °C and heart rate to a few beats per minute. Groundhogs are largely solitary, feed on grasses, clover, garden vegetables and fruit, and whistle when alarmed — hence "whistle-pig". Despite the tongue-twister, they do not chuck wood; "woodchuck" derives from an Algonquian name.

Groundhog Day

Groundhog Day (February 2), a Pennsylvania Dutch tradition in which a groundhog's emergence supposedly predicts the arrival of spring, has made the animal famous. The best-known prognosticator is Punxsutawney Phil of Punxsutawney, Pennsylvania, whose ceremonies date to the 1880s.

Relations with humans

Groundhogs are valued as soil aerators and prey animals but are often considered garden pests. They are also used in biomedical research on hepatitis B, for which they are a natural model.
