Hell's Gate is a narrow, turbulent section of the Fraser Canyon in southern British Columbia, Canada, where the walls of the Fraser River gorge close in so that the entire river is forced through a gap only about 35 metres (110 feet) wide. The explorer Simon Fraser, who descended the river in 1808 and gave the place its name, described the passage as a place where no human being should venture — "the gates of hell".

Geography

At Hell's Gate the Fraser River, which elsewhere can be several hundred metres wide, is compressed between steep rock walls, producing deep, fast and boiling water. The depth of the river at the narrows has been measured at over 60 metres (200 feet). The site lies on the Trans-Canada Highway south of Boston Bar, and is crossed by a pedestrian suspension bridge that serves a tourist attraction with viewing decks, a funicular-style airtram and exhibits.

Fishways and the 1913 slide

The canyon at Hell's Gate has always been a difficult point for migrating Pacific salmon, and an important Indigenous fishing site. In 1913, railway construction blasted rock into the river, and a further massive slide in 1914 partially blocked the channel, sharply increasing the current and devastating the sockeye salmon runs — one of the worst ecological disasters in the history of the Fraser fishery. To restore fish passage, concrete fishways were built on both banks in 1944–1946 by the International Pacific Salmon Fisheries Commission, allowing salmon to bypass the fastest water through a series of stepped pools. Salmon runs subsequently recovered in part, and the fishways remain in operation.

Transport

Both of Canada's transcontinental railways pass through the Fraser Canyon at Hell's Gate, the Canadian Pacific line on the east bank and the Canadian National line on the west, while the Trans-Canada Highway tunnels through the canyon walls nearby. The combination of river, highway and rail lines in the narrow gorge makes Hell's Gate one of the most striking transport corridors in Canada.
