Global dimming

Air pollution aerosols are the main cause of the observed decrease in surface sunlight.

Global dimming is the gradual reduction in the amount of global direct irradiance at the Earth's surface observed over several decades from the 1950s until the 1980s, with some evidence of a partial reversal ("brightening") since about 1990. The effect is thought to have been caused by particulate matter such as sulfate aerosols in the atmosphere due to human action. Global dimming cooled the surface by partially masking greenhouse warming, and its decline after air-pollution controls took effect may have contributed to the rapid warming observed since.

Cause and effects

The burning of fossil fuels releases both greenhouse gases and microscopic particles. The particles scatter and absorb sunlight and also act as cloud condensation nuclei, making clouds brighter and longer-lived; together these effects reduce the sunshine reaching the ground. Measurements showed declines in surface solar radiation of roughly 4–5% over the period 1960–1990 in many regions. Dimming alters evaporation, the hydrological cycle, and regional climates; it has been implicated in shifting monsoons and in droughts such as those in sub-Saharan Africa in the 1970s and 1980s.

Brightening

Clean-air legislation in Europe and North America, the economic collapse of the Soviet Union, and later pollution controls in China reduced aerosol emissions, and surface radiation measurements since about 1990 show widespread brightening. Because dimming had offset some greenhouse warming, its removal unmasks additional warming — a "Faustian bargain" of air pollution: cleaning the air improves health but accelerates climate change unless greenhouse gases are cut simultaneously.
