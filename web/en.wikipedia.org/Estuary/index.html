Estuary

Estuary
DefinitionPartially enclosed coastal water body where river water mixes with seawater
Water typeBrackish
TypesDrowned river valley, bar-built, tectonic, fjord
ExamplesChesapeake Bay, Thames Estuary, Río de la Plata

An estuary is a partially enclosed coastal body of brackish water with one or more rivers or streams flowing into it, and with a free connection to the open sea. Estuaries form a transition zone between river environments and maritime environments and are subject both to marine influences, such as tides, waves, and the influx of saline water, and to fluvial influences, such as flows of fresh water and sediment.

Classification

By origin, estuaries are classed as drowned river valleys (rias), bar-built estuaries behind barrier islands or spits, tectonic estuaries formed by subsidence — San Francisco Bay is an example — and fjords, carved by glaciers. By water circulation they range from salt-wedge estuaries, where river water overrides a wedge of seawater, through partially mixed systems, to well-mixed estuaries and inverse estuaries in arid climates.

Ecology

Estuaries are among the most productive ecosystems on Earth. Nutrient-rich waters, shallow sunlit flats and sheltered conditions support seagrass meadows, salt marshes and mangroves, and provide nursery habitat for fish and shellfish and feeding grounds for migratory birds. They also filter pollutants and buffer coasts against storms.

Human use

Many of the world's largest cities and ports — London, New York, Buenos Aires, Shanghai — grew on estuaries, which offer navigable sheltered water. The same concentration of people makes estuaries vulnerable to pollution, eutrophication, dredging, land reclamation and sea-level rise.
