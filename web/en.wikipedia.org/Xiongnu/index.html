The Xiongnu (Chinese: 匈奴; pinyin: Xiōngnú) were a tribal confederation of nomadic peoples who formed a state or empire on the eastern Eurasian steppe from the 3rd century BCE to the late 1st century CE. Centred on the Mongolian plateau, the Xiongnu empire under Modu Chanyu was the first great steppe empire and the principal northern rival of Han dynasty China.

Formation

In 209 BCE Modu killed his father Touman and united the Xiongnu tribes, conquering the Donghu to the east and the Yuezhi to the west. Xiongnu mounted archers raided deep into China, and in 200 BCE surrounded Emperor Gaozu at Baideng, forcing the Han into the heqin policy of marriage alliances and tribute payments.

Relations with Han China

Emperor Wu of Han abandoned appeasement and launched great campaigns from 133 BCE. Generals Wei Qing and Huo Qubing drove the Xiongnu court north of the Gobi desert, and China seized the Hexi Corridor. Civil war among Xiongnu claimants in 57 BCE led to rival chanyus; in 53 BCE the southern chanyu Huhanye submitted to the Han.

Split and decline

In 48 CE the confederation split permanently into Northern and Southern Xiongnu. The southern group settled inside the Han frontier as auxiliaries. The northern state was destroyed by a Han–Southern Xiongnu–Xianbei coalition in 89–91 CE, after which many northern Xiongnu migrated westward; proposed links to the later Huns of Europe remain debated. Southern Xiongnu descendants founded the Han-Zhao state in 304 CE, opening the Sixteen Kingdoms era.

Society and economy

Xiongnu society combined pastoral nomadism with farming settlements, metalworking, and trade along early Silk Road routes. Aristocratic burials such as those at Noin-Ula contained Chinese lacquerware, silk and chariots alongside steppe art in animal style. Their language remains unclassified; proposed affiliations include Turkic, Mongolic, Iranian and Yeniseian.
