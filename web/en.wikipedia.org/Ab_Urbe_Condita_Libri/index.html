Ab Urbe Condita Libri (Latin: "Books from the Founding of the City"), also known as the History of Rome, is a monumental history of ancient Rome written in Latin between about 27 and 9 BC by the historian Livy. The work covers Roman history from the legendary arrival of Aeneas and the founding of the city in 753 BC down to Livy's own time, ending with the death of Drusus in 9 BC.

Contents and survival

Livy organised the history in 142 books, conventionally grouped in "decades" of ten. Only 35 books survive complete: Books 1–10, covering the founding of Rome to 293 BC, and Books 21–45, covering the Second Punic War through events to 167 BC. The rest are known from summaries (the Periochae), excerpts, and fragments, which preserve the broad outline of the lost books — the conquests of Italy, the civil wars of the late Republic and the reign of Augustus.

Style and purpose

Livy wrote with a moral and patriotic purpose: in his preface he presents history as a gallery of examples — models of conduct to imitate and cautionary tales to avoid — for a Rome he believed was in moral decline. His style, praised by Quintilian for its clarity and eloquence, favours vivid narrative, speeches composed for historical actors, and dramatic episode over critical source analysis; he relied mainly on earlier annalists such as Polybius, Valerius Antias and Licinius Macer.

Influence

Livy's history shaped the European understanding of republican Rome for two millennia. It was a school text in the Roman Empire, copied throughout the Middle Ages, printed in Rome in 1469 among the earliest printed books, mined by Machiavelli for his Discourses on Livy, and translated into many languages; Dante called him "Livy, who does not err".
