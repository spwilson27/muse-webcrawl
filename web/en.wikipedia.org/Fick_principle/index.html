Fick principle

The Fick principle, developed by Adolf Eugen Fick (1829–1901), states that the total uptake of (or release of) a substance by the peripheral tissues is equal to the product of the blood flow to the peripheral tissues and the arterial–venous concentration difference (gradient) of the substance. In the determination of cardiac output, the substance most commonly measured is the oxygen content of blood.

Calculation

Cardiac output is computed as:

CO = VO₂ / (CaO₂ − CvO₂)

where VO₂ is oxygen consumption (about 250 mL/min in a resting adult), CaO₂ is the oxygen content of arterial (pulmonary venous) blood, and CvO₂ is the oxygen content of mixed venous blood, sampled from the pulmonary artery. With typical values this gives a cardiac output of about 5 litres per minute.

Assumptions and use

The method assumes a steady state in which the lungs are the only site of oxygen uptake, and it requires accurate measurement of oxygen consumption and simultaneous arterial and mixed venous blood samples, usually obtained during right heart catheterisation. Although labour-intensive, the Fick method remains a reference standard against which other cardiac output techniques, such as thermodilution, are validated, and variants of the principle are used to measure blood flow to individual organs.

Fick is also known for Fick's laws of diffusion, fundamental to physiology and physical chemistry.
