Traditional ecological knowledge

Traditional ecological knowledge (TEK) describes indigenous and other traditional knowledge regarding the sustainability of local resources. It is a field of study in anthropology and ecology, referring to "a cumulative body of knowledge, belief, and practice, evolving by accumulation of TEK and handed down through generations by cultural transmission" about the relationship of living beings (human and nonhuman) with one another and with their environment.

Characteristics

TEK is built from close, long-term observation of a particular place and is transmitted orally and through practice — in stories, ceremonies, hunting and farming techniques, and place names. It encompasses knowledge of species behaviour, seasonal cycles, fire management, soil and water stewardship, and weather prediction. Unlike much of Western science, TEK typically embeds knowledge in spiritual and moral relationships between humans and the natural world.

Applications

Resource managers increasingly draw on TEK for conservation, fisheries assessment, wildlife management (for example, Inuit knowledge of Arctic sea ice and polar bears), restoration ecology, and climate change adaptation. Indigenous burning practices in Australia and North America, long suppressed, have been re-adopted in managing wildfire risk. The inclusion of TEK is promoted by bodies such as the Intergovernmental Science-Policy Platform on Biodiversity and Ecosystem Services (IPBES).

Challenges

Scholars and indigenous communities raise concerns about the extraction of TEK from its cultural context, intellectual property, language loss, and the conditions under which such knowledge is documented and shared. Principles of indigenous data sovereignty, such as the CARE Principles, seek to ensure communities retain authority over their knowledge.
