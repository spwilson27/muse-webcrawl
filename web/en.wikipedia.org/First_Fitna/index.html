The First Fitna was the first major civil war of the Islamic Caliphate, lasting from 656 to 661 CE. It began with the assassination of the third caliph, Uthman ibn Affan, and ended with the assassination of the fourth caliph, Ali ibn Abi Talib, and the establishment of the Umayyad Caliphate under Muawiya I. The conflict produced enduring divisions in Islam, including the emergence of the Kharijites and the deepening of the Sunni–Shia divide over legitimate leadership of the community.

Course of the war

Ali, succeeding Uthman, faced rebellion from Aisha, Talha and Zubayr, whom he defeated at the Battle of the Camel near Basra in 656. He then confronted Muawiya, governor of Syria, who demanded vengeance for Uthman's death. At the Battle of Siffin (657), when Ali's forces seemed close to victory, Muawiya's soldiers raised copies of the Qur'an on their lances, forcing an arbitration that ultimately weakened Ali's position. Ali's acceptance of arbitration alienated the Kharijites, rigorist former supporters whom he defeated at Nahrawan in 658 — but a Kharijite assassin, Ibn Muljim, mortally wounded Ali in Kufa in 661.

Legacy

After Ali's death, his son Hasan briefly held the caliphate before abdicating to Muawiya under a treaty, and Muawiya moved the capital to Damascus and founded hereditary Umayyad rule. The First Fitna traumatised the early Muslim community; its memory shaped Islamic doctrines on rebellion, legitimate rule, and the status of sinful rulers, and its events remain central to Shia devotion to Ali and his family.
