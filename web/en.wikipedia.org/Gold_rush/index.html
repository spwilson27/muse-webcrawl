Gold rush

A gold rush is a new discovery of gold — sometimes accompanied by other precious minerals — that brings an onrush of miners seeking their fortune. Major gold rushes of the 19th century occurred in the United States, Australia, New Zealand, Brazil, Chile, South Africa, and Canada. While some miners made fortunes, those who sold goods and services to the miners often profited more reliably, and the rushes drove settlement, infrastructure, and sometimes violent dispossession of indigenous peoples in the regions affected.

Major gold rushes

The California Gold Rush (1848–1855), sparked by James Marshall's discovery at Sutter's Mill, drew some 300,000 "forty-niners" and transformed California, which entered the Union in 1850. Australia's rushes began in 1851 near Bathurst and then at Ballarat and Bendigo in Victoria, trebling the colony's population and provoking the Eureka Rebellion of 1854. The Witwatersrand Gold Rush (1886) in South Africa founded Johannesburg on the richest goldfield ever discovered and set the stage for the Boer Wars. The Klondike Gold Rush (1896–1899) drew about 100,000 prospectors to the Yukon, of whom only a fraction reached Dawson City. Other notable rushes include Colorado (1858–61), Otago in New Zealand (1861), the Black Hills (1874), and Nome, Alaska (1899).

Characteristics

Gold rushes typically follow a pattern: a strike, a stampede of prospectors, easily worked placer deposits quickly exhausted, then capital-intensive hard-rock mining dominated by companies. Boomtowns sprang up overnight, with extreme prices, lawlessness tempered by miners' codes and vigilance committees, and lasting environmental damage from hydraulic mining and mercury use. The rushes accelerated railways, ports, and state formation — and left behind ghost towns when the gold ran out.
