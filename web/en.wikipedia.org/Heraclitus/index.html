Heraclitus of Ephesus

Bornc. 535 BCE, Ephesus, Ionia

Main ideasUniversal flux ("panta rhei"), unity of opposites, logos, fire as arche

Heraclitus of Ephesus (c. 535 – c. 475 BCE) was an Ancient Greek pre-Socratic philosopher from the city of Ephesus in Ionia. He is famous for his doctrine that change is central to the universe — summed up in the saying attributed to him, panta rhei ("everything flows") — and for his insistence on the unity of opposites. His cryptic, aphoristic style earned him the epithet "the Obscure", and he was also called "the weeping philosopher" in contrast to the "laughing philosopher" Democritus.

Life

Little is known reliably of Heraclitus's life. Diogenes Laërtius reports that he came from an aristocratic Ephesian family, renounced a hereditary title in favour of his brother, and withdrew from public life in contempt for his fellow citizens. His book, On Nature, of which only fragments survive in quotations by later authors, was said to have been deposited in the temple of Artemis at Ephesus.

Philosophy

Heraclitus held that the world is in perpetual flux: in the most famous fragment, "upon those who step into the same rivers, different and again different waters flow". Stability is an illusion produced by balanced change. Opposites are interdependent and unified: "the way up and the way down are one and the same"; day and night, life and death, waking and sleeping are aspects of a single process. Strife (polemos) is "the father of all things", the engine of change.

Underlying the flux is the logos — a universal rational order or measure according to which all things happen, which most people fail to comprehend. Fire serves as his emblem of the cosmos, "an ever-living fire, kindling in measures and going out in measures", exchanged for all things as gold is exchanged for goods.

Influence

Heraclitus deeply influenced Plato (who combined his flux with Parmenides' permanence), the Stoics (who adopted his logos and cosmic fire), and later Hegel, Nietzsche and Heidegger. His fragments remain among the most commented texts in Western philosophy.
