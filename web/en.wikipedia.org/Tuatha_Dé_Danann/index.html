Tuatha Dé Danann

The Tuatha Dé Danann, the supernatural race of Irish mythology

The Tuatha Dé Danann (Old Irish, usually translated as "the folk of the goddess Danu") are a supernatural race in Irish mythology. In much of early literature they are depicted as kings, queens, warriors and heroes of ancient Ireland, and many of them were later associated with the fairies (the aes sídhe) of Irish folklore. They are thought to represent the gods of pre-Christian Ireland.

Mythology

According to the Lebor Gabála Érenn ("The Book of the Taking of Ireland"), the Tuatha Dé Danann were the fifth group of people to settle in Ireland. They were said to have come from four cities in the north of the world, bringing with them four magical treasures: the Stone of Fál (Lia Fáil), the Spear of Lugh, the Sword of Nuada and the Cauldron of the Dagda.

After arriving in Ireland, the Tuatha Dé Danann defeated the Fir Bolg at the First Battle of Mag Tuired, but their king Nuada lost an arm and was replaced by the tyrant Bres. Nuada was later restored with a silver arm wrought by Dian Cécht. At the Second Battle of Mag Tuired, led by Lugh, they defeated the Fomorians and killed their king Balor.

Eventually the Tuatha Dé Danann were defeated by the Milesians, the ancestors of the modern Irish. According to legend, they retreated into the sídhe mounds (fairy hills) and became the aes sídhe, the "people of the mounds".

Principal figures

  • Nuada — first king of the Tuatha Dé Danann
  • The Dagda — father-figure and chief of the gods
  • Lugh — hero and slayer of Balor
  • The Morrígan — goddess of war and fate
  • Brigid — goddess of poetry, healing and smithcraft
  • Manannán mac Lir — god of the sea
  • Danu — the mother goddess from whom they take their name
