Human anatomy
The scientific study of the structure of the human body, from gross (macroscopic) anatomy to microscopic anatomy and histology.

Human anatomy is the scientific study of the structure of the human body. It is one of the oldest basic medical sciences, having been formalised in ancient Egypt and Greece, and it remains a foundation of modern medicine. Unlike physiology, which deals with function, anatomy concerns itself with the organisation of the body: its cells, tissues, organs and organ systems, and how these are arranged in three-dimensional space.

History

The earliest anatomical knowledge came from Egyptian medical papyri and the work of Greek physicians such as Hippocrates and Galen. During the Renaissance, Andreas Vesalius revolutionised the field with De humani corporis fabrica (1543), based on direct dissection of human cadavers. The invention of the microscope opened microscopic anatomy, and later imaging technologies — X-rays, CT and MRI — allowed the living body to be studied without dissection.

Organ systems

The human body is conventionally divided into eleven major organ systems:

  • The skeletal system, of 206 bones in the adult, providing support and protection.
  • The muscular system, responsible for movement and posture.
  • The nervous system, including the brain, spinal cord and peripheral nerves.
  • The cardiovascular system, the heart and blood vessels.
  • The respiratory system, including the lungs — see also hypoxia for disorders of oxygen supply.
  • The digestive, urinary, reproductive, endocrine, lymphatic/immune and integumentary systems.

Anatomical terminology

Anatomists use a standardised vocabulary based on the anatomical position: standing upright, facing forward, arms at the sides with palms forward. Directions include superior/inferior, anterior/posterior and medial/lateral; planes include sagittal, coronal and transverse. The international standard terminology is maintained in Terminologia Anatomica.
