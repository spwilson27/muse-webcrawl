Short-line railroad

Classification: Class III railroad (US)
Typical length: Under a few hundred miles
Role: Local freight service and interchange

A short-line railroad is a small or mid-sized railroad company that operates a relatively short distance, usually connecting local industries and communities to the larger national rail network. In the United States, short lines are generally Class III railroads, the smallest category by revenue.

History

Many short lines began as independent local railways later absorbed by larger systems. Their modern growth followed the Staggers Rail Act of 1980, which made it easier for major railroads to sell or lease low-density branch lines to smaller operators who could run them more cheaply and with more flexible local service.

Operations

Short lines typically haul freight such as grain, lumber, chemicals and aggregates for local shippers, exchanging cars with Class I railroads at interchange points. Holding companies such as Genesee & Wyoming own dozens of short lines, while others remain family- or community-owned. Some also operate heritage passenger excursions.
