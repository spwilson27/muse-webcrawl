Christian humanism

Christian humanists combined classical learning with the study of Scripture in its original languages.

Christian humanism is an intellectual movement that emerged during the Renaissance, combining the humanist revival of classical learning — Latin and Greek literature, rhetoric, history and moral philosophy — with Christian faith. Its adherents sought to reform the Church and society by returning ad fontes ("to the sources"): the Bible in its original languages and the writings of the early Church Fathers, rather than relying on medieval scholastic commentaries alone.

Origins and figures

The movement grew out of Italian Renaissance humanism in the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries and flourished north of the Alps in the late fifteenth and early sixteenth centuries. Its leading figure was Desiderius Erasmus of Rotterdam, whose edition of the Greek New Testament (1516) and works such as In Praise of Folly and the Enchiridion promoted a simple, ethical "philosophy of Christ." Other prominent Christian humanists included Thomas More in England, John Colet, Jacques Lefèvre d'Étaples in France, and Juan Luis Vives in Spain.

Ideas

Christian humanists emphasised education, eloquence and the moral formation of the individual. They criticised corruption and empty ritual within the Church, championed the translation of Scripture into vernacular languages, and believed that classical pagan literature, rightly read, could support Christian virtue. Many sought reform without schism; the movement nonetheless prepared the ground for both the Protestant Reformation and the Catholic Counter-Reformation, whose leaders drew on humanist methods of textual scholarship.

Later usage

In the twentieth and twenty-first centuries the term has also been applied to modern thinkers who combine Christian commitment with humanist values such as human dignity, reason and social justice.
