Forearc

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A forearc is a region in a subduction zone between the oceanic trench and the associated volcanic arc. Forearcs are found at convergent plate boundaries, and include any accretionary wedge and forearc basin that may be present. They represent the part of the overriding plate that lies trenchward of the volcanic arc and above the shallow portion of the subducting slab.

Formation and structure

As the oceanic plate descends, sediments scraped from it may be accreted to the front of the overriding plate, forming an accretionary prism of deformed, thrust-faulted sediment. Behind this prism, relatively undeformed forearc basins can accumulate sediment eroded from the arc and the rising accretionary wedge. The forearc is underlain by the cold "nose" of the mantle wedge, so despite sitting above a subduction zone it is characteristically cool and non-volcanic; seismicity in the region is dominated by the plate interface (megathrust) itself.

Significance

Forearcs preserve records of past subduction and of great megathrust earthquakes. Because the forearc is mechanically coupled to the subducting plate, its deformation helps determine where strain accumulates and ruptures in events such as the 2011 Tōhoku earthquake off Japan and the 2004 Sumatra earthquake, both of which occurred along forearc margins. Examples of forearc basins include the Great Valley of California (a Mesozoic forearc basin) and the basins offshore Sumatra and Japan.
