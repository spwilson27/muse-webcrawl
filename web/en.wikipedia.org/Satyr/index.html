Satyr

In Greek mythology, a satyr (Greek: σάτυρος, sátyros) is a male nature spirit with ears and a tail resembling those of a horse, and a permanent, exaggerated erection. Early Greek art shows satyrs as men with the ears and tail of a horse; later they were increasingly depicted with goat-like features — horns, goat ears and sometimes goat legs — through conflation with the god Pan and the Roman faun. Satyrs are famous for their love of wine, music, dancing and women, and they form the riotous retinue of Dionysus, the god of wine and ecstasy.

Mythology

Satyrs accompany Dionysus alongside the maenads, carrying the thyrsus, drinking vessels and musical instruments. Individual satyrs in myth include Silenus, the aged, drunken tutor of Dionysus (often treated as the eldest of the satyrs, the sileni), and Marsyas, the satyr who challenged Apollo to a musical contest and was flayed alive for his presumption. In art they are stock figures of revelry, sexual pursuit and comic cowardice — fond of chasing nymphs and maenads, but easily frightened.

Satyr plays

In classical Athens, performances of tragedy at the City Dionysia were followed by a satyr play: a burlesque drama in which the chorus was dressed as satyrs and mythological stories were treated with obscenity and slapstick. The only complete surviving satyr play is Euripides' Cyclops; substantial fragments survive of Sophocles' Ichneutae (The Trackers). It is from these companions of Dionysus — not from any connection with the Latin word satura — that the association of satyrs with ribald performance developed, although the two words are etymologically unrelated despite the similarity that later linked "satyr" and "satire" in popular imagination.

Later depictions

Satyrs remained popular subjects in Renaissance and Baroque art and appear in modern fantasy literature, notably Mr. Tumnus, the faun of C. S. Lewis's The Chronicles of Narnia, and in role-playing games and films, where they are usually shown with the legs and horns of a goat.
