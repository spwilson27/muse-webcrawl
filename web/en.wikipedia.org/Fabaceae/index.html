The Fabaceae or Leguminosae, commonly known as the legume, pea, or bean family, are a large and agriculturally important family of flowering plants. It includes trees, shrubs, and perennial or annual herbaceous plants, easily recognized by their fruit (legumes) and their compound, stipulate leaves. With roughly 765 genera and more than 19,500 species, it is the third-largest family of land plants and the second most important plant family to human diets after the grasses.

Characteristics

Members typically bear alternate, pinnately compound leaves and irregular or pea-like (papilionaceous) flowers, though some subfamilies have small actinomorphic flowers in dense heads, as in the mimosas and acacias. The fruit is usually a pod (legume) that splits along two seams, releasing the seeds. Most legumes form root nodules housing symbiotic Rhizobium bacteria that fix atmospheric nitrogen, enriching soils and reducing fertilizer needs.

Classification

The APG IV system recognizes six subfamilies: Cercidoideae, Detarioideae, Duparquetioideae, Dialioideae, Caesalpinioideae (including the former mimosoid clade), and Faboideae (Papilionoideae), the latter containing most familiar crops.

Economic importance

Legumes are staple foods worldwide: soybean, peanut, common bean, pea, lentil, chickpea, broad bean, and alfalfa and clover as forage. Pulses are prized for their protein-rich seeds. Timber species include rosewoods; other members provide gums (gum arabic), dyes (indigo), medicines, and ornamentals such as lupins, wisteria, and the golden chain tree. Because of nitrogen fixation, legumes are central to crop rotation and sustainable agriculture.
