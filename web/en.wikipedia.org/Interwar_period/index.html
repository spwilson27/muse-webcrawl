Interwar period

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The interwar period is the era between the end of the First World War and the beginning of the Second World War, conventionally dated from the armistice of 11 November 1918 to the German invasion of Poland on 1 September 1939. It was marked by profound political, economic and cultural change: the collapse of empires, the rise of communism, fascism and Nazism, the Great Depression, and ultimately the failure of collective security.

Aftermath of the First World War

The Paris Peace Conference and the Treaty of Versailles (1919) redrew the map of Europe and the Middle East. The German, Austro-Hungarian, Ottoman and Russian empires disappeared, and new states emerged in central and eastern Europe. The League of Nations was established to prevent future wars, but it lacked an army and the United States never joined. Germany resented reparations and territorial losses, grievances exploited by revanchist movements.

The 1920s

The decade saw recovery and cultural ferment — the "Roaring Twenties" in the United States, Weimar culture in Germany, and the spread of jazz, cinema, radio and aviation. The Soviet Union consolidated after its civil war; in Italy, Mussolini's fascists took power in 1922. Locarno Treaties (1925) and the Kellogg–Briand Pact (1928) briefly raised hopes of lasting peace.

Great Depression and the rise of extremism

The Wall Street Crash of 1929 triggered a worldwide depression, mass unemployment and the collapse of international trade amid protectionism such as the Smoot–Hawley Tariff. In Germany, the crisis propelled Adolf Hitler's Nazi Party to power in 1933. The United States responded with Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal. Japan invaded Manchuria in 1931; Italy invaded Ethiopia in 1935; the Spanish Civil War (1936–1939) became a proxy struggle between fascism, communism and democracy.

Road to war

Through the late 1930s, Germany pursued expansion under the policy of appeasement by Britain and France: remilitarisation of the Rhineland (1936), the Anschluss with Austria and the Munich Agreement (1938), and the occupation of Czechoslovakia (1939). The Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact of August 1939 divided eastern Europe between Germany and the Soviet Union, and the invasion of Poland that followed began the Second World War.
