FaSoLa

FaSoLa is a method of teaching music reading using a system of shaped noteheads and solmisation syllables, associated above all with the Sacred Harp singing tradition of the American South. The name derives from three of the syllables of the system — fa, sol and la — which in the four-shape system are repeated to cover the scale. Singers using the fasola method sing the notes of a tune by their syllables before singing the words, a practice that allows a group of untrained singers to learn four-part hymn tunes quickly.

History

The fasola system descends from the Guidonian hexachord and the solmisation practices of medieval and Renaissance music. In colonial America, singing schools taught congregations to read music using four syllables — mi, fa, sol, la — each associated with a distinctive note shape. The approach was codified in tunebooks such as The Easy Instructor (1801) by William Little and William Smith, which introduced the four-shape notation that became standard in the South.

The most influential fasola tunebook is The Sacred Harp, first published in 1844 by Benjamin Franklin White and Elisha J. King. Later singing schools in some regions adopted seven-shape systems (adding do, re and si/ti), but the four-shape fasola tradition survived intact within Sacred Harp singing, particularly in Georgia, Alabama and Texas.

Practice

In a traditional singing, the class sits in a hollow square, with each vocal part (treble, alto, tenor and bass) on one side. A song leader stands in the centre, calls the page number, and the class first "sings the notes" — vocalising each part with its fasola syllables — before singing the verses with words. The leader beats time with a characteristic downward sweep of the arm.
