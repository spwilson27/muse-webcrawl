The Siebengebirge (German for "seven hills") is a hill range of the German Central Uplands on the eastern bank of the Middle Rhine, southeast of Bonn in the German state of North Rhine-Westphalia. Despite the name, the range consists of more than 40 hills and peaks of volcanic origin. The Siebengebirge is one of the most visited natural landscapes in Germany and was designated a nature park in 1958; quarrying restrictions imposed in the 19th century make it one of the country's oldest protected areas.

Geography

The hills rise steeply from the Rhine between Königswinter and Bad Honnef, extending eastward toward the Westerwald. The highest summit is the Großer Ölberg at 460 metres. The best-known peaks include the Drachenfels (321 m), crowned by a ruined castle and accessible by the Drachenfels Railway, a rack railway opened in 1883; the Petersberg (331 m), long used as a guest house of the German federal government; the Löwenburg; and the Wolkenburg. Schloss Drachenburg, a 19th-century villa in eclectic style, stands on the slopes below the Drachenfels.

Geology and history

The hills are the eroded remnants of volcanoes active some 25 million years ago during the Oligocene, when lava and tuff — including the trachyte quarried for Cologne Cathedral — accumulated over Devonian bedrock. Trachyte from the Drachenfels was transported down the Rhine for Roman and medieval building projects. Legends collected around the hills include the Nibelung hero Siegfried's slaying of the dragon Fafnir, said to have lived in a cave on the Drachenfels ("dragon's rock").

Tourism

The Siebengebirge became a celebrated destination during the Rhine Romanticism of the 19th century, when visitors such as Lord Byron — who praised the Drachenfels in Childe Harold's Pilgrimage — drew international attention to the landscape. Today the area offers extensive hiking trails, vineyards on the Rhine slopes, and views over the Rhine valley to Bonn and the Eifel.
