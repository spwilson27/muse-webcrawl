Korean Armistice Agreement

Signed27 July 1953, Panmunjom
SignatoriesUnited Nations Command; Korean People's Army; Chinese People's Volunteers
PurposeCeasefire ending active hostilities of the Korean War
StatusIn force; no peace treaty concluded

The Korean Armistice Agreement (Korean: 한국정전협정) is an armistice signed on 27 July 1953 that brought a halt to active fighting in the Korean War. It was signed at Panmunjom by the United Nations Command — principally the United States — together with North Korea's Korean People's Army and the Chinese People's Volunteers. South Korea, whose president Syngman Rhee opposed the settlement, was not a signatory. Because the agreement is a ceasefire rather than a peace treaty, North and South Korea remain technically at war.

Negotiations

Talks began in July 1951 at Kaesong and later moved to Panmunjom; they dragged on for two years while fighting continued, with the exchange of prisoners of war — particularly whether prisoners could refuse repatriation — the most contentious issue. Rhee's unilateral release of some 27,000 anti-communist prisoners in June 1953 nearly wrecked the settlement weeks before signing.

Terms

The agreement fixed a Military Demarcation Line roughly along the final battle lines, close to the 38th parallel, and created the Korean Demilitarized Zone (DMZ), a buffer 4 kilometres wide, on either side of it. It established the Military Armistice Commission and the Neutral Nations Supervisory Commission to monitor compliance, arranged the exchange of prisoners (Operation Big Switch), and recommended a political conference to settle the Korean question peacefully — a conference that, when held at Geneva in 1954, failed to produce a peace treaty.

Aftermath

Armistice Day, 27 July, is commemorated in North Korea as Victory Day. Border incidents and periodic North Korean declarations that it no longer considers itself bound by the armistice have repeatedly strained the arrangement, but the ceasefire has broadly held for over seventy years.
