Frequency-division multiplexing

In telecommunications, frequency-division multiplexing (FDM) is a technique by which the total bandwidth available in a communication medium is divided into a series of non-overlapping frequency bands, each of which is used to carry a separate signal. This allows a single transmission medium such as a cable or a band of the radio spectrum to convey multiple independent signals at once.

How it works

Each input signal is modulated onto a carrier at a distinct frequency, and the modulated subchannels are combined for transmission. Guard bands of unused spectrum between channels reduce interference. At the receiver, filters separate the channels and demodulators recover the original signals. When many channels are combined in a hierarchical scheme, groups of channels form supergroups and mastergroups, as in the classic analogue telephone carrier systems.

Applications

FDM underlies analogue radio and television broadcasting, cable television, and older telephone trunk systems. ADSL broadband divides the telephone line spectrum into many subcarriers using discrete multitone, a form of FDM. Its digital counterpart with overlapping orthogonal subcarriers is orthogonal frequency-division multiplexing (OFDM), used in Wi-Fi, LTE, and digital broadcasting. In optical networks the analogous technique is wavelength-division multiplexing.
