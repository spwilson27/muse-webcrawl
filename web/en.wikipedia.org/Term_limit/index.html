Term limit

A term limit is a legal restriction on the number of terms an officeholder may serve in a particular elected office. When term limits are found in presidential systems it generally describes a limit on the number of terms as head of state or government. Term limits may be lifetime limits or consecutive-term limits.

History and rationale

Rotation in office was practiced in ancient Athens and Rome and championed by republican thinkers. Supporters argue limits prevent entrenchment and corruption; critics argue they discard experience and shift power to staff, lobbyists, and bureaucrats.

Examples

In the United States, the Twenty-second Amendment (ratified 1951) limits the president to two elected terms, a reaction to Franklin D. Roosevelt's four elections. U.S. Supreme Court precedent (U.S. Term Limits, Inc. v. Thornton, 1995) holds that states may not impose term limits on members of Congress. Many states limit governors and state legislators. Other countries show wide variation: Mexico's president serves a single six-year term (sexenio), while some systems have abolished presidential term limits entirely, a move critics associate with democratic backsliding.
