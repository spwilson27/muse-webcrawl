Aymara (also Aymar aru) is an Aymaran language spoken by the Aymara people of the Bolivian Andes. It is one of only a handful of Native American languages with over a million speakers. Aymara is an official language in Bolivia and Peru, where it is spoken mainly around Lake Titicaca and on the Altiplano. It is also spoken in northern Chile and Argentina.

Classification

Aymara belongs to the small Aymaran family, whose other surviving member is Jaqaru, spoken in the Tupe district of Peru. A relationship with the neighbouring Quechuan languages has long been debated; the two families share a great deal of vocabulary through centuries of contact, but a genetic relationship is generally considered unproven.

Phonology and grammar

Aymara is famous among linguists for its three-way contrast between plain, aspirated and ejective stop consonants. It is a highly agglutinative, suffixing language: long words are built by chaining suffixes onto a root. Unusually, Aymara grammar obligatorily marks evidentiality — whether information was witnessed personally or known by hearsay — a feature that drew wide attention in popular discussions of the language. Aymara speakers also famously conceptualise the past as in front of the speaker (because it is known, like things seen) and the future as behind.

Status

Although Aymara has official status and is used in Bolivian government, media and intercultural bilingual education, Spanish continues to dominate, and the language is considered vulnerable. Radio broadcasting in Aymara, beginning in the 1960s, played an important role in standardising the language across dialect areas.
