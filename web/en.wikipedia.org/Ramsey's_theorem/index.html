Ramsey's theorem

FieldCombinatorics, graph theory
Named afterFrank P. Ramsey
Proved1930 (published posthumously)
Key conceptRamsey numbers R(s, t)

In combinatorics, Ramsey's theorem, named after the British mathematician Frank P. Ramsey, states that for any given integer colours and target clique sizes, there is a number — a Ramsey number — such that any sufficiently large complete graph whose edges are coloured will contain a monochromatic complete subgraph of the required size. Informally, the theorem captures the idea that complete disorder is impossible: in any large enough structure, some orderly pattern must appear.

Statement

In its best-known two-colour form: for any positive integers s and t there exists a smallest integer R(s, t) such that every red–blue edge colouring of the complete graph on R(s, t) vertices contains either a red complete subgraph on s vertices or a blue complete subgraph on t vertices. A classic example is R(3, 3) = 6: among any six people, there are three mutual acquaintances or three mutual strangers.

Ramsey numbers

Exact Ramsey numbers are notoriously hard to compute. Known values include R(3, 3) = 6 and R(4, 4) = 18, while R(5, 5) is known only to lie between 43 and 46. Paul Erdős famously remarked that if hostile aliens demanded the value of R(5, 5), humanity should marshal all its computers to find it, but if they demanded R(6, 6), humanity should instead attempt to destroy the aliens.

Generalizations

The theorem extends to more than two colours, to hypergraphs, and to an infinite version stating that an infinite complete graph with finitely coloured edges contains an infinite monochromatic complete subgraph. Ramsey's original paper, "On a Problem of Formal Logic" (1930), proved the result as a lemma in mathematical logic; it founded the field now called Ramsey theory.
