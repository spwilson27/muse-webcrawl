Gestational hypertension, also called pregnancy-induced hypertension, is the development of new high blood pressure during pregnancy in a woman whose blood pressure was previously normal. It is diagnosed when blood pressure reaches 140/90 mmHg or higher on two occasions after the 20th week of gestation, in the absence of protein in the urine or other signs of organ damage. When those additional features are present, the condition is classified as pre-eclampsia, a more serious disorder.

Classification

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy are generally divided into four categories: chronic hypertension (pre-dating pregnancy), gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia (including pre-eclampsia superimposed on chronic hypertension), and eclampsia, in which seizures occur. Together these disorders complicate roughly 5–10% of pregnancies and are among the leading causes of maternal and perinatal morbidity worldwide.

Signs and diagnosis

Gestational hypertension itself usually causes no symptoms and is detected at routine antenatal checks. Diagnosis requires properly measured blood pressure on at least two occasions at least four hours apart after 20 weeks. Laboratory tests — blood counts, liver and kidney function, and urine protein measurement — are used to exclude pre-eclampsia. Because a significant proportion of women initially diagnosed with gestational hypertension (estimates range from about 15% to nearly half, depending on gestational age at onset) go on to develop pre-eclampsia, ongoing surveillance of mother and fetus is essential.

Management

Management balances the risks of continuing the pregnancy against those of early delivery. It typically includes more frequent antenatal visits, home or clinic blood-pressure monitoring, serial ultrasound assessment of fetal growth, and laboratory follow-up. Antihypertensive medicines considered safe in pregnancy — including labetalol, methyldopa and extended-release nifedipine — are used when blood pressure reaches severe ranges (≥160/110 mmHg). Delivery is the definitive treatment and is usually recommended by 37–39 weeks in uncomplicated cases, earlier if severe features develop.

Prognosis

In most cases blood pressure returns to normal within days to weeks after delivery; by definition it resolves by 12 weeks postpartum, and persistence beyond that point suggests previously unrecognized chronic hypertension. Women who have had gestational hypertension have an increased long-term risk of chronic hypertension and cardiovascular disease, and a higher risk of recurrence in later pregnancies.
