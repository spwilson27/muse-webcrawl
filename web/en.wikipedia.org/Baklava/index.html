Baklava

Baklava is a layered pastry dessert made of filo dough, filled with chopped nuts and sweetened with syrup or honey. It is characteristic of the cuisines of the former Ottoman Empire and is widely eaten across the Balkans, Turkey, the Levant, and Central Asia.

Preparation

Layers of filo are brushed with butter or oil, sprinkled with walnuts, pistachios, or hazelnuts, baked, and soaked in a sweet syrup often flavored with rose water, orange blossom water, or lemon.

History

The dish's origins are debated; similar layered breads existed in Central Asian Turkic and ancient cuisines, but baklava took its modern form in the imperial kitchens of Topkapı Palace in Istanbul.

Regional varieties

Gaziantep baklava, made with Antep pistachios, holds protected status in Turkey and the EU. Greek, Armenian, Azerbaijani, and Arab versions vary in nut choice, spices, and cutting style.
