Genetic assimilation

Genetic assimilation is a process described by Conrad Hal Waddington by which a phenotype originally produced in response to an environmental condition, such as exposure to a teratogen, later becomes genetically encoded through artificial selection or natural selection, so that it appears even without the original environmental trigger.

Waddington's experiments

Waddington exposed pupae of the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster to a heat shock, producing adults lacking a crossvein in the wing in some individuals. By selectively breeding from the affected flies for many generations, he eventually obtained lines in which the crossveinless phenotype appeared even without heat treatment. In a parallel experiment, ether vapour induced a bithorax-like phenotype that likewise became constitutive after selection. Waddington interpreted these results as evidence that development is canalised, and that selection on standing genetic variation can stabilise an initially acquired response.

Mechanism

Unlike Lamarckian inheritance, genetic assimilation does not require the environment to induce new mutations. Instead, environmental stress reveals cryptic genetic variation that is normally buffered by developmental robustness — including heat-shock proteins such as Hsp90 — and selection then fixes combinations of alleles that lower the threshold for producing the trait.

Relation to the Baldwin effect

Genetic assimilation is often compared with the Baldwin effect, in which learned or plastic responses create selection pressures favouring innate equivalents. Waddington's concept is narrower and experimentally defined, emphasising developmental canalisation rather than learning.
