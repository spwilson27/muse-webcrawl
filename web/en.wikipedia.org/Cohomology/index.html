Cohomology

In mathematics, specifically in homology theory and algebraic topology, cohomology is a general term for a sequence of abelian groups, usually one associated with a topological space, often defined from a cochain complex. Cohomology can be viewed as a method of assigning richer algebraic invariants to a space than homology, with a ring structure given by the cup product that homology lacks.

Overview

Where homology groups are built from chains of simplices or singular simplices in a space, cohomology groups are built from cochains—functions on those chains—and inherit a graded-commutative ring structure. The singular cohomology groups Hn(X; A) of a space X agree with its homology when the coefficient group is a field in the sense of Poincaré duality for closed orientable manifolds, but they carry additional information, such as characteristic classes, that has proved fundamental in geometry and physics.

History and development

Cohomology emerged in the 1930s from the work of Kolmogorov, Alexander, Čech, and Whitney, as the dual notion to homology. De Rham cohomology, using differential forms, linked topology to analysis. Sheaf cohomology, developed by Leray, Cartan, and Grothendieck, generalized cohomology enormously and underlies modern algebraic geometry; étale cohomology provided the setting for the proof of the Weil conjectures. Other theories include group cohomology, Galois cohomology, Lie algebra cohomology, and the extraordinary cohomology theories of bordism and K-theory.

Applications

Cohomology classifies obstructions: for example, de Rham cohomology measures the failure of closed differential forms to be exact, and sheaf cohomology measures the failure of local solutions to glue into global ones. Characteristic classes such as Chern and Pontryagin classes live in cohomology and constrain the geometry of manifolds. In physics, cohomology appears in the BRST formalism of gauge theories and in the classification of anomalies.
