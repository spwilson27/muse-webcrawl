Zydeco

Zydeco (French: Zarico) is a music genre that was created by the Creole people of colour in southwest Louisiana, blending blues, rhythm and blues, and music indigenous to the Louisiana Creoles, such as la la and juré. Typically it features a button or piano accordion and a washboard instrument called a rub-board or frottoir. The genre originated as rural dance music — "la musique créole" — and by the mid-20th century had absorbed blues and R&B to become a distinct, driving party music centred on accordion-led two-steps and waltzes.

The word "zydeco" is often said to derive from the Louisiana French phrase les haricots sont pas salés ("the snap beans aren't salty", i.e. times are hard), slurred in Black Creole speech; another theory links it to the African word zari or the dance term zarico. Amédé Ardoin made the foundational recordings in the 1920s and 1930s. The genre's most famous figure is Clifton Chenier (1925–1987), the "King of Zydeco", who popularised the piano accordion, formed the first band built around the frottoir (played by his brother Cleveland), and won a Grammy Award. Other leading artists include Boozoo Chavis, Buckwheat Zydeco (Stanley Dural Jr.), Beau Jocque, Queen Ida and Rockin' Sidney, whose "My Toot Toot" (1985) was an international hit.

Characteristics

Zydeco is closely related to, but distinct from, the Cajun music of Louisiana's white French-speaking Acadiana: zydeco grew from Black Creole traditions, leans more heavily on blues, soul and Caribbean rhythms, and traditionally favours the piano accordion played with heavier syncopation. Songs are sung in English or Louisiana French/Creole, and the music remains the centrepiece of trail rides, church dances and festivals across southwest Louisiana and southeast Texas.
