Charles VII (22 February 1403 – 22 July 1461), called the Victorious (le Victorieux) and the Well-Served (le Bien-Servi), was King of France from 1422 until his death. Inheriting a kingdom dismembered by the Hundred Years' War — with the English and their Burgundian allies holding Paris and the north, and his own legitimacy impugned by the Treaty of Troyes — he reversed the war's course with the aid of Joan of Arc, reformed France's finances and army, and by 1453 had expelled the English from all of France except Calais.

The "King of Bourges"

The fifth son of Charles VI, Charles became Dauphin in 1417 after his elder brothers died. In 1418 he fled Burgundian-held Paris for Bourges; his partisans' murder of John the Fearless at Montereau (1419) drove Burgundy into the English alliance, and the Treaty of Troyes (1420) disinherited him in favour of Henry V of England. On his father's death in 1422 he claimed the crown but controlled only the centre and south of France and was mocked as the "King of Bourges," plagued by empty coffers and doubts — never dispelled — about his paternity.

Joan of Arc and the turning point

In 1429, with Orléans besieged, the peasant visionary Joan of Arc persuaded Charles to give her troops. The raising of the siege of Orléans (May 1429), the Loire campaign, and the victory at Patay were followed by the march to Reims, where Charles was crowned on 17 July 1429 in the traditional coronation city — a propaganda triumph that restored his legitimacy. Joan was captured in 1430 and executed by the English in 1431; Charles made no effective effort to save her, though in 1456 he authorised the nullification trial that annulled her condemnation.

Reform and victory

Guided by capable servants — the banker Jacques Cœur, the Bureau brothers in artillery, commanders Dunois, Richemont, and La Hire — Charles transformed the French state. At the Congress of Arras (1435) he detached Burgundy from England. The Pragmatic Sanction of Bourges (1438) asserted Gallican liberties. Royal taxation (the permanent taille, 1439) funded a standing army: the compagnies d'ordonnance (1445) and franc-archers (1448), while Jean Bureau built Europe's most effective siege artillery train. Paris was recovered in 1436; Normandy fell after Formigny (1450), and Gascony after Castillon (1453), where Talbot was killed — conventionally the end of the Hundred Years' War, leaving England only Calais.

Last years

Charles's later reign was shadowed by conflict with his son, the future Louis XI, who conspired against him and fled to Burgundy; the king grew fearful and reclusive, and died at Mehun-sur-Yèvre in 1461, reportedly starving himself in terror of being poisoned. His long liaison with Agnès Sorel, the first officially recognised royal mistress of a French king, was famous in its time.

Legacy

Charles VII inherited a collapsing monarchy and left a centralised, fiscally and militarily reformed France — the foundation of the early modern French state.
