Carnegie library

A typical Carnegie library, with the raised entrance and lamp of knowledge characteristic of the buildings

A Carnegie library is a library built with money donated by the Scottish-American businessman and philanthropist Andrew Carnegie. A total of 2,509 Carnegie libraries were built between 1883 and 1929, including 1,795 in the United States, 660 in Britain and Ireland, and others in Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, the Caribbean and elsewhere. At the time the last grants were made in 1919, nearly half of the 3,500 public libraries in the United States had been built with Carnegie funds.

History

The first Carnegie library was built in 1883 in Carnegie's birthplace, Dunfermline, Scotland. His first library in the United States opened in 1889 in Braddock, Pennsylvania, near his Edgar Thomson steel works. Carnegie believed that a public library was the best gift a community could receive and that the rich had a moral obligation to distribute their surplus wealth for public benefit, a philosophy he set out in his 1889 essay "The Gospel of Wealth".

The Carnegie formula

Grants were made under conditions later known as the "Carnegie formula": the community had to demonstrate a need, provide a building site, and — crucially — pledge tax support to maintain the library, typically committing to spend at least 10 per cent of the grant amount annually. Carnegie also insisted that libraries be free to all. Many Carnegie libraries pioneered open-stack access, letting readers browse the shelves themselves rather than requesting books from closed storage.

From 1908, grants in the United States were administered by the Carnegie Corporation of New York. James Bertram, Carnegie's secretary, standardized the application process and issued design guidance in his 1911 pamphlet Notes on Library Buildings, which encouraged simple, efficient plans. The buildings were constructed in a variety of the styles popular in the period — Beaux-Arts, Classical Revival, Italian Renaissance and Tudor Revival among them — and are typically recognizable by a prominent entrance stairway, symbolizing a person's elevation through learning, and a lamppost or lantern representing the light of knowledge.

Legacy

Carnegie library grants ended in 1919, by which time Carnegie had spent over $40 million in the United States alone on library construction. Hundreds of the buildings remain in use as public libraries, while others have been converted to museums, offices and arts centres; many are listed on historic registers. The programme is credited with seeding the modern American public library system and with promoting free access to books for immigrants, workers and rural communities. Critics note that some Southern communities excluded Black readers from Carnegie-funded libraries, and a small number of separate "colored" Carnegie libraries were built.
