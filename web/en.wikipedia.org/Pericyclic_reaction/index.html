Pericyclic reaction

In organic chemistry, a pericyclic reaction is a type of chemical reaction in which the transition state has a cyclic geometry, and bonds are broken and formed in a single concerted step through a cyclic redistribution of bonding electrons. Pericyclic reactions are usually rearrangement reactions.

Classes

The principal classes are electrocyclic reactions, cycloadditions (such as the Diels–Alder reaction), sigmatropic rearrangements (such as the Cope and Claisen rearrangements), cheletropic reactions, and group transfer reactions (such as the ene reaction).

Woodward–Hoffmann rules

Pericyclic reactions are governed by orbital symmetry: the Woodward–Hoffmann rules, formulated by Robert Burns Woodward and Roald Hoffmann in 1965, predict whether a reaction is thermally or photochemically allowed based on the number of π electrons and the stereochemistry (conrotatory versus disrotatory, suprafacial versus antarafacial) of the reacting components. Hoffmann shared the 1981 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this work.

Characteristics

Because they proceed without intermediates, pericyclic reactions are typically highly stereospecific and are relatively insensitive to solvent polarity and catalysts. They are of great importance in the synthesis of complex natural products.
