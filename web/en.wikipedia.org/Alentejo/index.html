Alentejo is a geographical and historical region of south-central Portugal. Its name derives from além-Tejo — "beyond the River Tagus". Covering roughly a third of mainland Portugal but home to only about 7% of its population, it is a land of rolling plains, cork oak and olive groves, whitewashed villages and intense summer heat.

Geography

The Alentejo is bounded by the Tagus to the north, Spain to the east, the Algarve to the south and the Atlantic to the west. Its landscape of montado — open savanna of cork oak and holm oak — supports extensive farming and grazing. The Alqueva Dam, on the Guadiana River, created one of Europe's largest artificial lakes and transformed irrigation in the region.

Economy and culture

Historically the "breadbasket of Portugal", the Alentejo produces wheat, olives and olive oil, wine, and about half the world's cork. Its red wines are among Portugal's most esteemed, and its black pigs, fed on acorns, yield prized presunto ham. The region is known for cante alentejano, a polyphonic singing tradition inscribed by UNESCO in 2014 on its list of Intangible Cultural Heritage. The capital, Évora, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site with a Roman temple and medieval walls; other towns include Beja, Portalegre, Elvas and Monsaraz.
