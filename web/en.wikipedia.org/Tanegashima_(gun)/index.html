Tanegashima (gun)

The tanegashima (種子島), also called hinawajū ("matchlock gun") or teppō ("iron cannon"), is a type of matchlock arquebus introduced to Japan by the Portuguese in 1543. It is named after the island of Tanegashima, south of Kyushu, where a Chinese junk carrying Portuguese adventurers was driven ashore by a storm.

Introduction

According to tradition, the lord of Tanegashima, Tanegashima Tokitaka, purchased two matchlock muskets from the Portuguese and ordered his swordsmith, Yaita Kinbei, to reverse-engineer them. Within years, Japanese smiths were producing the weapons in quantity, and the design spread across the country during the Sengoku period of civil war.

Impact on warfare

Firearms transformed Japanese warfare. Oda Nobunaga famously employed massed arquebusiers — commonly said to number some 3,000, firing in volleys — at the Battle of Nagashino in 1575, devastating the Takeda cavalry. Guns were also used extensively in Toyotomi Hideyoshi's invasions of Korea in the 1590s.

Design

The tanegashima followed the Portuguese snap matchlock pattern, with a serpentine arm holding a slow-burning match cord. Japanese versions were produced in various calibres and lengths, from short pistols to large-bore wall guns, often with lacquered stocks and brass fittings adapted to Japan's wet climate, including rain covers for the priming pan.

Later history

Contrary to the popular claim that Japan "gave up the gun" under the Tokugawa shogunate, firearms continued to be manufactured and maintained throughout the Edo period, though development stagnated and their social role was tightly controlled. Matchlocks were rapidly replaced by modern rifles after the opening of Japan in the 1850s.
