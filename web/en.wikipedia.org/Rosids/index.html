The rosids are a large clade of flowering plants comprising roughly 70,000 species—more than a quarter of all angiosperms—grouped into 17 orders and about 140 families. The clade is one of the two great divisions of the eudicots, alongside the asterids, and is recognised in the APG IV classification system, though it has no formal taxonomic rank.

Composition

The rosids divide into two major subclades, informally called the fabids (or eurosids I) and the malvids (or eurosids II). The fabids include the orders Rosales (roses, apples, elms), Fabales (legumes), Fagales (oaks, beeches, birches), Cucurbitales (melons, squashes), Malpighiales (willows, violets, passionflowers, spurges), Oxalidales, Celastrales and Zygophyllales. The malvids include Brassicales (cabbages, capers, papaya), Malvales (cotton, hibiscus, linden), Sapindales (maples, citrus, cashews), Myrtales (myrtles, eucalyptus, evening primroses), Geraniales, Crossosomatales, Picramniales and Huerteales. The order Vitales, containing the grape family, sits near the base of the rosids.

Characteristics

Rosids share a relatively recent common ancestor, dated to roughly 99–125 million years ago during the Cretaceous radiation of flowering plants. No single obvious morphological feature unites them; the clade is defined primarily by molecular (DNA sequence) evidence. Flowers are commonly polypetalous with numerous stamens, and many members form symbiotic root nodules with nitrogen-fixing bacteria—notably the legumes and actinorhizal plants, a distribution that prompted the hypothesis of a single evolutionary "predisposition" to nodulation within part of the fabid clade.

Importance

Rosids dominate many terrestrial ecosystems—oaks, beeches and legumes structure forests and grasslands worldwide—and supply a large fraction of human food, timber, fibre and medicines.
