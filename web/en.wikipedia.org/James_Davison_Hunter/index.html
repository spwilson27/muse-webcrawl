James Davison Hunter (born 1952) is an American sociologist at the University of Virginia, where he is the LaBrosse-Levinson Distinguished Professor of Religion, Culture and Social Theory and the founder and executive director of the Institute for Advanced Studies in Culture. He is best known for his 1991 book Culture Wars: The Struggle to Define America, which introduced the phrase "culture wars" into wide scholarly and public use.

Education and career

Hunter received his doctorate from Rutgers University and joined the University of Virginia faculty in the 1980s. His early work examined American evangelicalism, including American Evangelicalism: Conservative Religion and the Quandary of Modernity (1983) and Evangelicalism: The Coming Generation (1987), which documented generational change within evangelical colleges and seminaries.

Culture wars thesis

In Culture Wars (1991), Hunter argued that American public conflict had been reorganised around a new cleavage — between "orthodox" and "progressive" moral visions — that cut across older religious and denominational divides. On issues such as abortion, sexuality, education and the arts, alliances increasingly formed between people of different faiths who shared moral assumptions, rather than between co-religionists. The thesis became a standard reference point in debates about American polarisation, though later scholars questioned the degree to which ordinary citizens, as opposed to activists and elites, were mobilised along these lines.

Later work

Hunter's subsequent books include The Death of Character (2000), on moral education; To Change the World: The Irony, Tragedy, and Possibility of Christianity in the Late Modern World (2010), which criticised dominant evangelical strategies of cultural and political engagement and proposed "faithful presence" as an alternative; and Science and the Good (2018, with Paul Nedelisky), on moral arguments in science. With Alan Wolfe he co-edited Is There a Culture War? (2006). His book Democracy and Solidarity (2024) examines the crisis of American liberal democracy and the "cultural war" dynamics underlying it.
