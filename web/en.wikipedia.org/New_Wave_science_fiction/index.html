New Wave science fiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

New Worlds under Michael Moorcock's editorship

The New Wave is a movement in science fiction produced in the 1960s and 1970s and characterised by a high degree of experimentation with the form and content of stories, greater literary merit, and a high degree of sensitivity to scientific and literary sophistication, as opposed to the perception of science fiction's focus until then on hard science.

Origins

The term "New Wave" was borrowed from film, specifically the French nouvelle vague. The movement is most closely associated with the British magazine New Worlds, which under the editorship of Michael Moorcock from 1964 became a vehicle for experimental writing influenced by modernism and the counterculture. Writers such as J. G. Ballard argued for "inner space" — the psychological landscapes of modern life — rather than outer space as the true frontier for science fiction.

Writers and influence

Major figures include J. G. Ballard, Brian Aldiss, M. John Harrison, and Norman Spinrad in Britain, and in the United States Harlan Ellison — whose anthology Dangerous Visions (1967) became the movement's manifesto — Samuel R. Delany, Roger Zelazny, Ursula K. Le Guin, Joanna Russ, and Thomas M. Disch. The New Wave brought stylistic experiment, literary ambition, and themes of sexuality, politics, and entropy into the genre, permanently expanding what science fiction could do.

By the late 1970s the movement had dissipated, its innovations absorbed into mainstream science fiction, though traditionalists had criticised it for abandoning storytelling and scientific rigour.
