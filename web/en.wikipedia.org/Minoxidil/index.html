Minoxidil

Minoxidil
Type: vasodilator, potassium channel opener
Uses: hypertension; androgenetic alopecia
Brands: Loniten (oral), Rogaine/Regaine (topical)

Minoxidil is a medication used for the treatment of high blood pressure and pattern hair loss. It is an antihypertensive vasodilator originally developed by the Upjohn Company in the 1960s. Taken orally (as Loniten), it is reserved for severe hypertension that does not respond to other treatments, because of its side-effect profile. Applied topically to the scalp (as Rogaine or Regaine), low-dose minoxidil is widely used to promote hair growth in men and women with androgenetic alopecia, and is available over the counter in many countries.

Medical uses

Oral minoxidil was approved by the US Food and Drug Administration in 1979 for refractory hypertension. During clinical trials, patients unexpectedly developed hypertrichosis (excessive hair growth), which led researchers to develop a topical formulation for hair loss; the 2% solution was approved for men in 1988 and for women in 1991, with a 5% formulation and a foam preparation following later.

Mechanism of action

Minoxidil is a potassium channel opener that causes hyperpolarisation of cell membranes. In blood vessels this produces vasodilation. Its hair-growth effect is thought to involve its active metabolite, minoxidil sulfate, opening ATP-sensitive potassium channels in hair follicles, prolonging the anagen (growth) phase and enlarging miniaturised follicles, although the exact mechanism remains incompletely understood.

Side effects

Topical use commonly causes scalp irritation and unwanted facial hair growth. Oral use carries more serious risks, including fluid retention, tachycardia, and pericardial effusion, and it is usually combined with a beta blocker and a diuretic when used for blood pressure.
