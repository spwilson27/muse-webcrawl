Thomas Merton (31 January 1915 – 10 December 1968) was an American Trappist monk, writer, theologian, mystic, poet and social activist. A monk of the Abbey of Gethsemani in Kentucky, he was the author of more than 50 books and numerous essays and reviews. His autobiography, The Seven Storey Mountain (1948), became a bestseller and is considered one of the most influential spiritual works of the twentieth century.

Early life and conversion

Merton was born in Prades, France, to an American mother and a New Zealand artist father. Orphaned young — his mother died when he was six and his father when he was sixteen — he was educated in France, England and the United States, studying at Clare College, Cambridge, and at Columbia University in New York. After a restless, bohemian youth, he converted to Roman Catholicism in 1938 and in December 1941 entered the Abbey of Gethsemani, a monastery of the Order of Cistercians of the Strict Observance (Trappists).

Writings and activism

The Seven Storey Mountain, edited and championed by Robert Giroux, recounted Merton's conversion and sold in the hundreds of thousands, inspiring many postwar readers and not a few vocations. His later work explored contemplative prayer (New Seeds of Contemplation, 1961), Zen Buddhism and Eastern spirituality (Mystics and Zen Masters, 1967; Zen and the Birds of Appetite, 1968), and engaged urgently with the social questions of the day: he wrote against racism and nuclear war and in support of the civil rights movement and non-violence, corresponded with figures including Martin Luther King Jr., Dorothy Day and Thich Nhat Hanh, and was an important dialogue partner in early interfaith exchange between Christianity and Asian religions.

Death and legacy

Merton died on 10 December 1968 in Bangkok, Thailand, where he was attending a conference of Benedictine and Cistercian monks; he was found dead after apparently receiving an electric shock from a faulty fan. His journals and letters, published posthumously, have added to his reputation as one of the most significant Catholic writers of the century.
