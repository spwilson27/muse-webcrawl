Human–computer interaction

Human–computer interaction (HCI) is a multidisciplinary field of study focusing on the design of computer technology and, in particular, the interaction between humans (the users) and computers. Initially concerned with desktop computers, HCI has expanded to cover almost all forms of information technology design, including mobile devices, wearable computing, virtual and augmented reality, and voice interfaces.

HCI studies how people interact with computing systems and how to design interfaces for them.

History

The roots of HCI lie in early interactive systems such as Ivan Sutherland's Sketchpad (1963), Douglas Engelbart's oN-Line System — demonstrated in the 1968 "Mother of All Demos" with the mouse, hypertext and collaborative editing — and the graphical user interfaces developed at Xerox PARC in the 1970s. The field coalesced as an academic discipline in the early 1980s, with the founding of the ACM's SIGCHI conference and influential works such as Card, Moran and Newell's The Psychology of Human–Computer Interaction (1983), which introduced the Model Human Processor and keystroke-level models.

Principles and methods

HCI draws on computer science, cognitive psychology, design, and ergonomics. Central concerns include:

  • Usability — effectiveness, efficiency and satisfaction, often summarised by Jakob Nielsen's usability heuristics.
  • User-centred design — iterative design involving prototyping and evaluation with users.
  • Interaction paradigms — command languages, menus, direct manipulation, graphical user interfaces, touch, gesture, speech and tangible interfaces.
  • Evaluation methods — usability testing, heuristic evaluation, cognitive walkthroughs, A/B testing and field studies.
  • Accessibility — designing for users with disabilities, codified in guidelines such as the Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG).

Research areas

Modern HCI research addresses social computing, computer-supported cooperative work, affective computing, ubiquitous computing, brain–computer interfaces, and the ethics of persuasive and algorithmic systems. Donald Norman's concept of affordances and his book The Design of Everyday Things helped popularise human-centred design beyond computing.
