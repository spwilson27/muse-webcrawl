Micrometeorite

A micrometeorite is a micrometeoroid that has survived entry through the Earth's atmosphere and reached the surface. Micrometeorites are typically between about 50 micrometres and 2 millimetres in size and consist of dust particles originating largely from comets and asteroids. Tens of thousands of tonnes of extraterrestrial material fall to Earth each year, overwhelmingly in this dust form.

Formation and types

Interplanetary dust particles entering the atmosphere at high velocity are heated by friction; some vaporise as meteors, while slower or smaller particles melt partially or wholly and solidify into cosmic spherules — glassy or stony spheres — while others, called scoriaceous and unmelted micrometeorites, retain parts of their original mineralogy, including delicate hydrated minerals and organic matter. Unmelted micrometeorites are among the most primitive solar system materials available for study.

Collection

Micrometeorites are collected from places where they accumulate relatively uncontaminated: Antarctic ice and snow (notably at Dome C, where meltwater concentrates yielded large pristine collections), deep-sea sediments, Greenland ice, and — increasingly — urban rooftops, gutters and drainage sediment, where magnetic separation and careful microscopy can recover cosmic spherules. The first identifications came from the Challenger expedition's deep-sea samples in the 19th century, and collectors such as Jon Larsen popularised urban micrometeorite hunting from the 2010s.

Significance

Because they sample cometary and asteroidal dust directly, micrometeorites inform studies of the early Solar System, the delivery of water and organic molecules to the early Earth, and the zodiacal cloud.
