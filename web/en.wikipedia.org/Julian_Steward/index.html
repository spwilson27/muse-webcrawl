Julian Haynes Steward (1902 – 1972) was an American anthropologist best known for developing the theory and method of cultural ecology and the concept of multilinear cultural evolution. He directed the monumental Handbook of South American Indians and influenced generations of anthropologists through his teaching at Columbia University and the University of Illinois.

Life and career

Steward studied at Cornell and the University of California, Berkeley, where he took his doctorate in 1929 under Alfred Kroeber and Robert Lowie. He did fieldwork among the Shoshone and Paiute of the Great Basin, producing classic studies of how arid environments shaped small-scale social organization. After posts at Michigan and Utah and work at the Smithsonian's Institute of Social Anthropology, he joined Columbia in 1946, where his students — including Eric Wolf, Sidney Mintz and Marvin Harris — reshaped American anthropology. In 1952 he moved to the University of Illinois at Urbana.

Theory

In Theory of Culture Change (1955) Steward set out cultural ecology: the study of how a society's technology and economy — its "culture core" — adapt to its environment, and proposed multilinear evolution, the idea that cultures change along several distinct trajectories rather than a single universal ladder. He also led the interdisciplinary Virú Valley project in Peru (1946), a pioneering study of settlement patterns and long-term cultural development.
