Junzi

The junzi (Chinese: 君子; pinyin: jūnzǐ) is a Chinese philosophical term often translated as "gentleman", "superior person", or "noble man". In Confucianism, the junzi is the ideal ethical exemplar: a person who cultivates virtue, propriety and righteousness, in contrast to the xiaoren (小人), the "small" or petty person who is driven by profit and self-interest.

History of the term

Before Confucius, junzi denoted hereditary nobility — literally the son of a ruler. Confucius (551–479 BCE) transformed the word into an ethical category: nobility was redefined as a quality of character achieved through self-cultivation rather than birth. In the Analects, the junzi is described as impartial, at ease, devoted to righteousness (yi), and attentive to ritual propriety (li).

Characteristics

The junzi embodies the Confucian virtues of benevolence (ren), righteousness, propriety, wisdom and trustworthiness. Confucius taught that "the junzi understands what is righteous; the xiaoren understands what is profitable." Later Confucians such as Mencius and Xunzi developed the concept further, debating whether moral excellence arises from innate sprouts of virtue or from deliberate ritual training.

Influence

The ideal of the junzi shaped East Asian ethics, education and ideals of rulership for over two thousand years, and remains a common expression in modern Chinese for a person of integrity and honour.
