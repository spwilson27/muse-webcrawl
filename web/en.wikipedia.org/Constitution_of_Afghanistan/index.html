Constitution of Afghanistan

The National Assembly building in Kabul, Afghanistan.

The Constitution of Afghanistan is the supreme law of the country. Afghanistan has had a succession of constitutions since the early twentieth century, reflecting the country's shifting political systems, from monarchy to republic, communist rule, Islamic state, and, most recently, an Islamic republic established after 2001.

History

The first Afghan constitution was promulgated in 1923 under King Amanullah Khan, as part of his programme of modernisation. Subsequent constitutions were adopted in 1931, 1964, 1977, 1987 and 1990, each reflecting the ideology of the regime in power. The 1964 constitution, adopted under King Zahir Shah, is often regarded as the most liberal of the monarchical era, introducing a constitutional monarchy with an elected parliament.

Following the fall of the Taliban in 2001, the Bonn Agreement set in motion a process that culminated in a Constitutional Loya Jirga in late 2003 and early 2004. The resulting constitution was ratified on 4 January 2004 and signed by President Hamid Karzai on 26 January 2004.

The 2004 Constitution

The 2004 constitution established Afghanistan as an Islamic republic with a strong presidential system. It comprised a preamble and 162 articles arranged in twelve chapters. Islam was declared the religion of the state, and no law could be contrary to the beliefs and provisions of Islam. At the same time, the constitution guaranteed fundamental rights including equality before the law, freedom of expression, and equal rights for men and women.

The constitution created a bicameral National Assembly consisting of the Wolesi Jirga (House of the People) and the Meshrano Jirga (House of Elders), an independent judiciary headed by the Supreme Court, and reserved seats for women and for the Kuchi nomadic community. Pashto and Dari were recognised as official languages, with provision for other languages in areas where they were widely spoken.

Status after 2021

After the Taliban returned to power in August 2021, the status of the 2004 constitution became uncertain. Taliban officials indicated that governance would proceed under Islamic law, and in 2022 the movement's leadership effectively set the republican constitution aside, while suggesting that a new constitutional framework might be prepared. The precise legal position has remained unsettled.
