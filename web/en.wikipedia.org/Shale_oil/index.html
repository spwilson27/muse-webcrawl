Shale oil

Shale oil is an unconventional oil produced from oil shale rock fragments by pyrolysis, hydrogenation, or thermal dissolution. These processes convert the organic matter within the rock (kerogen) into synthetic oil and gas. The resulting oil can be used immediately as a fuel oil or upgraded to meet refinery feedstock specifications by adding hydrogen and removing sulfur and nitrogen impurities.

The term "shale oil" is also used for crude oil produced from shale formations (tight oil) by hydraulic fracturing — a distinct resource, properly called tight oil. This article concerns oil derived from oil shale kerogen.

History

Oil shale was used as a fuel in Mesopotamia as early as 3000 BC, and shale oil extraction was patented in France and Britain in the 17th and 19th centuries — James Young's 1850 patent in Scotland founded an industry that thrived until conventional crude undercut it in the early 20th century. Estonia built a durable kukersite shale industry from 1916, using shale oil for power generation and district heating, and China (Fushun) and Brazil (Petrosix process) have maintained significant production.

Production

In ex-situ retorting, mined shale is crushed and heated in the absence of oxygen to about 500 °C, decomposing kerogen into shale oil vapour, combustible gas and a carbonaceous residue (spent shale). In-situ methods heat the shale underground over months or years and pump the products to the surface, avoiding mining but presenting greater technical and environmental challenges. Raw shale oil typically contains high levels of nitrogen, sulfur and olefins, and must be hydrotreated before refining into gasoline, diesel and jet fuel.

Reserves and economics

The largest deposit is the Green River Formation in Colorado, Utah and Wyoming in the United States, estimated to hold the equivalent of several trillion barrels of oil in place; large deposits also exist in Australia, Jordan, Morocco and the Congo. Production remains small compared with conventional crude because of high costs, low energy return on investment, and the large volumes of spent shale and process water requiring disposal.

Environmental issues

Shale oil production is more carbon-intensive than conventional oil production, uses significant water, and generates alkaline waste rock that can leach contaminants; these factors have limited development in most countries.
