History of Pakistan


The Great Bath at Mohenjo-daro, a city of the Indus Valley Civilisation

The history of Pakistan spans the region of the Indus Valley and adjoining areas of north-western South Asia. The region was home to one of the world's earliest urban civilisations, the Indus Valley Civilisation (c. 3300–1300 BCE), and was successively part of Persian, Greek, Mauryan, Kushan, Gupta, Arab, Turkic, Mughal and British empires. The modern state of Pakistan emerged on 14 August 1947 as a homeland for the Muslims of British India, following the campaign led by Muhammad Ali Jinnah and the All-India Muslim League.

Ancient period

The Neolithic site of Mehrgarh (from c. 7000 BCE) shows early farming in the region. The Indus Valley Civilisation, centred on cities such as Mohenjo-daro and Harappa, developed sophisticated urban planning, drainage and trade links with Mesopotamia before declining after 1900 BCE. Vedic culture followed in the Punjab. The Achaemenid Empire incorporated Gandhara and the Indus satrapies in the 6th century BCE; Alexander the Great campaigned here in 326 BCE, defeating King Porus at the Hydaspes. The Mauryan Empire, and later the Indo-Greek, Kushan and Gupta states, made the region a crossroads of Buddhism, Hinduism and trade along the Silk Road; the university of Taxila was famed across Asia.

Medieval period

In 711–712 CE the Umayyad general Muhammad ibn al-Qasim conquered Sindh, introducing Islam to the region. From the 11th century, Turkic dynasties — the Ghaznavids (centred on Lahore), Ghurids and the Delhi Sultanate (from 1206) — extended Muslim rule across the Indus plain. Babur's victory at Panipat in 1526 founded the Mughal Empire, under which Lahore, Multan and Thatta flourished as centres of commerce, Sufi devotion and architecture. After Mughal decline in the 18th century, power fragmented among Afghan Durranis, Marathas, Baloch and Sindhi Talpur amirs, and the Sikh Empire of Ranjit Singh (1799–1849), which ruled Punjab from Lahore.

British rule

The British East India Company annexed Sindh in 1843 and Punjab in 1849 after two Anglo-Sikh wars. During the Indian Rebellion of 1857 the region largely supported the British. Under the Raj, the Punjab canal colonies transformed agriculture, and the region became a major recruiting ground for the British Indian Army. The All-India Muslim League was founded in 1906; Muhammad Ali Jinnah emerged as its leader, and the Lahore Resolution of March 1940 demanded separate states for Muslims in the north-west and east of India. The word "Pakistan" had been coined by Choudhry Rahmat Ali in 1933.

Independence and partition

Under the Indian Independence Act, British India was partitioned and Pakistan came into being on 14 August 1947, with Karachi as capital and Jinnah as Governor-General. Partition displaced an estimated 10–15 million people amid communal violence that killed several hundred thousand to a million or more. The new state comprised two wings, West Pakistan and East Bengal (East Pakistan), separated by 1,600 km of Indian territory. Dispute over the princely state of Jammu and Kashmir provoked the first Indo-Pakistani war (1947–48) and remains unresolved.

Early decades

Pakistan struggled with constitution-making, finally adopting a constitution in 1956 as an Islamic republic. Political instability led to the first military coup by Ayub Khan in 1958. Ayub's decade saw economic growth and the construction of the Indus Basin works, but also war with India in 1965. Yahya Khan succeeded in 1969 and held elections in 1970, won in the east by Sheikh Mujibur Rahman's Awami League; the refusal to transfer power triggered the Bangladesh Liberation War of 1971, after which East Pakistan became independent Bangladesh following Indian intervention. Zulfikar Ali Bhutto then led a civilian government, promulgating the 1973 constitution and starting a nuclear weapons programme; he was overthrown by General Zia-ul-Haq in 1977 and executed in 1979.

Zia era and return to democracy

Zia's Islamisation programme and Pakistan's role as a conduit for support to the Afghan mujahideen during the Soviet–Afghan War shaped the 1980s. After Zia's death in a plane crash in 1988, democracy returned with alternating governments of Benazir Bhutto (Pakistan Peoples Party) and Nawaz Sharif (Pakistan Muslim League), both repeatedly dismissed amid corruption allegations. In 1998 Pakistan conducted nuclear tests in response to India's. The Kargil conflict of 1999 was followed by Pervez Musharraf's coup.

21st century

After 2001 Pakistan allied with the United States in the war on terror while fighting its own insurgency by the Pakistani Taliban. Musharraf resigned in 2008; an elected government completed a full term for the first time in 2013. Imran Khan became prime minister in 2018 and was removed by a no-confidence vote in 2022. Pakistan has faced recurring economic crises, devastating floods (notably in 2010 and 2022), and continuing tensions with India, including the 2019 Pulwama–Balakot crisis.
