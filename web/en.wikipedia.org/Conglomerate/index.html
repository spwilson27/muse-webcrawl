Conglomerate

Headquarters of a diversified conglomerate.

A conglomerate is a multi-industry company—that is, a combination of multiple business entities operating in entirely different industries under one corporate group, usually involving a parent company and many subsidiaries. Conglomerates are often large and multinational. The term also describes a corporation formed by the acquisition of unrelated companies, in contrast to mergers within one industry.

History

Diversified conglomerates flourished in the United States in the 1960s, when firms such as ITT, Litton Industries, Gulf and Western, and Textron grew rapidly by acquiring businesses in unrelated fields, financed in part by favourable accounting and high share prices. Investors later demanded focus, and from the 1980s many conglomerates were broken up or spun off divisions; markets are said to apply a "conglomerate discount," valuing diversified groups below the sum of their parts. Industrial families and state-linked groups in Asia, such as the Japanese keiretsu, Korean chaebol, and India's Tata and Reliance groups, retain diversified structures.

Notable examples

Berkshire Hathaway, built by Warren Buffett from a textile company into a holding company spanning insurance, railroads, energy, and manufacturing, is the most celebrated modern conglomerate. Others include General Electric—long the archetype before its 2021 announcement of a split into three companies, completed in 2024—as well as Alphabet, Samsung, Tata Group, and Virgin Group.

Advantages and criticism

Supporters argue conglomerates allocate capital internally to their best uses, diversify risk across markets, and can nurture new businesses. Critics contend they often allocate capital poorly, obscure the performance of individual units, complicate management, and trade at depressed valuations, which has driven waves of demergers and activist campaigns for breakups.
