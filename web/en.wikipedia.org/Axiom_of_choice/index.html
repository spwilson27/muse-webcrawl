Axiom of choice

[Image placeholder: choosing one element from each of many sets]

In set theory, the axiom of choice, abbreviated AC or AoC, is an axiom of Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory stating that for every indexed family of non-empty sets there exists a choice function that selects exactly one element from each set in the family — even when there is no explicit rule by which the selections can be made.

History and controversy

Ernst Zermelo formulated the axiom in 1904 to prove that every set can be well-ordered. It provoked lasting controversy because it asserts the existence of objects (such as choice functions on the real numbers) that cannot be explicitly constructed. Kurt Gödel showed in 1938 that AC is consistent with the other axioms of set theory, and Paul Cohen showed in 1963 that its negation is also consistent, so AC is independent of ZF.

Equivalent statements

Many important theorems are equivalent to the axiom of choice over ZF, including: every set can be well-ordered; every vector space has a basis; every product of compact spaces is compact (Tychonoff's theorem); and Zorn's lemma, routinely used in algebra and analysis. Weaker consequences include the existence of non-measurable sets of real numbers.

Paradoxical consequences

AC implies the Banach–Tarski paradox: a solid ball can be decomposed into finitely many pieces and reassembled into two balls identical to the original. Such results led some mathematicians to seek constructive alternatives, though the axiom is accepted by most working mathematicians and is built into the standard ZFC system.
