Decipherment

Decipherment is the analysis of documents written in ancient languages that are unknown to the reader, or in writing systems that are no longer understood. It is distinguished from the cryptanalysis of modern codes in that no bilingual key, codebook, or living tradition of readers may exist. Successful decipherments have recovered whole literatures and histories, most famously those of Egyptian hieroglyphs, Mesopotamian cuneiform, and Maya script.

Famous decipherments

Egyptian hieroglyphs

The discovery of the Rosetta Stone in 1799, bearing the same decree in hieroglyphic, Demotic, and Greek, gave scholars a bilingual key. Thomas Young identified cartouches as royal names, and Jean-François Champollion announced the decipherment in 1822, recognising that the signs were partly phonetic and that Coptic preserved the ancient language.

Cuneiform

Georg Friedrich Grotefend made early progress with Old Persian cuneiform at Persepolis, and Henry Rawlinson's copying of the trilingual Behistun Inscription in the 1830s–1840s opened the way to Akkadian and Sumerian. In 1857 four scholars independently produced matching translations of an Assyrian text, publicly confirming the decipherment.

Linear B and Maya script

Michael Ventris, working from Alice Kober's analyses, showed in 1952 that Linear B encoded an early form of Greek. Maya hieroglyphs were deciphered through the 20th century, with Yuri Knorozov's phonetic approach and later work by Tatiana Proskouriakoff and others demonstrating that the inscriptions recorded dynastic history.

Methods

Decipherers typically look for known proper names, bilingual texts, repeated formulaic phrases, sign-frequency patterns, and related known languages. Numerical systems and place-name lists are often the first elements recovered. A claimed decipherment is generally accepted only when it yields consistent, predictive readings of previously unread texts.

Undeciphered scripts

Several scripts remain undeciphered, notably the Indus script, Linear A, Cretan hieroglyphs, Rongorongo of Easter Island, and the Voynich manuscript. Lack of bilingual texts and doubt about the underlying languages make these problems especially resistant.
