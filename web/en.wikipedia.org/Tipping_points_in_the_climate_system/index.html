Tipping points in the climate system

Tipping points in the climate system are critical thresholds at which a small change in forcing can push a component of the Earth system into a qualitatively different state, often irreversibly on human timescales. Once crossed, such a threshold can trigger self-perpetuating change even if the original forcing is later reduced.

Major tipping elements

Proposed tipping elements include the collapse of the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets, the dieback of the Amazon rainforest, the weakening or collapse of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation, the thawing of permafrost with associated methane release, shifts in major monsoon systems, and the loss of tropical coral reefs. Research published in 2022 suggested that several tipping points become possible even at around 1.5 °C of global warming.

Feedbacks and cascades

Tipping elements interact: warming from one transition can increase the likelihood of others, producing a "tipping cascade". Positive feedbacks such as the ice–albedo effect and carbon release from thawing permafrost amplify warming, while committed sea-level rise from ice-sheet loss would continue for centuries to millennia.

Policy relevance

The concept underlies arguments for rapid emissions reductions to keep warming well below 2 °C, as framing in the Paris Agreement. Because of uncertainty in threshold locations, risk assessments treat tipping points as low-likelihood but high-impact outcomes warranting precaution.
