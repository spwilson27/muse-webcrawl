Tariff in United States history

The tariff in United States history was a central political and economic issue from the founding of the republic until the mid-20th century. Tariffs were the federal government's largest source of revenue before the income tax, and protectionist duties were championed to shelter American industry from foreign competition.

Early republic

The Tariff Act of 1789 was among the first laws passed by Congress. Alexander Hamilton's Report on Manufactures (1791) argued for protective duties. The high Tariff of 1828, denounced in the South as the "Tariff of Abominations", provoked the Nullification Crisis of 1832–33.

19th and 20th centuries

After the Civil War, high protective tariffs became Republican orthodoxy, culminating in the McKinley Tariff of 1890 and the Dingley Act of 1897. The Underwood Tariff of 1913 lowered rates and introduced the modern income tax. The Smoot–Hawley Tariff of 1930 raised duties sharply and is widely blamed for deepening the Great Depression. The Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act of 1934 began a long shift toward liberalised trade, continued through the GATT and later the WTO.
