Scintigraphy

Field: Nuclear medicine

Uses: Imaging of internal organs and tissues

Equipment: Gamma camera

Scintigraphy (from Latin scintilla, "spark") is a form of diagnostic test in nuclear medicine in which radioisotopes attached to drugs that travel to a specific organ or tissue (radiopharmaceuticals) are taken internally, and the emitted gamma rays are captured by a gamma camera to form two-dimensional images. Unlike X-rays, which pass through the body from an external source, scintigraphy images radiation emitted from within the body.

Procedure

A radiopharmaceutical, commonly based on technetium-99m, is injected, swallowed or inhaled. After the tracer concentrates in the tissue of interest, the gamma camera records the distribution of radioactivity, producing a functional image that shows how the organ is working rather than merely its shape.

Uses

Scintigraphy is used to examine the bones, heart, lungs, thyroid, liver, kidneys and brain. Bone scans can reveal fractures, infection or cancer spread; myocardial perfusion scans assess blood flow to the heart muscle; ventilation–perfusion scans help diagnose pulmonary embolism.

Related techniques

Three-dimensional variants include single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT). Positron emission tomography (PET) uses a related principle with positron-emitting tracers.
