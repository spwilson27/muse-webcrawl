First Vatican Council

The First Ecumenical Council of the Vatican, commonly known as the First Vatican Council or Vatican I, was convoked by Pope Pius IX on 29 June 1868 and opened on 8 December 1869 in St. Peter's Basilica. It was the 20th ecumenical council recognized by the Catholic Church and the first held at the Vatican — hence its name.

Background

Pius IX summoned the council amid challenges to the Church from rationalism, liberalism and Italian nationalism, which threatened the Papal States. Nearly 800 council fathers, mostly European bishops, attended — then the largest gathering of bishops in history.

Decrees

The council issued two dogmatic constitutions. Dei Filius (April 1870) treated God, revelation and faith, condemning materialism, pantheism and rationalism. Pastor Aeternus (July 1870) defined the universal jurisdiction of the Pope and, most famously, the dogma of papal infallibility: that when the Pope speaks ex cathedra on faith and morals, he is preserved from error. A minority of bishops who opposed the definition left Rome before the vote; a small number, led by Ignaz von Döllinger, later formed the Old Catholic Churches.

Suspension

On 20 September 1870, Italian troops captured Rome, completing Italian unification and ending the Papal States. Pius IX suspended the council indefinitely on 20 October 1870. It was never resumed and was formally closed only in 1960, on the eve of the Second Vatican Council.
