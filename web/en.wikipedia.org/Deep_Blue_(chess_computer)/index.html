Deep Blue (chess computer)

Deep Blue was a chess-playing expert system run on a unique purpose-built IBM supercomputer. It was the first computer to win a game, and the first to win a match, against a reigning world champion under regular time controls. Developed at IBM by a team led by Feng-hsiung Hsu, Murray Campbell, and A. Joseph Hoane Jr., Deep Blue defeated Garry Kasparov in a six-game rematch in May 1997 by 3½–2½, a result that made headlines worldwide as a landmark in the history of artificial intelligence.

A Deep Blue cabinet, now exhibited at the Computer History Museum.

Development

The project began at Carnegie Mellon University in 1985, when Hsu and Campbell built ChipTest, a chess-playing system based on a custom VLSI search chip. Its successor Deep Thought won the 1988 North American Computer Chess Championship and became the first computer to defeat a grandmaster in tournament play (Bent Larsen, 1988). Kasparov beat Deep Thought decisively in a two-game exhibition in 1989. IBM hired the team, renamed the effort Deep Blue — a play on IBM's nickname "Big Blue" — and continued developing custom chess hardware at the Thomas J. Watson Research Center. Grandmasters, including Joel Benjamin, helped shape its opening book and evaluation function.

The matches

In February 1996 in Philadelphia, an earlier version of Deep Blue won the first game against Kasparov — the first computer victory over a world champion in a classical game — but Kasparov adjusted his strategy, avoided the machine's strengths, and won the match 4–2. IBM upgraded the machine, doubling its speed. In the May 1997 rematch in New York, Deep Blue could evaluate roughly 200 million positions per second using 480 special-purpose chess chips distributed across a 30-node RS/6000 SP system, searching with a sophisticated alpha–beta algorithm enhanced by quiescence search, singular extensions, and a handcrafted evaluation function with thousands of features.

Kasparov won game one; Deep Blue won game two after Kasparov, unnerved by a subtle positional move he later suspected might have been human-assisted, resigned in a position analysts later showed he could have drawn by perpetual check. After three draws, Kasparov, playing uncharacteristically passively in the final game, lost in 19 moves in a known opening line. Deep Blue won the match 3½–2½. Kasparov accused IBM of cheating by human intervention; IBM denied it and declined a further rematch, retiring Deep Blue. Logs later released showed no intervention; the "human-like" move had arisen from the machine's evaluation and a quirk in its search.

Legacy

Deep Blue's victory demonstrated the power of brute-force search combined with specialised hardware and expert-tuned evaluation, and it shifted public expectations about machine capability. The underlying parallel-search and hardware-acceleration techniques informed later IBM systems; the company framed its subsequent challenge, Watson's 2011 Jeopardy! victory, in similar terms. Modern chess engines such as Stockfish and the neural-network-based AlphaZero far exceed Deep Blue's strength on commodity hardware, using fundamentally different methods. One of Deep Blue's two racks is displayed at the Computer History Museum in California, and the other at the Smithsonian.
