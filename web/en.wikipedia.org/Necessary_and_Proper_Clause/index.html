Necessary and Proper Clause

The Necessary and Proper Clause (Article I, Section 8, Clause 18 of the United States Constitution), also known as the Elastic Clause, is a clause in the Constitution that grants Congress the power "To make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into Execution the foregoing Powers, and all other Powers vested by this Constitution in the Government of the United States, or in any Department or Officer thereof." It is the primary source of Congress's implied powers and one of the most contested provisions in American constitutional law.

Text and drafting

The clause was added by the Committee of Detail at the Constitutional Convention in 1787 and adopted with little recorded debate. Anti-Federalists criticised it during ratification as dangerously open-ended — Brutus called it part of a sweeping grant of power — while Alexander Hamilton defended it in The Federalist No. 33 as merely declaratory of powers implied by the grants themselves.

Early interpretation: McCulloch v. Maryland

The clause was first tested in the debate over the Bank of the United States. Hamilton argued in 1791 that "necessary" meant useful or conducive to the end, and Washington accepted the broad reading. In McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), Chief Justice John Marshall upheld Congress's power to charter a national bank and struck down Maryland's tax on it, writing:

"Let the end be legitimate, let it be within the scope of the constitution, and all means which are appropriate, which are plainly adapted to that end, which are not prohibited, but consistent with the letter and spirit of the constitution, are constitutional."

Marshall rejected the argument that "necessary" meant indispensable, noting that the word often denotes degrees of usefulness, and that the clause appears among Congress's powers rather than its limitations.

Modern doctrine

The clause, combined with the Commerce Clause, underlies much of the modern federal regulatory state. In United States v. Comstock (2010) the Supreme Court upheld a federal civil-commitment statute as necessary and proper, weighing factors including the breadth of the clause, the history of federal involvement, and the accommodation of state interests. In NFIB v. Sebelius (2012), however, five justices concluded that the clause could not sustain the Affordable Care Act's individual mandate, reasoning that the clause may not be used to create a "great substantive and independent power" not otherwise granted, and that compelling individuals into commerce was not a proper means of regulating it.
