Screening (medicine)

In medicine, screening is a strategy used to look for as-yet-unrecognised conditions or risk markers in a population of individuals without signs or symptoms of the target disease. This can include individuals with pre-symptomatic or unrecognised symptomatic disease. As such, screening tests are somewhat unusual in that they are performed on people who are apparently in good health.

Purpose and principles

The aim of screening is to identify disease at an early stage, when treatment may be more effective and less costly. The classic criteria for a worthwhile screening programme, set out by Wilson and Jungner in 1968, include that the condition should be an important health problem, that there should be a recognisable latent or early stage, and that an accepted, effective treatment should be available. Common examples include mammography for breast cancer, the Pap test for cervical cancer, and newborn screening for metabolic disorders.

Types of screening

Screening may be mass (offered to a whole population), multiphasic (several tests at once), targeted (directed at high-risk groups), or opportunistic (offered when a patient consults for another reason).

Benefits and harms

Screening tests are not diagnostic: positive results require further investigation, and false positives can cause anxiety and unnecessary procedures. Screening can also produce false negatives and lead to overdiagnosis — the detection of abnormalities that would never have caused harm. The accuracy of a screening test is described by its sensitivity and specificity and its predictive values, which depend on the prevalence of disease in the population tested.
