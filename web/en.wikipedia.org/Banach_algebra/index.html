Banach algebra

Banach algebra
FieldFunctional analysis
Named afterStefan Banach
DefinitionA Banach space that is also an algebra with submultiplicative norm
Key theoremGelfand–Mazur theorem; spectral theory

In mathematics, especially functional analysis, a Banach algebra, named after Stefan Banach, is an associative algebra A over the real or complex numbers (or over a non-Archimedean complete normed field) that is also a Banach space — that is, a normed space that is complete in the metric induced by its norm. The norm is required to satisfy ‖xy‖ ≤ ‖x‖‖y‖ for all x, y in A, which guarantees that multiplication is continuous.

Examples

The most familiar example is the space of bounded linear operators on a Banach space, with multiplication given by composition of operators and the operator norm. Other examples include the algebra of continuous functions on a compact Hausdorff space with the supremum norm; the space L1(ℝ) of integrable functions under convolution, an example lacking an identity element; and C*-algebras, the Banach algebras with an involution satisfying the C* identity, which include the algebras used in quantum mechanics and in the Gelfand representation of commutative algebras.

Spectral theory

Banach algebras support a powerful spectral theory generalising that of matrices. For an element x of a unital Banach algebra, the spectrum σ(x) is the set of complex numbers λ for which x − λ1 is not invertible; it is always a non-empty compact set, and the spectral radius formula of Gelfand expresses its radius as the limit of ‖xn‖1/n. The Gelfand–Mazur theorem states that a complex Banach algebra in which every non-zero element is invertible is isometrically isomorphic to the complex numbers themselves. In a commutative Banach algebra, the maximal ideals correspond to characters (non-zero homomorphisms into ℂ), and the Gelfand transform represents algebra elements as functions on the character space — the foundation for much of harmonic analysis and the modern theory of C*-algebras.

History

The theory grew out of Banach's work on normed spaces in the 1920s and was developed decisively in the 1940s by Israel Gelfand, whose theory of commutative Banach algebras ("normed rings") unified functional analysis with algebra and topology.
