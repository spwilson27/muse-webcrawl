Tidal bore

A tidal bore is a tidal phenomenon in which the leading edge of an incoming tide forms a wave (or series of waves) that travels up a river or narrow bay against the direction of the river's flow. Bores occur in relatively few locations worldwide, where a large tidal range (typically more than 6 metres) combines with a converging, shallow estuary that funnels the incoming water into a steep-fronted wave.

Characteristics

A bore may take the form of a smooth, undulating series of waves (an undular bore) or, when the tidal range is large and the front steepens, a single breaking, turbulent wall of water. The bore raises the river level rapidly behind it and can reverse the river's current for some hours. Famous examples include the bore of the Qiantang River in China — the world's largest, reaching heights of up to about 9 metres and watched by crowds of spectators at the Mid-Autumn Festival — the Severn bore in England, the mascaret of the Seine (much reduced by dredging), the pororoca of the Amazon, and the high bores of the Petitcodiac and other rivers flowing into Canada's Bay of Fundy, which has the greatest tidal range on Earth.

Risks and recreation

Tidal bores can be dangerous to shipping, riverbank spectators and wildlife; the Qiantang bore has swept away onlookers despite barriers and warnings. Some bores are surfed: surfers ride the Severn bore for miles, and similar bore surfing occurs on the Amazon's pororoca and Alaska's Turnagain Arm. Bores are also ecologically significant, mixing estuarine waters and transporting sediment and nutrients upstream.
