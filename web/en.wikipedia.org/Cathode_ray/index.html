Cathode ray

Cathode rays are streams of electrons observed in discharge tubes (Crookes tubes). If an evacuated glass tube is equipped with two electrodes and a voltage is applied, glass behind the positive electrode glows because of electrons emitted from the cathode. The rays travel in straight lines, cast shadows, and can be deflected by electric and magnetic fields.

History

German physicists Julius Plücker and Johann Wilhelm Hittorf first observed the rays in the 1850s–60s; Eugen Goldstein coined the term Kathodenstrahlen (cathode rays) in 1876. In 1897 J. J. Thomson showed that cathode rays consist of previously unknown negatively charged particles — electrons — far lighter than atoms, establishing the first discovered subatomic particle.

Properties

Cathode rays are invisible themselves but cause fluorescence where they strike glass or phosphor coatings. They carry energy and momentum, heat objects they hit, and penetrate thin foils. Their deflection by fields, measured by Thomson, gave the charge-to-mass ratio of the electron.

Applications

The cathode-ray tube (CRT), developed by Karl Ferdinand Braun in 1897, steered cathode rays onto a phosphorescent screen and became the basis of oscilloscopes, radar displays, and television and computer monitors for most of the twentieth century. Cathode-ray experiments also led directly to the discovery of X-rays by Wilhelm Röntgen, who noticed fluorescence produced by rays escaping a shielded tube in 1895.
