Spanish missions in California

The Spanish missions in California form a series of 21 religious outposts or missions established by Catholic priests of the Franciscan order between 1769 and 1833, to spread Christianity among the Native Americans of Alta California. The missions, together with presidios (military forts) and pueblos (towns), were the principal instruments of Spain's colonisation of the Pacific Coast north of the Baja California peninsula.

History

The first mission, San Diego de Alcalá, was founded in 1769 by Father Junípero Serra, who established nine missions before his death in 1784 and is regarded as the founder of the system. His successors, including Fermín Lasuén, extended the chain along El Camino Real ("The Royal Road"), from San Diego in the south to San Francisco Solano in Sonoma in the north — each roughly a day's journey from the next. The missions introduced European agriculture, livestock and crafts, and gathered Native peoples into mission communities (reducciones).

Impact on Native peoples

Mission life was coercive: baptised Native Americans, called neophytes, were required to remain at the missions, follow strict routines and abandon many traditional practices. European diseases devastated mission populations, and mortality was high. Historians and Native American communities continue to debate the missions' legacy, balancing their architectural and cultural heritage against the suffering and demographic collapse they caused.

Secularization and after

After Mexican independence, the Secularization Act of 1833 disbanded the missions and distributed their lands, much of which ended up in private ranchos. Many mission churches fell into ruin; restoration began in the late 19th and 20th centuries. Today the missions are among California's most visited historic sites, and most remain active Catholic parishes. In 2015 Pope Francis canonised Junípero Serra, a decision celebrated by many Catholics and criticised by many Native American activists.
