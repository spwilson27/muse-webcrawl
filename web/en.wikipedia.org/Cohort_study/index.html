Cohort study

A cohort study is a particular form of longitudinal study that samples a cohort (a group of people who share a defining characteristic, typically those who experienced a common event in a selected period, such as birth or graduation), performing a cross-section at intervals through time. It is a type of observational study used in medicine, epidemiology, social science, and actuarial science.

Design

In a cohort study, participants are classified by exposure status (for example, smokers versus non-smokers) and followed forward in time to compare the incidence of an outcome such as disease or death. Prospective cohort studies recruit and follow participants into the future; retrospective (historical) cohort studies reconstruct exposure and outcomes from existing records. Because exposure is measured before the outcome, cohort studies can establish temporal sequence and calculate incidence and relative risk directly.

Famous examples

Landmark cohorts include the Framingham Heart Study (begun 1948), which identified major cardiovascular risk factors; the British Doctors Study, which demonstrated the hazards of smoking; the Nurses' Health Study; and national birth cohort studies that follow everyone born in a particular week or year.

Strengths and limitations

Cohort studies can examine multiple outcomes of a single exposure and rare exposures, but they are inefficient for rare diseases, require large samples and long follow-up, and are vulnerable to loss to follow-up and confounding. Unlike randomized trials, exposure is not assigned, so causal inference depends on controlling differences between exposed and unexposed groups.
