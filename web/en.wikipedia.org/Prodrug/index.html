Prodrug

A prodrug is a pharmacologically inactive medication or compound that, after intake, is metabolized (i.e., converted within the body) into a pharmacologically active drug. Instead of administering a drug directly, a corresponding prodrug can be used to improve how the drug is absorbed, distributed, metabolized, and excreted (ADME). Prodrugs are often designed to improve bioavailability when a drug itself is poorly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, or to target the drug to specific organs or cells.

Types

Type I prodrugs are activated intracellularly (for example, antiviral nucleoside analogues phosphorylated by viral or cellular kinases, and statins such as simvastatin hydrolysed to the active acid). Type II prodrugs are activated extracellularly, in the gut or the circulation (for example, antibody-directed enzyme prodrug therapy in cancer research). "Mutual prodrugs" (co-drugs) link two active agents so that each acts as the carrier of the other.

Examples

Codeine is converted by the liver enzyme CYP2D6 into morphine; people lacking the enzyme get little analgesia, while ultra-rapid metabolisers risk toxicity. Levodopa crosses the blood–brain barrier and is decarboxylated to dopamine. Valaciclovir is the valine ester of aciclovir, improving oral absorption. Clopidogrel requires hepatic activation to inhibit platelet aggregation, and oseltamivir is hydrolysed to the active neuraminidase inhibitor oseltamivir carboxylate.

Design goals

Prodrug strategies aim to overcome poor solubility or permeability, first-pass metabolism, chemical instability, unpleasant taste (e.g. chloramphenicol palmitate for children), irritation at the injection site, or to achieve targeted delivery and prolonged action.
