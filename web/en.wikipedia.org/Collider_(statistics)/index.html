Collider (statistics)

In statistics and causal inference, a collider is a variable in a causal graph that is the common effect of two or more other variables — that is, a node with two arrows pointing into it along a path (X → Z ← Y). Colliders play a central role in the theory of directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) developed by Judea Pearl.

Collider bias

Unlike chains and forks, a path through a collider is naturally blocked: the two causes are independent (marginally) even though they share an effect. However, conditioning on a collider — by selecting, stratifying, or adjusting for it — opens the path and induces a spurious association between its causes. This is called collider bias or collider stratification bias, and is related to Berkson's paradox.

Examples

A classic illustration: among hospitalized patients, two diseases that are independent in the general population may appear negatively associated, because hospitalization is a common effect of both. Similarly, studying only successful actors can create a false trade-off between talent and attractiveness.

Implications

Recognizing colliders is essential when choosing adjustment variables in observational research: controlling for a collider (or its descendant) can create bias rather than remove it, a key lesson of modern causal inference.
