Macadam

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Macadam is a type of road construction pioneered by the Scottish engineer John Loudon McAdam around 1820. In a macadam road, a single layer of carefully graded broken stone is laid and compacted so that the angular fragments interlock, forming a hard, durable, free-draining surface. The method revolutionised road building in the early nineteenth century and its name survives in the word "tarmacadam", shortened to "tarmac".

McAdam's method

McAdam, who became surveyor to the Bristol Turnpike Trust and later surveyor general of metropolitan roads, argued that existing roads failed because of poor drainage and badly sorted material. His design raised the road slightly above the surrounding ground, provided side ditches, and laid stone broken to a uniform small size — he prescribed that each fragment should pass through a two-inch ring and that workmen break stones to roughly six ounces in weight. Layers were compacted by traffic itself; the angular stones locked together under load, and fine stone dust filled the voids, forming a smooth, waterproof crust. Unlike the heavy foundations of his contemporary Thomas Telford, McAdam's roads relied on the native soil, kept dry, as the true foundation.

Adoption

The first macadamised road in the United States was built in Maryland in the 1820s, and the technique spread along turnpikes and national roads. In Britain, macadam surfaces quickly replaced earth and gravel roads on the main coach routes, cutting journey times and maintenance costs. The surface suited iron-shod wheels and hoof traffic; its weakness appeared with fast pneumatic-tyred motor vehicles, whose suction lifted the binding dust and broke up the surface. The remedy — coating the stone with tar or bitumen — produced tar-bound macadam, patented in forms by E. Purnell Hooley in 1901, from which modern asphalt surfacing descends.

Modern usage

Today "macadam" commonly denotes simple unbound or water-bound gravel roads in rural areas, and the term survives in airport "tarmac" and the French word macadam for asphalt road surfaces. See also the article on Macintosh for another Scottish namesake in a different field.
