The Royal Ulster Constabulary (RUC) was the police force in Northern Ireland from 1922 to 2001. It was founded on 1 June 1922 out of the Royal Irish Constabulary (RIC), following the partition of Ireland. At its peak the force had around 8,500 officers, supported by some 4,500 part-time members of the RUC Reserve.

The RUC played a central role in the Troubles (late 1960s to 1998), during which 319 of its officers were killed and almost 9,000 injured, mostly by paramilitary attacks. Because of the threat from the Provisional IRA, the RUC became one of the most heavily armed and militarised civilian police forces in Western Europe, operating alongside the British Army under Operation Banner.

History

The force was established under the government of Sir James Craig after the Government of Ireland Act 1920 created Northern Ireland. It inherited much of the personnel and structure of the RIC in the six counties, and also absorbed members of the Ulster Special Constabulary (the "B Specials"), a largely Protestant auxiliary force retained until 1970. The prefix "Royal" was conferred in recognition of the force's service.

From the late 1960s, as civil unrest escalated into sustained violence, the RUC was at the centre of controversy. Nationalists accused it of one-sided policing and collusion with loyalist paramilitaries, while unionists praised its sacrifices in combating terrorism. The 1969 Hunt Report recommended disarming the force and disbanding the B Specials; the latter were replaced in 1970 by the Ulster Defence Regiment and the RUC Reserve.

Patten reforms and disbandment

Following the Good Friday Agreement of 1998, the Independent Commission on Policing for Northern Ireland, chaired by Chris Patten, recommended sweeping reforms, including a new name and badge, a 50/50 recruitment policy for Catholics and Protestants, and stronger accountability through a Policing Board and Police Ombudsman. On 4 November 2001 the RUC was replaced by the Police Service of Northern Ireland (PSNI).

In 2000 Queen Elizabeth II awarded the George Cross to the RUC, one of only a small number of collective awards of the decoration.

Legacy

The RUC remains a subject of historical debate. Supporters emphasise the heavy price paid by its officers; critics point to documented cases of collusion investigated by the Stevens Inquiries and the Police Ombudsman. The RUC George Cross Foundation and memorial garden at PSNI headquarters in Belfast commemorate the officers who died.
