Congestion pricing in New York City

Congestion pricing in New York City, officially the Central Business District Tolling Program (CBDTP), is a scheme that charges drivers a toll for entering Manhattan south of 60th Street, the borough's central business district. After years of delays, litigation, and a last-minute pause, the program began collecting tolls on January 5, 2025, making New York the first city in the United States to implement congestion pricing.

The program is administered by the Metropolitan Transportation Authority (MTA) through its Triborough Bridge and Tunnel Authority. Revenue is legally dedicated to capital improvements for the city's subways, buses, and commuter railroads, with the tolls expected to back roughly $15 billion in bonds.

How it works

Vehicles are charged once per day upon entering the tolling zone, which covers Manhattan at and below 60th Street, excluding the FDR Drive, the West Side Highway, and certain other roadways. Tolls are collected electronically using E-ZPass and license-plate cameras mounted on gantries. Passenger vehicles using E-ZPass pay a peak-period toll of $9 during the initial phase, with lower overnight rates; trucks and buses pay higher amounts. Exemptions and discounts apply to certain vehicles, including those carrying people with disabilities and low-income drivers enrolled in a discount plan.

History

The idea of charging drivers to enter Manhattan's core dates back decades. Mayor Michael Bloomberg proposed a congestion pricing plan in 2007 as part of PlaNYC, but the New York State Assembly declined to vote on it in 2008. Governor Andrew Cuomo championed a new version, and the state legislature authorized the program in the 2019 budget. Federal approval followed in 2023, but in June 2024 Governor Kathy Hochul abruptly "paused" the planned June 30 launch, citing concerns about affordability. In November 2024 she revived the program at a reduced $9 base toll, and it launched the following January despite legal challenges from New Jersey and other opponents.

Effects

Early data showed reduced traffic volumes within the zone, faster bus speeds, and increased transit ridership, though the program remained politically contested, with the federal government under President Donald Trump attempting to rescind its approval in 2025.
