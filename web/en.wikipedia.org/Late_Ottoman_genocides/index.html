Late Ottoman genocides

The late Ottoman genocides is a historiographical term for the systematic extermination of Christian minorities of the Ottoman Empire during and after the First World War, principally the Armenian genocide, the Greek genocide (including Pontic Greeks), and the Assyrian genocide (Seyfo). Many historians argue these campaigns, carried out under the Committee of Union and Progress government between roughly 1913 and 1923, should be studied as related parts of a single broader process of demographic engineering and ethnic homogenisation in Anatolia.

Background

By 1914 the Ottoman Empire had lost most of its European territory in the Balkan Wars, and hundreds of thousands of Muslim refugees had fled into Anatolia. The CUP regime, fearing partition of the empire and viewing Christian populations as potential collaborators with enemy powers, moved to create a nationally homogeneous Turkish-Muslim heartland.

The genocides

Beginning in April 1915, the Armenian population was subjected to arrests of community leaders, massacres, and death marches into the Syrian desert, in which an estimated 600,000 to more than 1 million people perished. Ottoman Greeks faced deportations, labour battalions, massacres, and forced marches, with deaths estimated in the hundreds of thousands; the surviving population was expelled in the 1923 population exchange with Greece. Assyrian Christians in southeastern Anatolia and northwestern Persia were massacred in 1915 in what Assyrians call the Seyfo ("Sword"), with deaths commonly estimated between 150,000 and 300,000.

Historiography

Scholars such as Benny Morris and Dror Ze'evi have argued that the destruction of the empire's Christians over the period 1894–1924 — beginning with the Hamidian massacres and culminating in the 1920s — constitutes a single sustained campaign. Others treat each case distinctly while acknowledging their connections. The Republic of Turkey officially rejects the characterisation of these events as genocide, a position that remains a subject of international debate and recognition resolutions by many states and parliaments.
