Consumer choice

Consumer choice theory
FieldMicroeconomics
Key conceptsUtility, budget constraint, indifference curve
OutcomesMarshallian demand, income and substitution effects

The theory of consumer choice is the branch of microeconomics that relates preferences to consumption expenditures and to consumer demand curves. It analyzes how consumers maximize the desirability of their consumption (their utility) subject to the constraint of their income and the prices of goods.

The standard model

A consumer is modelled as choosing a bundle of goods to maximize a utility function u(x) subject to the budget constraint that total spending not exceed income. Graphically, the optimum is the point where an indifference curve is tangent to the budget line, i.e. where the marginal rate of substitution equals the ratio of prices. Preferences are typically assumed complete, transitive, and non-satiated; convexity yields well-behaved demand.

Effects of price and income changes

A change in a good's price produces a substitution effect (movement along an indifference curve toward the relatively cheaper good) and an income effect (change in purchasing power), decomposed by the Slutsky or Hicks methods. Demand derived from this model underlies the analysis of welfare, taxation, and the consumer surplus. Behavioural economics has documented systematic departures from the rational model, such as framing effects, loss aversion, and present bias, while revealed-preference theory (Samuelson, Houthakker) seeks to recover preferences from observed choices alone.
