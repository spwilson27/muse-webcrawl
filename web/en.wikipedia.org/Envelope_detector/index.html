Envelope detector

A simple diode envelope detector: a diode, a capacitor and a load resistor recover the modulating signal from an AM waveform.

An envelope detector (sometimes called a peak detector) is an electronic circuit that takes a high-frequency amplitude-modulated input signal and produces an output equal to the envelope of the original signal. Envelope detectors are used to demodulate amplitude-modulated (AM) radio signals: the recovered envelope corresponds to the original audio or baseband waveform.

Circuit operation

The classic circuit consists of a diode in series with a parallel combination of a capacitor and a resistor. The modulated carrier charges the capacitor through the diode whenever the input exceeds the capacitor voltage; between peaks, the capacitor discharges through the resistor. If the RC time constant is chosen correctly — long compared with the carrier period but short compared with the period of the highest modulating frequency — the output voltage closely follows the upper envelope of the input.

Design considerations

If the time constant is too short, the output contains excessive carrier ripple. If it is too long, the capacitor cannot discharge fast enough to follow rapid downward changes in the envelope, a distortion known as diagonal clipping. Envelope detection also produces distortion with high modulation indices and overmodulation, because the detector cannot follow an envelope that touches or crosses zero.

Applications

Envelope detectors were the standard demodulator in early crystal radios and remain ubiquitous in inexpensive AM receivers. Variants of the circuit appear in automatic gain control (AGC) loops, in envelope followers used in audio effects and speech processing, and more generally wherever the slowly varying amplitude of a rapidly varying signal must be measured. Mathematically, ideal envelope detection is related to computing the magnitude of the analytic signal, obtained with the Hilbert transform.
