Replantation

Replantation requires microsurgical anastomosis of vessels and nerves.

Replantation or reattachment is the surgical reattachment of a body part (such as a finger, hand, arm, or toe) that has been completely severed from the body. It is distinguished from revascularisation, in which blood flow is restored to a part that remains partially attached. Replantation is possible because of advances in microsurgery, which allow surgeons to reconnect arteries, veins, and nerves less than a millimetre in diameter under an operating microscope.

Technique and indications

Success depends on the mechanism of injury — clean guillotine amputations fare better than crush or avulsion injuries — and on the ischaemia time of the amputated part, which should be cooled (but not frozen) during transport. Thumb amputations, multiple-digit amputations, and amputations in children are strong indications because of their functional importance, while single-finger amputations in adults are more selectively replanted. The sequence of repair typically involves bone fixation, tendon repair, arterial and venous anastomosis, and nerve coaptation, followed by lengthy rehabilitation.

History

The first successful replantation of a completely amputated arm was performed by Ronald Malt and his team in Boston in 1962, and the first successful replantation of a severed digit followed in Japan in 1965. Survival rates for replanted digits in specialised centres commonly exceed 80 to 90 per cent, though functional outcomes vary, and sensibility and motion are rarely fully normal.
