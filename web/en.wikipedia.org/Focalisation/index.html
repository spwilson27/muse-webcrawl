Focalisation

Focalisation: the perspective through which a narrative is presented.

Focalisation is a term coined by the French narratologist Gérard Genette in his work Narrative Discourse (1972) to describe the perspective through which a narrative is presented — the answer to the question "who sees?" as distinct from "who speaks?" (the narrator). The concept separates the agent who narrates a story from the position from which its events are perceived, a distinction earlier criticism often blurred in the loose notion of "point of view".

Genette's typology

  • Zero focalisation (non-focalised narrative) — the narrator knows more than any character; the traditional "omniscient" narrator, free to enter any mind and survey any scene.
  • Internal focalisation — the narrative is restricted to what a particular character perceives and knows. It may be fixed (one character throughout), variable (shifting between characters), or multiple (the same event retold through several characters).
  • External focalisation — the narrator says less than the characters know, presenting only outward behaviour, as in behaviourist or "camera-eye" writing such as Hemingway's short stories.

Development and criticism

Genette's model was refined by later narratologists. Mieke Bal revised the concept to include a focaliser (the subject of focalisation) and a focalised object, and stressed that focalisation colours the presentation of the story even where it is not tied to a character's literal perception. Shlomith Rimmon-Kenan proposed facets of focalisation — spatial, temporal, psychological, and ideological. Critics have debated whether focalisation should be defined by knowledge restriction, by perception, or by the "orientation" of the narrative, but the term remains standard in narrative theory and in film studies, where it is applied to point-of-view shots and the restriction of information to a protagonist.

Relation to point of view

Where traditional criticism distinguishes first-person and third-person narration, focalisation analysis allows finer distinctions: a third-person narrative may be internally focalised through a single character (as in much of Henry James or Virginia Woolf), achieving the intimacy of first person while retaining a narrator distinct from the hero.
