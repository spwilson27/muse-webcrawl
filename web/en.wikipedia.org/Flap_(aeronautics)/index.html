Flap (aeronautics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A flap is a high-lift device used to reduce the stalling speed of an aircraft wing at a given weight. Flaps are usually mounted on the trailing edges of the wings of a fixed-wing aircraft. They are used during takeoff and landing. Flaps are also used during takeoff, usually in a partial setting, to increase lift and reduce the takeoff run, and during landing, in a fuller setting, to increase both lift and drag so that the aircraft can fly more slowly and descend more steeply.

Extending flaps increases the camber or curvature of the wing, raising the maximum lift coefficient — the maximum lift the wing can produce — which allows the aircraft to generate the required lift at a lower speed, reducing the stalling speed. Extending flaps also increases drag, which can be useful during approach and landing because it allows the aircraft to descend at a steeper angle without gaining speed. In many aircraft, flaps are supplemented by leading-edge devices such as slats, which delay flow separation at high angles of attack, and by spoilers, which can supplement the drag function.

Principles of operation

The general airplane lift equation shows that lift is proportional to the lift coefficient and to the square of airspeed. Flaps work by increasing the effective camber of the aerofoil and, in the case of Fowler flaps, by increasing the wing area when extended. The net result is a higher maximum lift coefficient (CLmax), so the same lift can be produced at a lower airspeed. Because stalling speed varies inversely with the square root of CLmax, fitting flaps substantially reduces stall speed: transport aircraft typically achieve stall-speed reductions of 20–30 percent between the clean and fully extended configurations.

Flaps also increase the drag coefficient. The first portion of flap extension on many types produces proportionally more lift than drag and is therefore used for takeoff; the later portion produces proportionally more drag and is used for landing. Flap extension also tends to change the pitching moment of the aircraft, usually nose-down for trailing-edge flaps, which the pilot or trim system must counteract.

Types of flap

TypeDescription
Plain flapThe rear portion of the wing rotates downwards on a hinge. Simple and light, but produces a relatively modest increase in lift and considerable drag.
Split flapA plate deflected downward from the lower surface of the wing; the upper surface remains fixed. Used on the Douglas DC-3, among others. Produces high drag.
Slotted flapA slot between flap and wing ducts high-pressure air from below the wing over the flap's upper surface, delaying separation and allowing greater deflection.
Fowler flapSlides backwards on tracks before deflecting downward, increasing both wing area and camber; may be single-, double- or triple-slotted. Standard on modern airliners.
Junkers flapA plain flap mounted below and behind the trailing edge, acting like a small separate wing (also called a double-wing flap).
Krueger flapA leading-edge device that folds out from the lower surface of the wing leading edge; used on the Boeing 727 and 747 inboard sections.
Gurney flapA small fixed tab perpendicular to the trailing edge, increasing lift with minimal drag penalty; widely used in motor racing.
Blown flapEngine bleed air or dedicated blowing over the flap to energize the boundary layer, used on aircraft such as the Blackburn Buccaneer.

Use in flight

During takeoff, flaps are typically extended to an intermediate setting, trading a modest increase in drag for a lower liftoff speed and hence a shorter ground roll. After takeoff and once a safe climb is established, flaps are retracted in stages as speed increases. On approach, flaps are extended progressively (often in detents such as 1, 5, 15, 25, 30 and 40 degrees on Boeing types) to stabilize the aircraft at progressively lower speeds, with full flap used for the final approach and landing, giving the lowest touchdown speed and shortest landing roll. Flap extension is limited by maximum flap-extended speeds (VFE) placarded for each setting, because aerodynamic loads on extended flaps increase with speed.

Flap asymmetry — one side extending more than the other — is a serious failure case producing rolling tendencies; airliners therefore incorporate asymmetry detection and protection systems, and flap drives incorporate torque limiters and interconnection shafts to keep surfaces synchronized.

History

The earliest flaps appeared in the 1910s; Handley Page experimented with trailing-edge flaps and slots before the First World War. The Fairey Aviation Company made extensive use of its patented camber-changing gear on types such as the Fairey IIIF. Harlan D. Fowler patented the flap bearing his name in the 1920s; Fowler flaps were adopted on the Lockheed 14 and later on many transport aircraft. By the Second World War, flaps of the split, slotted, and Fowler types were standard on combat and transport aircraft, and the triple-slotted Fowler flaps of modern jetliners represent the mature form of the trailing-edge high-lift system.
