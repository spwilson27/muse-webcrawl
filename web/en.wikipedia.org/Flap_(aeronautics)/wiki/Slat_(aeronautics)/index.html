Slat (aeronautics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Slats are aerodynamic surfaces on the leading edge of the wings of fixed-wing aircraft which, when deployed, allow the wing to operate at a higher angle of attack. A slat is a small airfoil-shaped panel that moves forward and away from the leading edge, opening a slot through which air from below the wing is ducted over the upper surface. This delays separation of the boundary layer, so the wing keeps producing lift at angles where it would otherwise stall, and the aircraft can fly more slowly for take-off and landing.

Operation

Slats may be fixed (permanently open slots, as on the Fieseler Storch, whose low landing speed was legendary), automatic (held closed by aerodynamic forces in cruise and sucked open by the low pressure at the leading edge at high angles of attack), or powered (extended by hydraulic or electric actuators on tracks or screw jacks, as on airliners). On modern transports, slats have intermediate "sealed" positions for take-off, in which the slat extends but the slot stays closed, giving some camber increase with less drag.

Effects and use

Deploying slats typically raises the stalling angle of attack from about 15 degrees to 25 degrees or more and substantially increases maximum lift. Because the outboard wing carries the ailerons, slats over the outer wing are especially valuable in preserving roll control near the stall. Slats add weight and complexity and can be vulnerable to icing, but together with multi-slotted trailing-edge flaps they form the standard high-lift system of the modern airliner.

History

The slotted wing was developed independently by Frederick Handley Page in Britain and Gustav Lachmann in Germany around 1919, following wind-tunnel experiments by Ludwig Prandtl's circle at Göttingen; Handley Page's company licensed the device widely through the 1920s and 1930s.
