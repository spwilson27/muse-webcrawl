Stall (fluid dynamics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In fluid dynamics, a stall is a reduction in the lift coefficient generated by a foil as angle of attack increases. This occurs when the critical angle of attack of the foil is exceeded — typically about 15 degrees for a conventional airfoil, though it varies with design, Reynolds number, and configuration. Beyond this angle, the airflow separates from the upper surface of the foil, the smooth laminar and attached turbulent flow gives way to a broad turbulent wake, and lift decreases while drag rises sharply.

Mechanism

As angle of attack grows, the adverse pressure gradient on the upper (suction) surface strengthens until the boundary layer can no longer remain attached and separates, first at the trailing edge and progressively further forward. Stall behavior is classified by how separation develops: trailing-edge stall (gradual), leading-edge stall (abrupt, typical of thin airfoils), and thin-airfoil stall (separation from the leading edge with reattachment, forming a separation bubble). Ice, frost, or contamination roughening the leading edge can trigger premature stall at lower angles.

Aircraft stalls

For aircraft, the stall determines the minimum flying speed in a given configuration: the stall speed rises with weight, load factor (as in a turn), and decreases with high-lift devices such as flaps and slats. A stall can occur at any airspeed and any attitude, whenever the critical angle is exceeded — including in accelerated flight and at high speed in a "coffin corner" scenario at altitude. Recovery requires reducing the angle of attack, usually by lowering the nose. Asymmetric stall, in which one wing stalls first, can precipitate a spin. Modern aircraft employ stall warning systems, stick shakers and pushers, and stall strips or washout in the wing design to make stall onset benign and progressive.

Other applications

Stall also affects compressor and turbine blades (where rotating stall and surge can damage engines), propellers, wind turbines (which may use stall regulation to limit power), helicopter rotors (retreating blade stall), and hydrofoils. Dynamic stall — the delayed separation that occurs when angle of attack changes rapidly — temporarily produces higher lift and is exploited by insects, birds, and maneuvering aircraft.
