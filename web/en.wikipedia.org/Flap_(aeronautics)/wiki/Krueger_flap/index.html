Krueger flap

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Krueger flaps, also spelled Krüger flaps, are aircraft lift enhancement devices fitted under, and forward of, the wing leading edge. When stowed, the flap forms part of the underside of the wing; when deployed for take-off and landing, it rotates forward and downward, increasing the wing's camber and effective area and raising the maximum lift coefficient, allowing slower flight.

Design and operation

Unlike a leading-edge slat, which moves away from the wing to open a slot, the basic Krueger flap is a hinged panel that folds out from the lower surface. Some designs incorporate a "bullnose" rotating leading edge and a small slot, and variable-camber Krueger flaps — in which the panel flexes into an airfoil shape — were developed to reduce drag. The device was invented by Werner Krüger in Germany in 1943 and flight-tested after the war.

Use

Krueger flaps are famously used on the inboard leading edges of Boeing airliners, including the 727 and 747, typically combined with slats on the outboard wing where stall behavior is more critical. They are simpler and lighter than slats but less effective at very high angles of attack, since they do not re-energize the boundary layer over the upper surface to the same degree.
