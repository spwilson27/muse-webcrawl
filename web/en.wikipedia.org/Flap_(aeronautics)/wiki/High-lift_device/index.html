High-lift device

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In aircraft design, a high-lift device is a component or mechanism on an aircraft's wing that increases the amount of lift produced by the wing, or by which the design lift coefficient may be increased. High-lift devices may be movable surfaces, most commonly trailing-edge flaps and leading-edge slats, or fixed components such as the slots of early designs. They allow an aircraft designed for efficient high-speed cruise — with a small, thin, heavily loaded wing — to nevertheless fly slowly enough for safe take-off and landing.

How they work

High-lift devices work through one or more of four mechanisms: increasing the wing's camber (as most flaps do), increasing its area (as Fowler flaps do by sliding aft as well as down), delaying flow separation (as slats do, by ducting high-energy air from below the leading edge over the upper surface), and energizing the boundary layer (as vortex generators and blown flaps do). A modern airliner wing with slats and multi-slotted flaps can more than double its maximum lift coefficient compared with the clean wing, cutting stall speed by roughly a third.

Types

Trailing-edge devices include plain, split, slotted, and Fowler flaps, in single-, double-, and triple-slotted forms; leading-edge devices include slats, Krueger flaps, and droop noses. Their deployment is usually staged: a partial setting for take-off, balancing lift against drag, and full extension for landing, where the added drag is useful in steepening the approach and slowing the aircraft. Retractable systems add weight, complexity, and maintenance cost, which designers trade against the smaller, lighter wing they make possible.

History

Flaps first appeared during the First World War, and Handley Page's slotted wing and leading-edge slat were developed independently in Britain and Germany around 1919 after investigations into stall accidents. By the 1930s, flaps and slats were standard on transport aircraft, and the multi-slotted Fowler flap systems of the jet age made possible the high wing loadings of modern airliners.
