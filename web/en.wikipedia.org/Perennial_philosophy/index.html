Perennial philosophy

The perennial philosophy (Latin: philosophia perennis), also called perennialism, is a perspective in philosophy and spirituality holding that there exists a single universal metaphysical truth underlying all the world's religions, passed down through the ages. It claims that religious traditions share a common origin in a primordial tradition.

Origins

The term philosophia perennis was first used by the Catholic theologian Agostino Steuco in the 16th century, drawing on earlier ideas in Marsilio Ficino and Giovanni Pico della Mirandola during the Renaissance. It was later adopted by Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz. The idea also appears in the thought of Nicholas of Cusa, who proposed a unity of religions.

20th century

The concept was popularised in the English-speaking world by Aldous Huxley's 1945 book The Perennial Philosophy, which drew on mystical traditions from Christianity, Hinduism, Buddhism, Taoism and Sufism. Thinkers associated with the Traditionalist School, including René Guénon, Ananda Coomaraswamy and Frithjof Schuon, developed related accounts of a primordial tradition.

Criticism

Critics argue that perennialism flattens genuine doctrinal differences between religions, privileges mystical experience over communal and ethical practice, and reads modern assumptions back into ancient texts. Scholars of constructivism about religious experience, such as Steven T. Katz, contend that mystical experiences are always shaped by prior beliefs and expectations.
