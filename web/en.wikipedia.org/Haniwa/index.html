Haniwa

Haniwa are unglazed terracotta clay figures made for ritual use and buried with the dead in Japan.

The haniwa (埴輪) are unglazed terracotta clay figures that were made for ritual use and buried with the dead as funerary objects during the Kofun period (3rd to 6th centuries AD) of the history of Japan. Haniwa were created according to the wazumi technique, in which mounds of coiled clay were built up to shape the figure, layer by layer. The name means "circle of clay", referring to the arrangement of plain cylindrical haniwa around the great keyhole-shaped burial mounds (kofun) of the period.

Types and subjects

The earliest and most common haniwa are simple cylinders, which stabilised the mound surface and marked its sacred boundary. From the 5th century, representational (keishō) haniwa proliferated: houses, granaries, boats, shields, swords, musical instruments, animals (horses, deer, dogs, monkeys, and wild boar), and human figures — warriors in armour, female shamans (miko), falconers, farmers, and dancers. The famous "dancing people" haniwa from Saitama are among the best known. Armed warrior figures provide essential evidence for the armour, weapons, dress, and hairstyles of the period, since so few contemporary depictions survive.

Significance

Because Japan had no writing system in use for native records during much of the Kofun period, haniwa are a principal source for reconstructing the material culture, social ranks, and rituals of early Japanese society. Horse haniwa with elaborate trappings document the introduction of horses and continental culture from Korea. After the introduction of Buddhism and changes in burial practice in the 6th–7th centuries, the production of haniwa declined and ceased. Today haniwa are iconic symbols of ancient Japan; excavated examples are held by museums such as the Tokyo National Museum, and the figures frequently appear in Japanese popular culture.
