Dark romanticism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Dark Romanticism is a literary subgenre of Romanticism characterised by pessimism about human nature, a fascination with sin, guilt, and evil, and the presence of eerie, grotesque, or demonic elements. Where mainstream Romanticism and its American offshoot Transcendentalism celebrated individual intuition, nature, and human goodness, the dark Romantics — sometimes called anti-Transcendentalists — presented individuals as prone to self-destruction, haunted by inherited sin and psychological torment, and signs in nature as ominous rather than benign.

American writers

The movement is most closely associated with three nineteenth-century American authors. Edgar Allan Poe (1809–1849) epitomises the mode in tales such as "The Fall of the House of Usher" and "The Tell-Tale Heart" and poems like "The Raven", exploring madness, premature burial, and the perverse impulses of the mind. Nathaniel Hawthorne (1804–1864) drew on his Puritan ancestry in The Scarlet Letter and stories like "Young Goodman Brown" to examine guilt, secrecy, and the "blackness" of the human heart. Herman Melville (1819–1891) infused Moby-Dick and "Bartleby, the Scrivener" with metaphysical darkness about the indifference or malignity of the universe.

European connections

Dark Romantic themes descend in part from Gothic fiction — Horace Walpole's The Castle of Otranto (1764), the works of Ann Radcliffe, and Matthew Lewis's The Monk — and from German Schauerroman and writers such as E. T. A. Hoffmann. Mary Shelley's Frankenstein (1818) is often discussed alongside the dark Romantics for its treatment of transgression and alienation.

Themes

Recurring motifs include the doppelgänger, haunted houses and landscapes, forbidden knowledge, the femme fatale, and narrators whose sanity is in doubt. The dark Romantics questioned the perfectibility of humanity emphasised by Enlightenment and Transcendentalist optimism, and their explorations of the irrational anticipated later Gothic horror and psychological fiction.
