Public domain in the United States

Works published in the United States before 1930 are generally in the public domain.

The public domain in the United States comprises creative works and inventions not protected by intellectual-property law — because their copyright or patent has expired, because they were never eligible for protection, or because the rights holder dedicated them to the public — which anyone may use without permission. Works in the public domain form the raw material for much new creativity, scholarship, and publishing.

Copyright

Under current US law, copyright in works created on or after 1 January 1978 lasts for the author's life plus 70 years (or, for works made for hire, 95 years from publication or 120 years from creation, whichever is shorter). For works published before 1978, protection generally runs 95 years from publication, provided formalities such as notice and renewal were observed; failure to renew a pre-1964 copyright or to include a copyright notice on works published before 1989 frequently placed works in the public domain early. As a practical rule, all works published in the United States before 1930 are now in the public domain, with a new year's cohort entering each 1 January (marked as "Public Domain Day"). Works of the US federal government — statutes, court opinions, and works by federal employees — are not copyrightable at all (17 U.S.C. §105), though state and local government works may be protected, subject to the government edicts doctrine affirmed in Georgia v. Public.Resource.Org (2020). Foreign works whose US copyrights had lapsed for non-compliance with formalities were controversially restored by the Uruguay Round Agreements Act, upheld in Golan v. Holder (2012).

Patents and trademarks

Patented inventions enter the public domain when the patent expires — generally 20 years from filing for utility patents — and the disclosed invention may then be practised freely. Trademarks differ: they can last indefinitely while in use, but generic terms lose protection, and abandoned marks may be reused.

Dedication and licences

Creators can affirmatively waive rights using instruments such as Creative Commons' CC0 public-domain dedication, though the legal effectiveness of abandoning copyright varies by context. Software is also released into the public domain or under permissive terms, and databases such as Project Gutenberg, the Internet Archive, and Wikimedia Commons collect and verify public-domain material.
