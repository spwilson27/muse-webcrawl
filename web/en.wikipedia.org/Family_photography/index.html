Family photography

Family photography is the practice of photographing one's family members, occasions and domestic life, and the body of images — albums, snapshots and digital collections — that results. Emerging as soon as photography became affordable in the nineteenth century, it developed into one of the most widespread uses of the camera, shaping how families record milestones and construct their shared memory and identity.

History

Early family photographs were formal studio portraits: daguerreotypes and cartes de visite in which relatives posed in their best clothes. The arrival of roll film and Kodak's Box Brownie camera in 1900 moved photography into the home, and the "snapshot" aesthetic — birthdays, holidays, new babies, christenings and weddings — became the genre's core. Instant cameras and colour film expanded the practice in the post-war decades; the smartphone and social media have since transformed both the volume of family images and how they are shared, raising new questions about children's privacy and consent (sometimes termed "sharenting").

Study and significance

Sociologists and visual anthropologists, notably Pierre Bourdieu in Photography: A Middle-Brow Art (1965), have analysed family photography as a ritual that affirms family unity and integration, typically emphasising happy occasions and omitting conflict and hardship. Scholars of vernacular photography study albums and archives as primary sources for social history, dress, domestic interiors and migration. Artists have also reworked family photographs to explore memory, loss and identity.
