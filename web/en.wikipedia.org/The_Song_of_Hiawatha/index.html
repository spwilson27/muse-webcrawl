The Song of Hiawatha

AuthorHenry Wadsworth Longfellow
Published1855
LanguageEnglish
FormEpic poem in trochaic tetrameter

The Song of Hiawatha is an epic poem of 1855 by Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, written in trochaic tetrameter — the metre of the Finnish Kalevala — and drawing on Ojibwe and other Native American legends as transmitted, often unreliably, through the writings of ethnographer Henry Rowe Schoolcraft.

Plot

The poem recounts the life of Hiawatha, a culture hero born of the West Wind Mudjekeewis and the moon-maiden's daughter Wenonah. Raised by his grandmother Nokomis beside the "shining Big-Sea-Water" (Lake Superior), he learns the speech of birds and beasts, slays the evil magician Pearl-Feather, invents picture-writing, fasts and wrestles the corn-spirit Mondamin, wooes and weds Minnehaha ("Laughing Water") of the Dacotahs, and battles famine, winter and the fever that kills his wife. In the close, Hiawatha welcomes the coming of the white missionaries, bids farewell to his people, and departs westward into the sunset.

Sources and criticism

Longfellow conflated the Ojibwe trickster-culture figure Nanabozho with the historical Hiawatha, a co-founder of the Iroquois Confederacy, and drew incidents from Schoolcraft's compilations. Though the poem's portrait of Native life is romanticised and its ethnography unreliable, it was an enormous commercial and cultural success, among the best-selling American poems of the nineteenth century, translated worldwide, memorised by generations of schoolchildren, and endlessly parodied for its hypnotic galloping metre.

Influence

Hiawatha shaped the popular image of Native America for a century, seeded place-names across the Midwest, and inspired musical settings, most notably Samuel Coleridge-Taylor's cantata trilogy The Song of Hiawatha (1898–1900). Critics today read it as both a landmark of American romantic nationalism and an elegy that, in celebrating a "vanishing" people, served the consolations of an expansionist republic.
