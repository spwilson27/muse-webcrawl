Transit of Mercury

A transit of Mercury across the Sun takes place when the planet Mercury passes directly between the Sun and a superior planet and crosses the solar disc. During a transit, Mercury can be seen from Earth as a small black disc moving across the face of the Sun. Transits of Mercury occur much more often than transits of Venus, with about 13 or 14 per century, because Mercury is closer to the Sun and orbits it more rapidly.

Characteristics

Transits of Mercury can happen in May or November, near the nodes of Mercury's orbit. May transits occur at aphelion and repeat at intervals of 13 and 33 years; November transits occur at perihelion and repeat at intervals of 7, 13, and 33 years. The first observed transit of Mercury was recorded by Pierre Gassendi on 7 November 1631, following a prediction by Johannes Kepler. Recent transits occurred on 9 May 2016 and 11 November 2019; the next will be on 13 November 2032.

Observation

Because the Sun cannot be viewed safely with the naked eye, transits are observed with telescopes using solar filters or by projecting the Sun's image. Mercury's disc is only about 1/195 the Sun's diameter, so it appears as a tiny spot; the "black drop effect" noted in Venus transits is less prominent. Scientific observations of transits historically helped determine the scale of the Solar System and still aid studies of Mercury's thin exosphere.
