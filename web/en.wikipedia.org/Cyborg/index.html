Cyborg

A cyborg (short for "cybernetic organism") is a being with both organic and biomechatronic body parts. The term was coined in 1960 by Manfred Clynes and Nathan S. Kline in an article on the advantages of self-regulating human–machine systems in outer space. Unlike a robot, which is wholly artificial, or an android, which imitates a human, a cyborg is an organism whose abilities are extended or restored by integrated technological components.

Origins of the concept

Clynes and Kline proposed that adapting the human body itself, rather than only the environment, would be the pragmatic path to space exploration: an "exogenously extended organisational complex functioning as an integrated homeostatic system" could regulate its own physiology automatically, freeing the astronaut for exploration. The idea built on Norbert Wiener's cybernetics and on earlier fictional antecedents, such as Edgar Allan Poe's description of a heavily prostheticised general in "The Man That Was Used Up" (1839) and the artificial beings of early science fiction.

Real-world cyborgs

In a medical sense, cyborgs already exist. People with pacemakers, cochlear implants, insulin pumps, deep brain stimulators, retinal implants, and myoelectric prosthetic limbs all integrate technology with biology. Neural interfaces such as brain–computer implants have allowed paralysed patients to control cursors and robotic arms. Artists and self-described cyborgs — including Neil Harbisson, whose implanted antenna lets him perceive colour as sound, and Kevin Warwick, who experimented with implanted electrodes linked to his nervous system — have extended the term into art and biohacking. Grinders and biohackers implant magnets and RFID chips for sensory or functional augmentation.

Animal cyborgs have been produced in research, including insects with electronic flight controls and rats guided by neural stimulation, raising ethical questions about remote control of living beings.

In fiction and theory

Cyborgs are a staple of science fiction: the Cybermen and the Daleks of Doctor Who, the Borg of Star Trek, Darth Vader in Star Wars, the Terminator, RoboCop, and Motoko Kusanagi in Ghost in the Shell all explore the boundary between person and machine. Donna Haraway's influential 1985 essay "A Cyborg Manifesto" used the cyborg as a metaphor for dissolving boundaries — human/animal, organism/machine, physical/non-physical — and for rethinking feminist and socialist politics. Posthumanist and transhumanist writers take the cyborg as a figure for humanity's technological self-transformation.

Debates around cyborgs concern identity (how much replacement leaves a person themselves, a technological Ship of Theseus), equity of access to augmentation, military enhancement of soldiers, and the rights and legal status of heavily augmented people.
