Adaptive radiation

In evolutionary biology, adaptive radiation is a process in which organisms diversify rapidly from an ancestral species into a multitude of new forms, particularly when a change in the environment makes new resources available, alters biotic interactions or opens new environmental niches. This process, starting from a single point in evolutionary time, leads to speciation and phenotypic adaptation of an array of species exhibiting different morphological and physiological traits. The classic examples of adaptive radiation are island archipelagos.

Characteristics

Adaptive radiations generally show four features: a common ancestry of component species; a phenotype–environment correlation, in which different environments are associated with different adaptations; trait utility, where the adaptations demonstrably improve performance in their environment; and rapid speciation concentrated in a burst of diversification.

Examples

  • Darwin's finches of the Galápagos Islands, which evolved diverse beak shapes for different foods from one ancestor.
  • Hawaiian honeycreepers and the Hawaiian silversword alliance of plants.
  • Cichlid fishes of the African Great Lakes, which radiated into hundreds of species.
  • Anolis lizards of the Caribbean, evolving repeated sets of habitat specialists ("ecomorphs").
  • Mammals after the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event, filling niches vacated by the non-avian dinosaurs.

Triggers

Radiations are often triggered by ecological opportunity: colonisation of isolated islands, mass extinctions that empty niches, or the evolution of a "key innovation" (such as nectar spurs in columbines or antifreeze proteins) that opens new adaptive zones.
