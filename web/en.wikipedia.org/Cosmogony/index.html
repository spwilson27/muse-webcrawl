Cosmogony

Cosmogony
EtymologyGreek kosmogonia: kosmos (order/world) + gonos (offspring, begetting)
ScopeTheories of the origin of the universe or cosmos
FormsReligious creation myths; scientific cosmology
Modern scientific formBig Bang theory
Related fieldsCosmology, mythology, philosophy, astronomy

Cosmogony is any model concerning the origin of the cosmos or the universe. The term covers both the creation myths of religions and cultures — narratives explaining how the world began — and scientific theories of the universe's origin, which in modern cosmology take the form of the Big Bang model. Whereas cosmology studies the universe as a whole and its evolution, cosmogony focuses specifically on its beginnings.

The Big Bang is the prevailing scientific cosmogony, describing the universe's origin in a hot, dense early state.

Religious and mythological cosmogonies

Virtually every culture has a cosmogonic myth. Recurring motifs include creation from chaos or a primordial ocean, a cosmic egg, world parents separated by their offspring (as in the Greek succession of Ouranos and Gaia, and Hesiod's Theogony, the earliest surviving Greek cosmogony), a creator deity speaking or crafting the world into being (as in Genesis), and emergence from lower worlds. The Babylonian Enūma Eliš recounts creation from the bodies of defeated primordial gods. These narratives typically establish not just physical origins but the order of society, ritual, and morality.

Scientific cosmogony

Greek philosophers began naturalistic cosmogonies with the pre-Socratics, and Anaxagoras and Empedocles proposed formative principles. Modern scientific cosmogony grew from the Kant–Laplace nebular hypothesis for the Solar System's origin (1755/1796) to the expanding-universe models of the 1920s: Alexander Friedmann's solutions and Georges Lemaître's "primeval atom" hypothesis of 1927–31. The discovery of the cosmic microwave background in 1964 consolidated the Big Bang theory as the standard cosmogony, extended by cosmic inflation theory. Questions about what, if anything, preceded the hot Big Bang — quantum cosmogonies, cyclic models, the Hartle–Hawking no-boundary proposal — remain active research.
