Divergence

In vector calculus, divergence is a vector operator that operates on a vector field, producing a scalar field giving the quantity of the vector field's source at each point. In more technical terms, the divergence represents the volume density of the outward flux of a vector field from an infinitesimal volume around a given point.

Intuition

If air is heated at a point, it expands and flows outward in all directions, so the velocity field of the air has positive divergence there — the point acts as a source. If air is cooled and contracts, the velocity field points inward and has negative divergence, corresponding to a sink. A field with zero divergence everywhere is called solenoidal or incompressible; the velocity field of an incompressible fluid flow has zero divergence because fluid neither accumulates nor rarefies at any point.

Definition

For a vector field F = (Fx, Fy, Fz) in three-dimensional Cartesian coordinates, the divergence is the sum of the partial derivatives of the components with respect to their own coordinates: div F = ∂Fx/∂x + ∂Fy/∂y + ∂Fz/∂z. It is written with the del operator as ∇·F. Equivalent definitions exist in cylindrical and spherical coordinates and, invariantly, as the limit of the surface flux per unit volume as the volume shrinks to a point.

The divergence theorem

The divergence theorem (also called Gauss's theorem or Ostrogradsky's theorem) states that the flux of a vector field out of a closed surface equals the integral of the divergence over the volume enclosed. It is a fundamental result of vector calculus and a special case of the generalized Stokes theorem.

Applications

Divergence appears throughout physics. In electromagnetism, Gauss's law states that the divergence of the electric field is proportional to the charge density, and the absence of magnetic monopoles is expressed by the divergence of the magnetic field being zero. In fluid dynamics, the continuity equation uses the divergence of velocity to express conservation of mass. Maxwell's equations, the heat equation, and gravitational field theory all make essential use of the operator.

Generalizations

Divergence can be defined on Riemannian manifolds, where it is the trace of the covariant derivative of a vector field, and in that setting underlies results such as the integration-by-parts formula and the definition of the Laplace–Beltrami operator as the divergence of the gradient. The divergence of a tensor field is defined componentwise by contraction.
