Disaccharide

A disaccharide (also called a double sugar or biose) is the sugar formed when two monosaccharides are joined by a glycosidic linkage. Like monosaccharides, disaccharides are simple sugars soluble in water. Three common examples are sucrose, lactose, and maltose.

Formation

Disaccharides are formed by a condensation (dehydration) reaction that removes a water molecule as the glycosidic bond forms between the two monosaccharide units; hydrolysis, catalysed by enzymes called disaccharidases, reverses the process. Sucrose consists of glucose and fructose, lactose of galactose and glucose, and maltose of two glucose units.

Classification

Reducing disaccharides such as maltose and lactose retain a free anomeric carbon able to open to an aldehyde form, whereas non-reducing disaccharides such as sucrose and trehalose have both anomeric carbons engaged in the glycosidic bond. Disaccharides are one of the four chemical groupings of carbohydrates, alongside monosaccharides, oligosaccharides, and polysaccharides.

Digestion

In the small intestine, disaccharides are hydrolysed into monosaccharides before absorption. Deficiency of the enzyme lactase causes lactose intolerance.
