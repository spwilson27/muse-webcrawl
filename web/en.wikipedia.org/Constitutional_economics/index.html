Constitutional economics is a research program in economics and constitutionalism that describes and analyzes the specific interrelationships between constitutional issues and the functioning of the economy, including the budget process, the separation of powers, and fiscal and monetary rules. The term was introduced by the American economist James M. Buchanan, who received the Nobel Prize in Economics in 1986 in part for this work.

Origins and concepts

Buchanan argued that economists should study not only choices within rules but the choice of the rules themselves — the "constitutional" level of decision-making. In The Calculus of Consent (1962), written with Gordon Tullock, he applied economic reasoning to political decision-making, founding the public choice school. Constitutional economics distinguishes between everyday politics, conducted within constitutional constraints, and constitutional politics, in which the constraints themselves are chosen, ideally under something like a veil of uncertainty about one's future position.

Applications

The approach underlies proposals for balanced-budget rules, debt brakes, independent central banks, fiscal councils, and entrenched limits on taxation and government borrowing, all intended to control the incentive problems — deficits, rent-seeking, time inconsistency — that unconstrained majority rule may generate. Critics respond that rigid constitutional rules can impede necessary policy flexibility and democratic choice.

Relation to law and economics

Constitutional economics overlaps with the economic analysis of law but focuses on framework rules rather than individual legal rules, and on the legitimacy of constraints adopted by broad consent rather than mere efficiency.
