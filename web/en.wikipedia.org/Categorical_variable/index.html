Categorical variable

FieldStatistics
SubtypesNominal; ordinal; dichotomous (binary)
Contrasted withNumerical (quantitative) variables

In statistics, a categorical variable (also called a qualitative variable) is a variable that can take on one of a limited, and usually fixed, number of possible values, assigning each individual or other unit of observation to a particular group or nominal category on the basis of some qualitative property. Examples include blood type, political affiliation, eye colour, and the make of a car.

Types

  • Nominal variables have two or more categories with no intrinsic ordering, such as gender categories, colours, or countries.
  • Ordinal variables have categories with a clear order but no fixed spacing, such as socio-economic status (low, medium, high) or Likert-scale responses (strongly disagree to strongly agree).
  • Dichotomous (binary) variables are nominal variables with exactly two categories, such as yes/no or success/failure.

Analysis

Categorical data are commonly summarised in frequency tables and contingency tables and analysed with methods such as the chi-squared test, Fisher's exact test, log-linear models, and logistic regression. In regression modelling, categorical predictors are entered using dummy (indicator) variables or effects coding — a process known in machine learning as one-hot encoding. The number of values a categorical variable can take is called its number of levels, and variables with many levels, such as postal codes, may require special treatment.

Measurement scales

In Stevens' typology of measurement scales, categorical variables occupy the nominal and ordinal levels, whereas numerical variables occupy the interval and ratio levels. This distinction guides which statistics — mode for nominal data, median for ordinal data, mean only for numerical data — are meaningful for a given variable.
