Atlantic slave trade

The Atlantic slave trade (or transatlantic slave trade) involved the transportation by slave traders of enslaved African people to the Americas. It began in the 16th century and lasted until the 19th, carrying an estimated 12.5 million Africans aboard slave ships, of whom about 10.7 million survived the Middle Passage. Most were taken to Brazil and the Caribbean, with smaller numbers to mainland North America and Spanish America. The trade formed one leg of the "triangular trade" linking Europe, Africa and the Americas.

Operation

European ships carried manufactured goods to West and Central Africa, exchanging them with African rulers and merchants for captives taken in wars and raids. The enslaved were chained in the holds of slave ships for a voyage of weeks to months under conditions so lethal that roughly 15 percent died en route. In the Americas they were sold to labour on sugar, tobacco, cotton, coffee and rice plantations and in mines. Portugal and later Brazil carried the largest share, followed by Britain, France, Spain and the Netherlands.

Abolition

Abolitionist movements, slave resistance and changing economics eroded the trade: Denmark banned it in 1803, Britain and the United States in 1807–08, though illegal trading to Cuba and Brazil continued into the 1860s. Slavery itself was abolished across the Americas between 1834 (British colonies) and 1888 (Brazil).

Legacy

The trade depopulated parts of Africa, distorted its political development, and created large African-descended populations throughout the Americas whose cultures transformed the hemisphere. It remains a central subject of historical scholarship, public memory and debates over reparations.
