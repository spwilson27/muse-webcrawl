Artificial superintelligence

Artificial superintelligence (ASI) is a hypothetical form of artificial intelligence that possesses intelligence far surpassing that of the brightest and most gifted human minds in virtually every field, including scientific creativity, general wisdom and social skills. The philosopher Nick Bostrom defines a superintelligence as "any intellect that greatly exceeds the cognitive performance of humans in virtually all domains of interest".

Relationship to AGI

ASI is usually discussed as a stage beyond artificial general intelligence (AGI) — a system that merely matches human-level versatility. Theorists propose that an AGI able to improve its own design could trigger an "intelligence explosion", a rapid recursive cycle of self-improvement culminating in superintelligence, an idea explored by I. J. Good in 1965 and popularised by Vernor Vinge's concept of the technological singularity.

Feasibility and paths

Proposed routes to superintelligence include scaling up machine learning systems, whole brain emulation, brain–computer interfaces and genetic engineering of human intelligence. Surveys of AI researchers show wide disagreement about timelines, with many considering ASI possible in principle but uncertain in practice.

Risks and control

Bostrom's 2014 book Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies argues that a superintelligent system with goals misaligned to human values could pose an existential risk, and poses the "control problem": how to ensure such a system remains safe and beneficial. These arguments have influenced the field of AI safety and AI alignment, though critics dispute the plausibility of sudden takeoff scenarios or argue the risks are overstated relative to nearer-term concerns.
