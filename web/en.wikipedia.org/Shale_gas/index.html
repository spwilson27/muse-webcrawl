Shale gas

Shale gas drilling, Marcellus Shale

Shale gas is natural gas that is found trapped within shale formations — fine-grained sedimentary rocks rich in organic matter. Because shale has very low permeability, the gas does not flow freely to a wellbore; commercial production requires creating artificial fractures in the rock, principally by hydraulic fracturing combined with horizontal drilling. Shale gas is a type of unconventional gas, alongside tight gas and coalbed methane.

Production technology

Modern shale development uses directional (horizontal) wells drilled a mile or more vertically and then turned to run laterally through the gas-bearing layer, followed by multi-stage hydraulic fracturing ("fracking"), in which water, sand proppant, and chemicals are pumped at high pressure to open and hold fractures. The combination was pioneered in the Barnett Shale of Texas in the 1990s and 2000s, notably by George P. Mitchell's Mitchell Energy, building on US government research and price incentives dating to the 1970s energy crises.

The shale revolution

From the mid-2000s, shale gas output from plays such as the Barnett, Haynesville, Marcellus, Fayetteville, and later the Permian Basin transformed energy markets: by the 2010s shale supplied the majority of US natural gas, the United States became the world's largest gas producer and (in 2023) the largest exporter of liquefied natural gas, and cheap gas displaced coal in electricity generation, cutting power-sector CO₂ emissions even as total gas use rose. Major shale resources also exist in China, Argentina (Vaca Muerta), Canada, and Algeria, though development outside North America has been slower.

Environmental concerns

Shale gas development is controversial. Documented concerns include methane leakage (which can erode gas's climate advantage over coal), contamination risks to groundwater and surface water from spills and well integrity failures, very large water withdrawals and wastewater disposal — disposal wells have induced earthquakes in Oklahoma and elsewhere — air pollution, and local land and traffic impacts. Several jurisdictions, including France and New York State, have banned hydraulic fracturing, while others regulate it. Industry responses emphasise methane controls, recycling of flowback water, and the emissions benefit of replacing coal.
