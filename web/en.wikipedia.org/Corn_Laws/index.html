Corn Laws

The Corn Laws were tariffs and other trade restrictions on imported food and grain ("corn" in British English at the time denoting all cereal grains) enforced in the United Kingdom between 1815 and 1846. They were designed to keep grain prices high to favour domestic producers, and they represented the classic British application of mercantilism.

Background

The first of the modern Corn Laws, the Importation Act 1815, prohibited the import of foreign grain until domestic prices reached 80 shillings per quarter. A sliding scale of duties followed in 1828, reducing the duty as domestic prices rose. The laws were strongly supported by landowners, who formed a powerful bloc in Parliament before the Reform Act 1832, and opposed by industrialists and urban consumers, who wanted cheaper food and lower wage pressures.

Opposition and the Anti-Corn Law League

The Anti-Corn Law League, founded in Manchester in 1838 and led by Richard Cobden and John Bright, campaigned across the country for repeal, arguing that free trade in grain would lower food prices and open foreign markets to British manufactures. The cause became one of the great mass political movements of the nineteenth century.

Repeal

Prime Minister Sir Robert Peel, converted to free trade, pushed repeal through in 1846, aided by the urgency of the Irish Great Famine. The Importation Act 1846 phased out the duties, fully by 1849. Repeal split the Conservative Party, brought down Peel's government, and set Britain on a course of free trade for the rest of the century.
