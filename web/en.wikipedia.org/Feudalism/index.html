Feudalism

Feudalism, also known as the feudal system, was a combination of legal, economic, military, and cultural customs that flourished in medieval Europe from the 9th to 15th centuries. Broadly defined, it was a way of structuring society around relationships derived from the holding of land in exchange for service or labour. In its classic definition, described by François-Louis Ganshof, feudalism describes a set of reciprocal legal and military obligations which existed among the warrior nobility and revolved around the three key concepts of lords, vassals, and fiefs.

Homage: a vassal commends himself to his lord in return for a fief.

Structure

A lord granted land (a fief) to a vassal in return for homage, fealty, and military service — typically a set number of days of service with a retinue. Below the great lords stood knights, and at the base, peasants and serfs worked manorial estates, owing labour services and dues to their lord. The manor, rather than the market, was the basic economic unit, and jurisdiction was fragmented among holders of local rights ("banal" lordship).

Historiography and debate

The word "feudalism" is early modern, not medieval. Marc Bloch's Feudal Society (1939–40) gave the concept its broad social form. Later historians, notably Elizabeth A. R. Brown and Susan Reynolds (whose Fiefs and Vassals, 1994, argued the classic model was back-projected from later legal texts), have questioned whether a single "feudal system" ever existed. Marxist historiography uses "feudalism" more broadly for a mode of production based on coerced peasant labour, applying the term also to societies such as imperial China and Tokugawa Japan.

Decline

From the 13th century onward, monetisation, commutation of labour services, the growth of towns and royal administration, infantry warfare, and demographic shocks such as the Black Death eroded the system, though feudal dues survived in France until abolished in the Revolution of 1789.
