Canadian cuisine

Canadian cuisine comprises the cooking traditions of Canada, shaped by Indigenous foodways and successive waves of French, British, and global immigration, and by the country's regional ingredients — wheat and beef on the Prairies, seafood on both coasts, maple products in the east, and game, berries, and corn in the north and forests. Iconic dishes include poutine, tourtière, butter tarts, Nanaimo bars, and peameal bacon.

Indigenous foundations

First Nations, Inuit, and Métis peoples developed cuisines around the "Three Sisters" (corn, beans, squash), wild rice (manoomin), maple sap, pemmican, bannock, salmon, caribou, seal, and berries. Many of these foods remain central to regional cooking and are experiencing a modern revival led by Indigenous chefs.

Regional cuisines

Quebec contributes poutine (fries, cheese curds, gravy), tourtière (spiced meat pie), pea soup, Montreal-style bagels and smoked meat, and maple-sugar traditions of the cabane à sucre. The Maritimes feature lobster, scallops, hodge podge, rappie pie, and the Halifax donair. Ontario is known for butter tarts and peameal bacon. The Prairies showcase Saskatoon berry pie, perogies (from Ukrainian immigration), and beef. British Columbia emphasizes Pacific salmon, spot prawns, and Nanaimo bars. The North relies on Arctic char, caribou, and bannock.

Ingredients and symbols

Maple syrup is Canada's most famous food export, with Quebec producing the large majority of the world supply. Other emblematic items include Canadian whisky, icewine from the Niagara Peninsula, Cheezies, ketchup chips, Coffee Crisp, and the double-double coffee of Tim Hortons.

Immigrant influences

Immigration continually reshapes Canadian food: Chinese-Canadian dishes such as ginger beef (invented in Calgary), Japanese-Canadian sushi innovations like the California roll's rival BC roll, South Asian, Italian, and Caribbean restaurants in every major city, and the shawarma scene of Ottawa, which styles itself a shawarma capital.
