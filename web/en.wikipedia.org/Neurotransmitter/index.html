Major examplesGlutamate, GABA, dopamine, serotonin, acetylcholine, norepinephrine

A neurotransmitter is a signalling molecule secreted by a neuron to affect another cell across a synapse. When an action potential reaches the presynaptic terminal, voltage-gated calcium channels open and the resulting calcium influx triggers synaptic vesicles to fuse with the cell membrane, releasing neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft. The molecules bind to receptors on the postsynaptic cell, producing excitatory or inhibitory effects, and are then cleared by enzymatic degradation, diffusion, or reuptake into the presynaptic neuron.

Major neurotransmitters

Glutamate is the principal excitatory neurotransmitter of the central nervous system, while gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) and glycine are the main inhibitory ones. The monoamines—dopamine, serotonin, norepinephrine, epinephrine, and histamine—modulate mood, arousal, reward, and sleep. Acetylcholine is the transmitter at the neuromuscular junction and is also important in attention and memory. Many small peptides, such as endorphins and substance P, act as neurotransmitters or neuromodulators.

Receptors and termination

Neurotransmitters act on two broad classes of receptor: ionotropic receptors, which are ligand-gated ion channels mediating fast transmission, and metabotropic receptors, which are G protein-coupled receptors producing slower, longer-lasting effects. Signalling is terminated by enzymes such as acetylcholinesterase in the cleft, or by transporter proteins that pump the transmitter back into the presynaptic terminal or into glial cells.

Discovery

The chemical theory of synaptic transmission was established in 1921 when Otto Loewi demonstrated that stimulating the vagus nerve of a frog heart released a substance—"Vagusstoff", later identified as acetylcholine—that slowed a second heart. Loewi and Henry Dale shared the 1936 Nobel Prize for this work.

Clinical significance

Imbalances in neurotransmitter systems are implicated in depression, schizophrenia, Parkinson's disease, epilepsy, and anxiety disorders, and most psychiatric medications act by altering neurotransmitter signalling—for example, selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors block serotonin transporters, while antipsychotics antagonise dopamine receptors.
