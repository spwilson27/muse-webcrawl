Materials science

Materials science is an interdisciplinary field of researching and discovering materials. It incorporates elements of physics, chemistry, and engineering. The field is concerned with the discovery, design, and characterization of materials, particularly solids, and with understanding the relationships between a material's structure, its processing, and its properties. Materials science is closely related to materials engineering, which focuses on the design and application of materials in products and structures.

Until the 1960s — and in some cases decades after — many university materials science departments were named metallurgy departments, reflecting the nineteenth- and twentieth-century emphasis on metals. The broad field expanded to include ceramics, polymers, semiconductors, magnetic materials, biomaterials, and nanomaterials as industrial and military demand grew during and after the Second World War.

History

The development of materials has accompanied human civilization from its earliest stages; historians divide prehistory into the Stone Age, Bronze Age, and Iron Age according to the dominant material of each era. Early materials work was empirical: potters, smiths, and glassmakers refined recipes by trial and error without understanding atomic structure. The scientific study of metals began in earnest in the nineteenth century, when researchers such as Henry Clifton Sorby applied microscopy to polished metal sections, revealing the grain structures that determine mechanical behaviour.

The field's modern form emerged in the mid-twentieth century. The transistor, developed at Bell Telephone Laboratories in 1947, demonstrated the economic and strategic importance of semiconductor materials, and governments invested heavily in university research. In the United States, the Advanced Research Projects Agency established interdisciplinary laboratories at several universities in 1960, seeding departments that combined solid-state physics, chemistry, metallurgy, and ceramics. The first department explicitly named "materials science" is generally credited to Northwestern University in 1958.

Foundations

A central paradigm of the discipline is the materials science tetrahedron, whose corners are structure, processing, properties, and performance. Processing a material changes its structure; structure at every length scale — from atomic bonding to visible grains — determines properties such as strength, conductivity, and corrosion resistance; and properties in service define performance. Characterization techniques, including X-ray diffraction, electron microscopy, and spectroscopy, connect these corners experimentally.

Structure is conventionally divided into atomic structure, microstructure (the arrangement of grains and phases, typically at the micrometre scale), nanostructure, and macrostructure. Defects play an outsized role: perfect crystals are rare, and imperfections such as vacancies, dislocations, and grain boundaries govern diffusion, plastic deformation, and electrical behaviour.

Classes of materials

Materials are commonly grouped into families with shared bonding and properties:

  • Metals — crystalline solids with metallic bonding, generally strong, ductile, and electrically and thermally conductive. Examples include steel, aluminium, copper, and titanium alloys.
  • Ceramics — inorganic, non-metallic compounds such as alumina and silicon carbide; hard, stiff, and heat-resistant but brittle.
  • Polymers — long-chain molecules, including plastics, rubbers, and natural materials such as wood and wool; light and easily shaped but with lower strength and temperature limits.
  • Semiconductors — materials such as silicon and gallium arsenide whose electrical conductivity can be precisely controlled by doping; the basis of modern electronics.
  • Composites — combinations of two or more materials, such as carbon-fibre-reinforced polymer, designed to achieve properties unavailable in any single constituent.
  • Biomaterials — materials engineered to interact with biological systems, used in implants, prostheses, and drug delivery.
  • Nanomaterials — materials with structural features below about 100 nanometres, whose properties may differ sharply from bulk behaviour.

Properties and testing

Materials scientists measure mechanical properties (elastic modulus, yield strength, toughness, fatigue life), thermal properties (conductivity, expansion), electrical and magnetic properties, optical properties, and chemical stability. Standardized tests — tensile tests, hardness indentation, impact tests such as the Charpy test — allow comparison between materials and quality control in manufacturing. Failure analysis applies these methods retrospectively to determine why components fractured, corroded, or wore out in service.

Applications

Advances in materials underpin much of technological progress. High-temperature nickel superalloys enable efficient jet engines; lithium-ion battery electrodes and solid electrolytes are the focus of intense research for electric vehicles and grid storage; photovoltaic materials convert sunlight to electricity; and lightweight composites reduce aircraft fuel consumption. In medicine, titanium implants, biodegradable polymers, and hydrogels are products of materials research. Computational materials science — using simulation and, increasingly, machine learning to predict properties — is a growing subfield, exemplified by initiatives such as the Materials Genome Initiative launched in the United States in 2011.
