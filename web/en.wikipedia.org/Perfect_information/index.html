Perfect information

In economics and game theory, perfect information describes a situation in which each player, when making any decision, is perfectly informed of all the events that have previously occurred and of all decisions up to that point in the game. A game with perfect information is one in which every player knows the complete history of moves made by all players.

Examples

Chess is the canonical example of a perfect information game: both players can see all pieces at all times. Other examples include tic-tac-toe, checkers, Go and mancala. By contrast, poker and bridge are games of imperfect information because cards are hidden, and simultaneous-move games such as rock paper scissors do not reveal the opponent's current choice.

Formal properties

In extensive-form games, perfect information means every information set consists of a single node. A theorem due to Ernst Zermelo states that finite deterministic perfect information games are solvable: one player has a winning strategy, or both can force a draw. Perfect information differs from complete information, which concerns knowledge of the game's structure and payoffs rather than move history.

Applications

Perfect information models are used in economics for sequential bargaining and in artificial intelligence, where search algorithms such as minimax with alpha-beta pruning exploit the complete observability of the game state.
