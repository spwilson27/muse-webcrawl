Casparian strip

Root cross-section showing the endodermis and Casparian strips

The Casparian strip is a band-like wall formation found in the primary cell walls of the endodermis in the roots of vascular plants. It is a band of cell wall material — deposited in an early stage of endodermal development in the radial and transverse walls — that is impregnated with suberin (and sometimes lignin) and is chemically different from the rest of the cell wall. The strip is named after the German botanist Robert Caspary, who described it in the nineteenth century.

Function

The Casparian strip blocks the passive flow of water and dissolved substances through the apoplast (the spaces of the cell walls and intercellular spaces) in the endodermal layer. Water and solutes moving inward from the root cortex are thereby forced to cross the plasma membrane of an endodermal cell and enter the symplast before reaching the stele and the xylem. Because the membrane selectively controls what passes, the strip gives the plant a checkpoint over the uptake of nutrients and helps exclude harmful substances, while also preventing backflow of water and ions from the vascular tissue into the cortex.

Development

Casparian strips form progressively as endodermal cells mature, beginning as spots on the radial walls (Casparian dots in cross-section) that extend into a continuous band. In many plants the endodermis later deposits suberin lamellae over the whole inner cell surface, with some cells ("passage cells") remaining thin-walled to permit continued transport. Small plasmodesmata connections through the strip may persist in some species.
