The Russian Orthodox Old-Rite Church is the largest of the priestly Old Believer churches, tracing its origin to those Eastern Orthodox Christians who refused to accept the liturgical and textual reforms introduced by Patriarch Nikon in the 1650s–1660s and were anathematised in the Great Schism (Raskol) of 1666. The Old Believers preserved the pre-reform rites, including the two-fingered sign of the cross, the spelling of the name of Jesus, and the older chant traditions.

The Belokrinitsky hierarchy

Deprived of bishops after the schism, the priestly Old Believers relied on fugitive priests from the official church for nearly two centuries. In 1846, at Belaya Krinitsa in Austrian Bukovina, the deposed Greek metropolitan Ambrose (Amvrosy) joined the Old Believers and consecrated bishops, founding the Belokrinitsky hierarchy — the first fully independent Old Believer episcopate. Despite persecution in Russia, the hierarchy survived, centred on the Rogozhskoye Cemetery community in Moscow.

Organisation and history

After restrictions on Old Believers were lifted by the 1905 Edict of Toleration, the Church flourished briefly, building hundreds of churches. Soviet persecution drove it underground, but it endured. In 1988 the church adopted its present name, and its head, who had held the title of Archbishop of Moscow and All Russia, took the title of Metropolitan. The anathemas against the old rites were lifted by the Moscow Patriarchate in 1971, though this did not restore communion.

Practice

The Church maintains the old Russian liturgical books and znamenny chant, distinctive iconography, the lestovka prayer rope, and strict rules of fasting and dress in some communities. Parishes exist across Russia, Ukraine, Belarus, Romania (among the Lipovans), and the diaspora.
