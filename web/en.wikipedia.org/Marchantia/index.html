Marchantia is a genus of liverworts in the family Marchantiaceae and the order Marchantiales. The genus is found worldwide, typically on moist, shaded soil, rocks and the banks of streams.

Description

The plant body is a flat, ribbon-like thallus that forks dichotomously as it grows. The upper surface is marked by a network of polygonal areas, each with a central pore leading to an air chamber beneath — a primitive arrangement for gas exchange. Rhizoids on the underside anchor the thallus to the substrate.

Reproduction

Marchantia reproduces asexually by gemmae: tiny lens-shaped discs of tissue formed in cup-like structures (gemma cups) on the thallus surface, which are splashed out by raindrops and grow into new plants. Sexual reproduction involves separate male and female plants bearing umbrella-like reproductive structures — stalked antheridiophores and archegoniophores — beneath which the sex organs develop. Fertilisation requires a film of water so that swimming sperm can reach the eggs.

Ecology and human relevance

The best-known species, Marchantia polymorpha, is a common weed of damp greenhouses, nurseries and burnt ground, and has become an important model organism for studying plant evolution, development and genetics, since liverworts represent one of the earliest-diverging lineages of land plants.
