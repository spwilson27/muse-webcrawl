State of nature

In political philosophy, the state of nature is the hypothetical condition of human life before societies came into existence. Philosophers of social contract theory used the concept to explain the origins and legitimacy of government: humans, moved by the inconveniences of the state of nature, agree to form a civil society governed by laws.

Hobbes

Thomas Hobbes, in Leviathan (1651), portrayed the state of nature as a "war of all against all" in which life is "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short". To escape it, individuals covenant to surrender their freedoms to an absolute sovereign.

Locke

John Locke offered a more benign account in the Two Treatises of Government (1689): the state of nature is governed by natural law and natural rights to life, liberty, and property, but lacks an impartial judge and effective enforcement, justifying a limited government founded on consent.

Rousseau and Kant

Jean-Jacques Rousseau treated the state of nature as peaceful and pre-moral, corrupted by property and society; Immanuel Kant regarded it as a rational idea rather than a historical claim, requiring exit into a lawful civil condition.

Modern uses

The concept survives in theories of international relations, where the absence of a world government is described as an anarchical state of nature among states, and in contemporary contractarian ethics.
