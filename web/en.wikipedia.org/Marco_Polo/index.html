Marco Polo (c. 1254 – 1324) was a Venetian merchant and explorer whose account of his journey to Asia was one of the most famous travel books of the Middle Ages. His book introduced Europeans to the wealth and geography of China and the Mongol world.

Journey to China

Marco's father Niccolò and uncle Maffeo were merchants who had already travelled to the court of Kublai Khan, founder of China's Yuan dynasty. In 1271 the seventeen-year-old Marco joined their second expedition, travelling overland along the Silk Road through Persia, the Pamirs, and the Gobi Desert. They reached Kublai's summer capital at Shangdu in 1275. According to the book, Marco learned several languages, entered the Khan's service, and travelled on official missions to Yunnan, Burma, and perhaps India over seventeen years in China.

Return and the book

In 1291 the Polos escorted a Mongol princess by sea to Persia, then returned to Venice in 1295. Marco was captured by Genoa in a sea battle in 1298 and, while a prisoner, dictated his travels to the romance writer Rustichello da Pisa. The resulting work, Le Devisement du Monde — known in English as The Travels of Marco Polo — described paper money, coal, the imperial postal system, and the splendours of Hangzhou and the Khan's capital Khanbaliq (Beijing). Many contemporaries doubted its marvels, earning the book the nickname Il Milione, but it became a bestseller in manuscript and shaped European maps of Asia for centuries; Christopher Columbus carried an annotated copy.

Legacy

Historians still debate details of the account — such as the omission of the Great Wall and tea — but the consensus accepts the essential truth of Polo's journey. His name survives in a breed of sheep, an airport in Venice, and countless place names and products.
