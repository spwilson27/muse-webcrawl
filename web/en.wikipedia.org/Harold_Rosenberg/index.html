Harold Rosenberg (February 2, 1906 – July 11, 1978) was an American art critic and essayist best known for defining abstract expressionism as "action painting". As the chief art critic of The New Yorker from 1967 until his death, he was, with Clement Greenberg, one of the two dominant critical voices of the New York School era — and Greenberg's great intellectual rival.

Life and career

Born in Brooklyn to Russian Jewish immigrant parents, Rosenberg trained as a lawyer but was drawn into New York's bohemian and leftist intellectual circles in the 1930s, writing poetry and essays for journals including Partisan Review and work for the WPA. After the Second World War he emerged as the philosopher-critic of the new American painting, close to artists such as Willem de Kooning and Barnett Newman.

"The American Action Painters"

His 1952 essay in ARTnews, "The American Action Painters", argued that for the new painters "the canvas began to appear … as an arena in which to act — rather than as a space in which to reproduce, re-design, analyze or 'express' an object, actual or imagined. What was to go on the canvas was not a picture but an event." This existential account of painting as an encounter, emphasising the act over the finished object, contrasted sharply with Greenberg's formalist emphasis on medium and opticality.

Writings and later work

Rosenberg's essays were collected in The Tradition of the New (1959), The Anxious Object (1964), Act and the Actor (1966) and Artworks and Packages (1969); he also wrote monographs on de Kooning and Saul Steinberg. In later years he was sharply critical of Pop art, minimalism and the commercialisation of the art world, defending the seriousness of the avant-garde against what he saw as its academic and market routinisation.
