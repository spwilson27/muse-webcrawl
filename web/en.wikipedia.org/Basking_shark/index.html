Basking shark

Basking shark (Cetorhinus maximus)
FamilyCetorhinidae
SizeUp to about 8–10 m (second-largest fish)
DietZooplankton (filter feeder)
Conservation statusEndangered (IUCN)

The basking shark (Cetorhinus maximus) is the second-largest living fish, after the whale shark, and one of three plankton-eating shark species, along with the whale shark and the megamouth shark. It is a cosmopolitan migratory species found in all the world's temperate oceans.

Description and feeding

Basking sharks are usually greyish-brown with mottled skin, a greatly elongated snout, and enormous gill slits that nearly encircle the head. They swim slowly at or near the surface with mouths agape — the "basking" behavior that gives them their name — filtering zooplankton through bristle-like gill rakers. An adult can filter hundreds of cubic metres of water per hour. Adults commonly reach 6 to 8 metres, with exceptional individuals near 10 metres.

Behavior and life history

Satellite tracking has shown that basking sharks undertake long migrations and deep dives, sometimes crossing ocean basins and spending winter in deep water, where they were once mistakenly thought to hibernate. They are ovoviviparous, giving birth to live young after a long gestation. Despite their size, they are generally docile and pose no threat to humans.

Conservation

Historically hunted for their liver oil, meat, and fins, basking shark populations declined sharply in the 20th century. The species is now listed as Endangered by the IUCN and is protected in many countries and under international agreements, though bycatch and ship strikes remain threats.
