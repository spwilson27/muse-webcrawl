Vorticity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In fluid mechanics, vorticity is a pseudovector field that describes the local spinning motion of a continuum near a point — the tendency of something to rotate — as would be seen by an observer moving with the flow. It is defined mathematically as the curl of the velocity field:

ω = ∇ × v

where v is the flow velocity. The magnitude of vorticity is twice the local angular velocity of the fluid element. Flow with zero vorticity everywhere is called irrotational.

Examples

In a rigid-body rotation, vorticity is uniform and equal to twice the angular velocity. In a free vortex, such as water circling a drain, velocity falls off with radius and the vorticity is concentrated at the core. In a simple shear flow vorticity can be non-zero even though streamlines are straight, because adjacent layers move at different speeds — a paddlewheel placed in the flow would spin.

The vorticity equation

Taking the curl of the Navier–Stokes equations yields the vorticity transport equation, which shows how vorticity is advected, stretched, and diffused by viscosity. Vortex stretching is a key mechanism in the generation of turbulence and in the intensification of storms: in meteorology, vorticity (especially potential vorticity) is central to analysing cyclones and atmospheric dynamics.

Related concepts

The circulation around a closed curve equals the flux of vorticity through the surface it bounds (Kelvin–Stokes theorem). A vortex is a region where vorticity is concentrated; a vortex line is everywhere tangent to the vorticity vector. In aerodynamics, the vorticity trailing behind a wing accounts for lift through the Kutta–Joukowski theorem.
