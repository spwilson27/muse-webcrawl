The Great Altar of Pergamon is a monumental Hellenistic altar constructed during the reign of the Attalid king Eumenes II in the first half of the 2nd century BC on the acropolis of Pergamon in Asia Minor. It is one of the most celebrated works of ancient Greek sculpture, famous above all for its colossal frieze depicting the Gigantomachy, the mythical battle between the Olympian gods and the Giants.

Description

The altar stood on a raised terrace south of the Temple of Athena. A broad podium, nearly square at about 36 by 34 metres, supported a colonnaded hall in the Ionic order that enclosed the actual sacrificial altar. A monumental staircase some 20 metres wide cut into the west front. Around the podium ran the Great Frieze, 113 metres long and 2.3 metres high, carved in dramatic high relief with more than a hundred larger-than-life figures locked in combat.

Inside the colonnade, a second, smaller frieze recounted the life of Telephus, the mythical founder of Pergamon.

Excavation and removal

The remains were excavated from 1878 by a German team led by Carl Humann. Under agreements with the Ottoman Empire, the frieze panels were shipped to Berlin, where the altar was reconstructed as the centrepiece of the Pergamon Museum, opened in 1930 on Museum Island. The reconstructed altar hall has been closed for renovation since 2014.

Interpretation

The Gigantomachy is generally read as an allegory of the Attalids' victories over the Galatian Celts and as a statement of Pergamene kingship and Greek civilisation triumphing over barbarism. The altar is sometimes cited in debates about the restitution of cultural property, though Turkey's formal claims have focused on other objects.
