The Battle of Asculum (or Ausculum) was fought in 279 BC between the Roman Republic and the combined forces of King Pyrrhus of Epirus, who had come to aid the Greek city of Tarentum, during the Pyrrhic War. Although Pyrrhus won the battle, his losses were so severe that the engagement gave rise to the term "Pyrrhic victory".

The battle

Fighting lasted two days near Asculum in Apulia. On the first day, rough terrain neutralized Pyrrhus's cavalry and elephants, and the armies disengaged evenly. On the second day Pyrrhus maneuvered the Romans onto open ground where his elephants and cavalry could operate; the Roman anti-elephant wagons proved ineffective, and the legions were driven back to their camp.

Casualties and the saying

Ancient sources report Roman losses of around 6,000 and Pyrrhus's losses of some 3,500 — including many irreplaceable officers and veteran troops. Plutarch reports that when congratulated on the victory, Pyrrhus replied that "another such victory and I shall be utterly undone" — the origin of the expression "Pyrrhic victory". Rome, by contrast, could replenish its armies from its Italian allies; Pyrrhus was defeated at Beneventum in 275 BC and withdrew from Italy.
