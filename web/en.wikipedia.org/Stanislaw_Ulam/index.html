Stanislaw Ulam

Stanislaw Ulam (1909–1984), Polish-American mathematician.

Stanisław Marcin Ulam (13 April 1909 – 13 May 1984) was a Polish-American mathematician and nuclear physicist. He participated in the Manhattan Project, originated the Teller–Ulam design of thermonuclear weapons, discovered the concept of the cellular automaton, invented the Monte Carlo method of computation, and suggested nuclear pulse propulsion.

Early life and career

Ulam was born in Lwów, then part of Austria-Hungary, into a wealthy Jewish family. He studied mathematics at the Lwów Polytechnic Institute, where he was part of the Lwów School of Mathematics, and emigrated to the United States in 1936. During World War II he joined the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos, where he worked on the hydrodynamic calculations of implosion and collaborated with Edward Teller on the hydrogen bomb design that bears both their names.

Contributions

Beyond weapons work, Ulam made fundamental contributions to set theory, measure theory, ergodic theory, topology, and number theory. The Ulam spiral, a graphical depiction of prime numbers that reveals striking patterns, was devised by him in 1963. With John von Neumann he pioneered cellular automata, and his 1976 autobiography Adventures of a Mathematician is a classic account of 20th-century mathematics.
