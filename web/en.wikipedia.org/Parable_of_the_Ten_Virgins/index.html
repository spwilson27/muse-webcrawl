Parable of the Ten Virgins

The Parable of the Ten Virgins, also known as the Parable of the Wise and Foolish Virgins, is one of the parables of Jesus, found in the Gospel of Matthew (25:1–13). It is an eschatological parable about being prepared for the day of reckoning.

The parable

Ten virgins take their lamps and go out to meet the bridegroom at a wedding feast. Five are wise and bring extra oil; five are foolish and bring none. The bridegroom is delayed, and all fall asleep. At midnight the cry goes out that he is coming. The foolish virgins, whose lamps are going out, ask the wise for oil but are told to go and buy their own; while they are away, the bridegroom arrives, the prepared virgins enter the feast, and the door is shut. When the others return, they are turned away with the words "I do not know you." The parable concludes: "Watch therefore, for you know neither the day nor the hour."

Interpretation

Traditionally the parable is read as an exhortation to readiness for the Second Coming or for personal death: the oil has been interpreted as faith, good works, or the Holy Spirit, and the closed door as the irreversibility of judgement. It forms part of Matthew's discourse on the end times (the Olivet Discourse). The theme was popular in medieval and Renaissance art — the wise and foolish virgins flank portals at cathedrals such as Strasbourg and Magdeburg — and in music and literature, including the medieval Sponsus play.
