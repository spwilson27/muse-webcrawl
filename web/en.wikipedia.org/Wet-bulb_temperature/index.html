Wet-bulb temperature

Wet-bulb temperature
Measured byPsychrometer (wet-bulb thermometer)
Depends onDry-bulb temperature, humidity, pressure
Key limit~35 °C (95 °F) theoretical human survivability threshold

The wet-bulb temperature is the lowest temperature that can be reached by evaporative cooling of a water-soaked surface under current ambient conditions. It is measured with a wet-bulb thermometer — a thermometer whose bulb is wrapped in a water-soaked cloth (a muslin wick) over which air is passed, as in a sling psychrometer. At 100% relative humidity, the wet-bulb temperature equals the dry-bulb (ordinary air) temperature; at lower humidity it is lower, and the difference (the wet-bulb depression) is used to determine humidity and dew point.

Applications

Wet-bulb temperature governs the effectiveness of evaporative cooling systems and is a key index of heat stress. Because humans cool themselves by sweating, a wet-bulb temperature of about 35 °C (95 °F) represents a theoretical upper limit for human survivability outdoors without air conditioning: above it, even a healthy, shaded, well-ventilated person cannot shed metabolic heat fast enough. Climate scientists study extreme wet-bulb events, which have already briefly approached 35 °C at a few locations around the Persian Gulf and South Asia, as a marker of dangerous humid heat under global warming.
