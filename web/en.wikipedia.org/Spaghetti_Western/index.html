Spaghetti Western

The archetypal lone gunfighter of the Spaghetti Western

The Spaghetti Western is a broad subgenre of Western films produced in Europe. It emerged in the mid-1960s in the wake of Sergio Leone's film-making style and international box-office success. The term was used by foreign (mostly American) critics because most of these Westerns were produced and directed by Italians.

Characteristics

Spaghetti Westerns are often characterised by morally ambiguous antiheroes, stark violence, minimalist dialogue, extreme close-ups, and distinctive scores — most famously by Ennio Morricone. Many were filmed in the Tabernas Desert in Almería, Spain, whose arid landscapes resembled the American Southwest and northern Mexico. Productions were frequently multinational, with international casts whose dialogue was dubbed in post-production.

Notable films and directors

Sergio Leone's "Dollars Trilogy" — A Fistful of Dollars (1964), For a Few Dollars More (1965) and The Good, the Bad and the Ugly (1966) — made Clint Eastwood a star as the Man with No Name. Leone's later Once Upon a Time in the West (1968) is widely regarded as a masterpiece of the genre. Other major directors and series include Sergio Corbucci (Django, 1966; The Great Silence, 1968), Duccio Tessari, and the popular Trinity films starring Terence Hill and Bud Spencer. Around 600 European Westerns were produced between 1960 and 1978.

Influence

The genre reshaped the Western worldwide, influencing filmmakers from Sam Peckinpah to Quentin Tarantino, and its iconography — the poncho-clad gunfighter, the climactic three-way standoff, Morricone's whistled themes — remains deeply embedded in popular culture.
