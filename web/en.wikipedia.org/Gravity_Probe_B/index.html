Gravity Probe B (GP-B) was a NASA satellite mission, developed with Stanford University, that measured two predictions of Einstein's general theory of relativity: the geodetic effect — the warping of space and time by Earth's mass — and frame-dragging, the dragging of spacetime by Earth's rotation. It remains one of the most precise physics experiments ever flown.

Experiment

The spacecraft carried four ultra-precise gyroscopes: fused-quartz spheres about the size of ping-pong balls, coated with niobium and spun up in a cryogenic dewar of liquid helium, making them among the most spherical objects ever manufactured. As the satellite orbited the poles at 642 km altitude, a telescope kept it locked on the guide star IM Pegasi, while SQUID magnetometers read out changes in the gyroscopes' spin axes to a precision of milliarcseconds per year. Einstein's theory predicted the spin axes would drift by 6,606 milliarcseconds per year in the orbital plane (geodetic effect) and 39.2 milliarcseconds per year in the equatorial plane (frame-dragging).

History and results

Proposed in the early 1960s by Leonard Schiff and others, the mission spent four decades in development, surviving repeated cancellation attempts before launch on 20 April 2004. Science data were collected for about a year. Unexpected electrostatic "patch effect" torques on the rotors complicated the analysis and required years of modelling; the final results, announced in 2011, measured the geodetic effect to about 0.3% and frame-dragging to about 19%, confirming general relativity's predictions. The mission cost roughly $760 million over its lifetime.
