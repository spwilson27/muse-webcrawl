Phèdre is a tragedy in five acts by the French dramatist Jean Racine, first performed on 1 January 1677 at the Hôtel de Bourgogne in Paris. Written in rhyming alexandrine couplets, it adapts the classical myth of Phaedra — treated earlier by Euripides in Hippolytus and by Seneca — and is widely regarded as Racine's masterpiece and the supreme achievement of French classical tragedy.

Plot

Phèdre, wife of King Theseus of Athens and stepmother of Hippolytus (Hippolyte), burns with a forbidden passion for her stepson, a passion she experiences as a divine curse inherited from her mother Pasiphaë. Believing Theseus dead, she confesses her love to Hippolytus, who recoils. When Theseus unexpectedly returns, Phèdre's nurse Oenone protects her mistress by accusing Hippolytus of having assaulted the queen. Theseus invokes Neptune's curse, and Hippolytus is killed by a sea monster as he flees. Tormented by guilt, Phèdre poisons herself and, dying, confesses the truth to Theseus.

Themes and style

The play is celebrated for the intensity with which it renders obsessive, destructive desire within a rigorously classical form. Racine observed the unities of time, place, and action, kept violence offstage (Hippolytus's death is related in the famous récit de Théramène), and compressed the catastrophe into a single day. Critics have long contrasted Racine's psychological refinement and economy with the rhetorical grandeur of his older rival Corneille.

Reception

A rival Phèdre et Hippolyte by Nicolas Pradon, staged days later with powerful aristocratic backing, briefly eclipsed Racine's play, prompting the disillusioned Racine to abandon the secular stage for twelve years. Posterity reversed the verdict: Voltaire called Racine's Phèdre "the masterpiece of the human mind", and the role of Phèdre became a touchstone for great actresses, from Rachel to Sarah Bernhardt. The myth has also inspired operas, including Rameau's Hippolyte et Aricie, and modern reworkings such as Sarah Kane's Phaedra's Love.
