NASA's series of four Great Observatories were space telescopes designed to examine specific wavelength or energy regions of the electromagnetic spectrum, using very different technologies but sharing complementary observing capabilities. The four spacecraft were the Hubble Space Telescope, the Compton Gamma Ray Observatory, the Chandra X-ray Observatory and the Spitzer Space Telescope. Together, they covered the spectrum from gamma rays to the far infrared, and their multiple-wavelength approach greatly expanded astronomers' ability to study phenomena such as star formation, black holes and the early universe.

Hubble Space Telescope

Hubble observes in the visible, ultraviolet and near-infrared. Launched aboard Discovery (STS-31), it was famously found to have a flawed primary mirror, corrected by the COSTAR optics installed on the first servicing mission in 1993. Five Space Shuttle servicing missions upgraded its instruments; it has measured the expansion rate of the universe, imaged deep fields containing thousands of galaxies, and studied exoplanet atmospheres.

Compton Gamma Ray Observatory

Compton, named for physicist Arthur Holly Compton, carried four instruments that mapped the gamma-ray sky, discovered gamma-ray bursts' isotropic distribution (suggesting a cosmological origin), and observed blazars and pulsars. After a gyroscope failure, NASA conducted a controlled re-entry in 2000 to eliminate the risk of an uncontrolled debris fall.

Chandra X-ray Observatory

Chandra, named for astrophysicist Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar, has the sharpest X-ray optics ever flown. It has imaged supernova remnants, resolved X-ray sources in the galactic centre, provided evidence for dark matter in the Bullet Cluster, and studied the hot gas in galaxy clusters.

Spitzer Space Telescope

Spitzer observed the infrared sky from an Earth-trailing heliocentric orbit, allowing its telescope to cool passively. It peered through dust to study star-forming regions, discovered some of the most distant galaxies, and characterised exoplanets — including contributions to the study of the TRAPPIST-1 system. Its cryogenic mission ended in 2009 when its liquid helium ran out; it continued in a "warm" mission until its retirement in 2020.

Legacy

Simultaneous observations from several Great Observatories — for example the multi-wavelength imaging of the Crab Nebula and of supernova 1987A — demonstrated the value of panchromatic astronomy, a philosophy carried forward by newer facilities such as the James Webb Space Telescope, which is sometimes described as a successor to Hubble and Spitzer though not formally part of the program.
