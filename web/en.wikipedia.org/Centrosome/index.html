Centrosome

In cell biology, the centrosome is an organelle that serves as the main microtubule organizing center (MTOC) of the animal cell, as well as a regulator of cell-cycle progression. The centrosome provides structure for the cell. The centrosome is thought to have evolved only in the metazoan lineage of eukaryotic cells. Fungi and plants lack centrosomes and therefore use other structures to organize their microtubules. Although the centrosome has a key role in efficient mitosis in animal cells, it is not essential in certain fly and flatworm species.

Structure

The centrosome consists of two orthogonally arranged centrioles — barrel-shaped structures about 500 nm long built from nine triplet microtubules — surrounded by an amorphous mass of proteins termed the pericentriolar material (PCM). The PCM contains γ-tubulin ring complexes that nucleate microtubules, together with proteins such as pericentrin, ninein and Cep192. The two centrioles differ: the older "mother" centriole bears distal and subdistal appendages that can anchor microtubules and, in many cells, template the primary cilium as a basal body.

Centrosome cycle

Centrosomes duplicate once per cell cycle, in coordination with DNA replication. In G1 a cell has one centrosome; during S phase a new procentriole grows orthogonally from each existing centriole under the control of the kinase PLK4 and proteins including SAS-6 and STIL. In G2 the two centrosomes mature, recruiting additional PCM, and at mitosis they separate — driven by the kinesin Eg5 and the kinase Nek2 dissolving the linker between them — to form the two poles of the mitotic spindle.

Functions

By nucleating and anchoring microtubules, the centrosome organises the interphase cytoskeleton, influences cell polarity and migration, and positions the nucleus and other organelles. At division it defines spindle poles and helps ensure accurate chromosome segregation. In differentiated cells its roles vary: neurons and muscle cells down-regulate centrosomal nucleation, transferring MTOC function elsewhere.

Centrosomes and disease

Extra (amplified) centrosomes are common in cancer cells and can promote chromosome instability through transient multipolar spindles. Mutations in centrosomal proteins cause human developmental disorders, notably primary microcephaly and primordial dwarfism syndromes, reflecting roles in neural progenitor division. Theodor Boveri proposed in 1914 that centrosome abnormalities might cause cancer — an idea that modern work has partly revived.
