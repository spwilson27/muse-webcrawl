Sun Microsystems

Sun Microsystems, Inc. was an American technology company that sold computers, computer components, software, and information technology services. Founded in 1982, Sun created the Java programming language, the Solaris operating system, and the SPARC processor architecture, and was a major vendor of Unix workstations and servers. It was acquired by Oracle Corporation in 2010 for about US$7.4 billion.

History

Sun's name derived from the Stanford University Network (SUN), whose workstation design by Andy Bechtolsheim became the company's first product. With Bill Joy's work on BSD Unix, Sun workstations running SunOS became dominant in engineering, academia and, later, internet infrastructure. The company's motto, "The Network Is the Computer", anticipated network-centric computing. In 1995 Sun launched Java, whose "write once, run anywhere" model made it one of the world's most widely used programming platforms.

Peak and decline

During the dot-com boom Sun's servers powered much of the growing web, and its market capitalisation briefly exceeded $200 billion. The bust of 2001 hit Sun especially hard: customers failed, cheap x86 servers running Linux eroded its proprietary hardware business, and the company never durably returned to profitability. Under CEO Jonathan Schwartz (from 2006), Sun open-sourced Solaris and Java and acquired MySQL AB in 2008, but revenue continued to decline.

Acquisition by Oracle

Oracle announced its acquisition of Sun in April 2009 and completed it on January 27, 2010, after regulatory review, particularly by the European Commission over the future of MySQL. Oracle continued Solaris, SPARC and Java development, while many Sun staff and products were gradually wound down over the following decade.

Legacy

Sun's technologies remain widely used: Java is among the most popular programming languages, MySQL is a leading open-source database, and the ZFS filesystem, VirtualBox, and the SPARC architecture all originated at Sun. The company is also remembered for an engineering-driven culture that produced influential figures in computing.
