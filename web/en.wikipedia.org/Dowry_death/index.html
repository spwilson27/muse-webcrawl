Dowry death

A protest against dowry violence in India.

Dowry deaths are deaths of married women who are murdered or driven to suicide over disputes about dowry—payments made by the bride's family to the groom's family—most commonly reported in India, Pakistan, Bangladesh and Iran. A frequent form is "bride burning", in which a woman is set on fire, often staged as a kitchen accident.

Legal response

In India, dowry has been prohibited since the Dowry Prohibition Act of 1961. The Indian Penal Code was amended in 1986 to create a specific offence of "dowry death" (Section 304B), with a related presumption in evidence law where a woman dies under unnatural circumstances within seven years of marriage after dowry-related cruelty. Section 498A addresses cruelty by a husband or his relatives.

Statistics and criticism

India's National Crime Records Bureau has reported several thousand dowry deaths annually—around 6,000–7,000 in recent years—though activists argue the true figure is higher due to under-reporting. The laws have also drawn debate over alleged misuse, alongside persistent calls for stronger enforcement and social reform.
