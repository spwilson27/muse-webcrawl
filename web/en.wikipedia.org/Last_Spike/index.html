Last Spike

A last spike is the final spike driven into the construction of a railway line, traditionally marking the completion of the track and often celebrated with a formal ceremony. The best-known examples are the golden spike driven in the United States in 1869 to complete the first transcontinental railroad, and the last spike of the Canadian Pacific Railway, driven at Craigellachie, British Columbia, in 1885. In many cases the ceremonial spike is made of a precious metal, is engraved, and is removed after the ceremony and preserved in a museum; a regular iron spike is then driven in its place.

Golden spike (United States)

On 10 May 1869, at Promontory Summit, Utah Territory, the Central Pacific and Union Pacific railroads were joined, completing the First Transcontinental Railroad. Central Pacific president Leland Stanford drove a 17.6-karat gold spike — the "Golden Spike", also called the Last Spike — with a silver hammer. The spike, supplied by San Francisco industrialist David Hewes, was engraved with the names of railroad officers and the prayer "May God continue the unity of our Country as this Railroad unites the two great Oceans of the world." It is now held by the Cantor Arts Center at Stanford University. The event was one of the first national media events in the United States, the hammer blows being transmitted live by telegraph.

Canadian Pacific Railway

The last spike of the Canadian Pacific Railway was driven on the morning of 7 November 1885 at Craigellachie in Eagle Pass, British Columbia, by company director Donald A. Smith (later Lord Strathcona). Unlike the golden spike, the Canadian spike was an ordinary iron spike — reportedly, a first, inferior spike bent when struck and was replaced. Its driving completed Canada's coast-to-coast railway ahead of the five-year deadline set when British Columbia joined Confederation. Historian Pierre Berton's best-selling books The National Dream (1970) and The Last Spike (1971) fixed the event in Canadian national mythology.

Other last spikes

Last-spike ceremonies have marked many other lines: a silver spike for the Northern Pacific Railway at Gold Creek, Montana (1883), driven by former U.S. President Ulysses S. Grant; the completion of the Great Northern Railway (1893); and numerous regional and narrow-gauge lines, where golden, silver or aluminium spikes were presented to local dignitaries. The ceremony remains a standard feature of new railway and light-rail openings.
