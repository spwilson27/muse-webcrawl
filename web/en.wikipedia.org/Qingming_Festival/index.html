Qingming Festival

The Qingming Festival (Chinese: 清明節; pinyin: Qīngmíng Jié), also known as Tomb-Sweeping Day, is a traditional Chinese festival observed on the first day of the fifth solar term of the Chinese calendar, which falls on 4, 5, or 6 April (usually 4 or 5 April). It is a public holiday in mainland China, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and Macau, and is also observed by Chinese diaspora communities and, in related forms such as Hansik, in Korea.

Observances

The central rite is tomb sweeping: families visit ancestral graves to clean the site, remove weeds, repair inscriptions, and make offerings of food, tea, wine, and joss paper, burning incense and bowing in remembrance. The festival expresses Confucian filial piety and the continuing bond between the living and their ancestors. In recent years, online memorial services and "cloud tomb-sweeping" have emerged, and authorities promote eco-friendly offerings in place of burning.

Origins

Qingming originated in the Cold Food Festival (Hanshi), associated with the legend of Jie Zitui, a loyal retainer of the seventh-century BCE duke Wen of Jin, who starved in hiding after being forgotten for reward; the duke's remorseful ban on fires near Jie's death anniversary merged over time with the spring tomb rites of Qingming. The festival was institutionalized under the Tang dynasty, when Emperor Xuanzong promoted ancestor worship at graves in 732 CE.

Spring customs and culture

Beyond mourning, Qingming celebrates the arrival of spring: families take outings (taqing, "treading the green"), fly kites, plant trees, and play games such as tug of war and cuju (ancient football). Traditional foods include qingtuan, green rice balls colored with mugwort or barley grass, eaten especially in the Jiangnan region. The festival is immortalized in the Song dynasty handscroll Along the River During the Qingming Festival by Zhang Zeduan, one of China's most famous paintings, depicting festival-time life in the capital Kaifeng.
