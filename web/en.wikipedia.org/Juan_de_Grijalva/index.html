Juan de Grijalva (c. 1490 – 1527) was a Spanish conquistador and explorer, a nephew of Diego Velázquez de Cuéllar, the governor of Cuba. He is best known for leading the 1518 expedition that explored the Gulf coast of Mexico, the first European voyage to chart much of that coastline and to make contact with the Aztec (Mexica) world.

Early career

Grijalva arrived in the Americas in the early 1510s and took part in the conquest and settlement of Cuba under Velázquez. After Francisco Hernández de Córdoba's 1517 voyage had reported lands to the west, Velázquez commissioned his nephew to lead a follow-up expedition of four ships and some 240 men, which sailed from Cuba in April 1518.

The 1518 expedition

Grijalva's fleet reached the island of Cozumel, followed the Yucatán coast, and continued into the Gulf of Mexico, naming places such as Isla de Sacrificios and San Juan de Ulúa. At the mouth of a river in Tabasco — still called the Grijalva River — his men traded with local Maya people and received gold objects, the first strong evidence of the wealthy civilizations inland. Grijalva did not found a settlement, following his instructions to explore and trade, and returned to Cuba in late 1518. His reports of gold helped persuade Velázquez to mount the much larger expedition of Hernán Cortés the following year.

Later life and death

Grijalva later served in expeditions to the Honduran and Nicaraguan coast and was killed in 1527 during fighting with Indigenous peoples in Honduras, reportedly at Olancho.
