Weight distribution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Weight distribution is the apportioning of weight within a vehicle or structure, usually expressed as the proportion of total weight carried by each axle, wheel, or supporting region. In automobiles it is commonly given as a front-to-rear percentage (for example 60/40), and it strongly affects handling, traction, braking and stability. The concept applies equally to aircraft, ships, trains and buildings, wherever the position of the centre of mass relative to supports determines balance and performance.

In automobiles

A car's static weight distribution is set by the placement of heavy components — engine, transmission, battery and fuel — and by its layout: front-engine, front-wheel-drive cars are typically nose-heavy, while mid-engine sports cars approach an ideal near 50/50 balance. Distribution changes dynamically under acceleration, braking and cornering as load transfers between wheels, influencing understeer and oversteer. In motorsport, ballast is positioned deliberately, and series such as Formula One regulate permissible distribution ranges.

In aviation and maritime use

In aircraft, weight and balance calculations ensure the centre of gravity stays within certified limits for safe flight; loading charts and weight and balance documents are part of routine dispatch. On ships, the distribution of cargo and ballast determines stability, trim and stress on the hull, and is managed through loading computers and ballast tanks.
