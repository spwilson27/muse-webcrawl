Paraphyly

Paraphyly is a characterisation of a group of organisms in biological taxonomy. A group is paraphyletic if it consists of the group's last common ancestor and most of its descendants, but excludes one or more subgroups (clades) that are also descended from that ancestor.

Examples

The classic example is "reptiles": as traditionally defined, Reptilia includes lizards, snakes, turtles, and crocodilians but excludes birds, even though birds are descended from dinosaur reptiles. Similarly, "fish" is paraphyletic because tetrapods (four-limbed vertebrates, including humans) descend from lobe-finned fish but are not considered fish. Prokaryotes are paraphyletic because eukaryotes arose from within them.

Cladistic view

Under cladistic classification, only monophyletic groups — a common ancestor and all of its descendants — are recognised as valid taxa. Paraphyletic and polyphyletic groupings are therefore redefined or abandoned, although some taxonomists defend the use of paraphyletic groups as informative evolutionary grades.
