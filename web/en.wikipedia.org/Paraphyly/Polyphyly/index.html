Polyphyly

A polyphyletic group is a set of organisms grouped by a characteristic that does not imply they share an exclusive common ancestor; the group's most recent common ancestor is not itself a member of the group. Polyphyletic groupings usually arise from convergent evolution, where similar traits evolve independently in unrelated lineages.

Examples

Classic examples include "warm-blooded animals" (mammals and birds, whose endothermy evolved separately), "flying animals" (insects, pterosaurs, birds, and bats), and marine mammals grouped loosely by habitat. The old grouping of algae united unrelated photosynthetic organisms from several kingdoms.

Avoidance in taxonomy

Cladistic classification rejects polyphyletic taxa: a valid taxon should be monophyletic, comprising an ancestor and all its descendants. Polyphyly differs from paraphyly, in which a group contains an ancestor and some but not all descendants; polyphyletic groups do not even include the common ancestor of their members as one of their own.
