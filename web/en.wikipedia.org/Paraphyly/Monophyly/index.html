Monophyly

In biological cladistics, a group of organisms is monophyletic if it consists of a common ancestor and all of its descendants. A monophyletic group is the same as a clade, and monophyly is the property that modern phylogenetic taxonomy generally requires of recognized taxa.

Definitions

The term comes from the Greek monos, "alone", and phylon, "tribe". Usage has shifted historically: Ernst Haeckel used "monophyly" for descent from a single ancestral species, while Willi Hennig restricted the modern sense to groups containing all descendants of an ancestor. On a phylogenetic tree, a monophyletic group can be separated by cutting a single branch.

Contrasts

A paraphyletic group contains an ancestor and some, but not all, of its descendants (for example, reptiles without birds). A polyphyletic group is assembled from organisms of separate origins and does not include their most recent common ancestor (for example, "warm-blooded animals" grouping mammals and birds).

Testing monophyly

Monophyly is inferred from synapomorphies — shared derived characters — and is tested with morphological and molecular data; hypotheses of monophyly are revised as new evidence appears.
