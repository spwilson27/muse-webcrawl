Cladistics

Cladistics is an approach to biological classification in which organisms are grouped into clades based on shared derived characteristics (synapomorphies) inherited from their most recent common ancestor. Developed from the work of German entomologist Willi Hennig in the 1950s, it is also called phylogenetic systematics.

Methods

Cladistic analysis assembles a data matrix of characters for the taxa studied and infers the branching diagram — a cladogram — that best explains the distribution of shared derived traits, often using the principle of parsimony. Ancestral traits (plesiomorphies) cannot define groups, because they are shared with more distant relatives; only innovations that arose in a group's own stem lineage are evidence of common descent.

Terminology

Key concepts include the clade, monophyly, paraphyly (a group that excludes some descendants of its ancestor), and polyphyly (a group whose members descend from multiple ancestors and lack an exclusive common ancestor).

Impact

Cladistics transformed taxonomy in the late 20th century, and with molecular sequence data it underlies modern phylogenetics, reshaping classification across the tree of life — for example, placing birds firmly within the dinosaur clade.
