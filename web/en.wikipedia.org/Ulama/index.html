In Islam, the ulama (Arabic: علماء, romanized: ʿulamāʾ) are the guardians, transmitters, and interpreters of religious knowledge in Islam, including Islamic doctrine and law. Ulama are educated in religious schools called madrasas and in universities.

Role and authority

Historically, the ulama managed the religious and legal life of Muslim communities: they issued legal opinions (fatwas), served as judges (qadis), taught in mosques and madrasas, and advised rulers. Their authority derives from mastery of the Quran, the hadith (sayings of Muhammad), and the discipline of jurisprudence (fiqh) as developed in the four Sunni schools and the Ja'fari school of Shia Islam. In Shia Islam, the ulama form a more hierarchical clerical structure, culminating in Grand Ayatollahs who act as sources of emulation (marja' al-taqlid).

Institutional history

The ulama emerged as a distinct class in the 9th–10th centuries AD as Islamic law was systematized. In the Ottoman Empire, the ulama were integrated into a state hierarchy headed by the Sheikh al-Islam, and the ilmiye class staffed the judiciary and education system. In the 19th and 20th centuries, secular reforms, state control of religious endowments, and mass literacy challenged the ulama's traditional monopoly on religious interpretation, a tension still visible in contemporary Muslim politics.

Education

A traditional alim's education includes Arabic grammar and rhetoric, Quranic exegesis (tafsir), hadith studies, legal theory (usul al-fiqh), and theology (kalam). Students graduate with an ijazah (licence) from a master, linking them to a chain of transmission (isnad) of learning.
