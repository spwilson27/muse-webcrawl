Computer algebra

Computer algebra, also called symbolic computation or algebraic computation, is a scientific area that refers to the study and development of algorithms and software for manipulating mathematical expressions and other mathematical objects. Although computer algebra could be considered a subfield of scientific computing, they are generally considered distinct fields because scientific computing usually relies on numerical computation with approximate floating-point numbers, while symbolic computation emphasises exact computation with expressions containing variables that have no given value and are manipulated as symbols.

Overview

A software application that performs symbolic calculations is called a computer algebra system (CAS). Such systems can simplify expressions, factor polynomials, compute derivatives and integrals symbolically, solve equations exactly, manipulate matrices with symbolic entries, and perform exact integer and rational arithmetic of arbitrary precision. Well-known general-purpose systems include Mathematica, Maple, SageMath, Maxima, and the SymPy library for Python; specialised systems such as GAP focus on group theory, and PARI/GP on number theory.

The field's roots lie in the 1960s, with early programs such as Schoonschip (developed by Martinus Veltman for particle physics calculations), Macsyma at MIT, and REDUCE. Work on the algorithmic side produced some of the central algorithms of modern computer science, including polynomial greatest-common-divisor algorithms, the Buchberger algorithm for Gröbner bases (1965), the Risch algorithm for indefinite integration, and fast algorithms for integer and polynomial arithmetic based on the fast Fourier transform.

Mathematical aspects

Central problems in computer algebra include:

  • Representation. Mathematical objects must be encoded efficiently: polynomials may be stored densely or sparsely, and expressions are usually held as trees in internal memory.
  • Simplification. Unlike numerical evaluation, symbolic expressions admit many equivalent forms; systems must choose canonical or normal forms so that equality can be tested and results presented readably.
  • Polynomial algebra. Algorithms for factorisation, gcd computation, resultants, and solving systems of polynomial equations via Gröbner bases underpin much of the field.
  • Symbolic integration and summation. The Risch algorithm determines whether an elementary function has an elementary antiderivative and computes it; Gosper's algorithm and Zeilberger's algorithm perform analogous tasks for hypergeometric sums.
  • Exact linear algebra. Matrices over integers, rationals, and polynomial rings are manipulated without rounding error, using modular and fraction-free methods to control expression growth.

Expression swell and complexity

A persistent challenge in computer algebra is intermediate expression swell: during a computation, intermediate results can become vastly larger than either the input or the final answer. Techniques such as modular arithmetic with Chinese remainder reconstruction, evaluation–interpolation schemes, and careful algorithm choice are used to control this growth. Many natural problems in the field are computationally hard — Gröbner basis computation can be doubly exponential in the worst case — yet the algorithms perform well on most practical inputs.

Applications

Computer algebra is used throughout mathematics, physics, engineering, and computer science: in general relativity and high-energy physics for tensor manipulation, in cryptography and coding theory, in robotics and control theory for solving polynomial systems, in automated theorem proving, and increasingly in education, where CAS tools are built into calculators and courseware. Hybrid symbolic–numeric methods, which combine exact and approximate computation, are an active area of research.
