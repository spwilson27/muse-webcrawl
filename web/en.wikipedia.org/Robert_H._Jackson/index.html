Robert Houghwout Jackson (February 13, 1892 – October 9, 1954) was an American lawyer, jurist, and politician who served as an associate justice of the Supreme Court of the United States from 1941 until his death in 1954. He had previously served as United States Solicitor General (1938–1940) and United States Attorney General (1940–1941) under President Franklin D. Roosevelt.

Jackson is best known internationally as the chief United States prosecutor at the Nuremberg trials of Nazi war criminals following World War II. He negotiated the London Charter of the International Military Tribunal and delivered a celebrated opening statement on 21 November 1945, declaring that the trial represented "one of the most significant tributes that Power has ever paid to Reason".

Supreme Court career

Raised in western New York, Jackson never completed a university degree; he read law and attended Albany Law School for a year without graduating, an unusual path for a Supreme Court justice — he was the last justice appointed who had not graduated from law school. On the Court he was known for his eloquent and accessible opinions. His concurrence in Youngstown Sheet & Tube Co. v. Sawyer (1952), setting out a three-part framework for analysing presidential power, became one of the most influential opinions in American constitutional law. He also wrote the majority opinion in West Virginia State Board of Education v. Barnette (1943), protecting the free-speech right of schoolchildren not to salute the flag.

Death and legacy

Jackson died of a heart attack in 1954, shortly after taking part in the unanimous decision in Brown v. Board of Education. The Robert H. Jackson Center in Jamestown, New York preserves his papers and promotes his legacy in law and international justice.
