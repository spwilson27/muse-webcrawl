Civil service reform

Civil service reform refers to movements and legislation intended to make government bureaucracies more professional, efficient, and merit-based, typically by replacing patronage appointments (the "spoils system") with competitive examinations, tenure protections, and rules against political coercion of public employees.

United States

In the United States, the assassination of President James A. Garfield in 1881 by a disappointed office-seeker spurred passage of the Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act of 1883, which created the Civil Service Commission and required that a growing share of federal jobs be filled by competitive examination. Later measures included the Hatch Act of 1939, restricting political activity by federal employees, and the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978, which replaced the Commission with the Office of Personnel Management and the Merit Systems Protection Board and created the Senior Executive Service.

United Kingdom

In Britain, the Northcote–Trevelyan Report of 1854 recommended recruitment by open competition and promotion by merit; the Civil Service Commission was established in 1855 and open examinations generalised by Order in Council in 1870, laying the foundations of the modern impartial civil service.

Themes

Reform programmes typically address merit recruitment, political neutrality, pay comparability with the private sector, performance management, and protection for whistleblowers. Debate continues over the balance between responsiveness to elected governments and administrative independence.
