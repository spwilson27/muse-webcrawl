Block caving

Block caving is an underground hard-rock mining method in which a large block of ore is undercut so that it collapses under its own weight and gravity-induced stresses. The broken ore is then drawn off through a series of funnels and access tunnels beneath the collapsing block. It is one of the lowest-cost underground mining methods per tonne, suited to large, low-grade ore bodies that are too deep for open-pit mining.

Method

Development begins with a network of tunnels on a production level below the ore block. An undercut level is driven above it and blasted out, removing support from the base of the block. Stressed rock above the undercut fractures and caves progressively upward, while broken ore flows by gravity to drawpoints where it is mucked out by loaders and hauled to an ore pass.

Advantages and challenges

Because the ore breaks largely by itself, drilling and blasting costs are low and production rates can rival open-pit mines. The challenges include high upfront development costs and long lead times before production, unpredictable cave propagation, surface subsidence above the cave, dilution of ore with waste rock, and the risk of damaging hang-ups and air blasts.

Examples

Notable block cave operations include the Grasberg mine in Indonesia, Oyu Tolgoi in Mongolia, Cadia East in Australia, and the Northparkes and El Teniente mines.
