The Kʼicheʼ (also spelled Quiché; in their own language Kʼicheʼ, "many trees") are a Maya people of Guatemala and the speakers of the Kʼicheʼ language, the most widely spoken indigenous language in the country, with well over a million speakers. They live principally in the western highlands of Guatemala, in the departments of El Quiché, Totonicapán, Quetzaltenango, and Sololá.

History

Before the Spanish conquest, the Kʼicheʼ ruled a powerful highland kingdom centred on the city of Qʼumarkaj (Utatlán), which expanded dramatically during the 15th century under the king Kʼicheʼ Qʼuqʼumatz and his successors. The kingdom was conquered in 1524 by Pedro de Alvarado, whose forces defeated the Kʼicheʼ army led by Tecun Uman near Quetzaltenango; Tecun Uman is now celebrated as a national hero of Guatemala. The ruined capital of Qʼumarkaj lies near the modern city of Santa Cruz del Quiché.

Culture and literature

The Kʼicheʼ are the authors of the Popol Vuh, the most famous surviving work of Maya literature, which recounts the creation of the world, the deeds of the hero twins Hunahpu and Xbalanque, and the genealogy of the Kʼicheʼ kings. Another important document is the Título de Totonicapán, a colonial-era land title written in Kʼicheʼ. Traditional Kʼicheʼ culture retains strong elements of Maya cosmology and calendar practice, often interwoven with Roman Catholic observance in a syncretic folk religion, and traditional woven textiles with community-specific designs remain an important marker of identity.

Modern era

During the Guatemalan Civil War (1960–1996), Kʼicheʼ communities suffered severely from counterinsurgency campaigns in El Quiché department. The Kʼicheʼ activist Rigoberta Menchú, winner of the 1992 Nobel Peace Prize, brought international attention to the plight of Guatemala's indigenous peoples. Today the Kʼicheʼ remain the largest Maya group in Guatemala, and their language is taught in bilingual education programmes and broadcast on radio in the highlands.
