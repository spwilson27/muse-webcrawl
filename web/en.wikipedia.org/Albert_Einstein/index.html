Albert Einstein (14 March 1879 – 18 April 1955) was a German-born theoretical physicist who is widely regarded as one of the greatest and most influential scientists of all time. He developed the theory of relativity, one of the two pillars of modern physics, and his mass–energy equivalence formula E = mc² is frequently called "the world's most famous equation". He received the 1921 Nobel Prize in Physics for his explanation of the photoelectric effect.

Miracle year and relativity

After graduating from the Zurich Polytechnic in 1900, Einstein worked as a patent clerk in Bern. In 1905, his annus mirabilis, he published four groundbreaking papers: on the photoelectric effect (introducing light quanta), Brownian motion (providing empirical evidence for atoms), special relativity (reconciling mechanics with electromagnetism), and mass–energy equivalence. In 1915 he completed the general theory of relativity, which describes gravity as the curvature of spacetime; its prediction of the bending of starlight was dramatically confirmed by Eddington's observations of the 1919 solar eclipse, making Einstein world-famous.

Later life

Following academic posts in Zurich, Prague, and Berlin, Einstein emigrated to the United States in 1933 as the Nazis came to power, taking a position at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton. In 1939 he signed a letter to President Franklin D. Roosevelt warning that Nazi Germany might develop atomic weapons, urging American research; he later regretted the letter's role in sparking the Manhattan Project and campaigned for nuclear disarmament and world government. In his final decades he pursued an unsuccessful unified field theory and famously debated the completeness of quantum mechanics, co-authoring the EPR paradox paper in 1935.

Legacy

Einstein's work underlies technologies from lasers and GPS to nuclear energy, and his name has become synonymous with genius. The centenary of his miracle year was celebrated as the World Year of Physics in 2005, and predictions of general relativity such as gravitational waves — detected directly in 2015 — continue to be confirmed.
