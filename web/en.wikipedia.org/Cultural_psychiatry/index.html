Cultural psychiatry

Cultural psychiatry (also known as cross-cultural psychiatry or transcultural psychiatry) is a branch of psychiatry concerned with the cultural context of mental disorders and the challenges of providing psychiatric services to culturally diverse populations. It studies how social and cultural factors influence the onset, expression, course, and treatment of mental illness.

Scope

Cross-cultural research has shown that while many psychiatric disorders occur worldwide, their symptoms and idioms of distress vary by culture. Certain syndromes, historically called culture-bound syndromes — such as amok in Southeast Asia, koro in China and Southeast Asia, and susto in Latin America — appear closely tied to particular cultural contexts. The DSM-5 replaced the older notion of culture-bound syndromes with "cultural concepts of distress" and introduced the Cultural Formulation Interview to help clinicians assess patients' cultural backgrounds.

History

The field has roots in early twentieth-century comparisons of mental illness across societies, and in Emil Kraepelin's travels to Java in 1904, often regarded as the founding moment of comparative psychiatry. Anthropologists such as Ruth Benedict and Margaret Mead contributed to understanding how culture shapes personality and psychopathology. The World Health Organization's International Pilot Study of Schizophrenia (1973) and follow-up studies found, controversially, that schizophrenia outcomes appeared better in developing countries, prompting extensive debate about cultural influences on illness course.

Clinical practice

Cultural psychiatry emphasizes cultural competence: clinicians should elicit patients' explanatory models of illness, work with interpreters and cultural mediators, and recognize their own cultural assumptions. It also addresses disparities in diagnosis — for example, the overdiagnosis of schizophrenia and underdiagnosis of mood disorders in some minority populations.
