Metaethics

Metaethics is the branch of ethics and analytic philosophy that seeks to understand the nature of ethical properties, statements, attitudes and judgments. Whereas normative ethics asks what one ought to do, metaethics asks what it means to say that something is right or wrong, whether moral claims can be true or false, and how moral knowledge is possible.

Central questions

Metaethical inquiry is often organised around four sets of questions: semantic questions about the meaning of moral terms; metaphysical questions about whether moral facts exist and what kind of entities they are; epistemological questions about how moral truths could be known; and psychological questions about the relationship between moral judgment and motivation.

Major positions

Moral realism holds that there are objective moral facts. Cognitivism holds that moral utterances express beliefs and can be true or false; it is opposed by non-cognitivist theories such as emotivism and expressivism, which treat moral utterances as expressions of attitude or prescriptions. Error theory, associated with J. L. Mackie, holds that moral claims purport to state facts but are systematically false because there are no moral facts. Other influential views include ethical naturalism, non-naturalism as defended by G. E. Moore, and various forms of constructivism and relativism.

History

The discipline coalesced in the early twentieth century around Moore's Principia Ethica (1903) and its notion of the naturalistic fallacy, and was shaped by A. J. Ayer, C. L. Stevenson, R. M. Hare and later philosophers such as Bernard Williams, John McDowell and Derek Parfit.
