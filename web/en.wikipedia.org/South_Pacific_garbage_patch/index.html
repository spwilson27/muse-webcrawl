South Pacific garbage patch

The South Pacific garbage patch is an area of marine debris in the South Pacific Ocean, concentrated within the South Pacific Gyre. It is one of five major offshore plastic accumulation zones created by converging ocean currents. Unlike a solid island of trash, the patch consists largely of dispersed microplastics — tiny fragments suspended in the upper water column — making it difficult to detect visually.

The patch was confirmed by research expeditions in the 2010s led by Charles J. Moore and the Algalita Marine Research Foundation, which had earlier documented the better-known Great Pacific Garbage Patch in the North Pacific. A 2017 study estimated the South Pacific accumulation zone covers roughly 2.6 million square kilometres, with sampled microplastic concentrations increasing sharply toward the gyre's center.

Composition and effects

The debris consists mainly of polyethylene and polypropylene fragments from consumer plastics, fishing gear, and shipping. Microplastics enter marine food webs when ingested by zooplankton, fish, and seabirds, and remote islands at the gyre's edge — such as Henderson Island, part of the Pitcairn group — have recorded some of the highest densities of beached plastic debris in the world.
