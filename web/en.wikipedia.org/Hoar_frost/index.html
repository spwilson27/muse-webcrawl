Hoar frost

Hoar frost is a type of frost consisting of delicate, white ice crystals formed by the direct deposition of water vapor onto surfaces that have cooled below freezing, typically on clear, calm nights. The word "hoar" derives from an Old English term meaning showing the whiteness of age, referring to the frost's resemblance to an old man's white beard.

Formation

Hoar frost forms when a surface loses heat by radiation to a clear night sky and falls below both the frost point of the adjacent air and 0 °C. Water vapor then deposits directly as ice (skipping the liquid phase), growing feathery or needle-like crystals on grass, fences, trees, and other exposed objects. It is most common in low-lying areas where cold air pools, creating frost hollows.

Related phenomena

Hoar frost differs from rime ice, which forms when supercooled fog droplets freeze on contact, and from ground frost, a general term for frost on the ground. Surface hoar on snowpacks is significant in avalanche science: buried layers of fragile surface-hoar crystals can form persistent weak layers that later fail under load. In permafrost regions, deep "depth hoar" develops near the base of the snowpack through temperature-gradient metamorphism.
