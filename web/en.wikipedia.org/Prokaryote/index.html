A prokaryote (/proʊˈkærioʊt, -ət/) is a single-celled organism that lacks a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles. The word prokaryote comes from the Ancient Greek πρό (pró), meaning "before", and κάρυον (káruon), meaning "nut" or "kernel"—a reference to the absence of a defined cell nucleus. Prokaryotes are divided into two domains, the Bacteria and the Archaea.

In the two-empire system arising from the work of Édouard Chatton, prokaryotes were classified within the empire Prokaryota. In the three-domain system of biological classification introduced by Carl Woese and colleagues in 1977, the two prokaryotic domains are treated as separate major lineages alongside the Eukaryota.

Structure

The components of prokaryotic cells are all located within the cytoplasm, which is surrounded by a cell membrane and typically a cell wall. The genetic material consists of a single (usually circular) molecule of DNA in a region called the nucleoid, which is not separated from the rest of the cell by a membrane. Many prokaryotes also carry small circular DNA molecules called plasmids, which can confer traits such as antibiotic resistance and are exchanged between cells by horizontal gene transfer.

Ribosomes in prokaryotes are 70S in size, smaller than the 80S ribosomes of eukaryotes. Many prokaryotes have appendages such as flagella for motility, pili for attachment and conjugation, and a protective capsule or slime layer. Some bacteria form dormant endospores that can survive extreme conditions.

Reproduction and diversity

Prokaryotes reproduce asexually, mainly by binary fission, in which one cell divides into two genetically identical cells. Genetic variation arises through mutation and through horizontal gene transfer mechanisms: transformation (uptake of free DNA), transduction (transfer by viruses), and conjugation (transfer through a pilus).

Prokaryotes are found in almost every environment on Earth, from deep-sea hydrothermal vents and subglacial lakes to the human gut. They play essential roles in biogeochemical cycles such as nitrogen fixation and carbon cycling, and extremophile archaea thrive in environments of extreme heat, salinity, or acidity. Cyanobacteria were responsible for the oxygenation of Earth's atmosphere during the Great Oxidation Event around 2.4 billion years ago.

Evolution

Prokaryotes were the only form of life on Earth for roughly the first two billion years of life's history. Eukaryotic cells are thought to have arisen through endosymbiosis, in which an archaeal host cell engulfed a bacterial cell that became the mitochondrion; a later engulfment of a cyanobacterium gave rise to the chloroplast of plants and algae. Molecular evidence indicates that eukaryotes are most closely related to the Asgard archaea.
