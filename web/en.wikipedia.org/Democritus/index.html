Democritus (Greek: Δημόκριτος, c. 460 – c. 370 BC) was an Ancient Greek pre-Socratic philosopher from Abdera in Thrace. He is remembered primarily for formulating, with his teacher Leucippus, an atomic theory of the universe — the doctrine that everything consists of indivisible particles called atoms moving in a void.

Life

Little is known reliably of his life. Later tradition portrays him as widely travelled in search of knowledge and as the "laughing philosopher," amused by human folly, in contrast to the "weeping philosopher" Heraclitus. None of his many writings survive intact; his ideas are known through fragments and reports in later authors such as Diogenes Laërtius and Aristotle.

Atomism

According to Democritus, atoms are infinite in number, eternal, and differ only in shape, size, order and position. All perceptible qualities — colour, taste, heat — arise from the configurations of atoms: "By convention sweet, by convention bitter... in reality only atoms and the void." The theory answered Parmenides' arguments about the impossibility of change by positing a void in which atoms move.

Ethics

Democritus also wrote extensively on ethics, advocating cheerfulness (euthymia) attained through moderation and knowledge as the goal of life. His system was later adopted and modified by Epicurus and, through Lucretius's poem De rerum natura, transmitted to early modern science.
