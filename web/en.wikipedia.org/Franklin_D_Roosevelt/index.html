Franklin Delano Roosevelt (30 January 1882 – 12 April 1945), often called FDR, was the 32nd president of the United States, serving from 1933 until his death in 1945. The only American president elected to more than two terms, he led the country through the Great Depression and most of the Second World War.

Early life and career

Roosevelt was born into a wealthy family at Hyde Park, New York, and educated at Groton School and Harvard University. A distant cousin of President Theodore Roosevelt, he married Eleanor Roosevelt in 1905 and entered politics as a New York state senator, then Assistant Secretary of the Navy under Woodrow Wilson. In 1921 he contracted a paralytic illness, widely identified as poliomyelitis, which left him unable to walk unaided; he concealed the extent of his disability throughout his career. He returned to prominence as Governor of New York (1929–1932).

Presidency

Elected in a landslide in 1932 amid the Great Depression, Roosevelt launched the New Deal, a programme of banking reform, relief and recovery measures including the Civilian Conservation Corps, the Tennessee Valley Authority, Social Security and securities regulation. After the outbreak of the Second World War he supplied the Allies through Lend-Lease, and following the attack on Pearl Harbor in December 1941 he led the United States through the war in alliance with Britain and the Soviet Union, helping to plan the postwar order at conferences including Yalta. Re-elected to a fourth term in 1944, he died of a cerebral haemorrhage on 12 April 1945, weeks before victory in Europe, and was succeeded by Harry S. Truman.

Legacy

Roosevelt is consistently ranked by historians among the greatest American presidents. His presidency reshaped the role of the federal government, though aspects of his record — including the internment of Japanese Americans — remain criticised.
