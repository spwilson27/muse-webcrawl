Willem Einthoven

Willem Einthoven (21 May 1860 – 29 September 1927) was a Dutch physician and physiologist. He invented the first practical electrocardiograph (ECG or EKG) in 1895 and received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1924 "for the discovery of the mechanism of the electrocardiogram".

The string galvanometer

Earlier instruments such as Augustus Waller's capillary electrometer could record the heart's electrical activity only crudely. Einthoven, professor of physiology at Leiden University from 1886, developed the string galvanometer (published 1901–1903): a hair-thin silver-coated quartz filament suspended between the poles of a powerful electromagnet, whose minute deflections in response to the heart's currents were magnified and photographed. The machine weighed about 270 kg and required five operators, but it produced electrocardiograms of clinical quality for the first time.

Contributions to cardiology

Einthoven standardised the ECG: he assigned the letters P, Q, R, S and T to the successive deflections of the waveform (conventions still used today) and described the three bipolar limb leads, geometrically framed as "Einthoven's triangle". He correlated tracings with heart sounds and with conditions such as hypertrophy and conduction defects, laying the foundations of electrocardiography as a diagnostic discipline.

Legacy

Einthoven's design was commercialised by the Cambridge Scientific Instrument Company and spread worldwide; modern ECG machines are direct descendants of his device. Streets and institutes in the Netherlands bear his name.
