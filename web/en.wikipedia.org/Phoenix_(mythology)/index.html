The phoenix is a long-lived bird found in the mythologies of many cultures, most famously Greek and Egyptian, that is cyclically regenerated or otherwise born again. Associated with the Sun, a phoenix obtains new life by rising from the ashes of its predecessor. Some legends say it dies in a show of flames and combustion; others that it simply dies and decomposes before being reborn.

Origins

The Greek phoenix likely derives from the Egyptian bennu, a sacred bird associated with the sun god Ra, the inundation of the Nile, and resurrection. Herodotus (5th century BC) gives the earliest surviving Greek account, describing a bird that comes to Egypt once every 500 years bearing the body of its parent encased in myrrh. Later classical authors — including Pliny the Elder, Tacitus, and Ovid — elaborated the story, and the lifespan of the phoenix was variously given as 500, 540, 1,000, or 1,461 years.

Symbolism

Early Christians adopted the phoenix as a symbol of resurrection and eternal life, and it appears in the Physiologus and medieval bestiaries. In heraldry the phoenix is depicted rising from flames. The bird gives its name to the city of Phoenix, Arizona, the constellation Phoenix, and innumerable works of literature and popular culture, where it commonly symbolizes renewal after destruction — hence the phrase "rise from the ashes."

Similar creatures

Analogous fiery or self-renewing birds appear in many traditions, including the Chinese fenghuang, the Persian simurgh, the Slavic firebird, and the Native American thunderbird.
