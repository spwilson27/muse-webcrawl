Purushartha

Puruṣārtha (Sanskrit: पुरुषार्थ, literally "object of human pursuit") is a key concept in Hinduism referring to the four proper goals or aims of a human life. The four puruṣārthas are Dharma (righteousness, moral values), Artha (prosperity, economic values), Kama (pleasure, love, psychological values), and Moksha (liberation, spiritual values, self-actualization). Together they provide a framework in which worldly pursuits are legitimate when pursued in balance and subordinate to dharma, while moksha is regarded as the ultimate end.

The four aims

Dharma denotes conduct in accordance with the order that sustains life — duty, virtue, law, and right living according to one's stage of life and station. Artha is the pursuit of security and material prosperity: wealth, career, and the means of life. It is the subject of the Arthashastra attributed to Kautilya. Kama encompasses desire, pleasure, love, and aesthetic enjoyment; its classic treatise is Vatsyayana's Kama Sutra. Moksha is release from samsara, the cycle of death and rebirth, and realization of the self's true nature.

Relationships among the aims

Hindu texts discuss the relative priority of the aims extensively. The first three, grouped as the trivarga, are considered legitimate goals of household life, with dharma regulating the pursuit of artha and kama. Moksha, added as the fourth aim, transcends the others and is the focus of the Upanishads and of renunciant traditions. Commentators debated, for example, whether wealth or pleasure should rank higher, and whether liberation requires abandoning the other aims or fulfilling them within a dharmic life — the ideal of the householder's stages of life (ashrama) attempts to sequence them across a lifetime.

Influence

The purushartha framework shaped classical Indian literature and law: the great epics, the Mahabharata and Ramayana, present themselves as treating all four aims, and the traditional scheme of fourteen or eighteen branches of learning is organized around dharma and artha treatises. The concept remains a common reference point in modern discussions of Hindu ethics and the philosophy of a well-lived life.
