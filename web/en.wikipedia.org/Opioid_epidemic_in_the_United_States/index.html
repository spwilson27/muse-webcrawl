Opioid epidemic in the United States

The opioid epidemic in the United States refers to the extensive ongoing overuse of opioid medications, both from medical prescriptions and illegal sources, that has devastated communities since the 1990s. It has been described as "one of the most devastating public health catastrophes of our time," and in 2017 the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services declared it a public health emergency.

Waves

Public health authorities describe the epidemic in waves. The first wave began in the 1990s with increased prescribing of opioids such as oxycodone (marketed aggressively as OxyContin), promoted as low-risk for addiction. A second wave began around 2010 with rising heroin deaths as users shifted to a cheaper, more available drug. A third wave, from about 2013, involved synthetic opioids, principally illicit fentanyl, which drove overdose deaths above 80,000 per year in the early 2020s.

Response and litigation

Responses have included prescription drug monitoring programs, wider distribution of the overdose antidote naloxone, expanded medication-assisted treatment with methadone and buprenorphine, and massive litigation against manufacturers, distributors, and pharmacies. Settlements totalling tens of billions of dollars have been reached, and Purdue Pharma's bankruptcy and the role of the Sackler family became central public controversies.
