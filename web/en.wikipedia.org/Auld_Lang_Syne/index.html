Auld Lang Syne

"Auld Lang Syne" is a Scottish song, first written down in 1788 by Robert Burns from older folk sources. In the English-speaking world, it is traditionally sung to bid farewell to the old year at the stroke of midnight on New Year's Eve (Hogmanay in Scotland). By extension, it is also sung at funerals, graduations and other occasions of parting. The phrase "auld lang syne" translates literally to "old long since", or more idiomatically "days gone by".

"Should auld acquaintance be forgot, and never brought to mind?"

History

Burns sent the song to the Scots Musical Museum in 1788, claiming to have transcribed it "from an old man's singing", though some verses are his own work; similar earlier ballads exist, including a poem by James Watson from 1711. The melody commonly used today was first printed in 1799 in George Thomson's collection.

Lyrics

The song begins with a rhetorical question: "Should auld acquaintance be forgot, and never brought to mind? Should auld acquaintance be forgot, and auld lang syne?" Its verses recall shared childhood, wandering, and a draught of kindness shared between old friends, concluding with joined hands: "And there's a hand, my trusty fiere! and gie's a hand o' thine!"

Use

Canadian bandleader Guy Lombardo popularised the song in North America through his New Year's Eve broadcasts beginning in 1929. The melody serves as the tune of school songs and national songs in several countries, including South Korea's former anthem text and the Maldives' anthem, and it is played in Japan to signal closing time in shops.
