Ancient Athens

Athens was a prominent city-state (polis) of ancient Greece, located in Attica, and is widely regarded as the birthplace of democracy and a cradle of Western philosophy, drama, and art. At its height in the fifth century BC it headed the Delian League and built an empire financed by silver mines at Laurion and by tribute from its allies.

History

Settled since Neolithic times, Athens was a Mycenaean centre and survived the Bronze Age collapse. After monarchy and aristocracy came the lawgivers Draco (621 BC) and Solon (594 BC), the tyranny of Peisistratus and his sons, and then Cleisthenes' reforms of 508/7 BC, which founded the democracy by reorganising citizens into ten tribes and demes. Athens helped repel the Persian invasions at Marathon (490 BC) and Salamis (480 BC). Under Pericles the city was adorned with the Parthenon and other temples of the Acropolis. The Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC) against Sparta ended in Athens' defeat and a brief oligarchic regime, the Thirty Tyrants; democracy was restored but never regained its former power. Athens later came under Macedonian and then Roman rule, remaining a famed centre of learning into late antiquity.

Government and society

Classical Athenian democracy was direct: male citizens voted in the Assembly (ekklēsia) on the Pnyx, served by lot on the Council of 500 and in the popular courts, and could ostracise prominent figures for ten years. Citizenship excluded women, resident aliens (metics), and slaves, who together formed most of the population. The economy rested on agriculture, craft production, trade through the port of Piraeus, and slavery.

Culture

Athens produced the tragedies of Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides, the comedies of Aristophanes, the histories of Herodotus and Thucydides, and the philosophy of Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle. Its festivals, above all the City Dionysia and the Panathenaea, structured civic and religious life.
