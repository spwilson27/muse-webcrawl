Expansion of the universe

Key evidenceGalactic redshifts; cosmic microwave background
Governing theoryGeneral relativity (Friedmann equations)
RateHubble constant H0 ≈ 70 km/s/Mpc

The expansion of the universe is the increase in distance between gravitationally unbound parts of the observable universe over time. It is an intrinsic expansion: space itself expands, carrying galaxies apart, rather than galaxies moving through a pre-existing space. The expansion is described by the Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker metric of general relativity and is characterized by a scale factor that grows with time.

Discovery

In the 1920s, Georges Lemaître derived expanding solutions of Einstein's equations and proposed that the universe began from a "primeval atom". Edwin Hubble's 1929 measurements of galactic distances and redshifts, building on Vesto Slipher's spectroscopy, established the linear relation now called Hubble's law: recession velocity increases with distance.

History of the cosmos

Extrapolating backwards yields the hot, dense early universe of the Big Bang model, about 13.8 billion years ago. A brief epoch of exponential cosmic inflation is hypothesized to have preceded the hot Big Bang phase, explaining the universe's observed uniformity and flatness.

Accelerating expansion

In 1998, observations of distant Type Ia supernovae showed that the expansion is accelerating, driven by a form of energy dubbed dark energy, often modelled as Einstein's cosmological constant. The standard ΛCDM model combines this with cold dark matter.

Measurement

The present expansion rate, the Hubble constant, is measured at roughly 67–74 km/s per megaparsec; the small discrepancy between early- and late-universe determinations is known as the Hubble tension.
