Self-signed certificate

In cryptography and computer security, self-signed certificates are public key certificates that are not issued by a certificate authority (CA). These self-signed certificates are easy to make and do not cost money. However, they do not provide any trust value.

For instance, if a website owner uses a self-signed certificate to provide HTTPS services, people who visit that website cannot be certain that they are connected to their intended destination. For all they know, a malicious third-party could be redirecting the connection using another self-signed certificate bearing the same holder name. The connection is encrypted, but does not lead to its intended recipient. In comparison, a certificate signed by a trusted CA prevents this attack.

Security issues

In a CA-based PKI system, the CA must be trusted by both parties. This is usually accomplished by placing the CA's root certificate in a trust store of pre-configured, trustworthy certificates. A self-signed certificate, by contrast, is essentially a claim of identity with no third-party verification. Web browsers therefore display warnings for self-signed certificates, unless the user adds an exception.

Uses

Self-signed certificates are useful in many contexts:

  • Testing and development environments, where verification is unnecessary.
  • Internal networks, where the certificate can be distributed and trusted manually.
  • Root CA certificates are themselves self-signed.
  • Personal and small-scale uses, such as local mail servers or embedded devices.

For root certificates, an organization vouches for itself; users decide whether to trust it. The web of trust model used by PGP also relies partly on self-signed keys endorsed by others.

Comparison with CA certificates

Both types of certificates provide the same encryption. The difference lies solely in trust: CA-signed certificates are automatically trusted by browsers and operating systems that trust the issuing CA, whereas self-signed certificates require out-of-band verification, such as checking a fingerprint through a separate channel.
