Chipotle

A chipotle (from Nahuatl chilpoctli, "smoked chile") is a smoke-dried ripe jalapeño chile pepper used in Mexican cuisine and Mexican-influenced cooking elsewhere. Smoke-drying preserves the pepper, which cannot be kept fresh for long once fully ripe, and gives it a distinctive smoky, earthy heat. Chipotles are a key ingredient in adobos, salsas, and marinades.

Production

Jalapeños are left on the plant until they turn deep red and begin to lose moisture. They are then placed on racks in a smoking chamber, traditionally over wood fires, and turned periodically over several days until they dry to a leathery or brittle consistency. The process dates to pre-Hispanic Mesoamerica, where smoke-drying was one of the few reliable ways to preserve ripe chiles.

Types

  • Morita — smoked for less time, softer and fruitier; the most common type in Mexico and the United States.
  • Meco — smoked much longer, stiffer and tan or greyish in color, with a stronger smoke flavor.

Culinary use

Chipotles are frequently sold canned in adobo sauce — a puree of tomato, vinegar, garlic, and spices — and in this form are a staple of home cooking. Ground chipotle powder seasons rubs and sauces, and whole dried peppers are rehydrated for moles and stews. The pepper also gave its name to the American fast-casual restaurant chain Chipotle Mexican Grill, founded in 1993.
