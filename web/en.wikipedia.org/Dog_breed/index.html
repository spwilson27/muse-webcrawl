Dog breed

A dog breed is a particular type of dog that was purposefully bred by humans to perform specific tasks, such as herding, hunting and guarding. Dogs are the most variable mammal on Earth, with artificial selection producing around 450 globally recognised breeds. When dogs of the same breed reproduce, puppies are born with predictable traits including size, coat, temperament and working behaviour.

History

The domestic dog descends from wolves domesticated at least 15,000 years ago. For most of history, dogs were selected as functional types — sighthounds, mastiffs, terriers, herding dogs — rather than closed breeds. The modern breed system emerged in Victorian Britain with the founding of The Kennel Club (1873) and written breed standards. The Fédération Cynologique Internationale (FCI) now recognises more than 350 breeds, organised into ten groups.

Breed standards and registration

Kennel clubs maintain studbooks and publish standards describing the ideal appearance and temperament of each breed. Dogs whose ancestry is recorded are termed purebred; crossbreeds and mixed-breed dogs fall outside the registry system. Popular breeds in many countries include the Labrador Retriever, French Bulldog and German Shepherd.

Health issues

Closed gene pools have concentrated hereditary disorders in some breeds, and extreme conformation (such as very flat faces in brachycephalic breeds) can impair welfare. Genetic diversity studies, outcrossing programmes and revised standards are among responses debated by breeders and regulators.
