Air power

Air power or airpower is the ability of a nation or armed force to project military power through aviation — aircraft operating in the air and, by extension, space. It encompasses air superiority, strategic and tactical bombing, airlift, reconnaissance, and close air support, and is usually delivered by an air force, naval aviation, and army aviation.

Theory

Early theorists shaped doctrine between the world wars: Giulio Douhet argued in The Command of the Air (1921) that strategic bombing of cities and industry could win wars independently; Billy Mitchell championed independent air forces and air superiority; John Boyd later emphasized decision-cycle speed (the OODA loop). Hugh Trenchard's RAF, the first independent air force (1918), institutionalized the concept.

Roles

Core missions include gaining control of the air (counter-air operations), strike and interdiction, close air support of ground forces, strategic airlift and tanker support, intelligence-surveillance-reconnaissance (ISR), and deterrence, including nuclear delivery. The Second World War demonstrated both the power and the limits of bombing campaigns; later conflicts from Korea and Vietnam to the Gulf War (1991) — where a coalition air campaign preceded a 100-hour ground war — showed precision weapons and stealth reshaping what air power could achieve.

Modern developments

Unmanned aerial vehicles, precision-guided munitions, networked sensors, and space-based assets have extended air power, while modern integrated air defenses challenge it. Air superiority remains generally regarded as a prerequisite for successful surface operations.
