Water purification

Water purification is the process of removing undesirable chemicals, biological contaminants, suspended solids and gases from water. The goal is to produce water fit for a specific purpose — most commonly human consumption (drinking water), but also medical, industrial, agricultural and scientific applications. Purification reduces the concentration of particulate matter and parasites, bacteria, algae, viruses and fungi, as well as dissolved chemical pollutants.

Drinking-water treatment

A conventional municipal treatment train typically includes: coagulation and flocculation, in which chemicals such as alum cause fine particles to clump; sedimentation, where the floc settles out; filtration through sand, gravel or membranes to remove remaining particles and microorganisms; and disinfection, most commonly with chlorine or chloramine, and alternatively with ozone or ultraviolet light, to kill pathogens and protect the water in distribution. Fluoridation is practised in some jurisdictions for dental health. Additional processes — aeration, softening, activated-carbon adsorption and pH adjustment — are used as needed.

Other methods

Reverse osmosis forces water through a semipermeable membrane, removing salts and most contaminants; it is used for desalination and high-purity water. Distillation separates water by evaporation and condensation. At the household and point-of-use scale, methods include boiling, carbon filters, ceramic filters and solar disinfection. In emergencies and in areas without infrastructure, purification tablets and portable filters provide safe water. Wastewater treatment applies related but distinct processes to sewage before discharge or reuse.

Standards and health

Safe drinking water is one of the foundations of public health; the spread of filtration and chlorination in the early 20th century sharply reduced waterborne diseases such as cholera and typhoid fever. The World Health Organization publishes guidelines for drinking-water quality, and national regulators — such as the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency under the Safe Drinking Water Act — set enforceable limits for contaminants. Nevertheless, WHO and UNICEF estimate that billions of people still lack safely managed drinking-water services.
