Water cycle

The water cycle, also known as the hydrological cycle or the hydrologic cycle, is a biogeochemical cycle that describes the continuous movement of water on, above and below the surface of the Earth. The mass of water on Earth remains fairly constant over time but the partitioning of the water into the major reservoirs of ice, fresh water, saline water (ocean water) and atmospheric water is variable depending on a wide range of climatic variables.

Processes

Solar energy drives evaporation from oceans, lakes, and land, and transpiration from plants; together they form evapotranspiration. Rising moist air cools and condenses into clouds, returning water as precipitation. On land, water may run off into streams and rivers, infiltrate to recharge groundwater, or be stored as snow and ice, before eventually returning to the sea.

Reservoirs and residence times

The oceans hold about 96.5 percent of Earth's water. Residence times vary from days in the atmosphere to millennia in deep groundwater and Antarctic ice. Although the cycle has no starting point, the ocean is a convenient reference since most evaporation and precipitation occur there.

Human influence

Humans alter the cycle through damming rivers, abstracting groundwater, deforestation, urbanization, and climate change, which intensifies evaporation and precipitation extremes — heavier downpours and longer droughts — in many regions.
