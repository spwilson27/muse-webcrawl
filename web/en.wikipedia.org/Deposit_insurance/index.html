Deposit insurance

Deposit insurance or deposit protection is a measure implemented in many countries to protect bank depositors, in full or in part, from losses caused by a bank's inability to pay its debts when due. It is one component of a financial system safety net that promotes financial stability, reducing the incentive for depositors to stage bank runs.

United States

The best-known scheme is that of the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation (FDIC), created by the Banking Act of 1933 in response to the bank failures of the Great Depression. The FDIC insures deposits at member banks up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category, funded by premiums paid by banks rather than by public funds. Credit union deposits are insured by the National Credit Union Administration.

Other systems

The European Union requires member states to guarantee deposits up to €100,000 per depositor per bank. The United Kingdom's Financial Services Compensation Scheme covers £85,000. Many countries expanded coverage during the 2008 financial crisis to prevent runs.

Criticism

Economists debate whether deposit insurance creates moral hazard, encouraging banks to take greater risks; risk-based premiums and coverage limits are commonly used to mitigate this effect.
