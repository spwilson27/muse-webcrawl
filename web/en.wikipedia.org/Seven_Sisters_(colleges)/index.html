Seven Sisters (colleges)

The Seven Sisters were founded to offer women an education comparable to the Ivy League

The Seven Sisters are a group of seven private liberal arts colleges in the Northeastern United States that are historically women's colleges. They were founded between 1837 and 1889 as a counterpart to the men's Ivy League colleges, to provide women with an education of comparable rigour. The name, drawn from the Pleiades of Greek mythology, came into use after the colleges formed a conference in 1927 to coordinate on admissions, athletics and shared concerns.

The colleges

The seven are Barnard College (New York City, affiliated with Columbia University), Bryn Mawr College (Pennsylvania), Mount Holyoke College (Massachusetts, founded 1837, the oldest), Radcliffe College (Massachusetts, formerly Harvard's coordinate women's college), Smith College (Massachusetts), Vassar College (New York) and Wellesley College (Massachusetts).

Changes since the 1960s

Beginning in the late 1960s, several members changed their admissions policies. Vassar became coeducational in 1969. Radcliffe progressively merged with Harvard University, a process completed in 1999 when it became the Radcliffe Institute for Advanced Study. The remaining five colleges continue to admit women as undergraduates, with Barnard maintaining its affiliation with Columbia while remaining an independent women's college.

Legacy

The Seven Sisters educated generations of women leaders, scholars and reformers at a time when elite higher education excluded women, and they remain among the most selective liberal arts colleges in the United States.
