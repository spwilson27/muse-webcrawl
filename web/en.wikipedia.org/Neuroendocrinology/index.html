Neuroendocrinology

Neuroendocrinology is the branch of biology (specifically of physiology) which studies the interaction between the nervous system and the endocrine system; that is, how the brain regulates the hormonal activity in the body. The nervous and endocrine systems often act together in a process called neuroendocrine integration, to regulate the physiological processes of the human body.

The central structure of the field is the hypothalamic–pituitary axis. Neurons in the hypothalamus secrete releasing and inhibiting hormones (such as TRH, CRH, GnRH, GHRH, somatostatin and dopamine) into the hypophyseal portal circulation, controlling the anterior pituitary gland's release of tropic hormones including TSH, ACTH, LH, FSH, growth hormone and prolactin. Magnocellular neurons of the hypothalamus also release oxytocin and vasopressin (antidiuretic hormone) directly from the posterior pituitary into the systemic circulation.

Major axes and systems

Key neuroendocrine systems include the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis, governing the stress response and cortisol secretion; the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid (HPT) axis, regulating metabolism; the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal (HPG) axis, controlling reproduction; and systems regulating growth, lactation, water balance, appetite and circadian rhythms. Neuroendocrine cells are also found throughout the gut, lungs and other organs (the diffuse neuroendocrine system), and tumours arising from them are called neuroendocrine tumours.

History

The field emerged in the mid-20th century from the work of Geoffrey Harris, who demonstrated that the brain controls the pituitary via portal blood vessels, and was crowned by the isolation of hypothalamic releasing hormones by Roger Guillemin and Andrew Schally, who shared the 1977 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Modern neuroendocrinology encompasses topics from the hormonal control of behaviour and metabolism to the effects of endocrine-disrupting chemicals.
