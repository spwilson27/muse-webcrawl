Schenck v. United States, 249 U.S. 47 (1919), is a landmark decision of the United States Supreme Court concerning the enforcement of the Espionage Act of 1917 during the First World War. Writing for a unanimous Court, Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes Jr. introduced the famous "clear and present danger" test for determining when speech may be restricted under the First Amendment.

Background

Charles Schenck, general secretary of the Socialist Party of America, supervised the printing and mailing of some 15,000 leaflets to men subject to the draft. The leaflets argued that conscription violated the Thirteenth Amendment's prohibition of involuntary servitude and urged readers to assert their rights. Schenck was convicted of conspiring to obstruct recruiting and enlistment under the Espionage Act.

Decision

Holmes upheld the conviction. The Court reasoned that words used in wartime circumstances may create a "clear and present danger" of substantive evils—such as obstructing the draft—that Congress has a right to prevent. The opinion contains the celebrated analogy: "The most stringent protection of free speech would not protect a man in falsely shouting fire in a theatre and causing a panic." Holmes emphasized context: "The question in every case is whether the words used are used in such circumstances and are of such a nature as to create a clear and present danger."

Legacy

Within months, Holmes himself, dissenting in Abrams v. United States (1919), began to apply the test more protectively. Over the following decades the "clear and present danger" standard—often applied permissively toward government—was effectively superseded by the stricter test of Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969), which protects advocacy unless it is directed to inciting imminent lawless action and is likely to produce it. Schenck remains historically foundational: it began the Supreme Court's modern First Amendment jurisprudence and gave American law its most famous (and often misquoted) speech analogy.
