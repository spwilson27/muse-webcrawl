The International Monetary Fund (IMF) is a major financial agency of the United Nations, and an international financial institution funded by 191 member countries, with headquarters in Washington, D.C. It is regarded as the global lender of last resort to national governments, and a leading supporter of exchange-rate stability. Its primary mission is to ensure the stability of the international monetary system and foster global monetary cooperation, secure financial stability, facilitate international trade, promote high employment and sustainable economic growth, and reduce poverty around the world.

History

The IMF was conceived in July 1944 at the United Nations Monetary and Financial Conference held at Bretton Woods, New Hampshire, where delegates from 44 countries agreed on a framework for international economic cooperation. The institution formally came into existence on 27 December 1945, when the first 29 member countries signed its Articles of Agreement. The British economist John Maynard Keynes and the American official Harry Dexter White were the intellectual architects of the Bretton Woods system, under which currencies were pegged to the United States dollar, itself convertible to gold. That system collapsed in the early 1970s, after which the IMF shifted toward floating exchange rates, surveillance of member economies, and crisis lending.

Functions

Surveillance

The Fund monitors the economic and financial policies of its member countries through regular consultations, known as Article IV consultations, publishing assessments such as the World Economic Outlook and the Global Financial Stability Report.

Lending

The IMF lends to countries facing balance-of-payments problems, typically attaching conditions — often called "conditionality" — requiring policy reforms such as fiscal consolidation, monetary tightening, or structural changes. Major programmes have included support during the Latin American debt crisis of the 1980s, the Asian financial crisis of 1997–98, and the eurozone sovereign debt crisis after 2010.

Capacity development

The Fund provides technical assistance and training to member countries in areas such as central banking, tax policy, and statistics.

Special Drawing Rights

In 1969 the IMF created the Special Drawing Right (SDR), an international reserve asset whose value is based on a basket of major currencies — the US dollar, the euro, the Chinese renminbi, the Japanese yen, and the British pound sterling. SDR allocations supplement members' foreign exchange reserves; a record allocation equivalent to about US$650 billion was made in August 2021 in response to the COVID-19 pandemic.

Governance

The IMF is governed by its Board of Governors, on which each member country appoints one governor, and a resident Executive Board of 24 directors. Voting power is weighted by financial quota, giving the largest economies — the United States, Japan, China, Germany, France, and the United Kingdom — the greatest influence. The United States holds the largest quota share, effectively giving it veto power over major decisions requiring an 85 percent supermajority. The managing director is traditionally a European, while the president of the World Bank is traditionally an American.

Criticism

The Fund has been criticised for imposing austerity measures that worsen recessions, for one-size-fits-all policy prescriptions, and for governance structures that under-represent developing economies. The IMF itself has acknowledged shortcomings, notably in its handling of capital account liberalisation and some crisis programmes, and has moderated some positions on fiscal policy and capital controls.
