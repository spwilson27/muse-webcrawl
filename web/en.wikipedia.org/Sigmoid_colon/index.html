Sigmoid colon

Sigmoid colon
Part of: Large intestine
Precursor: Hindgut
Artery: Sigmoid arteries (from inferior mesenteric artery)
Vein: Sigmoid veins
Nerve: Pelvic splanchnic nerves, inferior mesenteric plexus
Latin: colon sigmoideum

The sigmoid colon is the S-shaped final section of the large intestine, connecting the descending colon to the rectum. Its name derives from the Greek letter sigma (σ), reflecting its curved shape. It is typically about 35–40 cm (14–16 in) long and lies within the pelvis, suspended by a mesentery called the sigmoid mesocolon, which gives it considerable mobility.

Structure

The sigmoid colon begins at the pelvic brim, where the descending colon loses its mesentery, and ends at the level of the third sacral vertebra, where it becomes the rectum and the taeniae coli spread out into a continuous longitudinal muscle layer. Its muscular wall produces strong peristaltic contractions that move faeces into the rectum.

Function

Like the rest of the colon, the sigmoid absorbs water and salts from the faecal material passing through it and compacts the residue for storage until defecation.

Clinical significance

The sigmoid colon is the most common site of diverticulosis and diverticulitis, conditions associated with its high-pressure contractions and narrow lumen. It is also prone to sigmoid volvulus — a twisting of the loop on its mesentery that can obstruct the bowel, especially in older adults — and it is a frequent site of colorectal cancer. Flexible sigmoidoscopy and colonoscopy are used to examine it.
