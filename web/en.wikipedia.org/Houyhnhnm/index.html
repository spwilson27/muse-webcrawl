Houyhnhnm

Houyhnhnms are a fictional race of intelligent horses described in the last part of Jonathan Swift's 1726 satirical novel Gulliver's Travels. They contrast with the Yahoos, brutish human-like creatures found in the same land, and represent Swift's ideal of reason and virtue — while also serving to satirise human pride.

Gulliver with the Houyhnhnms, from an illustrated edition of Gulliver's Travels.

The name is pronounced approximately like "whinny-him" or "win-im", intended to imitate the neighing of a horse. In the Houyhnhnms' language, the word signifies "horse" and, by its etymology, "the perfection of nature". Their society is governed purely by reason: they have no words for lying, evil, or opinion, hold no religious or political disputes, and practise a calm, communal life in which foals are raised collectively.

Role in the novel

Shipwrecked on their island, Lemuel Gulliver is taken in by a Houyhnhnm master and learns their language. Reporting on human wars, law, and politics, Gulliver horrifies his master, who concludes that humans are Yahoos endowed with just enough reason to aggravate their natural corruption. The Houyhnhnm assembly eventually resolves that Gulliver, as a Yahoo, must leave the island. On returning to England, Gulliver is disgusted by the sight and smell of his own family and prefers the company of horses in his stable — a conclusion read both as misanthropic satire and as a warning against excessive pride in reason.

Interpretation

Critics debate whether the Houyhnhnms are Swift's ideal society or are themselves satirised for their emotional coldness and their eugenic, passionless way of life. The contrast between "hard" readings, in which the Houyhnhnms are admirable, and "soft" readings, in which they are unattractive, is a long-standing theme of Swift scholarship.
