Extravehicular Mobility Unit

The Extravehicular Mobility Unit (EMU) is an independent anthropomorphic spacesuit developed by NASA that provides environmental protection, mobility, life support, and communications for astronauts performing extravehicular activity (EVA) — spacewalks — in Earth orbit. First flown in 1981 aboard the Space Shuttle, the EMU remains in use aboard the International Space Station.

Design

The EMU consists of a hard upper torso of fiberglass and aluminium, fabric arms and legs, gloves, a helmet with a protective extravehicular visor assembly, and the Primary Life Support System backpack, which supplies oxygen at a suit pressure of about 4.3 psi, removes carbon dioxide, and circulates cooling water through the liquid cooling and ventilation garment. A chest-mounted display and control module lets the astronaut operate the suit's systems, and the SAFER jetpack provides a self-rescue capability if an astronaut becomes untethered. Components are modular and resized on orbit to fit different crew members; a suit masses roughly 122 kg (270 lb) on Earth but is weightless in orbit.

History and future

The EMU was designed by ILC Dover and Hamilton Standard for the Shuttle programme and upgraded for station assembly, including improved gloves, helmet cameras, and lights. The fleet of suits is aging — a serious water leak into a helmet during a 2013 spacewalk by Luca Parmitano led to a major investigation — and NASA is developing the next-generation Exploration Extravehicular Mobility Unit (xEMU) for the Artemis lunar programme, while commercial partners develop suits for station and lunar surface missions.
