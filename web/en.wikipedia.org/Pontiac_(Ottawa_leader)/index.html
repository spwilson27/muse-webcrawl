Pontiac (Ottawa leader)

Pontiac or Obwaandi'eyaag (c. 1714/20 – April 20, 1769) was an Odawa (Ottawa) war chief known for his role in the war named for him, Pontiac's War, in which Native American nations of the Great Lakes and Ohio Country rose against the British after the French and Indian War. He became one of the most famous Native American leaders of the eighteenth century.

Early life

Little is known with certainty about Pontiac's early years. He was probably born between 1712 and 1725 along the Detroit or Maumee River, to an Odawa father and an Ojibwe mother according to one tradition. By the 1750s he was a war leader at Detroit and had fought alongside the French during the French and Indian War, possibly at the Battle of the Monongahela in 1755.

Pontiac's War

After France ceded Canada and the Great Lakes to Britain in 1763, British commander Jeffery Amherst cut off the diplomatic gift-giving on which Native alliances depended and restricted trade in arms and ammunition. Influenced by the Delaware prophet Neolin's teaching that Native peoples should reject European ways and unite, Pontiac rallied a coalition of Odawa, Ojibwe, Potawatomi, Huron, Shawnee, Delaware, Seneca and other nations. On May 9, 1763, he attempted to take Fort Detroit by surprise; the plan was betrayed, and Pontiac's forces settled into a siege that lasted until October. Other forts across the pays d'en haut fell in coordinated attacks, and the war continued into 1764–1766 before peace was made, with Pontiac formally submitting at Oswego in July 1766.

Later life and death

Pontiac's influence waned after the war, as other nations questioned his claims to speak for them. On April 20, 1769, he was murdered at Cahokia, in present-day Illinois, by a young Peoria warrior — an act some accounts say was instigated by a British trader. His burial place is unknown, though a tradition places it in St. Louis. The city of Pontiac, Michigan, the Pontiac automobile brand, and numerous other places and institutions are named after him.
