Modularity theorem


Image placeholder: elliptic curve associated with a modular form

The modularity theorem, formerly called the Taniyama–Shimura conjecture (or Taniyama–Shimura–Weil conjecture), states that every elliptic curve over the rational numbers is modular — that is, it can be associated with a unique modular form of weight two whose L-series matches the curve's L-series. The theorem creates a deep bridge between arithmetic geometry and harmonic analysis.

History

The conjecture grew from ideas of Yutaka Taniyama (1955) and was developed by Goro Shimura and André Weil, who gave it a precise formulation in 1967. In 1986 Gerhard Frey conjectured, and Ken Ribet proved (the "epsilon conjecture"), that a counterexample to Fermat's Last Theorem would yield a semistable elliptic curve that could not be modular; hence modularity for semistable curves would imply Fermat's Last Theorem. Andrew Wiles, with Richard Taylor, proved exactly that case in work announced in 1993 and published in 1995, establishing Fermat's Last Theorem.

Completion

The full modularity theorem for all elliptic curves over the rationals was completed in 2001 by Christophe Breuil, Brian Conrad, Fred Diamond, and Richard Taylor, building on Wiles's methods and generalising them to the remaining cases.

Significance

Modularity is a cornerstone of the Langlands program, which predicts sweeping correspondences between arithmetic objects and automorphic forms. Generations of the theorem — such as Serre's modularity conjecture (proved by Khare and Wintenberger) and modularity over other number fields — remain active research areas.
