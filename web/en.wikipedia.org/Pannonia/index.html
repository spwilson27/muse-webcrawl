Pannonia was a province of the Roman Empire bounded on the north and east by the Danube, and corresponding roughly to present-day western Hungary, eastern Austria and parts of Croatia, Serbia, Slovenia, Slovakia and Bosnia and Herzegovina. The region had been inhabited by Illyrian and Celtic peoples, among them the Pannonii, from whom the province took its name.

Roman conquest and administration

Rome began campaigns in the area under Octavian (the future Augustus) in 35 BC; resistance culminated in the Great Illyrian Revolt (Bellum Batonianum) of 6–9 AD, after whose suppression Pannonia was organised as a province. It was a heavily militarised frontier zone guarding the middle Danube (limes), with legionary fortresses at Carnuntum, Vindobona (Vienna), Aquincum (Budapest) and Brigetio. Around 103–107 AD Trajan divided it into Pannonia Superior (west) and Pannonia Inferior (east); Diocletian later subdivided it into four provinces. Several emperors came from the region, and Marcus Aurelius wrote part of his Meditations on campaign there and died at Vindobona in 180 AD.

Decline and aftermath

In the early fifth century the province was progressively abandoned amid the upheavals of the Migration Period; it was ceded to the Huns in the 430s and subsequently passed among Goths, Lombards, Avars and Slavs. The name survived in medieval usage for the Hungarian plain, and "Pannonian" is still used geographically for the Pannonian Basin (Carpathian Basin).
