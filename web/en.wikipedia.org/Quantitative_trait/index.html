Quantitative trait

A quantitative trait is a phenotype that can be measured on a continuous scale and that depends on the cumulative actions of many genes (polygenes) and the environment. Examples include height, weight, skin pigmentation, crop yield, milk production, and susceptibility to complex diseases such as diabetes.

Characteristics

Unlike Mendelian traits, which fall into discrete categories, quantitative traits vary continuously, often approximately following a normal distribution in a population. Their study is the subject of quantitative genetics, founded in the early 20th century when R. A. Fisher reconciled Mendelian inheritance with continuous variation by showing that many loci of small effect produce a smooth distribution.

QTL mapping

A quantitative trait locus (QTL) is a region of DNA associated with variation in a quantitative trait. QTLs are identified by statistical association between genetic markers and phenotype in crosses, pedigrees, or genome-wide association studies (GWAS). Most quantitative traits are highly polygenic: human height, for instance, is influenced by thousands of variants. The proportion of phenotypic variance explained by genetics is summarised by heritability.

Applications

Understanding quantitative traits underlies plant and animal breeding (marker-assisted and genomic selection), evolutionary biology, and medicine's search for the genetic architecture of common diseases.
