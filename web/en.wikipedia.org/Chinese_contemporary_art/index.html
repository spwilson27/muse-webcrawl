Chinese contemporary art

Chinese contemporary art refers to avant-garde and experimental art produced in China from the end of the Cultural Revolution in the late 1970s to the present. Emerging first in unofficial exhibitions and artist groups, it developed through movements such as the Stars Group, the '85 New Wave, Political Pop, and Cynical Realism, and from the 1990s onward became a major presence in the international art market and museum world.

Origins

After Mao Zedong's death in 1976 and the beginning of economic reform, artists began to experiment beyond socialist realism. In 1979 the Stars Group (Xingxing) held an unofficial exhibition outside the National Art Museum in Beijing, demanding artistic freedom; its members included Huang Rui and Ma Desheng. The ferment culminated in the '85 New Wave, a nationwide avant-garde movement, and the 1989 China/Avant-Garde exhibition in Beijing, which was shut down twice on its opening day.

1990s movements

In the 1990s, painters such as Wang Guangyi fused propaganda imagery with Western brand logos in the style critics called Political Pop, while Fang Lijun, Yue Minjun, and Zhang Xiaogang developed Cynical Realism — grinning or blank faces that registered disillusion after 1989. Performance, installation, and video art flourished in Beijing's East Village and in artist communities on the urban fringe.

International recognition

Chinese artists gained broad international exposure through exhibitions such as the Venice Biennale (from 1993, and with a Chinese pavilion from 2005), documenta, and major touring shows. In the 2000s Chinese contemporary works achieved record auction prices in Hong Kong, London, and New York, and figures such as Ai Weiwei became global public intellectuals as well as artists. Debates continue over commercialization, censorship, and the relationship between art and the state.
