Neurofibromatosis is a group of genetic disorders that cause tumours to form on nerve tissue. These tumours, which are usually benign, can develop anywhere in the nervous system, including the brain, spinal cord, and peripheral nerves. The group traditionally comprises neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF1, historically called von Recklinghausen disease), neurofibromatosis type 2 (now often termed NF2-related schwannomatosis), and the schwannomatoses. Although historically grouped by name, the conditions have distinct genetic causes and clinical features.

Neurofibromatosis type 1

NF1 is caused by mutations in the NF1 gene on chromosome 17, which encodes neurofibromin, a tumour suppressor that negatively regulates the Ras signalling pathway. It is one of the most common autosomal dominant disorders, affecting roughly 1 in 3,000 people. Characteristic signs include multiple café-au-lait macules (flat, pigmented skin lesions), freckling in the armpits and groin, benign tumours of the skin and nerves called neurofibromas, Lisch nodules (pigmented hamartomas of the iris), bony abnormalities, and learning disabilities. About half of cases arise from new mutations rather than inheritance. Plexiform neurofibromas, which grow along nerve plexuses, carry a risk of transformation into malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumours.

Neurofibromatosis type 2

NF2-related schwannomatosis is caused by mutations in the NF2 gene on chromosome 22, encoding the tumour suppressor merlin (schwannomin). Its hallmark is bilateral vestibular schwannomas—tumours of the nerves connecting the inner ear to the brain—which cause hearing loss, tinnitus, and balance problems. Meningiomas, ependymomas, and other schwannomas are also common. It is considerably rarer than NF1.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is based on clinical criteria and can be confirmed by genetic testing. There is no cure; management centres on surveillance for complications, surgical removal of symptomatic tumours, and treatment of associated problems such as optic pathway gliomas. The MEK inhibitor selumetinib was approved in 2020 for children with inoperable plexiform neurofibromas in NF1, the first targeted drug treatment for the condition.

History

NF1 is named in its older form after the German pathologist Friedrich von Recklinghausen, who described the condition in 1882. The disorder was once popularly—but incorrectly—associated with Joseph Merrick, the "Elephant Man", who is now thought to have had Proteus syndrome instead.
