Order of magnitude

An order of magnitude is an approximation of the logarithm of a value relative to some contextually understood reference value, usually 10, interpreted as the base of the logarithm and the representative of values of magnitude one. Two numbers differ by one order of magnitude if one is about ten times the other.

BaseUsually 10 (also 2, e)
NotationScientific notation; "orders of magnitude"
UsesEstimation, comparison of scales

Uses

Orders of magnitude are used to make approximate comparisons: the Earth is about four orders of magnitude wider than a human is tall, and there are roughly 1080 atoms in the observable universe. In science and engineering, expressing quantities this way communicates scale without false precision, and Fermi problems rely on order-of-magnitude estimation.

Definition and rounding

The order of magnitude of a number is most commonly the exponent when the number is written in scientific notation. Whether 3.2 × 105 counts as "of order 105" or 106 depends on the rounding convention; the geometric midpoint √10 ≈ 3.16 is often used as the threshold. In computing, "order of magnitude" sometimes refers to powers of two.

Scales

Standardised scales built on orders of magnitude include the decibel (power), the Richter and moment magnitude scales (earthquakes, base ~31.6), pH (hydrogen-ion concentration), and stellar magnitude (brightness, base ~2.512).
