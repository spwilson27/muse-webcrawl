Taxidermy

Taxidermy is the art of preserving an animal's body via mounting or stuffing for the purpose of display or study. Animals are often, but not always, portrayed in a lifelike state. The word taxidermy describes the process of preserving the animal, but the word is also used to describe the end product, which is often called a mount.

Methods

In the modern method, the animal's skin is removed, tanned, and draped over a sculpted form, often made of polyurethane foam, clay, or wood wool. Glass eyes are installed and anatomical details such as teeth and claws are reproduced. Earlier "stuffing" methods, which used straw and rags, produced cruder results and gave rise to the term "stuffed animal." Freeze-drying is an alternative technique, especially for small animals and pets.

History

Preservation of animal skins for display goes back to the sixteenth century, when collectors of cabinets of curiosities mounted birds. Taxidermy flourished in the Victorian era, when mounted specimens became fixtures of museums and private homes. Figures such as Carl Akeley revolutionized the craft in the early twentieth century, sculpting anatomically accurate forms and creating habitat dioramas for museums including the American Museum of Natural History.

Uses

Taxidermy serves science (museum study skins), education (dioramas), and commerce (hunting trophies and fishing mounts). Rogue taxidermy, the creation of fantastical composite creatures such as the jackalope, is a recognized folk-art offshoot.
