Taishō democracy

Taishō democracy (大正デモクラシー, Taishō demokurashī) was a period in pre-war Japan, roughly coinciding with the reign of Emperor Taishō (1912–1926) and extending somewhat beyond it, during which liberal, democratic, and party-political movements flourished. The era is generally dated from about 1905 or 1912 to the early 1930s, when militarism and ultranationalism eclipsed parliamentary politics.

Background

The movement grew out of discontent with the oligarchic genrō politics of the Meiji period, in which cabinets were dominated by elder statesmen from the Satsuma and Chōshū domains. Public protests such as the Hibiya incendiary incident of 1905 and the Movement to Protect Constitutional Government (1912–1913) demanded party cabinets answerable to the Imperial Diet rather than to the oligarchs.

Political developments

Intellectuals such as Yoshino Sakuzō articulated minponshugi ("government by the people" in practice, if not in name), while Minobe Tatsukichi's "emperor-organ theory" held that sovereignty resided in the state, with the emperor as its highest organ — a doctrine that legitimised parliamentary government. The journalist and politician Hara Takashi of the Rikken Seiyūkai became prime minister in 1918 after the nationwide rice riots, forming the first genuine party cabinet.

The high point of the movement came in 1924–1925, when the Katō Takaaki cabinet — the so-called "constitutional protection" cabinet formed by three parties — passed the Universal Manhood Suffrage Law of 1925, extending the franchise to all men aged 25 and over, regardless of tax paid. At the same time, however, the Diet passed the Peace Preservation Law of 1925, which criminalised advocacy of altering the kokutai (national polity) or abolishing private property, and was used to suppress socialists and communists.

Culture and society

The period saw a flourishing of urban mass culture: department stores, cafés, cinema, jazz, and the "modern girl" (moga) phenomenon. Mass-circulation newspapers and magazines expanded political discussion beyond elite circles, and labour unions, tenant farmers' unions, and women's organisations pressed new demands on the state.

Decline

The Shōwa financial crisis of 1927, the Great Depression, the Manchurian Incident of 1931, and assassinations of political leaders (including Prime Minister Inukai Tsuyoshi in the May 15 Incident of 1932) brought party cabinets to an end. From 1932 onward, "national unity" cabinets dominated by military and bureaucratic figures replaced party government, and the emperor-organ theory itself was denounced in 1935.
