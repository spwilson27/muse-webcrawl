Portuguese India, formally the Estado da Índia (State of India), was the aggregate of Portuguese colonial possessions in India. Founded in 1505 after Vasco da Gama's arrival in 1498, its capital moved from Cochin to Goa in 1530. At its greatest extent it included coastal enclaves and fortresses from Gujarat to Bengal, though by the 20th century it had shrunk to Goa, Daman, Diu and the inland enclaves of Dadra and Nagar Haveli.

History

Under Afonso de Albuquerque, the Portuguese seized Goa (1510), Malacca and Hormuz, dominating Indian Ocean trade with armed shipping and the cartaz pass system. The capital, Velha Goa ("Golden Goa"), was famed for its churches — the Sé Cathedral and the Basilica of Bom Jesus, holding the relics of St Francis Xavier — and for the Goan Inquisition (1560–1812). Territory was lost over the centuries to the Marathas, the Dutch and the British, leaving the small enclaves that survived Indian independence in 1947.

Annexation and legacy

India took Dadra and Nagar Haveli in 1954 and, after Portugal refused to negotiate, annexed Goa, Daman and Diu in Operation Vijay (18–19 December 1961), ending 451 years of Portuguese rule. Goa became a state of India in 1987. The legacy endures in Goan Catholic culture, Indo-Portuguese architecture and cuisine, and the Konkani language's Portuguese loanwords.
