Poaching is the illegal hunting, killing or capturing of wild animals, usually contrary to local and international conservation law — hunting out of season, without a licence, in protected areas, by prohibited methods, or of protected species. It has evolved from a largely local offence against game laws into a global organised-crime enterprise: the illegal wildlife trade is valued in the billions of dollars annually and is a principal driver of species decline.

Targets and drivers

Demand for ivory, rhinoceros horn, tiger bones and skins, pangolin scales and meat, shark fins, bushmeat and exotic pets fuels poaching. The African elephant population fell sharply during the 2007–2014 ivory poaching crisis, and rhino poaching in South Africa exceeded a thousand animals a year at its 2014 peak, driven by horn prices exceeding gold. Poverty, corruption, weak enforcement and consumer demand in distant markets sustain the trade; in fisheries, illegal, unreported and unregulated (IUU) fishing is the marine analogue, and plant poaching — of cycads, orchids and timber — also causes severe losses.

Responses

The 1973 Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species (CITES) regulates cross-border trade, and commercial ivory sales were banned internationally in 1989, with China closing its domestic ivory market in 2017. On the ground, responses include armed ranger patrols, sniffer dogs, drones and camera networks, dehorning rhinos, community conservancies that give local people a stake in live wildlife, and demand-reduction campaigns in consumer countries. Rangers face lethal danger; more than a thousand have been killed on duty worldwide in recent decades.

History

Poaching laws date to medieval forest law, when deer and other game were reserved to the monarch and aristocracy and poaching could be a capital offence — a history romanticised in Robin Hood legends. Modern wildlife law emerged in the late 19th century as market hunting devastated species such as the American bison and passenger pigeon.
