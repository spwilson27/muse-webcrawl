The Grange

The National Grange of the Order of Patrons of Husbandry, commonly called the Grange, is an American fraternal and advocacy organisation for farmers and rural communities, founded in 1867 by Oliver Hudson Kelley and six associates in Washington, D.C. It is the oldest national agricultural advocacy organisation in the United States, and its local halls once anchored the social life of thousands of farming towns.

History

Kelley, a clerk in the U.S. Bureau of Agriculture, toured the war-devastated South in 1866 and concluded that farmers needed a national organisation for education, cooperation and political voice. The Grange grew explosively during the economic distress of the 1870s, reaching hundreds of thousands of members. Grangers established cooperative stores, grain elevators, mills and insurance companies, and campaigned for regulation of railroad and warehouse rates.

The movement's political pressure produced state "Granger laws" in the Midwest, and the Illinois law was upheld by the U.S. Supreme Court in Munn v. Illinois (1877), a foundational case establishing the power of states to regulate private businesses "affected with a public interest". Membership declined by the 1880s but the organisation endured, later championing rural free mail delivery, the Farm Credit System, and direct election of senators.

Structure and character

The Grange is unusual among fraternal orders in admitting women to full membership and office from its founding. Its ritual, organised in seven degrees drawn from classical agricultural imagery, reflects its fraternal origins. Local ("Subordinate"), county ("Pomona"), state and national Granges form its hierarchy.
