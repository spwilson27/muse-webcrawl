Somali Civil War

The Somali Civil War is an ongoing civil war that has taken place in Somalia since 1991. It grew out of resistance to the military junta led by Siad Barre during the 1980s. By 1988–1990, armed clan-based opposition groups had emerged, and in January 1991 Barre was overthrown. In the ensuing power struggle, Somalia fragmented: the northwest declared itself the independent Republic of Somaliland, warlords contested the capital Mogadishu, and no effective central government existed for more than two decades.

International intervention

Famine in 1992 prompted a United States-led humanitarian intervention (Operation Restore Hope) under UN auspices. After the 1993 Battle of Mogadishu — in which two US Black Hawk helicopters were shot down — international forces withdrew by 1995. A Transitional Federal Government was formed in 2004, and in 2006 Ethiopian forces helped it defeat the Islamic Courts Union in Mogadishu; the ICU's radical youth wing, al-Shabaab, then waged a long insurgency.

Federal government and al-Shabaab

The Federal Government of Somalia was established in 2012, internationally recognised as the country's first permanent central government since 1991. Supported by the African Union mission (AMISOM, later ATMIS), federal forces recovered major cities, but al-Shabaab retained control of much of the rural south and continued to carry out major attacks, including the October 2017 Mogadishu bombings that killed more than 500 people.

Humanitarian impact

The war, compounded by recurrent drought, has killed hundreds of thousands of people and displaced millions. The 2011 famine alone killed an estimated 260,000 people. Somalia has repeatedly ranked at or near the top of the Fragile States Index.
