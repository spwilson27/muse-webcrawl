Neurofibromatosis type II

SpecialtyMedical genetics, neurology
SymptomsHearing loss, tinnitus, balance problems, cataracts
Usual onsetLate teens to early twenties
CausesMutation of the NF2 gene on chromosome 22
InheritanceAutosomal dominant
FrequencyAbout 1 in 33,000–40,000 people

Neurofibromatosis type II (NF2) is a genetic disorder characterised by the growth of non-cancerous tumours in the nervous system. It is distinct from neurofibromatosis type I, despite the similar name. The hallmark of NF2 is the development of bilateral vestibular schwannomas — tumours of the vestibulocochlear nerve (the eighth cranial nerve) — which typically lead to progressive hearing loss, ringing in the ears (tinnitus) and problems with balance.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms usually appear in the late teenage years or early twenties. In addition to vestibular schwannomas, people with NF2 may develop schwannomas of other cranial and peripheral nerves, meningiomas (tumours of the membranes surrounding the brain and spinal cord), ependymomas and gliomas. Eye problems are common and include juvenile posterior subcapsular cataracts, retinal hamartomas and epiretinal membranes. Skin tumours in NF2 tend to be less prominent than in NF1.

Genetics

NF2 is caused by mutations in the NF2 gene, located on chromosome 22 (22q12.2). The gene encodes a protein called merlin (also known as schwannomin), a tumour suppressor involved in contact inhibition of cell growth. The condition is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern: a person with NF2 has a 50% chance of passing the condition to each child. Roughly half of cases arise from new (de novo) mutations with no family history.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on clinical criteria, including the presence of bilateral vestibular schwannomas seen on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), or a combination of a family history of NF2 and other characteristic tumours. Genetic testing can confirm the diagnosis and identify the specific mutation.

Treatment

There is no cure for NF2. Management focuses on monitoring tumour growth with regular MRI scans and hearing tests, surgical removal of tumours when they cause symptoms, and hearing rehabilitation such as cochlear implants or auditory brainstem implants. The drug bevacizumab has been used in some cases to shrink vestibular schwannomas and preserve hearing. Care is usually coordinated by multidisciplinary teams.
