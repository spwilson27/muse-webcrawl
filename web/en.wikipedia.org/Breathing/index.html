Breathing (or ventilation) is the process of moving air into and out of the lungs of an organism, in order to facilitate gas exchange with the internal environment — principally bringing in oxygen and flushing out carbon dioxide. Breathing supplies the oxygen required for cellular respiration and removes its waste product.


The human respiratory tract.

Mechanism

In mammals, inhalation is driven mainly by the diaphragm, which contracts and flattens, expanding the thoracic cavity; the intercostal muscles also raise the rib cage. Air flows down the resulting pressure gradient through the nose or mouth, pharynx, larynx, trachea, and bronchi to the alveoli, where gas exchange occurs across thin membranes by diffusion. Exhalation at rest is largely passive, resulting from elastic recoil of the lungs and chest wall.

Control

Breathing is generated by respiratory centres in the medulla oblongata and pons, and is adjusted by chemoreceptors that sense carbon dioxide, pH, and oxygen levels in the blood. It is normally automatic but can be overridden voluntarily, for example during speech or breath-holding.

In other organisms

Amphibians use buccal pumping, birds have air sacs that make airflow through the lungs unidirectional, and insects deliver air through tracheae. Fish extract oxygen from water using gills rather than breathing air (with exceptions such as lungfish).
