The Great Game

The Great Game was a political and diplomatic confrontation that existed for most of the 19th century and the beginning of the 20th century between the British Empire and the Russian Empire over Central Asia, particularly Afghanistan, Persia and the approaches to British India. The term was popularised by Rudyard Kipling in his 1901 novel Kim.

Origins and course

Fear that Russia, expanding southward through the khanates of Khiva, Bukhara and Kokand, intended to threaten India — the "jewel in the crown" — drove British policy from the 1830s. Agents and explorers such as Arthur Conolly (who coined the phrase), Alexander Burnes and William Moorcroft mapped and spied across the region. Rivalry produced the disastrous First Anglo-Afghan War (1839–42), in which a British army was annihilated retreating from Kabul, and the Second Anglo-Afghan War (1878–80), after which Britain controlled Afghan foreign policy while leaving the country a buffer state.

Resolution

The Panjdeh incident of 1885 nearly caused war, but diplomacy gradually fixed boundaries: the Pamir Boundary Commission of 1895 created the Wakhan Corridor, and the Anglo-Russian Convention of 1907 divided Persia into spheres of influence and settled the status of Afghanistan and Tibet, ending the classic Great Game on the eve of the First World War.

Legacy

The phrase is now applied by analogy to later struggles for influence in Central Asia — over pipelines, bases and alliances after the Cold War and the 2001 Afghan war.
