Satellite navigation

Satellite navigation is the use of artificial satellites to provide autonomous geospatial positioning anywhere on Earth. A receiver calculates its position (latitude, longitude and altitude) and precise time by measuring the arrival times of radio signals transmitted by at least four satellites in a global navigation satellite system, or GNSS. Under open sky, typical accuracy is a few metres; with corrections it can reach centimetres.

How it works

Each satellite continuously broadcasts its orbital position and the reading of its onboard atomic clock. A receiver measures the signal travel time from several satellites, converting them into ranges (pseudoranges). With ranges from four or more satellites the receiver solves for its three position coordinates and its own clock error — a process akin to trilateration through space-time. Errors from the ionosphere, troposphere, satellite clock drift and orbital uncertainty are reduced by ground control segments that monitor the constellation and upload corrections, and by augmentation systems such as WAAS, EGNOS and differential GPS.

Global systems

  • GPS (United States) — the first GNSS, operated by the U.S. Space Force; full operational capability declared in 1995.
  • GLONASS (Russia) — developed by the Soviet Union and restored to full coverage in the 2010s.
  • Galileo (European Union) — a civilian-controlled system, in service since 2016.
  • BeiDou (China) — completed in 2020 with the launch of its final satellite.

Regional systems include India's NavIC and Japan's QZSS, which augments GPS over the Asia-Pacific region.

Applications

Satellite navigation underpins in-car and smartphone directions, aviation and maritime navigation, precision agriculture, surveying, emergency services, logistics and the timing of power grids and financial networks. Its vulnerability to jamming and spoofing has prompted work on resilient alternatives and backups.
