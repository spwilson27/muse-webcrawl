Dielectric

A dielectric is an electrical insulator that can be polarised by an applied electric field. When a dielectric is placed in an electric field, its internal charges do not flow freely as in a conductor; instead they shift slightly from their equilibrium positions, producing polarisation that reduces the effective field inside the material. The term was coined by William Whewell at Michael Faraday's request, from the Greek dia- ("through") plus electric.

A dielectric between capacitor plates polarises, increasing capacitance.

Properties

A dielectric's response is quantified by its permittivity, often expressed relative to vacuum as the dielectric constant (relative permittivity). Materials such as glass, ceramics, mica, many plastics, distilled water and dry air are common dielectrics. The maximum field a dielectric can withstand without breakdown is its dielectric strength. In alternating fields, the lag of polarisation behind the field causes dielectric loss, described by the loss tangent.

Mechanisms of polarisation

Polarisation arises through several mechanisms: electronic polarisation (displacement of electron clouds), ionic polarisation (relative displacement of ions), orientational polarisation (rotation of permanent molecular dipoles, as in water), and interfacial polarisation at boundaries. Each mechanism contributes over a characteristic frequency range, making permittivity frequency-dependent.

Applications

Dielectrics are essential in capacitors, where a dielectric layer increases capacitance and prevents conduction between the plates; as electrical insulation for cables and equipment; in radio-frequency and microwave devices; and in microelectronics, where high-κ materials serve as gate insulators in transistors.
