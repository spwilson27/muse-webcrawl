A breviary (Latin: breviarium) is a liturgical book used in Christianity for praying the canonical hours, which will usually include psalms, readings, hymns and prayers for each hour of prayer throughout the day and year. The term derives from the Latin brevis, "short" or "concise", because the book condensed into one volume texts that had previously been spread across several choir books.

Development

The breviary emerged in the 11th–13th centuries as clergy and travelling friars — especially the Franciscans — needed the entire Divine Office in a single portable volume. Manuscript breviaries, often richly illuminated (the Belleville Breviary and the Breviary of Queen Isabella of Castile are famous examples), were among the most common books of the late Middle Ages.

Roman Breviary

Following the Council of Trent, Pope Pius V promulgated a standardised Roman Breviary in 1568, suppressing most local variants less than two centuries old. Successive popes revised it, notably Pius X in 1911, who rearranged the psalter so that all 150 psalms could be prayed weekly, and John XXIII in 1960. After the Second Vatican Council, Pope Paul VI promulgated the revised Liturgia Horarum (Liturgy of the Hours) in 1970, with a four-week psalter, a greatly expanded cycle of readings, and permission to use vernacular languages.

Other traditions

Anglicanism replaced the breviary's multiple daily offices with the combined Morning and Evening Prayer of the Book of Common Prayer. Lutheran and other Protestant traditions produced their own breviaries and prayer books, and Eastern Christianity uses the Horologion for the fixed texts of the daily cycle.

Use today

Catholic priests and deacons are obliged to pray the Liturgy of the Hours daily, and many religious and laypeople use the breviary, now also available in digital and app form.
