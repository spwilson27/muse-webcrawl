Anaximander (c. 610 – c. 546 BC)

Anaximander (c. 610 – c. 546 BC) was a pre-Socratic Greek philosopher who lived in Miletus, a city of Ionia (in modern-day Turkey). He belonged to the Milesian school and learned the teachings of his master Thales. He succeeded Thales and became the second master of that school where he counted Anaximenes and, arguably, Pythagoras amongst his pupils.

Little of his life and work is known today. According to available historical documents, he is the first philosopher known to have recorded his studies, although only one fragment of his work remains. Fragmentary testimonies found in documents after his death provide a portrait of the man.

Cosmology

Anaximander held that the origin or first principle (archê) of all things is the apeiron — the boundless, indefinite, or infinite — from which all things arise and into which they return. He argued that the earth floats freely in space, unsupported, "because of its indifference" to direction — an early non-mythological account of the earth's place in the universe.

In his cosmology the Earth is a short cylinder at the centre of the cosmos, and the sun, moon and stars are rings of fire enclosed in tubes of mist with vents through which their light appears, an arrangement explaining eclipses and the phases of the moon.

Other work

Anaximander is credited with drawing one of the first maps of the known world, with introducing the gnomon to Greece, and with explanations of rain, earthquakes and the origin of life: he proposed that life arose from moisture warmed by the sun, and that humans must have descended from other animals because human infants are helpless for so long — an idea sometimes described as an anticipation of evolution. He also attempted the first systematic geography and meteorology in prose.
