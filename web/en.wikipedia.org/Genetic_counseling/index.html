Genetic counseling

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Genetic counseling is the process of investigating individuals and families affected by or at risk of genetic disorders to help them understand and adapt to the medical, psychological and familial implications of genetic contributions to disease. The process integrates interpretation of family and medical histories to assess the chance of disease occurrence or recurrence; education about inheritance, testing, management, prevention, resources; and counseling to promote informed choices and adaptation to the risk or condition.

Practice

Genetic counselors are health professionals with specialised graduate degrees (typically a master's in genetic counseling) and experience in medical genetics and counseling. Sessions commonly involve drawing a pedigree (family history diagram), explaining patterns of inheritance such as autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive and X-linked conditions, discussing the benefits, limitations and possible results of genetic tests, and supporting patients' decisions without directing them (a principle called non-directiveness). Counselors work in prenatal, paediatric, cancer, cardiovascular, neurology and general adult genetics settings, as well as in laboratories and public health.

Indications

Reasons for referral include a personal or family history of a hereditary condition (such as hereditary breast and ovarian cancer or Huntington's disease), abnormal prenatal screening results, recurrent miscarriage, infertility, a child with a birth defect or developmental delay, and questions about carrier status. As genomic testing has expanded — including exome and genome sequencing and direct-to-consumer tests — counselors increasingly interpret complex or incidental findings.

History and regulation

The term was coined by Sheldon Clark Reed in 1947, and the first master's programme was established at Sarah Lawrence College in 1969. Professional bodies such as the National Society of Genetic Counselors (founded 1979) oversee certification and practice standards; licensure exists in many US states and the profession is regulated in several other countries.
