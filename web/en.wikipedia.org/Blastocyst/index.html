Blastocyst

Structure of a blastocyst: inner cell mass, trophoblast, and blastocoel.

The blastocyst is a structure formed in the early development of mammals, including humans. It possesses an inner cell mass (embryoblast) which subsequently forms the embryo, an outer layer of cells called the trophoblast (trophectoderm) that surrounds the inner cell mass and a fluid-filled cavity known as the blastocoel. The blastocyst develops from the morula about four to five days after fertilization in humans.

Development

After fertilization, the zygote undergoes cleavage divisions to form the morula, a solid ball of cells. Fluid accumulation creates the blastocoel cavity, converting the morula into a blastocyst of roughly 70–100 cells in humans. The trophoblast will contribute to the placenta and fetal membranes, while the inner cell mass gives rise to the embryo proper and is the source of embryonic stem cells. Still enclosed in the zona pellucida, the blastocyst travels to the uterus, "hatches" from the zona, and implants into the endometrium around six to ten days after fertilization.

Clinical significance

In in vitro fertilization (IVF), embryos are frequently cultured to the blastocyst stage (day 5 or 6) before transfer, because blastocyst transfer is associated with higher implantation rates per embryo and allows better selection of viable embryos, reducing multiple pregnancies. Blastocyst grading systems assess expansion, inner cell mass quality, and trophectoderm quality. Preimplantation genetic testing can be performed by biopsying a few trophectoderm cells.
