Root

A root system with primary and lateral roots.

In vascular plants, the root is the organ of a plant that is typically below the surface of the soil. Roots can also be aerial or aerating, that is, growing above the ground or above water. A stem, by contrast, normally bears buds and leaves, whereas a root normally has neither. Roots anchor the plant, absorb water and dissolved minerals, conduct them to the stem, and store reserve foods.

Functions

The primary functions of roots are the absorption of water and inorganic nutrients, anchoring of the plant body to the ground, and storage of food and nutrients. In response to the concentration of nutrients, roots also synthesise cytokinin, which acts as a signal for how fast the shoots can grow.

Anatomy

Root morphology is divided into four zones: the root cap, the apical meristem, the elongation zone, and the maturation zone. The root cap protects the growing tip as it pushes through the soil. Behind it, the apical meristem produces new cells, which elongate and then differentiate into tissues such as the epidermis, cortex, and vascular cylinder. Root hairs, extensions of epidermal cells in the maturation zone, greatly increase the absorptive surface area.

Types of root systems

Two main architectures are recognised. In a taproot system, a single primary root grows downward and gives off smaller lateral roots; this is typical of dicotyledons such as the oak and carrot. In a fibrous root system, common in monocotyledons such as grasses, many similarly sized roots arise from the base of the stem. Some plants also form adventitious roots from stems or leaves, and modified roots serve specialised functions, as in the prop roots of maize or the pneumatophores of mangroves.

Symbioses

Many roots form mutualistic associations with fungi, known as mycorrhizae, which improve nutrient uptake, particularly of phosphorus. The roots of legumes host nitrogen-fixing bacteria of the genus Rhizobium in specialised nodules, enriching the soil with usable nitrogen.
