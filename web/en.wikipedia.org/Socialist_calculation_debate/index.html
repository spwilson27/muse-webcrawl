Socialist calculation debate

The socialist calculation debate (also called the economic calculation debate) was a major twentieth-century controversy in economics over whether rational economic calculation is possible in a socialist economy without market prices for the means of production. It pitted Austrian School economists Ludwig von Mises and Friedrich Hayek against proponents of planned and market-socialist models including Oskar Lange, Abba Lerner, and Fred M. Taylor.

Mises's challenge

In a 1920 paper, "Economic Calculation in the Socialist Commonwealth," Mises argued that without private ownership of capital goods there would be no market for them, hence no money prices expressing their relative scarcity, and hence no way for planners to compare alternative uses of resources — reducing economic decision-making to "groping in the dark." Hayek later reframed the problem as one of knowledge: the information needed for coordination is dispersed, local, and often tacit, and prices function as a communication system aggregating it; his 1945 essay "The Use of Knowledge in Society" became the classic statement.

The socialist response

Lange (1936–37) proposed market socialism: a Central Planning Board would set prices parametrically and adjust them by trial and error in response to shortages and surpluses, while state-firm managers followed rules to minimize costs and produce where price equals marginal cost — formally mimicking a competitive market. Lerner's Economics of Control (1944) developed related rules. Earlier, Enrico Barone (1908) had shown that the equilibrium conditions of a collectivist economy could in principle be written down, while granting the practical difficulty of solving them.

Assessment

Opinions remain divided. Many economists regard the collapse of the Soviet-style economies as vindicating the Austrian critique, especially its knowledge and incentive aspects; others argue that modern computing, input-output methods, and large firms' internal planning show calculation without markets is more feasible than Mises allowed, and debates continue in the literatures on participatory economics, digital planning, and the economics of information. The debate is foundational to comparative economic systems and to the theory of prices.
