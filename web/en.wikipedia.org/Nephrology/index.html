Nephrology

Nephrology is a subspecialty of internal medicine concerned with the study of the kidneys — their normal function, diseases, and treatment, including the preservation of kidney health and the management of kidney failure. The term derives from the Greek nephros, "kidney". A physician specialising in nephrology is a nephrologist. (Nephrology is distinct from urology, the surgical specialty of the urinary tract.)

Scope

Nephrologists diagnose and manage chronic kidney disease and acute kidney injury; glomerular diseases such as glomerulonephritis and the nephrotic syndrome; tubulointerstitial disorders; electrolyte and acid–base disturbances; kidney stones in their medical aspects; and the systemic consequences of kidney failure, including anaemia and mineral-bone disease. Nephrologists also manage hypertension related to the kidneys and oversee renal replacement therapy: haemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, and the long-term care of kidney transplant recipients.

Procedures and tools

Diagnostic tools include urinalysis and microscopy, imaging, and percutaneous kidney biopsy, which many nephrologists perform. Nephrologists also place temporary dialysis catheters and manage dialysis prescriptions, anticoagulation, and ultrafiltration targets. Experimental and clinical treatment of kidney disease advanced rapidly in the mid-20th century: Willem Kolff built the first working dialysis machine in the 1940s, and the first successful kidney transplant was performed in 1954.

Training

In most countries, nephrology training follows qualification in internal medicine (or paediatrics for paediatric nephrologists), with fellowship training of two or more additional years. Major professional bodies include the American Society of Nephrology and the International Society of Nephrology.
