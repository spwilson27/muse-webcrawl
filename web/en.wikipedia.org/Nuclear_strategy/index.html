Nuclear strategy

Nuclear strategy is the set of concepts and policies that govern the deployment, targeting, and threatened or actual use of nuclear weapons. Because nuclear weapons are so destructive, strategy focuses less on battlefield employment than on deterrence, escalation control, and war termination.

Core concepts

Strategies are commonly classified by targeting: countervalue strategies threaten an adversary's cities and population, while counterforce strategies target its military forces and nuclear weapons. Doctrines differ on whether nuclear weapons should be used first (first use) or only in retaliation (no first use), a policy formally adopted by China and India. The idea of a limited nuclear war — using a small number of weapons to signal resolve — remains controversial, since many analysts doubt escalation could be controlled.

Cold War development

United States doctrine evolved from the "massive retaliation" of the 1950s through the "flexible response" of the Kennedy administration to the Schlesinger Doctrine of 1974 and Presidential Directive 59 in 1980, which added limited options. The Soviet Union publicly disavowed some Western concepts while planning large counterforce strikes of its own. Strategic planning in the United States is embodied in the Single Integrated Operational Plan (SIOP) and its successors.

Post–Cold War

Since 1991, arsenals have shrunk greatly, but nuclear strategy has been complicated by missile defence, precision conventional weapons, cyber capabilities, and new nuclear states. Nuclear deterrence remains the organising principle for all declared nuclear powers.
