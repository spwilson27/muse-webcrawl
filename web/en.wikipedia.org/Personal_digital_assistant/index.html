Personal digital assistant

A personal digital assistant (PDA) is a multi-purpose mobile device which functions as a personal information manager. PDAs were largely displaced in the 2010s by the widespread adoption of highly capable smartphones; the term nevertheless survived in the notion of the digital assistant — software that manages a user's information. Almost all modern PDAs had the ability to connect to the Internet, and many featured touchscreens.

History

The term "personal digital assistant" was coined by Apple CEO John Sculley in 1992 with reference to the Apple Newton, launched in 1993, which pioneered handwriting recognition. The category took off with the PalmPilot (1996), whose simplified Graffiti handwriting system and easy synchronisation with desktop computers made it a mass-market success. Competitors included Psion's organisers, Microsoft's Pocket PC and Windows Mobile devices, Handspring's Treo, and BlackBerry handsets, which added mobile e-mail.

Typical functions

  • Calendar, address book and to-do lists ("PIM" functions);
  • Note-taking and document viewing;
  • E-mail and web browsing over Wi-Fi or cellular data;
  • Media playback and third-party applications;
  • In later devices, telephony, cameras and GPS navigation.

Decline

The launch of the iPhone in 2007 and the rise of Android smartphones absorbed the PDA's functions into phones with larger screens, app stores and always-on connectivity. Palm was sold to Hewlett-Packard in 2010 and its webOS line discontinued the following year, marking the end of the standalone PDA era. Voice-activated "personal digital assistants" such as Siri, Alexa and Google Assistant inherited the name in software form.
