Earl

Earl is a rank of the nobility in the United Kingdom. The title originates in the Old English word eorl, meaning "a man of noble birth or rank". The word is cognate with the Scandinavian form jarl. After the Norman Conquest, it was the equivalent of the continental count. In modern Britain, an earl is a member of the peerage, ranking below a marquess and above a viscount. A feminine form of earl never developed; instead, the wife of an earl is styled countess.

History

In Anglo-Saxon England, earls were the chief royal officers in the shires, succeeding the ealdormen. Under Canute, England was divided into four great earldoms — Wessex, Mercia, East Anglia and Northumbria — whose holders, such as Godwin, Earl of Wessex, wielded near-viceregal power. After 1066 the Normans retained the title but steadily reduced the earls' governing functions, and the dignity became largely honorary.

Styles and insignia

An earl is addressed as "Lord [title]" and styled "The Right Honourable the Earl of [title]". At coronations, earls wear a coronet bearing eight strawberry leaves and eight silver balls on points. Famous earldoms include the Earldom of Wessex, created in 1999 for Prince Edward, and historic creations such as the Earl of Warwick.
