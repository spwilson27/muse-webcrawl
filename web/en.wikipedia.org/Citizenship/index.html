Citizenship

Citizenship is the legal status of membership in a political community, usually evidenced by documents such as a passport.

Citizenship is the status of a person recognised under the law of a state (or political community) as a legal member of that state. Citizenship carries both rights — such as the right to vote, to live and work in the state, and to receive its protection abroad — and duties, such as obeying its laws and, in some states, paying taxes or performing military service.

Acquisition

Citizenship is most commonly acquired at birth, either by descent from a citizen parent (jus sanguinis, "right of blood") or by birth within the state's territory (jus soli, "right of the soil"), or by a combination of the two. Adults may acquire citizenship later through naturalisation, usually after a period of residence and demonstration of language knowledge and good character. Citizenship may also be acquired through marriage or adoption in some jurisdictions, and can in some cases be renounced or revoked; a person with no citizenship is described as stateless.

History

The concept originated in the city-states of ancient Greece, where citizens participated directly in political life, and was extended across the Roman Empire, most completely by the Edict of Caracalla (AD 212). Modern citizenship developed with the nation-state after the French Revolution and expanded over the nineteenth and twentieth centuries with the abolition of property qualifications and the enfranchisement of women.

Multiple and supranational citizenship

Many states permit dual or multiple citizenship. Since 1992, citizens of European Union member states also hold citizenship of the European Union, conferring rights of free movement and residence across the Union.
