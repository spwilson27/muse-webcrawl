Scientific literature

Scientific literature comprises scholarly publications that report original empirical and theoretical work in the natural and social sciences. Within an academic field, scientific literature is often collectively referred to as "the literature". The most common form is the scientific paper published in a peer-reviewed academic journal; the literature also includes conference proceedings, scholarly books and chapters, review articles, theses, patents, and preprints.

Types of publications

Original research articles report new findings, typically structured with an abstract, introduction, methods, results, and discussion (the IMRaD format). Review articles synthesize and evaluate previously published research; highly cited types include narrative reviews, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses. Letters and short communications announce urgent results rapidly, while registered reports and data papers are newer formats.

Peer review and dissemination

Before publication, manuscripts are generally assessed through peer review by independent experts. The scientific literature is indexed in bibliographic databases such as Web of Science, Scopus, PubMed, and Google Scholar, and articles are persistently identified by digital object identifiers (DOIs). The volume of literature has grown exponentially since the 17th century, when the first journals — the Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society and the Journal des sçavans, both founded in 1665 — appeared.

Status and critique

The literature is the primary medium through which scientific knowledge is certified, cited, and built upon; citation counts and journal impact factors are widely used (and widely criticized) measures of influence. Concerns about the literature include publication bias toward positive results, the replication crisis, predatory journals, and paywalls restricting access — issues that have fueled the open access and open science movements.
