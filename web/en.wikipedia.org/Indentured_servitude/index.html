Indentured servitude

Indentured servitude is a form of labor in which a person (an indentured servant) contracts to work without salary for an employer for a fixed period, typically in exchange for transportation, food, clothing, lodging, and other necessities during the term. The contract, called an indenture, could often be bought and sold. Indentured servitude was a major source of labor in European colonies in the Americas from the early 17th century, and later systems moved large numbers of Indian and Chinese laborers around the British, French, and Dutch empires in the 19th and early 20th centuries.

Colonial America

In British America, a large share of European immigrants — estimates range from one-half to two-thirds of those arriving in the Chesapeake colonies — came as indentured servants, signing terms of usually four to seven years in exchange for passage across the Atlantic. At the end of the term, servants were legally free and might receive "freedom dues" such as land, tools, or clothing, though many died before completing their terms and conditions could be harsh. Servants could be punished by courts, and their terms extended, for running away or other offenses. The system declined as chattel slavery expanded in the late 17th and 18th centuries.

Indian indenture system

After the abolition of slavery in the British Empire in the 1830s, planters recruited indentured laborers from India for sugar plantations in Mauritius, the Caribbean, Natal, Fiji, Malaya, and elsewhere. Between the 1830s and 1917, well over a million Indians emigrated under indenture. Recruits signed agreements (the origin of the term girmit, from "agreement") for five-year terms, after which they could re-indenture or eventually claim return passage. Abuses led to regulation and, under pressure from Indian nationalists including Mahatma Gandhi, the system's abolition in 1917–1920. Descendant communities (Indo-Caribbean, Indo-Fijian, and others) remain significant today.
