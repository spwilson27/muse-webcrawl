Source criticism

Source criticism (or information evaluation) is the process of evaluating the qualities of an information source, such as a document, speech, picture, or any other artefact, in order to assess its reliability and usefulness as evidence.

Principles

Historians conventionally distinguish external criticism — establishing a source's authenticity, authorship, date, and provenance — from internal criticism — evaluating the accuracy and meaning of its contents. Classic criteria, formulated by Scandinavian historians and systematised by writers such as Bernheim and Langlois and Seignobos, ask whether a source is close in time and place to the events it describes, whether it depends on other sources, what tendencies or biases its author had, and whether independent sources corroborate it.

Applications

In biblical studies, source criticism specifically denotes the analysis of composite texts into their underlying written sources — classically, the division of the Pentateuch into the J, E, D, and P sources of the documentary hypothesis. In journalism and modern media literacy, source evaluation tests such as lateral reading and the CRAAP test serve an analogous purpose for contemporary information. In law, related questions concern the admissibility and weight of evidence.
