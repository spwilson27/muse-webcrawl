Toramana (c. 493 – 515 CE) was a ruler of the Alchon Huns who extended Hunnic power deep into northern India at the beginning of the 6th century CE. He is known from his coins and from inscriptions, including the Eran boar inscription, which records that in the first year of his reign the region around Eran in present-day Madhya Pradesh was under his authority.

Reign

Toramana's coinage imitated Gupta and earlier Hunnic types and circulated widely across the Punjab, Rajasthan, Gujarat and Malwa. Inscriptions associated with his reign, such as the Kura inscription from the Salt Range and the Gwalior inscription of his son, indicate that his authority was recognised from Gandhara to central India. Unlike his son and successor Mihirakula, who became notorious in later literary tradition for persecution of Buddhists, Toramana appears in some sources as a patron of learning; the Jain text Kuvalayamala associates scholarly activity with his court at Pavvaiya on the Chenab.

Legacy

Toramana's campaigns contributed to the decline of the Gupta Empire's control over western and central India. He was succeeded by Mihirakula, whose defeat by Indian rulers in the 520s–530s marked the turning point of Hunnic power in India.
