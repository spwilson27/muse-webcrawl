X-ray binary

TypeBinary star system
Compact objectNeutron star or black hole
EmissionX-rays from accreted matter

An X-ray binary is a class of binary star system that is luminous in X-rays, in which one component is a compact object — a neutron star or a black hole — accreting matter from a normal companion star. The infalling gas forms an accretion disk, heats to millions of degrees, and radiates strongly in X-rays before falling onto or into the compact object.

Classification

X-ray binaries are divided by the mass of the donor star. In high-mass X-ray binaries (HMXBs), the companion is a massive young star, often an O or B star or a Be star; the first discovered example was Centaurus X-3, and the famous Cygnus X-1 (a black hole system) belongs to this class. In low-mass X-ray binaries (LMXBs), the donor is a low-mass, older star that transfers mass by Roche-lobe overflow; examples include Scorpius X-1, the brightest persistent X-ray source in the sky, and many X-ray transients and microquasars.

Phenomena

Accreting neutron stars in these systems can show type I X-ray bursts — thermonuclear flashes on the neutron star surface — and millisecond pulsations. Black hole binaries exhibit state changes, quasi-periodic oscillations, and relativistic jets. X-ray binaries are key laboratories for studying accretion physics and strong gravity.
