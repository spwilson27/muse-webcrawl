Log-normal distribution

Log-normal probability density functions

In probability theory, a log-normal (or lognormal) distribution is a continuous probability distribution of a random variable whose logarithm is normally distributed. Thus, if the random variable X is log-normally distributed, then Y = ln(X) has a normal distribution. Equivalently, if Y has a normal distribution, then the exponential function of Y, X = exp(Y), has a log-normal distribution.

A log-normal process is the statistical realisation of the multiplicative product of many independent random variables, each of which is positive — just as the normal distribution describes the additive effect of many independent variables (central limit theorem). The distribution is named by analogy with the normal distribution.

Characterisation

The probability density function of a log-normal random variable with parameters μ (the mean of the logarithm) and σ (the standard deviation of the logarithm) is:

f(x) = (1 / (x σ √(2π))) · exp( −(ln x − μ)² / (2σ²) ),   x > 0.

The expected value is exp(μ + σ²/2) and the variance is [exp(σ²) − 1] · exp(2μ + σ²). The distribution is positively skewed, with a long right tail; the mode is exp(μ − σ²) and the median is exp(μ).

Applications

Log-normal distributions model quantities that arise from multiplicative growth processes: sizes of organisms, incomes within an economy, particle sizes in crushed materials, latency in networks, lengths of words and sentences, and the distribution of mineral deposits. In finance, the Black–Scholes model assumes asset prices follow a log-normal distribution (geometric Brownian motion).
