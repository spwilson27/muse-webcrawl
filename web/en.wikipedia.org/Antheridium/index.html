Antheridium

An antheridium is a haploid structure or organ that produces and contains the male gametes (antherozoids or sperm) of many plants, algae and fungi. It is the male counterpart of the archegonium, the female organ, and the two together feature in the life cycles of bryophytes, pteridophytes and various algae.

Structure

A typical antheridium consists of a sterile outer jacket of cells enclosing spermatogenous tissue. In mosses and ferns it is often club-shaped and borne on a short stalk; in many algae the structures are simpler. When mature and wetted, the antheridium opens and releases numerous small, usually flagellated sperm cells, which swim through a film of water to reach an egg cell in an archegonium — one reason these plants reproduce most successfully in moist environments.

Occurrence

Antheridia occur in the gametophyte generation of bryophytes (mosses, liverworts and hornworts), in ferns and their allies, and in a reduced form within the pollen of some gymnosperms. In fungi, analogous male gametangia are found in groups such as the oomycetes. The term was coined in the nineteenth century as plant anatomy and alternation of generations were worked out by botanists such as Wilhelm Hofmeister.
