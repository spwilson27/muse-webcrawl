Anti-satellite weapon

Anti-satellite weapons (ASAT) are space weapons designed to incapacitate or destroy satellites for strategic or tactical purposes. Several nations possess operational ASAT capabilities, and others are pursuing development. No ASAT weapon is known to have been used in warfare, although several countries have successfully shot down their own satellites to demonstrate their capabilities.

Types

Direct-ascent weapons are launched from the ground or from aircraft to collide with a satellite in orbit, as in the 1985 US ASM-135 test, China's 2007 destruction of the Fengyun-1C weather satellite, India's Mission Shakti test in 2019, and Russia's 2021 destruction of Cosmos 1408. Co-orbital weapons are placed in orbit to approach and disable a target. Non-kinetic methods include jamming, dazzling sensors with lasers, and cyber attacks.

Debris and concerns

Kinetic destruction creates clouds of long-lived orbital debris that endanger other satellites and crewed spacecraft, raising fears of the Kessler syndrome. The 2007 and 2021 tests each generated thousands of trackable fragments and drew international condemnation. In 2022 the United States committed to not conducting direct-ascent ASAT missile tests, and a UN General Assembly resolution supporting such a moratorium gained wide support.
