Chimeric antigen receptor T cell

Chimeric antigen receptor T cells (also known as CAR T cells) are T cells that have been genetically engineered to produce an artificial T-cell receptor for use in immunotherapy. The receptors are chimeric because they combine an antibody-derived antigen-binding domain with T-cell signaling domains, allowing the engineered cells to recognize and kill cells displaying a chosen target antigen, typically CD19 on B-cell cancers.

Production

A patient's own T cells are collected by leukapheresis, activated, and modified — usually with a viral vector — to express the CAR. The cells are expanded in culture and infused back into the patient after lymphodepleting chemotherapy.

Clinical use

The first CAR T-cell therapies, tisagenlecleucel (Kymriah) and axicabtagene ciloleucel (Yescarta), were approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration in 2017 for certain relapsed or refractory B-cell leukemias and lymphomas. Several further products have followed, including therapies targeting BCMA for multiple myeloma. Response rates in some blood cancers have been strikingly high, with durable remissions in patients who had exhausted other options.

Safety

Serious adverse effects include cytokine release syndrome, a systemic inflammatory response to mass T-cell activation, and immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS). Efforts to extend CAR T therapy to solid tumors face challenges of antigen heterogeneity and the tumor microenvironment.
