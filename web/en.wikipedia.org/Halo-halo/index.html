Halo-halo

Halo-halo is a popular cold dessert in the Philippines made of crushed ice, evaporated milk, and various ingredients.

Halo-halo, officially spelled haluhalo, is a popular cold dessert in the Philippines made up of crushed ice, evaporated milk or coconut milk, and various ingredients including ube jam (ube halaya), sweetened garbanzo beans, coconut strips, sago, gulaman (agar), pinipig, boiled taro or soft yams in cubes, flan, slices or portions of fruit preserves and other root crop preserves. The dessert is often topped with a scoop of ube ice cream and a slice of leche flan. It is usually served in a tall glass or bowl.

The name derives from the Tagalog word halo, "mix", repeated for emphasis — the dessert is mixed before eating.

History

The origins of halo-halo are traced to the pre-war Japanese Filipino community and the Japanese dessert kakigōri, shaved ice sweetened with milk. Japanese immigrants introduced sweetened mung beans, kidney beans, and garbanzos served over shaved ice; during the American period, the widespread availability of evaporated milk and refrigeration, along with ice plants built under the US administration, allowed the dessert to develop into its modern form. After the Second World War, the recipe absorbed local ingredients such as ube and macapuno.

Preparation

There is no single canonical recipe: sweetened beans, jellies, fruits (such as jackfruit, saba bananas, nata de coco, and kaong), and root crops are layered at the bottom of a tall glass, covered with finely shaved or crushed ice, and drizzled with evaporated milk. Premium or "special" versions are topped with ube halaya, leche flan, and ice cream. The diner mixes everything together with a long spoon before eating. Halo-halo is sold by street vendors, market stalls, and restaurant chains throughout the Philippines, and is especially popular during the hot summer months. Versions also exist in the Filipino diaspora and it has been widely featured in international food media as an emblematic Filipino dessert.
