Byronic hero

The Byronic hero is a type of antihero named after the English Romantic poet Lord Byron. Both Byron's own persona and the characters in his works — notably the heroes of Childe Harold's Pilgrimage (1812–1818) and the verse drama Manfred (1817) — embodied the type, which became one of the most durable character archetypes in Western literature.

Characteristics

The Byronic hero is typically a brooding, passionate and self-destructive figure. Common traits include intelligence and perceptiveness, sophistication, emotional sensitivity, cynicism and contempt for social institutions and moral norms, a troubled or mysterious past, arrogance, self-exile from society, and a capacity for deep but doomed love. Unlike the traditional hero, his flaws are central: he is simultaneously attractive and morally compromised, and his rebellion often leads to his own ruin.

Origins and development

Antecedents include Milton's Satan, the Gothic villains of Ann Radcliffe and Matthew Lewis, and Goethe's Faust. Byron fused these influences with his own scandalous public image, creating heroes who seemed autobiographical. The type spread rapidly through European literature, appearing in works such as Pushkin's Eugene Onegin and Lermontov's Pechorin in A Hero of Our Time.

Influence

The Byronic hero shaped later fiction from Emily Brontë's Heathcliff in Wuthering Heights and Charlotte Brontë's Mr Rochester in Jane Eyre to the moody protagonists of Gothic and Romantic fiction generally. In modern popular culture, descendants of the type appear across film, comics and fantasy, in morally ambiguous, charismatic outsiders who defy society while carrying inner torment.
