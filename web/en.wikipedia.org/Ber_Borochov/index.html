Ber Borochov

Born3 July 1881, Zolotonosha, Russian Empire (now Ukraine)
Died17 December 1917 (aged 36), Kyiv
Known forFounder of Poale Zion; Marxist Zionism
OccupationTheorist, journalist, philologist

Dov Ber Borochov (3 July 1881 – 17 December 1917) was a Marxist Zionist theorist and one of the founders of the Poale Zion movement. He is best known for attempting to synthesize Marxism with Jewish nationalism, arguing that the Jewish people required a national territory in order to undergo normal class development.

Early life

Borochov was born in Zolotonosha, in the Poltava Governorate of the Russian Empire, and grew up in Poltava. He received a secular education and taught himself several languages, later becoming a noted philologist of the Yiddish language. Barred from university study as a Jew, he pursued independent scholarship while becoming active in socialist politics.

Political theory

In works such as The National Question and the Class Struggle (1905) and Our Platform (1906), Borochov argued that Jewish workers in the diaspora occupied an "inverted pyramid" of occupations, concentrated in commerce and light industry rather than primary production. Because of this abnormal economic structure, he held, Jewish class struggle could only succeed if Jews achieved territorial concentration, and he saw Palestine as the natural focus of Jewish immigration driven by what he called "stychic" (spontaneous) processes.

Poale Zion

Borochov co-founded the Poale Zion (Workers of Zion) party in the Russian Empire in 1906 and helped organize its world federation. He traveled widely in Europe and, in 1914, toured the United States and Canada promoting the movement and raising funds for Jewish workers in Palestine. He returned to Russia after the February Revolution of 1917.

Death and legacy

Borochov died of pneumonia in Kyiv in December 1917 while on a speaking tour. His synthesis of Marxism and Zionism became the ideological foundation of Labor Zionism, influencing figures such as David Ben-Gurion and Berl Katznelson. The kibbutz movement and the Israeli labor parties drew heavily on his writings. He is also remembered for foundational work in Yiddish philology, including the journal Der Pinkes.
