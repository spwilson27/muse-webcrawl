The Prince (Italian: Il Principe) is a 16th-century political treatise by the Italian diplomat and political theorist Niccolò Machiavelli. It was written around 1513, after Machiavelli's dismissal and exile from Florentine politics, and published posthumously in 1532, five years after his death. The work is dedicated to Lorenzo de' Medici and presents a candid analysis of how princes acquire, hold, and lose power.

Contents

Across 26 chapters, Machiavelli distinguishes hereditary principalities from new ones, examines the use of one's own arms versus mercenaries, and argues that a ruler must be prepared to act against conventional morality when necessity requires. Famous chapters discuss Cesare Borgia as a model new prince, the balance between being loved and feared ("it is better to be feared than loved, if one cannot be both"), and the role of fortuna (fortune) versus virtù (skill and will) in political success. The final chapter exhorts an Italian prince to liberate Italy from foreign domination.

Reception and legacy

The book was placed on the Catholic Church's Index of Prohibited Books, and "Machiavellian" became a byword for cunning and amorality in politics. Yet modern scholars read The Prince as a founding work of realist political science — an empirical study of power as it is practised rather than as it ought to be. Some interpret it as satire or as a coded republican warning. Its influence extends from early modern statecraft through the writings of political philosophers such as Hobbes and the American Founders, to modern discussions of leadership and strategy.
