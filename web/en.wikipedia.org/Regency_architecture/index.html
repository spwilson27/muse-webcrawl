Regency architecture

Regency architecture refers to classical buildings built in the United Kingdom during the Regency era in the early 19th century, when George IV was Prince Regent, and to earlier and later periods of similar style. The period coincides with the Biedermeier style in the German-speaking lands, Federal architecture in the United States, and the French Empire style.

Characteristics

Regency buildings are typically constructed of brick stuccoed and painted in a light colour, with elegant detailing. Many are arranged in terraces or crescents, presenting a unified palace-like façade to the street while individual houses sit behind. Characteristic features include bow windows, wrought- or cast-iron balcony railings and verandas, shallow-pitched slate roofs hidden behind parapets, and fine, attenuated classical mouldings.

The style is strongly associated with the architect John Nash, whose work for the Prince Regent included Regent Street, the terraces around Regent's Park, and the remodelling of Buckingham Palace. The seaside resort of Brighton, with Nash's exotic Royal Pavilion and the stucco squares of Kemp Town and Brunswick Town, is among the finest surviving ensembles, as are parts of Cheltenham, Leamington Spa and Bath.

Later influence

The Regency style was succeeded by the more archaeologically varied tastes of the Victorian era, but its disciplined terraces and stucco crescents were widely imitated in 20th-century neo-Georgian and speculative housing.
