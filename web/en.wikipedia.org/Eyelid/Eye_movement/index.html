Eye movement

Eye movement includes the voluntary or involuntary movement of the eyes. Eye movements are used in a number of disciplines including psychology, ophthalmology, and neuroscience. The eyes are never completely still: they are moved by six extraocular muscles to direct the fovea — the region of sharpest vision — toward objects of interest.

Types of eye movement

Saccades are rapid, ballistic movements that shift fixation between points; they are among the fastest movements the body produces. Between saccades, fixations hold the image steady on the fovea while visual information is acquired. Smooth pursuit movements follow a moving target. Vergence movements rotate the two eyes in opposite directions to fixate near or far objects. The vestibulo-ocular reflex stabilizes the image during head movement, while optokinetic movements respond to large-field motion. Small involuntary movements during fixation include tremor, drift, and microsaccades.

Reading

In reading, the eyes move in a series of saccades averaging 7–9 letter spaces, separated by fixations of about 200–250 ms, with roughly 10–15% of saccades moving backward as regressions. Skilled readers process words in the perceptual span extending asymmetrically in the direction of reading.

Clinical and research use

Abnormal eye movements can indicate neurological disease; examination of eye movements is part of the standard neurological exam. Eye tracking technology records gaze for research in perception, marketing, and human–computer interaction, and for assistive devices.
