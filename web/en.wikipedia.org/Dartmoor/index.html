Dartmoor is an upland area in southern Devon, South West England. The moorland and surrounding land have been protected by National Park status since 1951. Dartmoor National Park covers 954 km² (368 sq mi) and is known for its granite tors — exposed outcrops of the Dartmoor granite batholith — its Bronze Age monuments, its ponies, and its changeable weather. The highest points, High Willhays and Yes Tor, are the highest land in southern England.

History

Dartmoor is one of the richest prehistoric landscapes in Britain, with thousands of hut circles, stone rows, cists and cairns dating from the Neolithic and Bronze Age, including the ceremonial complex at Merrivale and the settlement of Grimspound. In the medieval period the moor was a royal forest and the centre of the Devon stannary (tin-mining) industry; the stannary towns of Ashburton, Chagford, Tavistock and Plympton administered the tin trade. The remote prison at Princetown, built from 1806 for Napoleonic prisoners of war, became the famous Dartmoor Prison.

Geography and wildlife

The moor consists of a high granite plateau, boggy in the north and west, incised by the valleys of rivers including the Dart, Teign, Tavy, Plym and Erme. Blanket bog, heather moorland and ancient oak woodland such as Wistman's Wood support wildlife including Dartmoor ponies, otters, ring ouzels and the rare marsh fritillary butterfly. The northern third of the park is used as a military training area, with live-firing ranges at Okehampton, Merrivale and Willsworthy.

In culture

Dartmoor's bleak mists and legends — including pixies, phantom hounds and the Hairy Hands — made it the setting of Arthur Conan Doyle's The Hound of the Baskervilles (1902). Access and wild-camping rights on the moor have been the subject of major modern legal disputes, culminating in Supreme Court litigation over the public's right to camp on Dartmoor commons.
