Epley maneuver

The Epley maneuver, or canalith repositioning procedure, is a series of head and body movements used to treat benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) of the posterior semicircular canal. It was described by Dr. John Epley in 1992. The maneuver guides loose calcium carbonate crystals (otoconia) out of the semicircular canal and back into the utricle of the inner ear, where they no longer provoke vertigo.

The sequence of positions used in the Epley maneuver for right-sided BPPV

Procedure

The patient begins seated, with the head turned 45 degrees toward the affected side. The patient is then moved quickly back so the head hangs slightly over the edge of the table; after remaining in each position for about 30 seconds to a minute (or until vertigo and nystagmus stop), the head is rotated 90 degrees to the opposite side, then the body and head are rolled a further 90 degrees so the patient lies on their side looking toward the floor. Finally the patient sits up. The maneuver may be repeated until no nystagmus is provoked.

Effectiveness

Clinical studies and systematic reviews find the Epley maneuver highly effective, with a single treatment resolving symptoms in a large majority of patients with posterior canal BPPV; modified versions can be performed at home. A related procedure, the Semont maneuver, is an alternative for the same condition.
