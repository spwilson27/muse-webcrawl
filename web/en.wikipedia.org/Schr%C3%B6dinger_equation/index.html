Schrödinger equation

The Schrödinger equation is a linear partial differential equation that governs the wave function of a non-relativistic quantum-mechanical system. Its discovery by Austrian-Irish physicist Erwin Schrödinger in 1925 was a landmark result of quantum theory, and he received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1933 for it.

Form

In its time-dependent form, the equation states that the Hamiltonian operator acting on the wave function Ψ equals iħ times the time derivative of Ψ. In the time-independent case it becomes an eigenvalue equation HΨ = EΨ, whose solutions give the allowed energy levels of systems such as the hydrogen atom.

Interpretation

Following Max Born's statistical interpretation, the squared modulus of the wave function gives the probability density of measurement outcomes. The Schrödinger equation plays the role in quantum mechanics that Newton's laws and conservation of energy play in classical mechanics.

Solutions

Exactly solvable cases include the particle in a box, the quantum harmonic oscillator, and the hydrogen atom; most systems require approximation methods such as perturbation theory.
