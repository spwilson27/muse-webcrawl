Network science

A visualization of a complex network showing nodes, edges, and highly connected hubs.

Network science is an academic field which studies complex networks such as telecommunication networks, computer networks, biological networks, cognitive and semantic networks, and social networks, considering distinct elements or actors represented by nodes (or vertices) and the connections between the elements or actors as links (or edges). The field draws on theories and methods including graph theory from mathematics, statistical mechanics from physics, data mining and information visualization from computer science, inferential modeling from statistics, and social structure from sociology.

History

The study of networks has emerged in diverse disciplines as a means of analyzing complex relational data. The earliest known paper in this field is the celebrated Seven Bridges of Königsberg written by Leonhard Euler in 1736. Euler's mathematical description of vertices and edges was the foundation of graph theory. In the 20th century, sociologists such as Jacob Moreno developed sociograms to study social relationships, and Paul Erdős and Alfréd Rényi formalized random graph models. The modern era of network science began around 1998–2000 with the work of Duncan Watts and Steven Strogatz on small-world networks and of Albert-László Barabási and Réka Albert on scale-free networks governed by preferential attachment.

Network properties

Networks are often described by measures such as degree (the number of connections a node has), clustering coefficient, centrality, and path length. Many real networks exhibit a small-world property — most nodes can be reached from any other in a small number of steps — and heavy-tailed degree distributions in which a few hubs hold a disproportionate share of connections. Community structure, in which nodes group into densely connected modules, is another common feature.

Applications

Network science is applied to epidemiology (modeling the spread of disease over contact networks), neuroscience (brain connectomics), ecology (food webs), infrastructure resilience, and the study of the World Wide Web and online social media.
