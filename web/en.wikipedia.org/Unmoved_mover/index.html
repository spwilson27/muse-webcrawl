Unmoved mover

The unmoved mover (Ancient Greek: ὃ οὐ κινούμενον κινεῖ, lit. "that which moves without being moved") or prime mover is a concept advanced by Aristotle as a primary cause (or first uncaused cause) or "mover" of all the motion in the universe. As is implicit in the name, the unmoved mover moves other things, but is itself not moved by any prior action.

Aristotle's argument

In Book XII of the Metaphysics and Book VIII of the Physics, Aristotle argues that motion is eternal and cannot have had a beginning, because every motion is caused by something moved by another, and an infinite regress of moved movers cannot explain motion. There must therefore exist an eternal, immaterial substance whose activity is the cause of motion without itself being moved. The unmoved mover causes motion as a final cause — as the object of desire and thought moves the desirer and thinker — in the way that a beloved moves a lover.

Nature of the unmoved mover

Aristotle describes the unmoved mover as pure actuality (actus purus), without potentiality or matter, whose life is the activity of thought thinking itself (Greek: νόησις νοήσεως, "thought of thought"). It is responsible for the eternal circular motion of the outermost celestial sphere, which in Aristotle's cosmology is transmitted to the sublunary world. Aristotle's discussion also entertains a plurality of unmoved movers corresponding to the motions of the planets, though a single supreme mover governs the whole.

Later influence

The concept profoundly influenced medieval philosophy and theology. Islamic philosophers such as al-Farabi and Avicenna, the Jewish philosopher Maimonides, and Christian scholastics — most famously Thomas Aquinas in the first of his Five Ways — adapted the argument from motion into proofs for the existence of God, identifying the prime mover with God. Critics from later periods questioned both the premise that motion requires a continuing mover and the step from a first cause to a single deity.
