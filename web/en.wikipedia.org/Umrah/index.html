The Umrah (Arabic: عُمْرَة, "to visit a populated place") is an Islamic pilgrimage to Mecca, the holiest city for Muslims, that can be undertaken at any time of the year, in contrast to the Hajj, which has specific dates according to the Islamic lunar calendar. It is sometimes called the "lesser pilgrimage" or "minor pilgrimage", the Hajj being the "major" pilgrimage which is obligatory for every able Muslim who can afford it. The Umrah is not compulsory but is highly recommended in Islamic tradition.

Rites

The pilgrim performs a series of ritual acts, principally:

  • Ihram — entering a state of purity and consecration, marked for men by wearing two white seamless cloths, at a designated station (miqat) before reaching Mecca
  • Tawaf — circumambulating the Kaaba seven times in an anticlockwise direction
  • Sa'i — walking or running seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwah, commemorating Hagar's search for water for her son Ishmael
  • Halq or Taqsir — concluding the pilgrimage by shaving the head (for men) or cutting a small portion of the hair

History

According to Islamic tradition, pilgrimage rites at Mecca predate Islam and were reformed by the prophet Muhammad, who performed the Umrah four times and the Hajj once. The Treaty of Hudaybiyyah (628 CE) allowed the Muslims to perform the pilgrimage the following year, an event known as the First Pilgrimage. In modern times, Saudi Arabia issues dedicated Umrah visas, and several million pilgrims perform the rite annually, with numbers peaking during the month of Ramadan.

Differences from Hajj

Unlike the Hajj, the Umrah can be performed at any time of the year, takes only a few hours, and does not include the rites at Mina, Arafat, and Muzdalifah outside Mecca. Because of this flexibility, many pilgrims combine an Umrah with visits to Medina, where the Prophet's Mosque is located, although Medina is not a formal part of the Umrah rites.
