Isotopic labeling

PurposeTrack atoms through reactions and metabolic pathways
Common labels²H, ¹³C, ¹⁵N, ¹⁸O, ³²P, ³⁵S, ¹⁴C
DetectionMass spectrometry, NMR, radioactivity counting

Isotopic labeling is a technique used to track the passage of an isotope through a chemical reaction, metabolic pathway or cell. Atoms in a molecule are replaced with an isotope of the same element — either a radioactive isotope, detected by its emissions, or a stable but rarer isotope, detected by mass spectrometry or NMR — and the labelled compound is then followed through the system.

History

The method was pioneered by George de Hevesy in the 1920s–30s using naturally radioactive lead isotopes, work that won him the 1943 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. It became central to biochemistry after the Second World War: Melvin Calvin used carbon-14 to map the path of carbon in photosynthesis, and Meselson and Stahl used nitrogen-15 in 1958 to prove semi-conservative DNA replication.

Methods

Labels can be introduced by chemical synthesis, by exchange reactions, or by growing organisms on labelled media — as in SILAC, where cells are cultured with heavy amino acids for quantitative proteomics. Radioactive tracers such as ³H, ¹⁴C and ³²P are detected by autoradiography or scintillation counting; stable isotopes such as ¹³C, ¹⁵N and ²H shift the mass spectrum of metabolites without radiation risk and underpin techniques such as isotope-ratio mass spectrometry and metabolic flux analysis.

Applications

Isotopic labeling is used to determine reaction mechanisms, trace nutrient and pollutant pathways in ecology, date groundwater, authenticate foods, study drug metabolism, and measure protein turnover. Because heavy and light isotopologues react at slightly different rates (the kinetic isotope effect), labelling must be interpreted with care.
