The Seleucid Empire was a Greek state in West Asia that existed from 312 BC to 63 BC. Founded by Seleucus I Nicator, a general (one of the Diadochi) of Alexander the Great, it succeeded the Achaemenid Empire over much of the Near East and at its greatest extent stretched from Anatolia and the Levant through Mesopotamia and Persia to the Indus valley.

History

In the Wars of the Diadochi, Seleucus secured Babylon in 312 BC (the start of the Seleucid era) and, after the Battle of Ipsus in 301 BC, gained Syria and eastern Anatolia. He founded Antioch and Seleucia on the Tigris as twin capitals. His son Antiochus I and grandson Antiochus II struggled to hold the empire's far east and west; Bactria and Parthia broke away in the mid-3rd century BC.

Antiochus III "the Great" (r. 222–187 BC) restored the eastern provinces and intervened in Greece, but was defeated by Rome at Magnesia (190 BC) and forced to sign the Treaty of Apamea (188 BC), surrendering Anatolia west of the Taurus and paying a huge indemnity. Under Antiochus IV Epiphanes, Seleucid persecution of Judaism provoked the Maccabean Revolt (167 BC), which led to the independent Hasmonean kingdom.

Decline and fall

Dynastic civil wars between rival branches of the royal house consumed the 2nd and early 1st centuries BC as Rome, Parthia, Armenia and local dynasts stripped away territory. In 64–63 BC the Roman general Pompey annexed the remaining Syrian rump as the province of Syria, ending the empire.

Culture and administration

The Seleucids governed through a Persian-derived satrapal system staffed largely by Greeks and Macedonians, and founded dozens of Greek cities — Antioch became one of the greatest cities of the ancient world. Greek language and culture spread widely, though the administration ruled a highly diverse population. The dynasty is the Hellenistic state behind the Jewish festival of Hanukkah's historical background.
