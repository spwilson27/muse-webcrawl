The Timurid Renaissance was a flourishing of art, architecture, science and literature in the Timurid Empire of Central Asia and Persia during the 15th and early 16th centuries, centred on the courts of Samarkand and Herat. Although Timur's conquests were famously destructive, his successors turned imperial wealth to lavish cultural patronage, making the period a high point of Persianate civilisation.

Architecture

Timurid builders pioneered monumental scale, turquoise glazed tilework, bulbous double domes and vast iwans. Landmarks include Timur's Bibi Khanum Mosque and the Gur-e Amir mausoleum in Samarkand, the necropolis of Shah-i-Zinda, and later the madrasas of the Registan, mostly rebuilt under the Shaybanids in Timurid style.

Science

Ulugh Beg, governor of Samarkand and later sultan, was himself an astronomer: his madrasa and observatory (1420s) housed a giant mural sextant with which Jamshid al-Kashi and others produced the Zij-i Sultani star catalogue (1437), the most accurate before Tycho Brahe. Al-Kashi computed pi to 16 decimal places and pioneered decimal fractions; Ali Qushji later carried Timurid astronomy to the Ottoman court.

Arts and letters

Herat under Shah Rukh and Sultan Husayn Bayqara became a centre of Persian poetry (Jami), Chagatai Turkish literature (Ali-Shir Nava'i, who championed Turkic as a literary language and influenced Babur, founder of the Mughal Empire), historiography, calligraphy and the miniature painting of Kamal ud-Din Behzad. Timurid visual and architectural idioms strongly shaped early Mughal and Safavid art.
