Effigy mound

An effigy mound is a raised pile of earth built in the shape of a stylised animal, symbol, human or other figure. In North America, effigy mounds were constructed by various Native American cultures, most famously by the peoples of the Effigy Mound Culture of the Upper Midwest between about 550 and 1200 CE, who built thousands of mounds in the shapes of birds, bears, panthers, deer, water spirits and other beings in what is now Wisconsin, Iowa, Minnesota and Illinois.

Distribution and construction

Effigy mounds are typically low — often less than a metre high — but can extend more than 100 metres in length. They often occur in groups on ridges overlooking rivers and lakes, and many contain burials, although burial does not appear to have been their only function; interpretations also involve clan symbols, territorial markers and cosmological or ceremonial landscapes.

Preservation

Large numbers were destroyed by ploughing, development and looting in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. Surviving examples are protected at sites such as Effigy Mounds National Monument in Iowa, established in 1949, which preserves more than 200 mounds, including the Great Bear Mound and the Marching Bear group.
