Internet censorship

Internet censorship is the control or suppression of what can be accessed, published, or viewed on the Internet, enacted by regulators, or on their own initiative. Individuals and organizations may engage in self-censorship for moral, religious, or business reasons, or to conform to societal norms, without overt state involvement.

The extent of internet censorship varies on a country-to-country basis. While some democracies have moderate censorship focused on child protection, copyright or extremist content, other states go further and censor political opposition, news coverage, social media, and communication tools. At the extreme, countries such as North Korea restrict access to a tightly controlled national intranet, and China operates the world's most extensive filtering system, the Great Firewall.

Methods

  • Technical censorship — IP address blocking, DNS redirection and poisoning, URL and keyword filtering, deep packet inspection, port blocking, and full network shutdowns or throttling, as used during protests and elections in several countries.
  • Legal and regulatory pressure — licensing of ISPs and platforms, takedown orders, data-localization laws, and liability rules that push companies to remove content pre-emptively.
  • Surveillance and deterrence — monitoring that produces chilling effects and self-censorship even without formal blocks.
  • Content removal requests — government requests to platforms for removal of posts, accounts or search results.

By region

China's system combines the Great Firewall with real-name registration and domestic platform regulation. Russia, especially after its 2022 invasion of Ukraine, blocked independent media and major foreign platforms and expanded its "sovereign internet" (RuNet) controls. Iran operates the National Information Network and periodically imposes near-total shutdowns. India is among the countries most frequently imposing regional internet shutdowns, notably the long communications blockade in Jammu and Kashmir beginning in 2019. Democratic states also regulate content — the European Union's Digital Services Act imposes obligations on large platforms, and many countries block child-abuse material through systems such as the UK's IWF list.

Circumvention

Users bypass restrictions with VPNs, the Tor anonymity network, proxy servers, encrypted DNS, mirror sites and satellite connections. Censoring states respond by blocking known VPN endpoints and fingerprinting circumvention protocols, producing an ongoing arms race studied by measurement projects such as OONI and the OpenNet Initiative.

Debate

Supporters argue censorship protects public order, children, and national security; critics — including press-freedom organizations such as Reporters Without Borders and Freedom House, whose annual Freedom on the Net report has recorded many consecutive years of global decline in internet freedom — contend it undermines free expression and democratic accountability. Private platform moderation raises parallel questions about the power of companies over online speech.
