Taq Kasra (Persian: طاق کسری, "Arch of Khosrow"), also known as the Archway of Ctesiphon, is a Sasanian-era Persian monument located near the modern town of Salman Pak, Iraq, on the eastern bank of the Tigris. It is the only surviving above-ground structure of the ancient city of Ctesiphon, once the capital of the Parthian and Sasanian empires. The monument is famed for its enormous iwan — an open vaulted hall — whose arch is the largest single-span vault of unreinforced brickwork in the world.

History

The exact date of construction is debated. The palace complex of which the iwan formed part is generally attributed to the reign of Khosrow I (r. 531–579), the great Sasanian shahanshah, though some scholars place its origins in the 3rd century under Shapur I. The façade, with its blind arcades arranged in tiers, originally flanked a throne hall where the shah held audience, seated beneath the arch with an enormous carpet — the legendary "Spring of Khosrow" — spread below.

After the Muslim conquest of Persia in the 7th century, Ctesiphon declined and its palaces were quarried for building material as nearby Baghdad rose. The northern wing of the façade collapsed in the 19th and 20th centuries, leaving the surviving southern portion. The monument has been damaged by flooding of the Tigris, and Iraqi and international teams have undertaken repeated stabilisation work. In 2021 a further section of the arch's vault collapsed, prompting renewed conservation efforts.

Architecture

The iwan is approximately 37 metres high, 26 metres across, and 50 metres deep. The arch itself springs from ground level in a roughly parabolic (catenary-like) profile, a form that distributed the thrust of the massive brick vault. It is considered a landmark in the history of architecture: the largest vault ever built of fired brick without centring or reinforcement, and an ancestor of later Persian and Islamic iwan architecture.
