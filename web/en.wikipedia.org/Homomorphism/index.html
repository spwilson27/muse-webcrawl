Homomorphism

In algebra, a homomorphism is a structure-preserving map between two algebraic structures of the same type (such as two groups, two rings, or two vector spaces). The word homomorphism comes from the Ancient Greek words homos meaning "same" and morphe meaning "form" or "shape". However, the word was actually introduced by Felix Klein in his Erlangen program, and popularized by Emmy Noether in her work in the 1920s.

Definition

A homomorphism between two algebraic structures A and B is a function f : A → B that preserves the operations of the structure. For example, a group homomorphism satisfies f(x · y) = f(x) · f(y) for all x, y in A, and maps the identity element of A to the identity element of B. A ring homomorphism preserves both addition and multiplication, while a linear map between vector spaces is a homomorphism of vector spaces preserving addition and scalar multiplication.

Types

Special kinds of homomorphisms include isomorphisms (bijective homomorphisms whose inverse is also a homomorphism), endomorphisms (homomorphisms from a structure to itself), and automorphisms (bijective endomorphisms). The set of elements mapped to the identity is called the kernel of the homomorphism; for groups and rings, the kernel measures how far the homomorphism is from being injective, and the quotient by the kernel is isomorphic to the image, a result expressed by the fundamental homomorphism theorem (first isomorphism theorem).

Examples

The exponential function is a homomorphism from the additive group of real numbers to the multiplicative group of positive reals, since exp(x + y) = exp(x)·exp(y). The determinant is a homomorphism from the group of invertible matrices under multiplication to the multiplicative group of nonzero scalars. The reduction of integers modulo n is a ring homomorphism from the integers onto the ring Z/nZ.
