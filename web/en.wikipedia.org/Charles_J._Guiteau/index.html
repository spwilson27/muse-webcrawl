Charles J. Guiteau

Charles Julius Guiteau (September 8, 1841 – June 30, 1882) was an American preacher, failed lawyer and office-seeker who assassinated President James A. Garfield in 1881. His trial was one of the first major insanity-defence cases in the United States, and the assassination helped spur civil service reform.

Early life

Guiteau was born in Freeport, Illinois, and raised in a family influenced by the religious communalism of the Oneida Community, which he briefly joined. He failed at careers in theology and law, moved frequently, and drifted into politics as a minor campaign speechmaker. He supported Garfield in the 1880 election and convinced himself that a speech he had delivered had won the presidency, entitling him to appointment as consul to Paris.

Assassination

Repeatedly rebuffed in seeking office, Guiteau concluded that Garfield's removal would unite the Republican Party and reward him. On July 2, 1881, he shot Garfield twice at the Baltimore and Potomac Railroad Station in Washington, D.C. Garfield lingered for eleven weeks before dying on September 19, 1881, his wounds worsened by infection.

Trial and execution

Guiteau's trial opened in November 1881. He behaved erratically, insulted his lawyers and claimed he was legally insane but not medically so. The jury found him guilty in January 1882, and he was hanged in Washington on June 30, 1882. The assassination contributed directly to the Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act of 1883, which began replacing patronage appointments with merit-based selection.
