Craton

A craton (from Greek krátos, "strength") is an old and stable part of the continental lithosphere, consisting of the Earth's two topmost layers, the crust and the uppermost mantle. Having survived cycles of merging and rifting of continents for hundreds of millions — in places billions — of years, cratons are generally found in the interiors of tectonic plates and form the ancient cores around which continents have grown.

Structure

A craton is typically divided into an exposed region of Precambrian basement rock called a shield, and a platform where the basement is buried beneath flat-lying sedimentary rocks. Beneath the crust, cratons possess deep lithospheric roots ("keels") extending 200 kilometres or more into the mantle; this cold, buoyant, chemically depleted root is thought to explain their long-term stability. Cratonic crust includes some of the oldest rocks on Earth, such as the Acasta Gneiss of Canada and the Jack Hills zircons of Australia, both around 4 billion years old.

Examples

Major cratons include the Canadian Shield (North American Craton), the Baltic (East European) Craton, the Siberian Craton, the Kaapvaal and Congo cratons of Africa, the West African Craton, the Amazonian and São Francisco cratons of South America, and the Pilbara and Yilgarn cratons of Western Australia.

Economic significance

Cratons host many of the world's great mineral deposits: gold, iron ore, nickel, copper and platinum-group elements. Their deep roots are also the source of diamonds, which are carried to the surface by kimberlite eruptions that occur almost exclusively through cratonic lithosphere.
