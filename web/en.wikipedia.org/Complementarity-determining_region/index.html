Complementarity-determining region

Complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) are short amino acid sequences found in the variable domains of antigen receptors—antibodies (immunoglobulins) and T cell receptors—that complement the shape of an antigen and confer binding specificity. They are the most variable parts of these molecules and are also called hypervariable regions.

Location and structure

Each variable domain contains three CDRs (CDR1, CDR2, and CDR3) interspersed among four more conserved framework regions. In an antibody, the six CDRs of the paired heavy and light chains come together at the tip of the molecule to form the antigen-binding site (paratope). CDR3, which spans the junction of variable, diversity, and joining gene segments in the heavy chain, is usually the longest and most diverse and often contributes most to antigen contact.

Generation of diversity

CDR diversity arises from V(D)J recombination of gene segments, imprecise joining and addition of non-templated nucleotides at the junctions (concentrated in CDR3), combinatorial pairing of chains, and somatic hypermutation, which preferentially targets the CDRs during affinity maturation in germinal centres.

Applications

Because the CDRs determine specificity, antibody engineering focuses on them: in CDR grafting, the CDRs of a non-human antibody are transplanted onto a human framework to "humanise" therapeutic antibodies. Numbering schemes such as Kabat, Chothia, and IMGT define CDR boundaries for sequence analysis and engineering.
