Dodecahedron

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In geometry, a dodecahedron (from Greek dōdekáedron, "twelve faces") is any polyhedron with twelve flat faces. The most familiar dodecahedron is the regular dodecahedron with regular pentagons as faces, which is a Platonic solid. There are also three regular star dodecahedra, which are composed of twelve congruent pentagonal faces.

Regular dodecahedron

The regular dodecahedron is one of the five Platonic solids. It has 12 pentagonal faces, 30 edges, and 20 vertices, with three pentagons meeting at each vertex. Its dual polyhedron is the regular icosahedron. The surface area and volume of a regular dodecahedron of edge length a involve the golden ratio, which also appears in the coordinates of its vertices.

History

Regular dodecahedra have been known since antiquity; stone models resembling them date to the late Neolithic. Plato, in the Timaeus, associated the dodecahedron with the element of the cosmos or aether, the fifth element beyond earth, air, fire, and water. Kepler nested the Platonic solids in his early model of the solar system.

Other dodecahedra

Beyond the regular form, the term covers many twelve-faced polyhedra, including the rhombic dodecahedron (important as the shape of the Voronoi cell of the face-centered cubic lattice), the pyritohedron, and the pentagonal trapezohedron used for ten-sided dice variants.

Occurrences

Dodecahedral shapes appear in dice, in the geometry of certain virus capsids and fullerenes, and in the dodecahedral cell structure proposed in some cosmological models of the universe.
