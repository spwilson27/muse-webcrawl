mTOR

mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin, formerly mammalian target of rapamycin) is a serine/threonine protein kinase in the PI3K-related kinase (PIKK) family that regulates cell growth, proliferation, protein synthesis, autophagy, and metabolism in response to nutrients, growth factors, energy status, and stress. In humans it is encoded by the MTOR gene on chromosome 1.

mTOR nucleates two distinct multiprotein complexes. mTORC1, which contains the scaffold protein Raptor, is acutely sensitive to the immunosuppressant rapamycin and drives anabolic processes such as cap-dependent translation through phosphorylation of S6K1 and 4E-BP1, while suppressing autophagy. mTORC2, which contains Rictor, is largely rapamycin-insensitive acutely and phosphorylates AKT at Ser473, regulating survival and cytoskeletal organization.

Discovery

The TOR genes were identified in the early 1990s in budding yeast through mutations conferring resistance to rapamycin, a macrolide isolated from Streptomyces hygroscopicus found in soil samples from Easter Island (Rapa Nui), which gave rapamycin its name. The mammalian homologue was cloned in 1994–1995 by several groups.

Regulation

mTORC1 is activated at the lysosomal surface by the small GTPase Rheb when amino acids are sufficient, a process controlled by the Rag GTPases and the GATOR1 complex. The tumor suppressor TSC1–TSC2 complex acts as a GAP for Rheb and integrates growth-factor and energy signals; energy stress activates AMPK, which inhibits mTORC1. Hypoxia and DNA damage also suppress the pathway via REDD1 and p53.

Clinical significance

Dysregulation of mTOR signaling is implicated in cancer, type 2 diabetes, obesity, and neurological disorders. Rapamycin (sirolimus) and its analogues ("rapalogs") such as everolimus and temsirolimus are used as immunosuppressants in transplantation and as anticancer agents. Mutations in pathway genes cause tuberous sclerosis complex and focal cortical dysplasias associated with epilepsy.
