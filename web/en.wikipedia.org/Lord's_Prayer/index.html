Lord's Prayer

The Lord's Prayer, also known by its Latin incipit Pater Noster, is a central Christian prayer that Jesus taught his disciples. Two versions appear in the New Testament: a longer form in the Sermon on the Mount in the Gospel of Matthew (6:9–13) and a shorter form in the Gospel of Luke (11:2–4), given in response to a disciple's request to be taught to pray.

Text (Matthew, traditional English)

Our Father, which art in heaven, hallowed be thy name. Thy kingdom come, thy will be done, in earth as it is in heaven. Give us this day our daily bread. And forgive us our debts, as we forgive our debtors. And lead us not into temptation, but deliver us from evil. For thine is the kingdom, and the power, and the glory, for ever. Amen.

The closing doxology ("For thine is the kingdom...") is absent from the earliest manuscripts of Matthew and from the Roman Catholic liturgical tradition, but is used in most Protestant worship and in the Byzantine rite.

Analysis

The prayer consists of an address to God as Father, petitions concerning God's name, kingdom and will, and petitions for bread, forgiveness and deliverance. The unusual Greek word epiousios, translated "daily" in "daily bread", has been debated since antiquity. The prayer has been set to music countless times and is recited across virtually all Christian traditions, making it the best-known prayer in Christianity.
