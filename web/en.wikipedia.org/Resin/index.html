Resin

Resin is a viscous organic substance secreted by many plants, particularly conifers, usually as a protective response to injury. Natural resins harden on exposure to air and consist mainly of terpenes and related compounds. In modern industrial usage, “resin” also denotes synthetic polymeric materials that can be moulded, cast, coated, or cured.

Natural resin

Plants produce resin in specialised ducts or cavities. When bark is damaged, the fluid seals the wound and can trap insects or inhibit fungi and bacteria. Volatile components evaporate or oxidise, leaving a harder material. Amber is fossilised tree resin. Other familiar substances include frankincense, myrrh, copal, dammar, mastic, and pine rosin.

Composition

Plant resins are complex mixtures. Monoterpenes and sesquiterpenes often provide the volatile fraction, while diterpenes and triterpenes contribute to the solid fraction. Resin acids such as abietic acid are important in pine-derived products. Composition varies by species and environment and affects aroma, hardness, solubility, and commercial value.

Uses

Resins have long been used in varnishes, adhesives, incense, medicines, chewing materials, waterproofing, and musical-instrument rosin. Naval stores obtained from pines historically supplied turpentine and rosin for shipping and industry.

Synthetic resins

Synthetic resins include epoxy, polyester, phenolic, acrylic, alkyd, and polyurethane systems. Some are thermoplastics that soften on heating; others cure by chemical cross-linking into thermosets. They are used in composites, paints, printed-circuit boards, moulded goods, dentistry, and casting. The word is sometimes applied to an uncured polymer before it becomes the final plastic product.
