Paul Ludwig Hans Anton von Beneckendorff und von Hindenburg (2 October 1847 – 2 August 1934) was a German military officer and statesman who led Imperial Germany's war effort during the second half of the First World War and served as President of the Weimar Republic from 1925 until his death in 1934. In January 1933, persuaded by conservative allies, he appointed Adolf Hitler as Chancellor; the Nazi seizure of power and the decrees that followed effectively ended the republic he headed.

Military career

Born in Posen into an old East Prussian Junker family, Hindenburg entered the Prussian army as a cadet and fought in the Austro-Prussian War (1866) and the Franco-Prussian War (1870–71). He rose through the General Staff and corps commands before retiring in 1911. Recalled in August 1914 after Russia invaded East Prussia, he was paired with Erich Ludendorff as his chief of staff. Their victory at the Battle of Tannenberg (August 1914), followed by the Masurian Lakes, made Hindenburg a national hero, celebrated in monuments such as the giant wooden "Iron Hindenburg" statue in Berlin.

In August 1916 Hindenburg succeeded Erich von Falkenhayn as Chief of the General Staff, with Ludendorff as First Quartermaster General. The "Hindenburg Programme" mobilised the economy for total war, and the duo exercised near-dictatorial influence, demanding unrestricted submarine warfare and maximal war aims. After the failure of the 1918 spring offensives, Hindenburg pressed the government to seek an armistice, yet later lent his prestige to the "stab-in-the-back" myth blaming defeat on civilians.

Presidency

Elected president in 1925 as the candidate of the nationalist right, Hindenburg at first governed constitutionally but distrusted parliamentary democracy. From 1930, amid the Great Depression, he ruled increasingly through presidential cabinets and emergency decrees under Article 48, appointing Heinrich Brüning, Franz von Papen, and Kurt von Schleicher as chancellors. Re-elected in 1932—defeating Hitler with Social Democratic and Centre support—he nonetheless, on 30 January 1933, appointed Hitler Chancellor of a coalition cabinet, expecting conservatives to control him. He signed the Reichstag Fire Decree and the Enabling Act that dismantled democracy, and remained head of state until his death at his estate at Neudeck on 2 August 1934, whereupon Hitler merged the offices of president and chancellor as "Führer."
