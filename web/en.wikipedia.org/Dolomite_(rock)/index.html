Dolomite (rock)

Dolomite is a sedimentary carbonate rock composed predominantly of the mineral dolomite, calcium magnesium carbonate CaMg(CO3)2. It is also called dolostone, the term preferred by many geologists to distinguish the rock from the mineral of the same name. The rock and mineral were named after the French naturalist Déodat Gratet de Dolomieu, who described them in 1791.

Formation — the dolomite problem

Most dolomite is thought to form by the post-depositional replacement of limestone by magnesium-rich fluids, a process called dolomitisation, because ordered dolomite precipitates only very slowly from surface waters today — the long-standing "dolomite problem." Proposed settings include hypersaline lagoons (the sabkha model), mixing zones of fresh and sea water, burial environments, and microbial mediation. Famous ancient dolomite platforms include the Triassic formations of the Dolomites in northern Italy.

Properties and identification

Dolomite resembles limestone but is slightly harder and reacts only weakly with cold dilute hydrochloric acid unless powdered — a standard field test distinguishing it from calcite-rich rock. It is typically buff, grey, or pink, often with sugary, crystalline textures and vuggy porosity.

Uses

Dolomite is used as an aggregate and dimension stone, as a source of magnesium, in glass and steel making as a flux, and as an agricultural soil conditioner. Porous dolomites are important petroleum reservoir rocks, hosting significant oil and gas fields in North America and the Middle East.
