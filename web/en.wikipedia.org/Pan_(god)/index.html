Pan (god)

Pan, the goat-legged god of the wild, playing his pipes (the syrinx).

Pan (Ancient Greek: Πᾶν, Pán) is the god of the wild, shepherds and flocks, nature, rustic music and impromptus, and the companion of the nymphs in Greek mythology and religion. He has the hindquarters, legs and horns of a goat, in the manner of a faun or satyr. With his homeland in rustic Arcadia, he is also recognised as the god of fields, groves and wooded glens — and of sudden, groundless fear: "panic" derives from his name.

Mythology

Pan's parentage varies by source; in the Homeric Hymn to Pan he is the son of Hermes and the nymph Dryope, whose goat-footed child delighted the Olympians. He is famous for inventing the panpipes (syrinx) from the reeds into which the nymph Syrinx was transformed to escape him, and for his music contest with Apollo, judged by Midas. The Athenians credited Pan with aiding them at the Battle of Marathon (490 BC) by spreading panic among the Persians, and with sending the runner Pheidippides a message on his run from Marathon.

Worship

After Marathon, Athens established a shrine to Pan on the Acropolis slope and an annual torch-race festival. His worship, centred on caves, grottoes and rustic shrines rather than temples, spread from Arcadia across the Greek world. A famous anecdote in Plutarch reports a voice announcing "Great Pan is dead" during the reign of Tiberius, later read by Christians as marking the end of the pagan age.

In later culture

Pan has been a recurring figure in art, literature and music, from Renaissance pastoral to Debussy's Syrinx (1913) and the mischievous Pan of J. M. Barrie's Peter Pan and Kenneth Grahame's The Wind in the Willows. In modern paganism and Wicca, Pan is sometimes associated with the Horned God.
