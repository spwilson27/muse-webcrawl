Union shop

A union shop is a form of a union security agreement under which the employer agrees to hire either union members or nonmembers who are willing to join the union within a specified time after being hired. In a union shop, an employee does not lose their job for failure to pay union dues or fees, but may be required to pay an amount equal to union dues as a condition of continued employment.

Under the United States' National Labor Relations Act, a union shop agreement is permitted in the private sector, but individual states may prohibit such agreements under so-called "right-to-work" laws, authorized by Section 14(b) of the Taft–Hartley Act of 1947. In states without right-to-work laws, workers covered by a union shop agreement who object to full union membership may instead pay agency fees covering the costs of collective bargaining and contract administration.

Variants and related arrangements

The union shop differs from the closed shop, in which only existing union members may be hired; closed shops were outlawed in the United States by the Taft–Hartley Act. It also differs from an agency shop, in which nonmembers must pay a fee but are not required to join, and from an open shop, in which no union-security requirement exists at all.

Legal status

In the United States public sector, the Supreme Court's 2018 decision in Janus v. AFSCME held that agency fees for public employees violate the First Amendment, effectively making the entire public sector an open shop. In the private sector, union shop clauses remain lawful except where state right-to-work statutes ban them.
