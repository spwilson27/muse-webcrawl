Homo economicus

Homo economicusType: theoretical model of human behaviour
Domain: economics, decision theory
Traits: rational, self-interested, utility-maximising

Homo economicus, or economic man, is a theoretical construct in economics representing a human agent who is consistently rational and narrowly self-interested, and who pursues their subjectively defined ends optimally. The concept is used in economic models to simplify human decision-making: the agent ranks preferences, weighs costs and benefits, and chooses the option that maximises utility.

Origins

Although the phrase came into use in the late nineteenth century — partly as a criticism of John Stuart Mill's political economy — the underlying idea of a self-interested maximiser traces back to Adam Smith's The Wealth of Nations (1776), which argued that individuals pursuing their own interest often promote the public good as if guided by an "invisible hand".

Characteristics

The model agent is assumed to have complete and transitive preferences, access to relevant information, and the computational ability to evaluate alternatives. In game theory, homo economicus underpins the standard prediction that players settle at Nash equilibria when each best-responds to the others.

Criticism

Behavioural economists such as Daniel Kahneman, Richard Thaler and Herbert Simon have shown that real people systematically deviate from the model: they use heuristics, exhibit loss aversion, discount the future hyperbolically, and care about fairness and reciprocity. Simon's notion of bounded rationality proposes that people satisfice rather than optimise. Critics also note that preferences are often unstable and context-dependent, and that social norms, identity and altruism motivate much behaviour the model cannot explain. Alternatives include homo reciprocans and prospect-theoretic agents.
