Electra complex

In neo-Freudian psychology, the Electra complex is a girl's psychosexual competition with her mother for possession of her father. The term was proposed by Carl Jung in 1913 as the female counterpart of Sigmund Freud's Oedipus complex. Freud himself rejected the term, preferring to speak of the "feminine Oedipus attitude".

Origin of the term

Jung named the complex after the Greek mythological figure Electra, daughter of Agamemnon and Clytemnestra, who persuaded her brother Orestes to kill their mother in revenge for their father's murder. The myth was dramatised by Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides.

Freudian theory

In Freud's theory of psychosexual development, both boys and girls pass through the phallic stage (around ages three to five). Freud argued that the girl, discovering anatomical difference, experiences "penis envy", blames her mother, and turns her desire toward her father, only later identifying with the mother again. The resolution of this stage was said to shape the adult personality and sexual identity.

Criticism

Like most of classical psychoanalytic theory, the Electra complex is regarded by most modern psychologists as lacking empirical support and as unfalsifiable; it is primarily of historical and literary interest today, though the terms "Oedipus" and "Electra complex" persist widely in popular culture and literary criticism.
