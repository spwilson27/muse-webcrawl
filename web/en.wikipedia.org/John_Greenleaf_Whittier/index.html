John Greenleaf Whittier

Born17 December 1807, Haverhill, Massachusetts, U.S.
Died7 September 1892 (aged 84), Hampton Falls, New Hampshire, U.S.
OccupationPoet, journalist, abolitionist

John Greenleaf Whittier (December 17, 1807 – September 7, 1892) was an American Quaker poet and advocate of the abolition of slavery. He is remembered for his long narrative poem Snow-Bound (1866) and is counted among the "fireside poets".

Early life and journalism

Whittier was raised on a farm in Haverhill, Massachusetts. Encouraged by William Lloyd Garrison, he became a newspaper editor in Boston and Hartford and served briefly in the Massachusetts legislature. His eyes and health suffered, and he returned home, but he continued writing.

Abolitionism

Whittier was a founding member of the American Anti-Slavery Society and a lobbyist for abolition in Washington. His pamphlet Justice and Expediency (1833) and the poetry collection Voices of Freedom made him a leading literary voice of the movement; he was twice mobbed for his views.

Poetry

After the Civil War, Whittier turned to gentler themes of rural New England life, faith and nature. Snow-Bound, a nostalgic account of his family gathered around the fire during a blizzard, was a bestseller. Other well-known works include "Barbara Frietchie" and the ballad "Maud Muller".

Death and legacy

Whittier died in 1892 at Hampton Falls, New Hampshire. The city of Whittier, California, and many schools bear his name.
