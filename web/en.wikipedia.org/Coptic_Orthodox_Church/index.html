The Coptic Orthodox Church is an Oriental Orthodox Christian church based in Egypt, with adherents — the Copts — throughout the Middle East and a large global diaspora. It is headed by the Pope of Alexandria and Patriarch of All Africa on the Holy See of Saint Mark.

History

By tradition, Christianity was brought to Egypt by Saint Mark the Evangelist in the first century CE, and Alexandria became one of the great centres of early Christian learning, home to figures such as Clement and Origen. The church's distinctive path was set by the Council of Chalcedon in 451, whose Christological definition the Alexandrian church rejected, holding instead to the miaphysite formula of Cyril of Alexandria. After the Arab conquest of Egypt in the seventh century, the Copts gradually became a minority in their homeland but preserved their church, language, and monastic traditions; Egyptian monasticism, founded by figures such as Anthony the Great, profoundly influenced Christianity worldwide.

Beliefs and practices

The church shares the faith of the Oriental Orthodox communion, accepting the first three ecumenical councils. Its liturgy, the Divine Liturgy of Saint Basil, is celebrated largely in Coptic — the final stage of the ancient Egyptian language, written in a modified Greek alphabet — alongside Arabic and local languages. Copts observe lengthy fasts, including the Great Fast before Easter and the Fast of the Apostles. Christmas is celebrated on 7 January.

Modern era

Under Pope Shenouda III (1971–2012) the church experienced a revival of monasticism and Sunday schools, and expanded greatly abroad. His successor, Pope Tawadros II, was enthroned in 2012. Copts constitute the largest Christian community in the Middle East, though they have periodically faced discrimination and violence, including attacks on churches in the twenty-first century.
