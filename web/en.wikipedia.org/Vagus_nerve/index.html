Vagus nerve

InnervatesLarynx, pharynx, heart, lungs, most of the gastrointestinal tract and other viscera
NerveCranial nerve X
FunctionParasympathetic control of heart, lungs and digestive tract; taste and sensation from the viscera; motor supply to palate, pharynx and larynx

The vagus is the longest cranial nerve, wandering from the medulla to the abdomen — hence its name, from Latin vagus, "wandering".

The vagus nerve, also known as the tenth cranial nerve (CN X), is the longest and most complex of the cranial nerves. It interfaces with the parasympathetic control of the heart, lungs and digestive tract. It exits the medulla of the brainstem, passes through the jugular foramen, and descends in the carotid sheath through the neck, thorax and diaphragm into the abdomen, supplying branches along the way.

The vagus contains motor, sensory and parasympathetic fibres. Around 80–90 percent of its fibres are afferent, carrying information from the viscera to the brain — a major channel of the gut–brain axis.

Structure

The nerve arises from several nuclei in the medulla: the dorsal motor nucleus (parasympathetic output to the viscera), the nucleus ambiguus (motor output to the muscles of the palate, pharynx and larynx, and parasympathetics to the heart), and the solitary nucleus (receiving visceral and taste afferents). In the neck it gives off pharyngeal branches and the superior and recurrent laryngeal nerves — the right recurrent laryngeal nerve loops under the subclavian artery, the left under the aortic arch, a detour that lengthens dramatically in animals such as the giraffe. In the thorax the two vagi form the oesophageal plexus and continue as anterior and posterior vagal trunks into the abdomen, where they supply the stomach, liver, pancreas and gut as far as the splenic flexure of the colon.

Function

  • Parasympathetic: slows the heart, constricts the bronchi, stimulates peristalsis and glandular secretion throughout most of the gastrointestinal tract;
  • Motor: speech and swallowing via the muscles of the soft palate, pharynx and larynx;
  • Sensory: sensation from the external ear, larynx, and the thoracic and abdominal viscera; taste from the epiglottis region;
  • Reflexes: mediates the cough, gag and baroreceptor reflexes, and the oculocardiac reflex.

Clinical significance

Damage causes hoarseness, dysphagia, loss of the gag reflex and deviation of the uvula; bilateral lesions can be life-threatening. Overactivity contributes to vasovagal syncope. Vagus nerve stimulation (VNS), using an implanted pulse generator, is an approved treatment for drug-resistant epilepsy and treatment-resistant depression, and is being studied for inflammatory diseases via the "inflammatory reflex". Surgical vagotomy was once performed for peptic ulcer disease before effective acid-suppressing drugs made it obsolete.
