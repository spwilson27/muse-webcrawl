Braille

Braille (pronounced /breɪl/) is a tactile writing system used by people who are visually impaired, including people who are blind, deafblind or who have low vision. It can be read either on embossed paper or by using refreshable braille displays. Braille is conventionally written on slate and stylus, the Perkins Brailler typewriter, or produced with a braille embosser connected to a computer. It is named after its creator, the French educator Louis Braille, who developed the system in 1824, at the age of fifteen, adapting a military "night writing" code devised by Charles Barbier.

The braille cell

Each braille character is formed within a rectangular cell of six dot positions, arranged as two columns of three. A dot may be raised at any of the six positions, giving 63 meaningful combinations plus the blank cell. Positions are numbered 1–3 down the left column and 4–6 down the right. The first ten letters (a–j) use only the upper four dots; the next ten (k–t) add dot 3; the remaining letters add dots 3 and 6. A cell with only dot 6 raised before a letter indicates a capital; a cell with dots 3–6 raised (the number sign) turns the letters a–j into the digits 1–0.

Grades and codes

Grade 1 braille is a letter-for-letter transcription. Grade 2 (contracted) braille adds some 180 contractions — short forms for common letter groups and whole words — saving space and reading effort; it long predominated in published books. Grade 3 is a personal shorthand, not standardised. Standard codes include Unified English Braille (UEB), adopted across the English-speaking world in the 2000s–2010s, as well as specialised codes for mathematics (Nemeth Code), music notation, and many national languages, each mapping the 63 cells to its own letters and diacritics.

History

Louis Braille, blinded in childhood by an accident in his father's workshop, studied at the Royal Institute for Blind Youth in Paris, where pupils used Valentin Haüy's embossed-print books. In 1821 Captain Charles Barbier demonstrated his dot-based "night writing" for silent battlefield communication; Braille refined it into a smaller, six-dot cell that could be felt with one fingertip and read and written fluently. He published his system in 1829 and a revised version in 1837. It spread only slowly in his lifetime — it was officially adopted by his school two years after his death in 1852 — but by the late 19th century it had become the standard tactile literacy system worldwide. Braille's remains were transferred to the Panthéon in Paris in 1952; World Braille Day is observed on his birthday, 4 January.

Use today

Braille remains the primary medium of literacy for blind people: studies associate braille literacy with markedly higher employment among blind adults. Modern refreshable braille displays render computer and smartphone text via pins raised by piezoelectric actuators, and braille signage is required in public buildings in many jurisdictions. Critics of declining braille instruction — sometimes called the "braille literacy crisis" — note the shift toward audio technology, while advocates argue audio is to braille as listening is to print: a complement, not a substitute.
