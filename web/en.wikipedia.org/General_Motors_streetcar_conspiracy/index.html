The General Motors streetcar conspiracy refers to a period in the mid-20th century when General Motors and other companies were accused of deliberately dismantling streetcar systems in many American cities in order to replace them with buses, and were prosecuted in the case United States v. National City Lines (1949).

National City Lines

National City Lines (NCL), founded in 1936, acquired transit systems in numerous cities. Together with subsidiaries such as Pacific City Lines and American City Lines, it received financing and equipment from GM, Firestone Tire and Rubber, Standard Oil of California, Phillips Petroleum, and Mack Trucks. In many of the systems NCL acquired, electric streetcar lines were converted to bus operation.

The 1949 trial

In 1947, federal prosecutors indicted the companies and several executives. In 1949 a jury convicted them of conspiring to monopolize the sale of buses, tires, and petroleum products to the NCL operating companies, but acquitted them of the charge of conspiring to monopolize the ownership of transit companies. The corporate defendants were fined $5,000 each; one executive was fined $1.

Historical debate

Historians debate how much of the decline of American streetcars the conspiracy explains. Streetcar systems were already under pressure from rising automobile ownership, suburbanization, fare regulations, and the cost of maintaining track and overhead wires, and many systems converted to buses without any NCL involvement. Nevertheless, the case remains a prominent example of alleged corporate action against public transit and is frequently cited in discussions of transport policy.
