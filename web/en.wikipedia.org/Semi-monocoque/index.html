Semi-monocoque

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A semi-monocoque fuselage section: frames and stringers support the load-bearing skin.

The semi-monocoque is a type of stressed-skin structure used in aircraft and vehicle construction in which the outer skin carries part of the load but is reinforced by an internal framework of frames, longerons, stringers, and bulkheads. It contrasts with the pure monocoque, in which the skin alone carries all the load, and with conventional frame construction, in which the skin is a non-structural covering.

Design

In an aircraft fuselage of semi-monocoque construction, bending loads are shared between the skin and the longitudinal stringers and longerons; circular or near-circular frames maintain the cross-sectional shape and distribute loads between the skin and longitudinal members, while bulkheads carry concentrated loads such as wing and engine attachments and serve as pressure boundaries. Because the loads are shared, the skin need not be as thick as in a pure monocoque, and cutouts for doors, windows, and access panels can be reinforced locally without compromising strength.

Advantages and use

Semi-monocoque construction offers an excellent strength-to-weight ratio, damage tolerance — cracks tend to be arrested at frames and stringers, giving the structure fail-safe characteristics — and ease of inspection and repair. It became the standard for metal airliners and military aircraft from the 1930s onward, exemplified by aircraft such as the Douglas DC-3 and virtually all modern jet transports, and is also used in rocket bodies, submarines, and high-performance automobile chassis.
