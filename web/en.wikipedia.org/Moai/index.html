Moai are monolithic human figures carved by the Rapa Nui people on Easter Island in eastern Polynesia between about 1250 and 1500 AD. Nearly 1,000 are known. The statues have oversized heads — popular depictions show only the heads, but the moai possess complete, if shortened, torsos, many of which were buried up to the neck by later sediment.

Construction and transport

Almost all moai were carved from the compressed volcanic ash (tuff) of the crater at Rano Raraku, where hundreds remain in various stages of completion. Finished statues were transported across the island to stone ceremonial platforms (ahu) around the coast. How they were moved was long debated; experimental archaeology and Rapa Nui oral tradition support the theory that teams "walked" the statues upright by rocking them side to side with ropes — a technique demonstrated with a replica in 2012. Many moai were given inlaid eyes of white coral and red scoria pupils and crowned with cylindrical red scoria topknots (pukao).

Meaning

The statues are believed to represent deified ancestors (aringa) and were erected, facing inland, to watch over the villages of their descendant clans and embody their prestige and sacred power (mana).

Decline and restoration

By the time of European contact in 1722 the statue culture had ended or was ending amid social upheaval; by the mid-nineteenth century every moai had been toppled. From the 1950s onward, projects led by archaeologists including Thor Heyerdahl's expedition and later Chilean and international teams re-erected many statues, notably the fifteen moai of Ahu Tongariki, restored in the 1990s after a tsunami had scattered them. The moai are protected within Rapa Nui National Park, a UNESCO World Heritage Site. A single moai, Hoa Hakananai'a, held by the British Museum, has been the subject of repatriation requests from the Rapa Nui people.
