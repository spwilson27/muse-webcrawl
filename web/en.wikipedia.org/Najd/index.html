Najd (Arabic: نَجْد, "highland") is the geographic central region of Saudi Arabia and the Arabian Peninsula. A rocky plateau sloping eastward from the Hejaz mountains toward the Persian Gulf, it is one of Arabia's three historic regions alongside the Hejaz and Al-Ahsa (Eastern Arabia).

Geography

Najd is bounded by deserts on several sides: the Nafud to the north, the Dahna to the east, and the Rub' al Khali (Empty Quarter) to the south. Its heartland, the valley system of Wadi Hanifa and the region of Al-Yamama, supported oasis towns famous for palm groves and mud-brick architecture. The escarpment of Jabal Tuwayq runs north–south through the region's center.

History

In pre-Islamic times Najd was home to the Kindah kingdom and figures such as the poet Imru' al-Qais. The region remained politically fragmented for centuries until, in 1744, the Diriyah pact between Muhammad ibn Saud and the reformer Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab launched the first Saudi state. Najd became the power base of the House of Saud; its forces, under Abd al-Aziz (Ibn Saud), conquered the Hejaz in the 1920s, unifying the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia in 1932. Riyadh, its capital city, is now Saudi Arabia's political and financial center.

Culture

Najdi dialect, architecture, and cuisine (such as jareesh and marqooq) are distinct within Saudi culture, and the region's conservative religious tradition, Wahhabism, originated here. See also Riyadh and Hejaz.
