Shark Bay

LocationGascoyne region, Western Australia
AreaAbout 10,000 km² of water; average depth 9 m
UNESCO World HeritageInscribed 1991
Notable featuresStromatolites at Hamelin Pool, dugong population, Monkey Mia dolphins

Shark Bay (Gathaagudu in the Malgana language) is a large bay in the Gascoyne region of Western Australia, the westernmost point of the Australian continent. The bay and its islands, covering a heritage area of some 2.2 million hectares, were inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1991 for their exceptional natural features.

Natural features

Shark Bay is best known for the stromatolites at Hamelin Pool — living structures built by cyanobacteria, among the oldest life forms on Earth, resembling fossils dating back 3.5 billion years. Its vast seagrass meadows, among the largest in the world, support one of the world's largest populations of dugongs, around 10,000 animals. At Monkey Mia, wild Indo-Pacific bottlenose dolphins regularly come to shore and interact with visitors under ranger supervision.

History

Aboriginal people have lived around Shark Bay for at least 22,000 years. The Dutch navigator Dirk Hartog made one of the first recorded European landfalls in Australia on an island in the bay in 1616, leaving an inscribed pewter plate. William Dampier visited in 1699 and gave the bay its English name. The town of Denham is the main settlement; the region's economy has rested on pearling, fishing, salt production and tourism.
