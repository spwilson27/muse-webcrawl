Scotland in the Early Middle Ages

Scotland in the Early Middle Ages, between the departure of the Romans from northern Britain in the early fifth century and the establishment of a unitary Scottish kingdom in Alba in the ninth and tenth centuries, was a period in which the region north of the Firth of Forth was divided between several competing peoples and kingdoms.

The peoples

Four main groups occupied the territory of modern Scotland. The Picts held the north and east; the Gaelic kingdom of Dál Riata lay in the west (modern Argyll); the Brittonic kingdom of Strathclyde held the southwest; and the Anglian kingdom of Northumbria controlled the southeast as far as the Forth.

Christianity

Christianity spread through the region in the fifth and sixth centuries. St Ninian is traditionally associated with Whithorn in Galloway, while St Columba founded the monastery of Iona in 563 and missionised among the Picts. The Ionan church, with its distinctive dating of Easter and tonsure, came into conflict with the Roman practice; the Synod of Whitby (664) resolved the question in Northumbria in Rome's favour, and the Pictish king Nechtan adopted Roman usages in the early eighth century.

Viking Age

From the 790s, Norse raiders attacked coastal monasteries — Iona was attacked repeatedly — and Norse settlement transformed the Northern and Western Isles. The pressure of Viking warfare is often credited with accelerating the union of Picts and Scots under Kenneth MacAlpin (Cináed mac Ailpín), traditionally dated to 843, creating the kingdom of Alba.
