Telophase

Telophase (from Ancient Greek telos, "end" and phasis, "stage") is the final stage in both meiosis and mitosis in a eukaryotic cell. During telophase, the effects of prophase and prometaphase — the breakdown of the nuclear membrane and the condensation of chromosomes — are reversed: two new nuclear membranes form around each set of chromosomes, and the chromosomes decondense back into chromatin.

Process

As telophase begins, the sister chromatids, now individual chromosomes, reach opposite poles of the cell. The mitotic spindle disassembles, nuclear pore complexes assemble, and the nucleolus reappears in each new nucleus. In many cells, cytokinesis — the physical division of the cytoplasm, by a cleavage furrow in animal cells or a cell plate in plant cells — begins during late telophase, completing the production of two genetically identical daughter cells in mitosis.

Meiotic telophase

Meiosis has two telophases, telophase I and telophase II, corresponding to its two successive divisions. In telophase I the homologous chromosomes reach the poles; in telophase II sister chromatids separate into four haploid daughter cells.
