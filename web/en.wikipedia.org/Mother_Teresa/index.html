Mary Teresa Bojaxhiu MC (born Anjezë Gonxhe Bojaxhiu; 26 August 1910 – 5 September 1997), better known as Mother Teresa or Saint Teresa of Calcutta, was an Albanian-Indian Catholic nun, founder of the Missionaries of Charity and a Catholic saint. Born in Skopje, then part of the Ottoman Empire, she was raised in a devout Catholic family and at 18 joined the Sisters of Loreto in Ireland, moving to India in 1929 to teach in Calcutta.

Missionaries of Charity

In 1946, on a train journey to Darjeeling, Teresa experienced what she described as a "call within a call" to serve "the poorest of the poor." She left her convent with permission in 1948 and in 1950 founded the Missionaries of Charity, whose sisters took a fourth vow of wholehearted free service to the poorest. The congregation opened Nirmal Hriday, a home for the dying, in 1952, followed by leprosy clinics and orphanages; by her death it operated hundreds of centres in more than 100 countries.

Recognition and criticism

Mother Teresa received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1979 and India's highest civilian honour, the Bharat Ratna, in 1980. She was also criticised during and after her life for the standard of medical care in her homes, her opposition to contraception and abortion, and the opacity of her order's finances — a debate documented by critics such as Christopher Hitchens and by scholarly studies. Letters published posthumously revealed that for decades she experienced a profound sense of God's absence, which she accepted as sharing in the suffering of the poor.

Canonisation

She was beatified by Pope John Paul II in 2003 as Blessed Teresa of Calcutta and canonised by Pope Francis on 4 September 2016. Her feast day is 5 September.
