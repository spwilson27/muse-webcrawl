Molecular biology

The central dogma: DNA is transcribed to RNA, which is translated into protein.

Molecular biology is a branch of biology that seeks to understand the basis of biological activity at the molecular level, particularly the interactions between the various systems of a cell, including the interrelationship of DNA, RNA and protein synthesis and how these interactions are regulated.

Central dogma

The field is organised around the central dogma of molecular biology, formulated by Francis Crick (1958, restated 1970): sequence information flows from DNA to RNA to protein. DNA replication copies the genome; transcription produces RNA; translation, carried out by ribosomes reading messenger RNA, assembles proteins. Exceptions and additions include reverse transcription in retroviruses and the many regulatory roles of non-coding RNAs.

History

Molecular biology emerged in the 1930s–1950s as physicists and chemists turned to biological problems. Key milestones include Oswald Avery's demonstration (1944) that DNA carries hereditary information, the Hershey–Chase experiment (1952), the structure of DNA by Watson, Crick, Franklin, and Wilkins (1953), the cracking of the genetic code in the 1960s, and the development of recombinant DNA technology in the 1970s.

Techniques

  • DNA sequencing — from Sanger sequencing to high-throughput next-generation platforms.
  • Polymerase chain reaction (PCR) — amplification of specific DNA sequences (Kary Mullis, 1983).
  • Molecular cloning and expression — plasmids, restriction enzymes, and heterologous expression of proteins.
  • Gel electrophoresis, blotting, and qPCR — separation and quantification of nucleic acids and proteins.
  • Genome editing — CRISPR–Cas systems adapted for targeted modification of DNA in living cells.
  • Structural methods — X-ray crystallography, NMR, and cryo-electron microscopy.

Relation to other fields

Molecular biology overlaps heavily with biochemistry and genetics, and underpins genomics, proteomics, cell biology, developmental biology, and much of modern medicine and biotechnology.
