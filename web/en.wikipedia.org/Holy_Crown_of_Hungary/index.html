Holy Crown of Hungary

The Holy Crown of Hungary (Hungarian: Szent Korona), also known as the Crown of Saint Stephen, is the coronation crown used by the Kingdom of Hungary for most of its existence, with kings required to be crowned with it to be considered legitimate. Traditionally attributed to the coronation of Stephen I in the year 1000 or 1001, the surviving crown is a composite of two main parts made in the 11th and 12th centuries. Its cross famously tilts to one side. Since 1 January 2000 the crown has been displayed in the Dome Hall of the Hungarian Parliament Building in Budapest.

Construction and origins

Art historians distinguish two principal components. The lower band, the Corona graeca, is a Byzantine crown of the 1070s: its enamel plaques bear Greek inscriptions and depict Christ, angels, saints, the Byzantine emperor Michael VII Doukas, his son Constantine, and King Géza I of Hungary — it was evidently a gift from Constantinople to Géza's wife. The upper part, the Corona latina, consists of intersecting gold straps with Latin-inscribed enamel images of Christ and the apostles, probably made in Hungary in the late 12th century, possibly under Béla III. The two were joined at some point to form the present crown, surmounted by a cross that was bent — according to tradition by accident, when the crown was hastily hidden or dropped during one of its many turbulent journeys; the tilt was left unrepaired and became iconic.

Doctrine of the Holy Crown

Uniquely in Europe, Hungarian constitutional thought developed a "Doctrine of the Holy Crown": the crown itself, rather than the king, embodied sovereignty and the corporate body of the realm — the king and the estates were "members" of the Holy Crown, and lands of the crown could not lawfully be alienated. Codified from the late medieval period (notably in István Werbőczy's Tripartitum, 1514), the doctrine survived into the 20th century and still echoes in Hungarian public law: the crown appears on Hungary's coat of arms and is named in the preamble of the 2011 Fundamental Law.

History

The crown's biography mirrors Hungary's: it was fought over in succession crises, seized by rivals, taken abroad by widowed queens, pawned, buried, and hidden repeatedly. It was taken to Vienna by Habsburg rulers, recovered by Hungarian patriots, buried near Orsova in 1849 to hide it from Austrian forces after the failed revolution (recovered in 1853), and used for the coronations of Franz Joseph in 1867 and Charles IV in 1916 — the last Hungarian coronation. In 1945 the Hungarian Crown Guard carried it west to escape the Red Army and entrusted it to the United States Army; it was stored at Fort Knox. President Jimmy Carter returned it to Hungary on 6 January 1978, over the objections of some Hungarian émigrés. Displayed in the Hungarian National Museum until 1999, it was transferred with the coronation mantle, orb, and sceptre to Parliament on 1 January 2000 under the guard of the Hungarian Defence Forces' Crown Guard.
