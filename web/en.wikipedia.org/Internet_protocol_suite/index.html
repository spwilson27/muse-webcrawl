The Internet protocol suite, commonly known as TCP/IP, is the conceptual model and set of communications protocols used on the Internet and similar computer networks. It provides end-to-end data communication specifying how data should be packetised, addressed, transmitted, routed, and received. The suite is named after its two foundational protocols: the Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) and the Internet Protocol (IP).

The suite's four-layer model abstracts communication from link hardware up to applications.

Layers

  • Link layer — communication within a single network segment: Ethernet, Wi-Fi, PPP, ARP (in some classifications).
  • Internet layer — addressing and routing across networks: IP (IPv4 and IPv6), ICMP, IPsec.
  • Transport layer — host-to-host delivery: TCP (reliable, connection-oriented), UDP (lightweight, datagram), SCTP, and QUIC (built on UDP).
  • Application layer — process-to-process protocols: HTTP, DNS, SMTP, FTP, SSH, DHCP, TLS (often treated as sitting between transport and application).

History

The suite emerged from DARPA-funded research on packet switching. Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn's 1974 paper described a protocol for interconnecting dissimilar networks; by 1978 the design had been split into TCP and IP. The ARPANET switched from NCP to TCP/IP on 1 January 1983, and the suite's inclusion in Berkeley Unix (4.2BSD) spread it through universities. Its open, freely available standards — published as RFCs through what became the IETF — helped it prevail over the rival OSI model and proprietary stacks.

Principles

The suite embodies the end-to-end principle: the network core is kept simple and general, with intelligence and reliability functions placed at the endpoints. Encapsulation lets each layer treat the layer above's data as an opaque payload, so applications can evolve independently of the underlying hardware — the "hourglass" architecture that let the Internet scale across technologies from dial-up to fibre and 5G.
