Suspense

Suspense is a state of anxiety or excitement caused by mysteriousness, uncertainty, doubt, or undecided events. In works of narrative fiction, suspense is the feeling of pleasurable fascination and excitement mixed with apprehension that develops in an audience as it anticipates the outcome of a plot, particularly when the outcome matters to characters with whom the audience sympathises.

Definition

Suspense is generally treated as one of the basic emotions of narrative, alongside surprise and mystery. Unlike surprise, which depends on the audience not knowing what will happen, suspense depends on the audience knowing (or strongly suspecting) something that the characters do not — or knowing the stakes of an outcome whose timing or exact form remains uncertain. The philosopher Noël Carroll analysed suspense as arising when a narrative poses a question with two opposed possible answers, one morally right but improbable and the other morally wrong but probable.

In film and literature

Alfred Hitchcock, often called the "Master of Suspense," famously illustrated the technique with his example of a bomb under a table: if the bomb explodes without warning the audience gets fifteen seconds of surprise, but if the audience is shown the bomb first, an ordinary conversation becomes fifteen minutes of suspense. Suspense is a central device in the thriller, horror, mystery, and adventure genres, and literary devices used to generate it include cliffhangers, red herrings, foreshadowing, and dramatic irony.

Psychology

Psychologists and narratologists have studied suspense as a form of pleasurable negative emotion: readers and viewers voluntarily seek out stories that make them anxious, a paradox related to the enjoyment of tragedy and horror. Research suggests suspense is strongest when the audience cares about the characters, when the threat is vivid, and when the resolution is delayed.
