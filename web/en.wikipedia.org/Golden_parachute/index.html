Golden parachute

A golden parachute is an agreement between a company and a senior executive specifying that the executive will receive substantial benefits — such as severance pay, bonuses, stock options, or accelerated vesting — if their employment is terminated, typically following a merger or takeover (a "change of control"). The term arose during the takeover wave of the early 1980s.

Purpose and criticism

Supporters argue that golden parachutes help attract and retain executives, align their interests with shareholders by removing personal incentives to resist value-enhancing takeovers, and compensate for takeover risk. Critics regard them as rewards for failure and a symptom of weak corporate governance, especially when payouts are large relative to shareholder returns. A related device, the "tin parachute," extends smaller protections to broader groups of employees.

Regulation

In the United States, the Tax Reform Act of 1984 imposed an excise tax and loss of corporate deduction on "excess parachute payments" exceeding three times an executive's average compensation. The Dodd–Frank Act of 2010 required shareholder advisory votes ("say on golden parachutes") on such arrangements in merger proxies.
