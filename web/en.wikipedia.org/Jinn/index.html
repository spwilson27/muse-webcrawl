Jinn

[Image placeholder: a jinn in traditional depiction]

Jinn (Arabic: جنّ, jinn), also known as djinn or genies, are invisible creatures in early pre-Islamic Arabian mythology and later in Islamic culture and beliefs. Like humans, they are accountable for their deeds and can be either believers (Muslims) or unbelievers (kuffar), depending on whether they accept God's guidance. Since jinn are neither innately evil nor innately good, Islam acknowledged spirits from other religions and integrated them into its own understanding.

In an Islamic context, the term jinn is used for both a collective designation for any supernatural creature and also to refer to a specific type of invisible entity made of smokeless fire. Belief in jinn is a central part of the Muslim faith; the Quran mentions them frequently, and the 72nd surah is titled Sūrat al-Jinn.

Etymology

The word jinn derives from the Arabic root j-n-n (جنّ), whose primary meaning is "to hide" or "to conceal". Some authors interpret the word to mean, literally, "beings that are concealed from the senses". The English word "genie" comes from the French génie, which translators of One Thousand and One Nights borrowed because of its similarity in sound and meaning to jinnī.

Pre-Islamic Arabia

In pre-Islamic Arabia, jinn were believed to inspire poets and soothsayers. They were not regarded as particularly powerful compared with gods, but were thought to frequent desolate and impure places such as graveyards, ruins, and deserts. Individual jinn were sometimes worshipped, though they were never elevated to the level of true deities in the way the gods of the Arabian pantheon were.

In Islam

According to the Quran, jinn were created from "smokeless fire" (Quran 15:27) before humans were created from clay. Like human beings, jinn have free will and will be judged on the Day of Judgment. Believing jinn are sometimes called Muslim jinn. Iblis, the Islamic equivalent of Satan, is regarded in most traditions as a jinn who refused to prostrate himself before Adam.

Jinn are mentioned in the Quran in numerous verses, and a well-known incident describes a group of jinn listening to Muhammad recite the Quran and converting to Islam (Quran 46:29–32 and 72:1–19).

In folklore and popular culture

Jinn feature prominently in Middle Eastern folklore, most famously in One Thousand and One Nights, where tales such as "Aladdin and the Magic Lamp" and "Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves" depict jinn (genies) bound to objects such as lamps and rings, compelled to grant wishes to whoever possesses them. In modern popular culture, jinn appear in films, television series, literature, and games around the world.
