Hirschsprung's disease

SpecialtyPediatric gastroenterology, pediatric surgery
SymptomsConstipation, abdominal distension, delayed meconium
CauseAbsence of enteric ganglion cells in part of the colon
TreatmentSurgery (pull-through procedure)

Hirschsprung's disease is a congenital disorder in which nerve cells (ganglion cells) are missing from a segment of the large intestine, so that the affected segment cannot relax and pass stool. It is named after the Danish physician Harald Hirschsprung, who described it in 1888. It occurs in roughly 1 in 5,000 live births and is more common in males and in children with Down syndrome.

Signs and symptoms

Newborns typically fail to pass meconium within the first 48 hours of life and develop abdominal distension, vomiting, and constipation. A serious complication is Hirschsprung-associated enterocolitis. In older children, chronic severe constipation and failure to thrive predominate.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is suggested by imaging showing a narrowed aganglionic segment and confirmed by rectal suction biopsy demonstrating absent ganglion cells. Treatment is surgical: the aganglionic segment is removed or bypassed in a "pull-through" operation (such as the Swenson, Duhamel, or Soave procedures), after which most children achieve good bowel function.
