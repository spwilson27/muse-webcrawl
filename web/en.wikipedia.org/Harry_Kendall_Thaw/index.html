Harry Kendall Thaw (February 12, 1871 – February 22, 1947) was an American heir to a Pittsburgh railroad and mining fortune. He is best known for shooting and killing the renowned architect Stanford White on the rooftop of Madison Square Garden in New York City on June 25, 1906, in front of hundreds of witnesses. His two sensational murder trials — the first ended in a hung jury, the second in a verdict of not guilty by reason of insanity — were dubbed "the trial of the century" by the press.

Early life

Thaw was the son of William Thaw Sr., a coal and railroad baron whose fortune made the family among Pittsburgh's wealthiest. Harry attended the University of Pittsburgh and Harvard University, where he was expelled, reportedly for threatening a cab driver with a shotgun. He spent his inheritance — some $40,000 a year in allowance — on extravagant living, and was known for erratic and sadistic behaviour fuelled by drug use.

Marriage and the murder of Stanford White

In 1905 Thaw married Evelyn Nesbit, a famous artists' model and chorus girl. Nesbit had previously been involved with Stanford White, the celebrated architect of Madison Square Garden and other landmarks, beginning when she was about 16. Thaw became obsessed with White, whom he regarded as having ruined Nesbit, and attempted to have him exposed and ruined professionally. On the night of June 25, 1906, during a performance of the musical Mam'zelle Champagne at the Madison Square Garden roof theatre, Thaw approached White's table and shot him three times in the head and shoulder, reportedly declaring that White had ruined his wife (or, in other accounts, his life).

Trials and later life

Thaw's 1907 trial ended with a hung jury. At his second trial in 1908, his lawyers advanced a temporary-insanity defence — coined "dementia Americana" by his attorney — and Evelyn Nesbit's testimony about White's alleged abuses was central. Thaw was found not guilty by reason of insanity and committed to the Matteawan State Hospital for the Criminally Insane. He escaped to Canada in 1913, was extradited, and in 1915 was retried, found sane, and released. He was arrested again in 1916 for the kidnapping and whipping of a 19-year-old man, was again found insane, and was eventually released in 1924. He died of a heart attack in Miami in 1947, leaving an estate worth over a million dollars. The case has inspired books and films, including the 1955 film The Girl in the Red Velvet Swing and E. L. Doctorow's novel Ragtime.
