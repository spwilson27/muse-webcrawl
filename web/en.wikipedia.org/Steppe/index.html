Steppe

In physical geography, a steppe is an ecoregion characterised by grassland plains without closed forests except near rivers and lakes. Steppes are temperate grasslands found typically in regions too dry for forests but not dry enough to be deserts, with hot summers and cold winters. The world's largest steppe region, the Eurasian Steppe, stretches some 8,000 kilometres from Hungary and Ukraine across Central Asia to Manchuria.

Climate and ecology

Steppes receive roughly 250–500 mm of precipitation annually, most of it in spring and early summer. Deep, fertile soils known as chernozems ("black earth") develop under steppe grasslands, making them among the world's most productive agricultural soils when cultivated. Typical vegetation is dominated by perennial grasses and herbs; characteristic fauna includes saiga antelope, marmots, sousliks, corsac foxes and birds of prey such as steppe eagles. Large grazers — wild horses, and historically vast herds of bison-like ungulates — shaped the ecosystem.

The Eurasian Steppe in history

The steppe was the homeland of nomadic pastoralism and the corridor of the Silk Road. From it emerged successive nomadic peoples and empires — Scythians, Huns, Turks and Mongols — whose mastery of horse archery repeatedly transformed the history of the settled civilisations of Europe, Persia, India and China. The domestication of the horse on the western steppe around 3500 BC was one of the pivotal events of prehistory.

Other steppes

Related temperate grasslands include the North American prairies, the South American pampas and the South African veld, all of which share the steppe's climate regime and much of its ecology. Subtropical steppes form a transition zone at the margins of the great deserts.
