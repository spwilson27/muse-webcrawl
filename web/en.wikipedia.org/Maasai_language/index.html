The Maasai language (autonym: ɔl Maa) is an Eastern Nilotic language spoken by the Maasai people of southern Kenya and northern Tanzania. It belongs to the Maa group of languages, which also includes Samburu and Chamus, and it is classified within the Nilo-Saharan language family. Maasai society is traditionally semi-nomadic and pastoralist, and the language has developed an exceptionally rich vocabulary relating to cattle, grazing land, weather and age-set organisation.

Classification

Maasai is a member of the Eastern branch of the Nilotic languages, themselves part of the Eastern Sudanic grouping. Its closest relatives are the other Maa varieties; somewhat more distant relatives include Turkana, Kalenjin, Dinka and Nuer belong to other Nilotic branches (Southern and Western Nilotic respectively). Together with Lotuko (Otuho) of South Sudan, the Maa languages form the Lotuxo–Maa subgroup.

Sound system

Maasai has a consonant system that includes the implosives /ɓ/, /ɗ/, /ʄ/ and /ɠ/, along with a series of plain stops, fricatives, nasals, liquids and glides. The vowel system comprises nine or ten vowels arranged in two harmonic sets distinguished by tongue-root position, a widespread feature in Nilotic and other African languages. Tone is phonemic: high and low tones distinguish words and also encode grammatical information such as case on nouns.

Grammar

The language is head-marking and follows verb-initial word order (VSO/VOS tendencies appear in some constructions, with VSO most commonly cited). Nouns are marked for gender (masculine and feminine), number, and case; the nominative is tonally marked. The verb complex encodes subject agreement, tense-aspect-mood, directionals and applicative derivations. Number marking in nouns is famously irregular, using many different suffixes and stem changes.

Writing and use

Maasai is written in a Latin-based orthography developed during the twentieth century, partly through missionary work. A dictionary and grammar by Frans Mol, published in cooperation with Maasai speakers, is a standard reference. Despite pressure from Swahili and English, Maasai remains the first language of most ethnic Maasai. Community radio, including stations broadcasting in Maa, plays a role in maintaining the language.
