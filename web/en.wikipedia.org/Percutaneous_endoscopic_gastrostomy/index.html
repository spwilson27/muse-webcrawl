Placement of a feeding tube through the abdominal wall into the stomach.

Percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) is an endoscopic medical procedure in which a tube (PEG tube) is passed into a patient's stomach through the abdominal wall, most commonly to provide a means of feeding when oral intake is not adequate (for example, because of swallowing disorders or sedation). PEG administration of enteral nutrition is now one of the most common applications of endoscopy worldwide.

History

The PEG technique was introduced in 1980 by Michael W. L. Gauderer and Jeffrey L. Ponsky at the Children's Hospital of Cleveland as a means of establishing gastrostomy feeding without the risks of a laparotomy. The first successful procedure was performed on 12 June 1979.

Technique

An endoscope is passed into the stomach, which is insufflated with air so that it comes into close apposition with the anterior abdominal wall. Transillumination and finger indentation identify the placement site. A cannula is passed through the abdominal wall into the stomach under endoscopic vision; a guidewire or suture is grasped and drawn out through the mouth, and the feeding tube is pulled or pushed into place so that its internal bumper rests against the gastric wall.

Indications and complications

PEG tubes are indicated for long-term enteral nutrition in patients with dysphagia from stroke, motor neurone disease, multiple sclerosis, head and neck cancers, and cerebral palsy, among other conditions. Complications include peristomal infection, buried bumper syndrome, tube displacement, peritonitis, and aspiration. Reported complication rates are around 8–30 per cent for minor problems and under 2 per cent for major complications.
