Cradle of civilization

A cradle of civilization is a location and a culture where civilization was developed independently of other civilizations. The formation of urban settlements (cities) is the primary characteristic of a society that can be characterized as “civilized”. Other characteristics include a sedentary agriculture-based society, a social class structure, government, and systems of communication such as writing.

Scholars generally identify half a dozen cradles of civilization: Mesopotamia, Ancient Egypt, Ancient India (the Indus Valley), Ancient China, the Caral–Supe civilization of coastal Peru, and the Olmec civilization of Mexico.

Mesopotamia

In Mesopotamia (modern Iraq), in the Fertile Crescent between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, the Sumerians built some of the earliest known cities, such as Uruk, by about 4500–4000 BCE. Cuneiform writing was developed there around 3400–3300 BCE, and the region later hosted the Akkadian, Babylonian and Assyrian empires.

Egypt

Along the Nile valley, a unified Egyptian kingdom emerged around 3100 BCE, with hieroglyphic writing, monumental architecture such as the pyramids of Giza, and centralised administration lasting, with interruptions, for three millennia.

Indus Valley

The Indus Valley Civilization flourished from about 3300 to 1300 BCE in present-day Pakistan and northwest India, with planned cities such as Mohenjo-daro and Harappa featuring grid streets and sophisticated drainage systems.

China

In China, civilisation developed along the Yellow River; the Shang dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BCE) is the earliest Chinese dynasty documented by contemporary written records, including oracle bone inscriptions.

Mesoamerica and the Andes

In the Americas, the Caral–Supe civilization of Peru (c. 3500–1800 BCE) and the Olmec of Mexico (c. 1500–400 BCE) developed urban centres, monumental constructions and, in Mesoamerica, writing systems independently of the Old World.
