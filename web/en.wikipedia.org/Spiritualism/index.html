Spiritualism is a religious and social movement based on the belief that the spirits of the dead exist and can communicate with the living, typically through a medium during a séance. It emerged in the United States in 1848 and reached its peak of popularity from the 1840s to the 1920s, attracting millions of adherents in the English-speaking world and influencing movements such as Kardecist spiritism in Brazil and France.

Origins and spread

The movement is conventionally dated to March 1848, when sisters Kate and Margaret Fox of Hydesville, New York, claimed to communicate with a spirit through rapping sounds. The Fox sisters became celebrities and, despite Margaret's 1888 confession of fraud (later retracted), séances spread rapidly, aided by grief after the American Civil War and the First World War. Famous adherents included Sir Arthur Conan Doyle; famous investigators and critics included the magician Harry Houdini, who campaigned against fraudulent mediums.

Beliefs and practice

Spiritualists hold that the soul survives death and continues to evolve in a spirit world; communication aims at consolation, moral teaching and evidence of survival. Churches (such as Britain's Spiritualists' National Union, 1901) formalised the "Seven Principles", including the fatherhood of God, the brotherhood of man, and eternal progress. Phenomena claimed by mediums include trance speaking, materialisations, apports, table-turning and spirit photography — many repeatedly exposed as trickery, which contributed to the movement's decline, though spiritualist churches persist today.
