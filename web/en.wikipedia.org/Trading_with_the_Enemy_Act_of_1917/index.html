Trading with the Enemy Act of 1917

The Trading with the Enemy Act of 1917 (TWEA) is a United States federal law, enacted on 6 October 1917, that gives the President the power to oversee or restrict any and all trade between the United States and its enemies in times of war. It was passed during the First World War, shortly after the United States entered the conflict.

Provisions

The Act created the Office of Alien Property Custodian, which was empowered to seize the assets of enemy nationals. It also regulated patents, copyrights and trademarks held by enemy subjects, and prohibited most commerce with enemy states except under licence from the President.

Later use

Amendments in 1933 extended the President's powers to national emergencies in peacetime, and the Act underpinned measures such as the bank holiday of 1933. In 1977 the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) took over most peacetime emergency economic powers, leaving TWEA principally applicable to Cuba, which remained subject to a TWEA-based embargo for decades.
