The Hindu–Arabic numeral system (also known as the Indo-Arabic numeral system, Western Arabic numeral system, Hindu numeral system, or simply Arabic numerals in the West) is a positional decimal numeral system for representing numbers. It uses ten symbols — the digits 0 through 9 — whose value depends on their position, and a symbol for zero acting both as a placeholder and as a number in its own right. It is the most common system for the symbolic representation of numbers in the world today.

History

The system originated with Indian mathematicians. Positional notation with nine digits appeared in India by about the 6th century CE, developing from Brahmi numerals; the concept of zero (śūnya) as a full number with arithmetic rules was set out by Brahmagupta in 628 CE in the Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta. The system was transmitted westward through the Islamic world: al-Khwarizmi's treatise On the Calculation with Hindu Numerals (c. 825) spread it through the Arabic-speaking world, where the digit shapes evolved into distinct Eastern Arabic and Western ("ghubār") forms.

Europe learned of the system through Spain and Sicily. Fibonacci's Liber Abaci (1202) championed the "modus Indorum" (method of the Indians), and the digits gradually displaced Roman numerals in commerce and science between the 13th and 16th centuries, aided by the printing press, which standardised the glyph shapes into essentially their modern forms.

Properties

Because the system is positional with base ten, any number can be written with the ten digits, arithmetic algorithms (long addition, multiplication, division) are uniform and mechanical, and fractions can be extended into decimal notation (systematised by Simon Stevin in 1585). Positional systems with zero are dramatically more efficient for computation than additive systems such as Roman numerals.

Variants

The Eastern Arabic numerals (٠١٢٣٤٥٦٧٨٩) are used in much of the Arab world alongside or instead of the Western digits; Devanagari and many other Indic scripts have their own digit sets derived from the same source, as do Thai, Khmer and other Brahmic scripts. These are all members of the Hindu–Arabic numeral family.
