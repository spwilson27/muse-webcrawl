Royal commission

Image: Letters patent issued under the royal sign-manual

A royal commission is a major ad-hoc formal public inquiry into a defined issue, appointed in the name of a monarch (or their viceregal representative) in Commonwealth realms and some other constitutional monarchies. Royal commissions are called to investigate matters of great importance and usually of controversy.

Form and powers

A commission is appointed by letters patent on the advice of the government, and its members — the commissioners, frequently headed by a judge — are given terms of reference and, in many jurisdictions, coercive powers to summon witnesses and compel documents. Royal commissions can last for months or years and report with findings and recommendations; they cannot themselves prosecute or legislate, and governments are not bound to adopt their recommendations.

Examples

Notable commissions include the British Royal Commission on the Poor Laws (1832–34), Canada's Royal Commission on Aboriginal Peoples (1991–96), and numerous Australian commissions, culminating in exercises such as the Royal Commission into Institutional Responses to Child Sexual Abuse (2013–2017). In the United Kingdom, statutory public inquiries have largely supplanted royal commissions since the late 20th century.
