Hawking (birds)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hawking is a feeding strategy in birds involving catching flying insects in the air. The term is primarily used for birds that make short sallies from a perch to snatch an insect and then return to the perch, a behaviour also called flycatching or sallying. Birds that hawk continuously on the wing, such as swallows, swifts, and nightjars, are sometimes described as aerial hawkers.

Description

In the classic hawking pattern, a bird sits on an exposed perch with a clear view of its surroundings, watches for passing insects, and flies out — often with an audible snap of the bill — to intercept prey before returning to the same or a nearby perch. The broad, flat bills and prominent rictal bristles of many flycatchers are adaptations to this technique. Hawking contrasts with gleaning, in which insects are picked from foliage, and with ground-foraging.

Groups that hawk

The behaviour gives its name to the Old World flycatchers (Muscicapidae) and the tyrant flycatchers (Tyrannidae) of the Americas, the largest bird family. Drongos, monarch flycatchers, fantails, bee-eaters (which sally from perches after bees and wasps), some shrikes, and jacamars also hawk. Swallows, martins, swifts, and nightjars pursue insects continuously in flight, often over water or grassland, and many bats use an equivalent aerial-hawking strategy with echolocation.
