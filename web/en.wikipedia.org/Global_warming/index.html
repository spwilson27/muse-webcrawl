Global warming

Global warming is the long-term heating of Earth's surface observed since the pre-industrial period (between 1850 and 1900) due to human activities, primarily fossil fuel burning, which increases heat-trapping greenhouse gas levels in Earth's atmosphere. The term is commonly used interchangeably with climate change, though the latter encompasses both human-caused and natural changes.

Global surface temperature has risen by about 1.1–1.2 °C since the late 19th century, with the rate of warming since 1970 faster than in any comparable period in at least 2,000 years. Observed consequences include shrinking ice sheets and glaciers, sea-level rise, ocean warming and acidification, and shifting precipitation patterns.

Causes

The IPCC concludes it is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land. Carbon dioxide from burning coal, oil and gas is the largest driver; methane, nitrous oxide and industrial gases add further forcing, partially offset by cooling from aerosols. Concentrations of CO₂ are higher than at any time in at least two million years.

Effects and projections

Future warming depends on emissions pathways. Impacts already observed include more frequent and intense heatwaves, heavy precipitation events and droughts in many regions, retreating glaciers, and rising seas threatening coastal communities. Limiting warming to 1.5 °C, the aspirational goal of the Paris Agreement, requires deep, rapid emissions cuts reaching net zero CO₂ around mid-century.
