Henry Gustav Molaison (February 26, 1926 – December 2, 2008), known widely as Patient H.M., was an American man with severe epilepsy who, after undergoing a bilateral medial temporal lobectomy in 1953 to control his seizures, developed profound anterograde amnesia: he became unable to form new long-term declarative memories. His case was studied for over five decades, most famously by neuropsychologist Brenda Milner and later Suzanne Corkin, and it transformed the scientific understanding of memory.

Background and surgery

Molaison suffered debilitating epileptic seizures, attributed in part to a childhood head injury, that by his twenties resisted medication. In 1953, neurosurgeon William Beecher Scoville performed an experimental operation removing much of his medial temporal lobes on both sides, including large parts of the hippocampi, amygdalae, and surrounding cortex. The seizures were reduced, but Molaison was left with a severe memory impairment.

Memory impairment

Molaison's intelligence, personality, and short-term (working) memory remained largely intact, and he retained memories from before the surgery, especially from childhood, though memories of the years just before the operation were partly lost (graded retrograde amnesia). He could learn new motor skills — famously, his performance on mirror-drawing tasks improved with practice even though he never remembered doing them. This showed that procedural (implicit) memory depends on brain systems separate from those supporting conscious declarative memory.

Scientific significance

The 1957 paper by Scoville and Milner reporting the case established that the medial temporal lobe, and the hippocampus in particular, is critical for forming new declarative memories, and helped establish the modern distinction between short-term and long-term memory and between declarative and procedural memory systems. Molaison participated in hundreds of experiments at the Montreal Neurological Institute and MIT over 55 years. His identity was protected by the initials "H.M." until his death in 2008. After his death, his brain was preserved and sectioned into 2,401 slices for research at the University of California, San Diego.
