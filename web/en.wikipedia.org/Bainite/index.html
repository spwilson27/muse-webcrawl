Bainite

Bainite is a plate-like microstructure that forms in steels at temperatures between those at which pearlite and martensite form. It was first described by Edgar Bain and his colleagues at the United States Steel Corporation in the 1920s and 1930s, and is named after Bain.

Formation and structure

Bainite forms when austenite is cooled at an intermediate rate and held isothermally, or cooled continuously through the appropriate range. It consists of aggregates of ferrite plates (or laths) separated by regions enriched in carbon, which may contain cementite particles or retained austenite. Two main morphologies are recognised: upper bainite, formed at higher temperatures, with cementite between the ferrite laths; and lower bainite, formed at lower temperatures, with fine carbides also inside the ferrite plates.

Properties and applications

Bainitic microstructures can combine high strength with good toughness and wear resistance. Modern carbide-free bainitic steels, which exploit very fine ferrite plates and films of retained austenite, achieve exceptional strength and are used in rails, armour, bearings and automotive components. The kinetics and mechanism of the bainite transformation — whether displacive or diffusional in character — have been the subject of long-running scientific debate.
