Inuktitut (ᐃᓄᒃᑎᑐᑦ, "like the Inuit") is one of the principal Inuit languages of Canada, spoken by the Inuit in the Canadian Arctic. It is part of the Inuit language continuum stretching from Alaska across northern Canada to Greenland. Inuktitut is an official language of Nunavut and the Northwest Territories, and one of the official languages of the Nunavik region of Quebec.

Distribution and status

Most Inuktitut speakers live in communities north of the tree line, in Nunavut, the Inuvialuit Settlement Region of the Northwest Territories, Nunavik in northern Quebec, and Nunatsiavut in northern Labrador. The 2016 Canadian census reported roughly 39,000 speakers. In Nunavut, Inuktitut has co-official status alongside English and French, and territorial law requires government services to be available in the language.

Writing systems

Inuktitut is written in two main scripts. In the eastern Arctic (Nunavut and Nunavik), a syllabic writing system adapted from Cree syllabics — itself derived from the work of missionary James Evans — is predominant. In the western Arctic and Labrador, the Latin alphabet is more common. The syllabary represents consonant–vowel pairs with rotated triangular characters; long vowels are marked with a dot above the character, and final consonants with small superscript forms.

Phonology and grammar

Inuktitut is a polysynthetic language: single words can carry the meaning of entire sentences through long chains of suffixes attached to a root. It typically has three vowels (i, u, a, each short or long) and around fifteen consonants, though inventories vary by dialect. The language is ergative–absolutive in alignment and distinguishes singular, dual, and plural number.

Dialects

Dialects include Inuinnaqtun in the west, the Kivalliq and Aivilik varieties, North and South Baffin dialects, Nunavik dialect, and Labrador's Nunatsiavummiutut. The Inuit Circumpolar Council promotes standardization efforts while recognizing regional variation.
