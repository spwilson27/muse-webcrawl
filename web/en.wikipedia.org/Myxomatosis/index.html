Myxomatosis is a disease caused by the myxoma virus, a poxvirus that naturally infects South American rabbits of the genus Sylvilagus, in which it causes only mild skin tumours. In the European rabbit (Oryctolagus cuniculus) the same virus causes severe, usually fatal disease, with gelatinous skin swellings (myxomas), swollen and discharging eyes, fever and immunosuppression.

Biological control

The virus is notorious for its deliberate release as a biological control agent. In 1950 it was introduced into Australia, where it killed an estimated 99 percent or more of infected rabbits in the first epizootics and initially reduced the huge feral rabbit population dramatically; a release in France in 1952 spread across Europe and devastated wild and domestic rabbits in Britain and elsewhere. Over subsequent decades, rabbit populations evolved greater resistance and the virus evolved intermediate virulence — a classic case study in coevolution studied by Frank Fenner and others, since continuing transmission requires that hosts survive long enough to infect vectors.

Course of disease

Infection is spread mainly by biting insects — rabbit fleas in Britain, mosquitoes in Australia. In susceptible rabbits death typically occurs within about two weeks, often from secondary bacterial infection. Vaccines exist for domestic rabbits, and deliberate infection of rabbits is illegal in some jurisdictions.
