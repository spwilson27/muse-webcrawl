Many-worlds interpretation

In the many-worlds interpretation, each measurement splits the universe into branches.

The many-worlds interpretation (MWI) is an interpretation of quantum mechanics that asserts that the universal wave function is objectively real, and that there is no wave function collapse. It implies that all possible outcomes of quantum measurements are physically realised in some "world" or universe.

Origin

The interpretation was formulated by Hugh Everett in 1957 as "relative state" theory. Bryce DeWitt popularised it in the 1960s and 1970s and gave it the name "many-worlds." Everett proposed that measurements do not produce single outcomes but correlate the observer's state with each possible result, so that the universe effectively branches.

Key ideas

In MWI, decoherence explains why the branches do not interfere with one another, and the Born rule's probabilities are understood in terms of self-locating uncertainty or decision theory. The interpretation removes the special role of measurement and the observer, at the cost of an enormous multiplicity of universes. It is one of the interpretations most favoured by physicists in informal polls, though it remains controversial, particularly regarding the origin of probability.
