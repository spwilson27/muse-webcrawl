Touch typing

Touch typing (also called touch type or touch keyboarding) is typing without using the sense of sight to find the keys. A touch typist knows the location of the keys on the keyboard through muscle memory and can type while looking at the screen or at source material rather than at their fingers. The term is also used for the method of learning to type this way, traditionally taught with the fingers resting on the home row.

Home row

On a QWERTY keyboard, the home row is the middle row of letter keys. The fingers of the left hand rest on A, S, D, and F, and those of the right hand on J, K, L, and the semicolon key. The index fingers locate the row by means of small raised bumps found on the F and J keys of most keyboards. Each finger is responsible for a column of keys above and below its home position, and the thumbs operate the space bar.

History

Touch typing developed alongside the commercial typewriter in the late nineteenth century. Frank Edward McGurrin, a court stenographer from Salt Lake City, is credited with popularizing the method; in 1888 he won a widely publicized typing contest in Cincinnati using touch typing on a Remington typewriter, defeating a rival who used the sight-based "hunt and peck" method. The victory helped establish touch typing and the QWERTY layout as standards.

Speed and technique

Typing speed is measured in words per minute (WPM), conventionally counting five characters as one word. Skilled touch typists commonly sustain 50–80 WPM, and professional typists and transcriptionists can exceed 100 WPM. Proper technique emphasizes keeping the fingers on the home row, striking keys with the assigned finger, maintaining posture, and avoiding looking at the keyboard, which over time builds automatic, accurate motor patterns.

Learning

Touch typing is taught in schools and through typing-tutor software and online courses that introduce keys row by row and drill letter combinations. Regular deliberate practice over a period of weeks is usually sufficient to internalize the layout, after which speed increases with continued use.
