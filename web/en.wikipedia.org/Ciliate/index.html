Ciliate

DomainEukaryota
CladeAlveolata
PhylumCiliophora
DiversityAbout 8,000 described species

The ciliates are a group of alveolate protists characterised by the presence of hair-like organelles called cilia, which are used in swimming, crawling, attachment, feeding, and sensation. The phylum Ciliophora includes some of the most complex single-celled organisms known, such as Paramecium, Tetrahymena, and Stentor.

Cell structure

Ciliates typically possess two kinds of nuclei: a large macronucleus, which controls the cell's everyday functions and is generated from the micronucleus by amplification of the genome, and one or more small micronuclei involved in sexual reproduction. Food is often taken in through a specialised mouth region, the cytostome, and digestion occurs in food vacuoles.

Reproduction

Ciliates reproduce asexually by transverse fission. They also undergo conjugation, a sexual process in which two cells exchange haploid micronuclear material, producing new genetic combinations without an increase in cell number.

Ecology

Most ciliates are free-living in freshwater, marine, and damp terrestrial habitats, feeding on bacteria, algae, and other small organisms, and are important in aquatic food webs and in wastewater treatment. A few species are parasitic, including Balantidium coli, the only ciliate known to infect humans.
