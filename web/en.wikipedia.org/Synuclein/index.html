Synuclein

Synucleins are a family of small, soluble proteins found primarily in neural tissue and in certain tumours. The family comprises three known members in vertebrates: alpha-synuclein, beta-synuclein, and gamma-synuclein (also called synoretin). Synucleins are typically 113–143 amino acids long, are natively unfolded in isolation, and fold into alpha-helical structures when binding membranes, particularly synaptic vesicles.

Members and structure

All synucleins share a conserved N-terminal region containing KTKEGV repeat motifs that mediate lipid binding, a central hydrophobic region (in alpha-synuclein, the NAC domain, which drives aggregation), and an acidic C-terminal tail that interacts with calcium and other proteins. Alpha-synuclein is abundant at presynaptic terminals and is thought to modulate synaptic vesicle release and neurotransmitter signalling; beta-synuclein lacks part of the NAC region and may inhibit aggregation of the alpha form; gamma-synuclein is also expressed in peripheral tissues and some cancers.

Disease association

Alpha-synuclein is the major component of Lewy bodies and Lewy neurites, the pathological inclusions characteristic of Parkinson's disease, dementia with Lewy bodies, and multiple system atrophy—collectively termed synucleinopathies. Point mutations in the SNCA gene (encoding alpha-synuclein), and multiplications of the whole gene, cause familial forms of Parkinson's disease, establishing a direct causal link between the protein and neurodegeneration. Misfolded alpha-synuclein can propagate between cells in a prion-like manner, seeding further aggregation.

History

The first synuclein was identified in 1988 in the electric ray Torpedo, named for its localisation to the synapse and nucleus. Alpha-synuclein was later recognised as the precursor of the non-amyloid-beta component of Alzheimer's disease plaques before its starring role in Parkinson's pathology was established in 1997.
