James VI and I (James Charles Stuart; 19 June 1566 – 27 March 1625) was King of Scotland as James VI from 1567 and King of England and Ireland as James I from 1603 until his death — the first monarch to reign over all three kingdoms, in the Union of the Crowns.

King of Scots

James was the son of Mary, Queen of Scots, and her second husband, Lord Darnley. He was crowned at thirteen months old after his mother's forced abdication, and raised under a series of regents during a turbulent minority marked by civil war between his mother's supporters and the Protestant lords. Tutored by the historian George Buchanan, he became an accomplished scholar. He took personal control in the 1580s, weathered the Gowrie conspiracy of 1600, and cultivated his claim to succeed the childless Elizabeth I of England.

King of England

On Elizabeth's death in March 1603 James rode south to take the English throne, proclaiming himself "King of Great Britain" and promoting closer union, though the English and Scottish parliaments remained separate. His reign saw the Gunpowder Plot of 1605, the colonisation of Ulster and the founding of Jamestown in Virginia (1607), and escalating conflict with Parliament over royal finance, impositions, and his belief in the divine right of kings, which he expounded in The True Law of Free Monarchies (1598) and Basilikon Doron (1599).

Culture and scholarship

James commissioned the Authorized Version of the Bible (1611), the King James Version, which shaped English prose for centuries. He wrote on witchcraft in Daemonologie (1597) after the North Berwick witch trials, patronised Shakespeare's company — renamed the King's Men — and disliked tobacco, attacking it in A Counterblaste to Tobacco (1604). His court was marked by conspicuous favourites, notably Robert Carr and George Villiers, Duke of Buckingham.

Death and succession

James died in 1625 and was succeeded by his son Charles I. His daughter Elizabeth became Queen of Bohemia, the "Winter Queen"; through her, James is an ancestor of the Hanoverian line that inherited the British throne in 1714.
