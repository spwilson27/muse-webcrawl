Portal hypertension

Portal hypertension is elevated pressure in the portal venous system, the network of veins that carries blood from the gastrointestinal tract, spleen, and pancreas to the liver. It is defined by an increase in the hepatic venous pressure gradient (HVPG) to above 5 mmHg; pressures of 10 mmHg or more are considered clinically significant because they predict the development of complications such as varices and ascites.

Portal hypertension is most commonly caused by cirrhosis of the liver, in which fibrosis and regenerative nodules increase resistance to blood flow through the hepatic sinusoids. It is a major driver of morbidity and mortality in chronic liver disease.

Causes

Causes are conventionally classified by the site of increased resistance:

  • Prehepatic: portal vein thrombosis, splenic vein thrombosis, congenital portal vein stenosis.
  • Intrahepatic: cirrhosis (the most common cause worldwide), schistosomiasis, nodular regenerative hyperplasia, congenital hepatic fibrosis, and sinusoidal obstruction syndrome.
  • Posthepatic: Budd–Chiari syndrome (hepatic vein outflow obstruction), constrictive pericarditis, and severe right-sided heart failure.

Signs and complications

The principal consequences of portal hypertension arise from the formation of portosystemic collateral vessels and from sodium and water retention:

  • Gastroesophageal varices — dilated veins in the esophagus and stomach that may rupture and cause life-threatening bleeding.
  • Ascites — accumulation of fluid in the peritoneal cavity.
  • Splenomegaly with hypersplenism, causing thrombocytopenia and leukopenia.
  • Hepatic encephalopathy — neuropsychiatric disturbance as nitrogenous waste bypasses the liver.
  • Caput medusae — visible collateral veins radiating from the umbilicus.
  • Hepatorenal syndrome and hepatopulmonary syndrome in advanced disease.

Diagnosis

The reference standard is measurement of the hepatic venous pressure gradient via a balloon catheter wedged in a hepatic vein. Non-invasive assessment includes abdominal ultrasound with Doppler, transient elastography, cross-sectional imaging (CT or MRI) demonstrating collaterals and splenomegaly, and upper endoscopy to screen for varices.

Treatment

Management targets both the pressure itself and its complications. Non-selective beta blockers such as propranolol, nadolol, and carvedilol reduce portal inflow and are used for prophylaxis of variceal bleeding. Acute variceal hemorrhage is treated with vasoactive drugs (terlipressin, octreotide), endoscopic band ligation, antibiotics, and resuscitation. Refractory cases may require a transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS), which creates a channel between the portal and hepatic veins. Treating the underlying cause — antiviral therapy, abstinence from alcohol, anticoagulation where appropriate — and ultimately liver transplantation offer the only definitive remedies in advanced cirrhosis.
