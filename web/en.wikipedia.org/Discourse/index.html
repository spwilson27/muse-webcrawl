Discourse

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Discourse is a generalization of the notion of a conversation to encompass all forms of communication. In semantics and discourse analysis, discourse is a conceptual generalization of conversation within each modality and context of communication. In the humanities and social sciences, the term is used in several theoretical traditions, most prominently in the work of Michel Foucault, where a discourse is a system of thought, knowledge, or communication that constructs our experience of the world.

Definitions

The seminal work of Émile Benveniste defines discourse as "every utterance assuming a speaker and a hearer, and in the speaker, the intention of influencing the other in some way." In linguistics, discourse is a unit of language longer than a single sentence; discourse analysis studies how texts and conversations fit together and how language is used in social contexts.

Discourse in the social sciences

Following Foucault, a discourse is a way of constituting knowledge, together with the social practices, forms of subjectivity, and power relations which inhere in such knowledges. Discourses are not simply ways of speaking or of presenting things; they define what can be said about a topic, who can speak with authority, and what counts as true within a given historical period. Examples frequently discussed include discourses of medicine, law, psychiatry, and sexuality.

In political theory, Ernest Laclau and Chantal Mouffe used the concept of discourse to describe the structured totality resulting from articulatory practice, extending the notion beyond language to social relations generally.

Discourse analysis

Discourse analysis is a broad interdisciplinary field encompassing critical discourse analysis, conversation analysis, and discursive psychology. It examines written, spoken, and signed language use, the social contexts in which discourse occurs, and the relations between discourse and power, identity, and ideology.
