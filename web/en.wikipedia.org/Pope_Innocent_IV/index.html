Pope Innocent IV

Pope Innocent IV (Latin: Innocentius IV; c. 1195 – 7 December 1254), born Sinibaldo Fieschi, was head of the Catholic Church and ruler of the Papal States from 25 June 1243 until his death. A distinguished canon lawyer before his election, he is remembered chiefly for his protracted conflict with the Holy Roman Emperor Frederick II, whom he deposed at the First Council of Lyon in 1245.

Early life and election

Sinibaldo Fieschi was born in Genoa into a noble family, studied law at Parma and Bologna, and became one of the leading canonists of his age, serving as vice-chancellor of the Roman Church and being created cardinal in 1227 by Gregory IX. After the death of Celestine IV and a long vacancy, the cardinals elected him pope at Anagni in June 1243.

Conflict with Frederick II

Although Frederick II had once been Fieschi's friend, relations collapsed over the Lombard cities and imperial claims in Italy. In 1244 Innocent fled secretly from Italy to Lyon, beyond the emperor's reach, and there convened the First Council of Lyon in 1245, which pronounced Frederick deposed. The sentence fueled decades of war between the papacy and the Hohenstaufen, ending only after Frederick's death in 1250 and the destruction of his house.

Other actions

Innocent IV dispatched diplomatic missions to the Mongols, including the Franciscan Giovanni da Pian del Carpine (1245–47), seeking alliance and conversion. His bull Ad extirpanda (1252) regulated the inquisitions in Italy, controversially permitting torture to extract confessions. As a jurist he wrote an influential commentary on the Decretals (Apparatus in quinque libros decretalium) and developed the legal concept of the corporation as a "persona ficta" (fictitious person). He died in Naples on 7 December 1254, shortly after the death of Frederick's son Conrad IV.
