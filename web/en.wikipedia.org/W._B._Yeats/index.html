W. B. Yeats

William Butler Yeats (13 June 1865 – 28 January 1939) was an Irish poet, dramatist and writer, one of the foremost figures of twentieth-century literature. A driving force of the Irish Literary Revival, he co-founded the Abbey Theatre and served as a senator of the Irish Free State. He received the Nobel Prize in Literature in 1923. His best-known poems include "The Second Coming", "Sailing to Byzantium", "Among School Children" and "The Lake Isle of Innisfree".

Early life and the Celtic Twilight

Yeats was born in Dublin, son of the painter John Butler Yeats, and spent much of his childhood in County Sligo, whose landscape and folklore pervade his work. His early poetry, such as The Wanderings of Oisin (1889) and "The Lake Isle of Innisfree" (1890), drew on Irish mythology and a dreamy, Pre-Raphaelite style. With Lady Gregory and Edward Martyn he founded the Irish Literary Theatre, which became the Abbey Theatre in 1904, for which he wrote plays including Cathleen ni Houlihan.

Personal life and occult interests

Yeats loved Maud Gonne, the nationalist activist who inspired much of his poetry, but she refused his proposals; he eventually married Georgie Hyde-Lees in 1917, whose automatic writing produced the material of his esoteric system in A Vision (1925), with its interlocking "gyres" of history. He had been a member of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn and was deeply influenced by mysticism and the occult throughout his life.

Later work and public life

The Easter Rising of 1916 and the Irish War of Independence and Civil War darkened his poetry into the austere greatness of The Tower (1928) and The Winding Stair (1933), with poems such as "Easter, 1916", "The Second Coming" (1919) and "Sailing to Byzantium". He served two terms as a senator of the Irish Free State from 1922. He died in France in 1939 and, after the war, his remains were reburied at Drumcliff, County Sligo, beneath his own chosen epitaph: "Cast a cold Eye / On Life, on Death. / Horseman, pass by!"
