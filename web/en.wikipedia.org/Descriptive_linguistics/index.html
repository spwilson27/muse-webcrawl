Descriptive linguistics

Descriptive linguistics is the branch of linguistics concerned with describing how a language is actually spoken or signed by its users, without judging usage as correct or incorrect. It stands in contrast to prescriptive linguistics, which lays down rules of proper usage. The descriptive linguist records and analyzes the phonology, morphology, syntax, semantics and discourse patterns of a language as found in the speech community — work known as language description or field linguistics.

History

Systematic description has ancient roots: Pāṇini's grammar of Sanskrit (c. 4th century BCE) is a rigorously descriptive account of the language of his time. In the modern era, the descriptive tradition was shaped by Franz Boas and Edward Sapir, whose documentation of Indigenous American languages demanded describing each language "in its own terms" rather than through the categories of Latin grammar. Leonard Bloomfield's Language (1933) codified the American descriptivist programme, focused on distributional analysis of recorded utterances. After the rise of generative grammar, many linguists shifted toward theoretical explanation, but descriptive fieldwork remained essential, particularly as thousands of the world's languages face extinction.

Method

Descriptive work typically begins with elicitation sessions and text collection with native speakers, producing a corpus from which the linguist extracts the sound system (phonemes and their allophones), word structure, and grammatical rules. The outputs are reference grammars, dictionaries and archived text collections. Modern projects emphasize multimedia corpora, interlinear glossing, and community access, and are often linked to language revitalization efforts.

Relation to other fields

Language description feeds typology (the cross-linguistic comparison of structures), historical linguistics, and applied fields such as language teaching and computational linguistics, where descriptive corpora train language models and speech technology for low-resource languages.
