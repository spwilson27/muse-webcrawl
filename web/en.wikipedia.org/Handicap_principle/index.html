Handicap principle

The handicap principle is a hypothesis in evolutionary biology proposed by Israeli biologist Amotz Zahavi in 1975. It holds that reliable signals between animals — especially in sexual selection — are guaranteed honest precisely because they are costly to produce: only a genuinely high-quality individual can afford the waste.

The idea

The classic example is the peacock's extravagant train: a conspicuous burden that hampers escape from predators. Because a weak or unhealthy male could not survive while bearing such a cost, the trait honestly advertises fitness to females. Zahavi's idea was initially dismissed as counterintuitive, but Alan Grafen's 1990 game-theoretic model showed that costly signalling can be an evolutionarily stable strategy when costs fall more heavily on low-quality signallers.

Examples and debate

Proposed examples include the long tail of the barn swallow, stotting in gazelles, alarm calls, and conspicuously bright plumage correlated with parasite resistance. In human behaviour the principle has been applied to conspicuous consumption, costly religious rituals, and philanthropy. Critics note that some signals once thought costly are cheaper than assumed, and that "handicap" explanations must be tested against alternatives such as Fisherian runaway selection.
