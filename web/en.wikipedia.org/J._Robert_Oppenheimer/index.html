Julius Robert Oppenheimer (April 22, 1904 – February 18, 1967) was an American theoretical physicist. He was the scientific director of the Manhattan Project's Los Alamos Laboratory during World War II, and is often called the "father of the atomic bomb" for his role in the development of the first nuclear weapons.

Early life and education

Oppenheimer was born in New York City to a family of German Jewish immigrants. He studied at Harvard University, graduating in chemistry in 1925, then pursued physics at Cambridge and at the University of Göttingen, where he earned his PhD in 1927 under Max Born. With Born he developed the Born–Oppenheimer approximation for molecular wave functions.

Academic career

Returning to the United States, Oppenheimer taught at the University of California, Berkeley, and the California Institute of Technology. He made important contributions to theoretical astrophysics, including early work on neutron stars and black holes (the Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit), quantum field theory, and cosmic rays.

Manhattan Project

In 1942 Oppenheimer was recruited to lead the Manhattan Project's weapons laboratory at Los Alamos, New Mexico. Under his direction the first atomic bomb was tested on July 16, 1945, in the Trinity test. Watching the explosion, he later recalled a line from the Bhagavad Gita: "Now I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds."

Later years and security hearing

After the war Oppenheimer directed the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton and chaired the General Advisory Committee of the Atomic Energy Commission, opposing development of the hydrogen bomb. In 1954, amid McCarthy-era suspicion, his security clearance was revoked after a controversial hearing. He was politically rehabilitated in part when President Kennedy selected him for the Enrico Fermi Award, presented by President Johnson in 1963. In 2022 the U.S. government formally vacated the 1954 decision.
