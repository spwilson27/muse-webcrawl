Separation of church and state

The separation of church and state is a philosophical and jurisprudential concept denoting a distance in the relationship between organized religion and the state. The phrase derives from Thomas Jefferson's 1802 letter to the Danbury Baptists, in which the United States president described the First Amendment as building "a wall of separation between Church & State". At its core the principle holds that civil government and religious institutions should be kept independent of one another, though its practical meaning — strict exclusion, neutrality, or mutual accommodation — is contested.

History

Early advocates included Roger Williams, who argued in 1644 for a "hedge or wall of separation" to protect the church from worldly corruption, and John Locke, whose Letter Concerning Toleration (1689) argued that magistrates have no business in the care of souls. The United States was the first nation to constitutionally prohibit an establishment of religion (1791); several American states, however, retained established churches into the 19th century (Massachusetts until 1833).

Models worldwide

Arrangements vary widely. France's laïcité, codified in a 1905 law, strictly separates religion from public institutions and restricts religious symbols in state schools. Some democracies maintain weak establishments (the Church of England; Lutheran churches in Scandinavia, Denmark retaining its folk church) combined with broad religious freedom. Other models include state atheism in communist countries, which suppressed religion, and theocracies such as Iran and Vatican City, where religious and state authority coincide.

Debates

Recurring disputes concern public funding of religious schools, religious displays on public property, tax exemptions, school prayer, and exemptions from general laws for religious objectors. The US Supreme Court has developed shifting tests — from the Lemon test (1971) toward history-and-tradition approaches in recent decades.
