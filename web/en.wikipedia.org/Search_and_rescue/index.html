Search and rescue

Search and rescue (SAR) is the search for, and provision of aid to, people who are in distress or imminent danger. It embraces many specialised fields, including mountain rescue, maritime and aeronautical search and rescue, urban search and rescue in collapsed structures, cave rescue, swift-water rescue, wilderness search for missing persons, and combat search and rescue by military forces.

Organisation

Responsibility is usually shared between state agencies and volunteers. Coast guards — such as the United States Coast Guard and His Majesty's Coastguard in the United Kingdom — coordinate maritime and aeronautical SAR, dividing ocean areas into regions of responsibility under international conventions (the 1979 International Convention on Maritime Search and Rescue and ICAO standards for aviation). Mountain and missing-person searches are often conducted by police together with volunteer teams such as Britain's mountain rescue teams or county SAR units in North America. Many services rely on specially trained dogs, which detect human scent in rubble, snow, water and wilderness.

Methods

A search is planned around the subject's last known point, behaviour statistics for the category of missing person, and the probability of detection of each searched segment; techniques range from hasty teams on likely routes to grid searches by lines of searchers. Modern SAR uses helicopters with winches and thermal imaging, drones, satellite distress beacons (the Cospas-Sarsat system detects 406 MHz beacons from emergency locator transmitters and personal locator beacons), mobile-phone location data, and probability-mapping software. Rescue proper may involve technical rope work, helicopter hoisting, swift-water techniques or the heavy shoring, cutting and lifting of urban search and rescue task forces deployed internationally after earthquakes.

Notable bodies and history

Organised lifeboat rescue dates to the early nineteenth century; Britain's Royal National Lifeboat Institution was founded in 1824. The Second World War drove the development of air-sea rescue, and post-war conventions built the global system in place today. Well-known providers include the Swiss Air-Rescue (Rega), the United States Air Force pararescuemen ("PJs"), and national cave and dive rescue organisations. The guiding maxim of many services is that rescuers should risk much to save the savable, while not endangering themselves for those beyond help.
