Filioque (Ecclesiastical Latin: [filiˈokwe]), a Latin term meaning "and [from] the Son", was added to the Latin text of the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed in the Western church. The phrase describes the Holy Spirit as proceeding from the Father and the Son, in contrast to the original Greek text, which states that the Spirit proceeds from the Father alone.

The addition and its underlying doctrine — the "double procession" of the Holy Spirit — became one of the central theological and ecclesiological disputes between the Eastern Orthodox and Roman Catholic churches, and a principal cause of the East–West Schism of 1054. Eastern objections are twofold: that the unilateral addition to an ecumenically agreed creed was canonically illegitimate, and that the doctrine risks subordinating the Spirit or confusing the persons of the Trinity.

History

The clause first appears in Spain, where the Third Council of Toledo (589), marking the Visigothic kingdom's conversion from Arianism, confessed the Spirit as proceeding from the Father and the Son. It spread through the Carolingian world — Charlemagne's theologians championed it, and the Libri Carolini defended it — though Pope Leo III, while accepting the doctrine, refused to alter the creed and had the original text inscribed on silver tablets at St Peter's. Rome finally adopted the interpolated creed in 1014, at the coronation mass of Emperor Henry II.

Controversy and dialogue

The 9th-century patriarch Photius denounced the Filioque in his Mystagogy of the Holy Spirit. The dispute featured in the mutual excommunications of 1054 and remains unresolved, though the failed reunion councils of Lyons (1274) and Florence (1439) both attempted formulas of agreement. Modern ecumenical dialogues, and clarifications issued by Rome in 1995 distinguishing the Greek ekporeusis from the Latin processio, have softened but not eliminated the disagreement. Most Western churches, including those of the Reformation, retain the clause; Eastern Orthodox churches do not.
