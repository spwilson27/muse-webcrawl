Oceanography

Oceanography, also known as oceanology or marine science, is the scientific study of the ocean. It is an Earth science that covers a wide range of topics, including ecosystem dynamics, ocean currents, waves, plate tectonics and the geology of the sea floor, and the chemical and physical properties of the ocean and its boundaries. These topics are studied by specialists across four main branches: biological, chemical, geological, and physical oceanography.

Branches

Physical oceanography studies the ocean's physical conditions and motions—currents such as the Gulf Stream, tides, waves, and the thermohaline circulation that redistributes heat globally. Chemical oceanography examines seawater composition, nutrient cycles, and the carbon cycle, including ocean acidification. Biological oceanography (marine biology) studies organisms from phytoplankton to whales and processes such as primary production. Geological oceanography studies the sea floor, sediments, mid-ocean ridges, and the history recorded in marine deposits.

History

Scientific oceanography is often dated to the voyage of HMS Challenger (1872–1876), which systematically sounded the oceans and collected thousands of marine specimens. In the 20th century, figures such as Harald Sverdrup built the theoretical foundations, while echo sounding, research submersibles, and drilling ships (such as JOIDES Resolution) transformed knowledge of the deep sea. Since the 1990s, satellite altimetry and the Argo network of autonomous floats have provided near-global, continuous observation of temperature, salinity, and sea level.

Importance

The oceans regulate climate, absorb a large share of anthropogenic carbon dioxide and excess heat, drive weather, and support global fisheries and trade. Oceanography underpins weather and climate prediction, tsunami warning, fisheries management, and offshore industries.
