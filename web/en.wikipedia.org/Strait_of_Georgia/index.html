Strait of Georgia

The Strait of Georgia (French: Détroit de Géorgie) or the Georgia Strait is an arm of the Salish Sea between Vancouver Island and the extreme southwestern mainland coast of British Columbia, Canada, and the extreme northwestern mainland coast of the U.S. state of Washington. It is approximately 240 kilometres (150 mi) long and varies in width from 20 to 58 kilometres (12 to 36 mi). Along with the Strait of Juan de Fuca and Puget Sound, it is part of the Salish Sea.

Geography

The strait separates Vancouver Island from the British Columbia mainland and connects at its north end with Queen Charlotte Strait via Discovery Passage and Johnstone Strait, and at its south end with the Strait of Juan de Fuca and Puget Sound. Archipelagos and narrow channels mark each end: the Discovery Islands and Desolation Sound at the north, and the Gulf Islands in British Columbia and the San Juan Islands in Washington to the south. The Fraser River, which accounts for roughly 80 percent of the fresh water entering the strait, discharges into it near Vancouver.

History

First Nations communities have inhabited the shores of the strait for thousands of years. The first European exploration was by Spanish and British expeditions in 1791–92; Captain George Vancouver named the water the "Gulph of Georgia" in 1792 after King George III. In 2010 the name Salish Sea was officially recognised for the combined waters of the strait, Puget Sound and the Strait of Juan de Fuca.

Ecology and transport

The strait supports important salmon runs, herring, shellfish, marine mammals including orca, and large populations of seabirds. It is crossed by BC Ferries routes linking the mainland with Vancouver Island and the Gulf Islands and is a major commercial shipping lane serving the Port of Vancouver.
