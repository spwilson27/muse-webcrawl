Roguelike

A roguelike (or rogue-like) is a subgenre of role-playing video games traditionally characterized by a dungeon crawl through procedurally generated levels, turn-based gameplay, grid-based movement, and permanent death of the player character. Most roguelikes are based on a high-fantasy narrative, reflecting their influence from tabletop role-playing games such as Dungeons & Dragons.

The genre is named after the 1980 video game Rogue, developed by Michael Toy and Glenn Wichman. Early roguelikes were developed for mainframe computers and terminals and used ASCII graphics, in which the player character is conventionally represented by the "@" symbol. Classic examples include Hack and its successor NetHack, Moria and Angband, ADOM, and Linley's Dungeon Crawl.

Characteristics

The 2008 International Roguelike Development Conference produced the "Berlin Interpretation", a set of high-value and low-value factors defining the genre, including random environment generation, permadeath, turn-based combat, grid-based movement, complexity, resource management, and hack-and-slash gameplay.

Roguelites and modern revival

From the late 2000s, independent developers popularized "roguelites"—games borrowing procedural generation and permadeath while adding persistent progression between runs, real-time action, and other departures from tradition. Notable examples include Spelunky, The Binding of Isaac, FTL: Faster Than Light, Dead Cells, and Hades, which won the Hugo Award for Best Video Game in 2021. Traditional roguelikes continue to be developed, including commercial releases such as Caves of Qud and Dwarf Fortress.
