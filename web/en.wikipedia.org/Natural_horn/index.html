Natural horn

The natural horn is a musical instrument in the brass family that has no valves, distinguished by its long coiled tubing and funnel-shaped bell. It can produce only the notes of the harmonic series for the length of tubing used, so players employ interchangeable crooks to change the instrument's key, and modify pitch further with embouchure and by inserting the hand into the bell (hand-stopping).

History

The natural horn descends from hunting horns and became a regular orchestral instrument in the Baroque and Classical eras. Composers such as Handel, Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven wrote prominently for it; Mozart's four horn concertos were composed for the natural horn. Its characteristic hunting-call figures and the contrast between open and stopped notes shaped the Classical horn style.

Decline and revival

The invention of valves in the early 19th century — associated with Heinrich Stölzel and Friedrich Blühmel — led to the valved French horn, which gradually displaced the natural horn in orchestras by the later 19th century. Since the mid-20th century, historically informed performance has revived the natural horn, and modern period-instrument orchestras regularly perform Classical repertoire on it.
