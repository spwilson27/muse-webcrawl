Pseudouridine

Pseudouridine

IUPAC name: 5-(β-D-ribofuranosyl)pyrimidine-2,4(1H,3H)-dione

Symbol: Ψ

Formula: C9H12N2O6

Role: Most abundant modified nucleoside in cellular RNA

Pseudouridine (abbreviated Ψ, psi) is an isomer of the nucleoside uridine in which the uracil base is attached to the ribose sugar through a carbon–carbon bond instead of the usual nitrogen–carbon glycosidic bond. It is the most abundant of the more than one hundred chemically distinct modified nucleosides found in cellular RNA, and it occurs universally in transfer RNA, ribosomal RNA, and small nuclear RNA.

Biosynthesis and function

Pseudouridine is formed after transcription by enzymes called pseudouridine synthases, which isomerize specific uridine residues in place without requiring a template. The modification adds an extra hydrogen-bond donor, stiffens the sugar-phosphate backbone, and improves base stacking, thereby stabilizing RNA structures such as the TΨC loop of tRNA and the decoding center of the ribosome. In humans, defects in pseudouridylation are linked to diseases including dyskeratosis congenita and some mitochondrial disorders.

Medical applications

Synthetic messenger RNA in which uridine is replaced by N1-methylpseudouridine evades recognition by innate immune sensors such as Toll-like receptors while increasing protein production. This discovery, developed by Katalin Karikó and Drew Weissman, underpins the mRNA vaccines against COVID-19 produced by Pfizer–BioNTech and Moderna; Karikó and Weissman received the 2023 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the work.
