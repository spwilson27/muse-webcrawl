Jerome David Salinger (1 January 1919 – 27 January 2010) was an American author best known for his 1951 novel The Catcher in the Rye. Salinger published several short stories in Story magazine in 1940 before serving in World War II. In 1948, his critically acclaimed story "A Perfect Day for Bananafish" appeared in The New Yorker, which became home to much of his later work.

Early life and war service

Salinger was born in New York City to a prosperous family; his father, Sol Salinger, was a Jewish importer of kosher cheese, and his mother, Marie Jillich, was of Scots-Irish descent. He attended Valley Forge Military Academy and briefly several colleges before turning to writing classes at Columbia University under Whit Burnett. Drafted in 1942, he served with the 4th Infantry Division, landing at Utah Beach on D-Day and fighting in the Battle of the Bulge and the Hürtgen Forest; he was hospitalized for combat stress reaction in 1945. He reportedly carried chapters of The Catcher in the Rye with him through the war.

Literary career

The Catcher in the Rye, narrated by the alienated teenager Holden Caulfield, was an immediate popular success, though critical reaction was mixed; it has since become a classic of American literature and a frequent target of book challenges. Salinger followed with the collections Nine Stories (1953), Franny and Zooey (1961), and Raise High the Roof Beam, Carpenters and Seymour: An Introduction (1963), the latter works centring on the fictional Glass family. His last published work, the novella "Hapworth 16, 1924", appeared in The New Yorker in 1965.

Reclusion

As his fame grew, Salinger withdrew from public life, moving to Cornish, New Hampshire, in 1953 and publishing ever less frequently. He guarded his privacy fiercely, bringing lawsuits to block publication of his letters and an unauthorized biography. After his death in 2010, it was reported that he had left a large body of unpublished work, and his son and estate have indicated that previously unpublished writings will eventually be released.

Legacy

Salinger's work, with its themes of adolescent innocence, alienation, and spiritual searching — influenced by his lifelong interest in Zen Buddhism, Advaita Vedanta, and other Eastern religions — has influenced generations of writers. The Catcher in the Rye remains widely read and taught, selling hundreds of thousands of copies annually.
