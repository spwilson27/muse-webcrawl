Maternal death

Maternal death, or maternal mortality, is defined by the World Health Organization as the death of a woman while pregnant or within 42 days of termination of pregnancy, from any cause related to or aggravated by the pregnancy or its management, but not from accidental or incidental causes. The maternal mortality ratio (MMR) is expressed per 100,000 live births.

Causes

The leading direct causes worldwide are severe bleeding (postpartum haemorrhage), infections (usually after childbirth), high blood pressure disorders such as pre-eclampsia and eclampsia, complications of delivery, and unsafe abortion. Indirect causes include malaria, HIV/AIDS, anaemia and cardiovascular disease aggravated by pregnancy. The "three delays" model identifies delays in seeking care, reaching a facility, and receiving adequate treatment as key contributors.

Global picture

An estimated 287,000 maternal deaths occurred in 2020. Global MMR has fallen substantially since 1990 — from about 385 to roughly 223 per 100,000 live births in 2020 — but progress has stalled in many regions, and sub-Saharan Africa accounts for about 70 per cent of deaths. Almost all maternal deaths occur in low- and middle-income countries and most are preventable with skilled birth attendance, emergency obstetric care, contraception and postnatal care.

Measurement and goals

Because many deaths go unregistered, estimates rely on vital registration, household surveys, censuses and confidential enquiries. Sustainable Development Goal 3.1 aims to reduce global MMR to below 70 per 100,000 live births by 2030.
