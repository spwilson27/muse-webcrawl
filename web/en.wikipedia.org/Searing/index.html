Searing


Searing a steak in cast iron

Searing (or pan searing) is a cooking technique used in grilling, baking, braising, roasting, sautéing, and similar applications in which the surface of the food, usually meat such as beef, chicken, pork, or seafood, is cooked at a high temperature until a browned crust forms. Similar techniques, such as browning and blackening, are typically used to sear all sides of a particular piece of meat, fish, poultry, or other food before finishing it in a lower-temperature oven.

Technique

Achieving a proper browning or sear requires the meat surface to be free of water. The surface of the food is cooked at temperatures in excess of 150 °C (302 °F), well above the boiling point of water. Common cooking mediums include dry hot pans, metal grills, and broilers. Searing is similar to roasting in that it is usually performed in an oven or other dry-heat environment, but the meat is cooked at a higher temperature for a shorter time to form the crust.

The Maillard reaction

The browning is largely the result of the Maillard reaction, a complex set of chemical reactions between amino acids and reducing sugars that begins at around 140–165 °C. The reactions create hundreds of new flavor compounds, giving seared food its characteristic savoury taste. Despite popular belief, searing does not "seal in juices"; scientific tests, popularised by writers such as Harold McGee, show that seared meat loses just as much moisture during cooking as unseared meat — the crust is not waterproof.

Applications

Searing is frequently the first step in braising or stewing, where the browned fond left in the pan contributes flavor to the sauce. It is also used in the sous-vide method, where food is first cooked gently in a water bath and then seared briefly at very high temperature for texture and appearance.
