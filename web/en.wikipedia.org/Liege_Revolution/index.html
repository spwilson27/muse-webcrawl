Liège Revolution

The Liège Revolution (Révolution liégeoise), sometimes called the Happy Revolution, was a popular uprising in the Prince-Bishopric of Liège beginning in August 1789 that destroyed the old regime, expelled Prince-Bishop César-Constantin-François de Hoensbroeck, and proclaimed a republic modelled on Enlightenment and early French Revolutionary principles. It unfolded alongside, but independently of, the Brabant Revolution in the neighbouring Austrian Netherlands.

Course of events

Disputes over a gambling house at Spa and long-standing constitutional grievances escalated when, on 18 August 1789, crowds occupied Liège's city hall and compelled the magistrates to be chosen by popular election. The prince-bishop fled to Trier. The new regime issued a declaration of the rights of man modelled on the French text, confiscated church property, and proclaimed the "Franches-Montagnes" patriot government; many Liègeois radicals, such as Jean-Nicolas Bassenge, later influenced politics in Paris.

Suppression and legacy

Imperial and Prussian forces intervened, and in January 1791 Austrian troops restored the prince-bishop. Patriots fled abroad, returning with French revolutionary armies in 1792–94. In 1795 the prince-bishopric was formally abolished and its territory annexed to France, ending eight centuries of ecclesiastical rule. The revolution is remembered in Wallonia as a pioneering episode in Belgian democratic history.
