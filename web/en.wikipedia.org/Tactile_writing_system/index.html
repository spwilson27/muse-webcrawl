Tactile writing system

A tactile writing system is a writing system designed to be read by touch, enabling literacy for people who are blind or visually impaired. Such systems use raised dots, embossed lines, or other tangible symbols pressed into paper or displayed on refreshable devices. The most widely used tactile writing system in the world is Braille.

Braille, the dominant tactile writing system, uses cells of up to six raised dots

History

Early attempts at tactile reading included books printed with embossed Roman letters, such as those produced by Valentin Haüy, who founded the first school for the blind in Paris in 1784. Embossed alphabets proved slow to read and hard to write. Charles Barbier's "night writing", a twelve-dot code devised for soldiers, inspired the young Louis Braille, who in 1824—at the age of fifteen—developed a simplified six-dot system that could be both read rapidly and written by hand with a slate and stylus.

Other systems

Rival and complementary systems developed in the 19th century included William Moon's Moon type, an embossed simplified alphabet still used by some late-blinded readers; the Boston line letter and New York Point, which competed with braille in the United States during the so-called "War of the Dots"; and various embossed shorthand systems. Modern extensions include braille codes for mathematics (the Nemeth Code) and music, contracted (Grade 2) braille, and eight-dot computer braille used with refreshable braille displays.

Production and use

Tactile text is produced with manual slates, mechanical braille writers such as the Perkins Brailler, embossers, and refreshable displays that raise and lower pins. Tactile writing provides independent access to literacy, labelling, signage, and education, and is recognized in many countries' accessibility standards and legislation.
