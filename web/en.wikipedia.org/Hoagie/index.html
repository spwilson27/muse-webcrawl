Hoagie

A hoagie is a type of submarine sandwich built on a long Italian-style roll, filled with meats, cheese, vegetables, and dressings. The term is strongly associated with Philadelphia and the surrounding Delaware Valley, where the hoagie is an emblematic local food; elsewhere in the United States the same sandwich goes by names such as sub, hero, grinder, wedge, or po' boy. A classic Philadelphia hoagie layers Italian deli meats and provolone on a seeded roll with shredded lettuce, sliced tomato and onion, oregano, and oil.

Composition

The roll is essential: a sturdy, often sesame-seeded Italian loaf, crusty outside and soft inside, typically 20–30 cm long. The canonical Italian hoagie combines cured meats such as Genoa salami, capicola, mortadella, and pepperoni (or ham) with provolone cheese. Vegetables are finely shredded iceberg lettuce, thin-sliced tomatoes, and raw onion; the sandwich is dressed with olive oil (sometimes oil and vinegar), dried oregano, and salt and pepper, with sweet or hot peppers optional. Hoagies are usually served cold, though some varieties (such as the cheesesteak hoagie) are hot. Regional chains including Wawa and Primo Hoagies have standardised the form across the mid-Atlantic.

History and the name

The sandwich emerged among Italian-American communities in the early 20th century. The most popular etymology ties "hoagie" to Hog Island, the Philadelphia shipyard (once the largest in the world) where Italian immigrant workers lunched on large stuffed sandwiches during the First and Second World Wars: "Hog Island" became "hoggie" and then "hoagie". Competing accounts cite the slang "hog" (to eat greedily), "honky sandwich", or a Philadelphia deli owner named Al DePalma, who in the 1930s claimed customers needed to be a "hog" to eat his sandwiches. The Philadelphia Bulletin used the term in print by the 1940s, and in 1992 Mayor Ed Rendell officially declared the hoagie the "Official Sandwich of Philadelphia".

Regional names

Long sandwiches on rolls carry distinct regional names across the United States: "sub" (widespread, from submarine), "hero" (New York City), "grinder" (New England), "wedge" (New York's northern suburbs), "spukie" (Boston), "blimpie", "torpedo", "rocket", and the distinct Louisiana "po' boy". "Hoagie" remains the shibboleth of the Philadelphia area, where asking for a "sub" marks one as an outsider, and the word anchors local businesses, festivals, and the annual hoagie-eating traditions of the region.
