The llama (Lama glama) is a domesticated South American camelid, widely used as a meat and pack animal by Andean cultures since the pre-Columbian era. It was domesticated from the wild guanaco in the central highlands of Peru roughly 6,000 years ago and was central to the economy of the Inca Empire.

Characteristics

Llamas are social herd animals with long necks, slender limbs, and a double coat of coarse outer hair over a softer undercoat. Like other camelids they have a three-chambered stomach, oval red blood cells, and hemoglobin adapted to thin high-altitude air, and they are efficient browsers on coarse mountain forage, needing little water. They communicate with hums and alarm calls, and they famously spit — usually at other llamas in disputes over food or rank. A full-grown llama is about 1.7 to 1.8 meters tall and can carry roughly a quarter of its body weight over long distances.

Uses

Historically Andean peoples used llamas as pack animals (in caravans carrying goods between coast, highlands, and jungle), as a source of meat, fiber, hides, tallow, and dung fuel. Their fiber is coarser than alpaca wool but lanolin-free and warm. In modern times llamas are also kept as livestock guards — a single gelded male bonded with sheep or alpacas will confront coyotes and dogs — as therapy and trekking animals, and as pets.

Classification

Llamas belong to the tribe Lamini within Camelidae, alongside the alpaca (domesticated from the vicuña), the guanaco, and the vicuña. Along with Old World camels they are even-toed ungulates of the suborder Tylopoda. Crosses with alpacas (huarizo) and camels ("camas") have been produced.
