Depression (economics)

In economics, a depression is a sustained, long-term downturn in economic activity characterised by sharply reduced output and trade, mass unemployment, deflation, widespread business failures and poverty. A depression is more severe and prolonged than a recession, though there is no universally agreed formal definition; an informal rule of thumb is a decline in GDP exceeding 10 percent, or a recession lasting several years.

The Great Depression

The most famous example is the Great Depression of the 1930s, which began with the Wall Street Crash of 1929 and affected virtually every country, with unemployment reaching around 25 percent in the United States. Earlier downturns, such as the Long Depression after the Panic of 1873, were also called depressions at the time.

Causes and policy

Explanations of depressions include collapses in aggregate demand, monetary contraction, debt deflation, and banking panics. Governments respond with fiscal stimulus, monetary expansion and financial stabilisation to prevent downturns from deepening into depressions.
