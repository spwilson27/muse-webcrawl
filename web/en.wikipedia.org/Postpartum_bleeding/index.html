Postpartum bleeding or postpartum hemorrhage (PPH) is significant blood loss following childbirth. Definitions vary, but it is commonly defined as blood loss of 500 mL or more after vaginal delivery, or 1,000 mL or more after cesarean delivery; the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists uses a cumulative loss of 1,000 mL or more, or blood loss accompanied by signs of hypovolemia, within 24 hours of birth. Primary PPH occurs within the first 24 hours; secondary PPH occurs between 24 hours and 12 weeks after delivery.

Causes

The causes are often summarized as the "four Ts": Tone (uterine atony, failure of the uterus to contract, the most common cause), Tissue (retained placenta or clots), Trauma (lacerations of the cervix, vagina, or perineum, uterine rupture or inversion), and Thrombin (coagulation disorders). Risk factors include prolonged or rapid labor, an overdistended uterus (from twins or excess amniotic fluid), chorioamnionitis, and a history of previous hemorrhage.

Signs and symptoms

Signs include visibly heavy bleeding, passage of large clots, a boggy or poorly contracted uterus, rising heart rate, falling blood pressure, pallor, and dizziness or loss of consciousness in severe cases. Blood loss is frequently underestimated by visual inspection, so quantitative measurement is recommended.

Prevention

Active management of the third stage of labor — administration of a uterotonic such as oxytocin around delivery of the baby, controlled cord traction, and uterine massage — substantially reduces the risk. Where oxytocin is unavailable, misoprostol may be used.

Management

Initial management includes calling for help, uterine massage, intravenous access and fluids, bladder emptying, and uterotonic drugs: oxytocin first-line, with methylergonovine, carboprost, and misoprostol as additional agents, plus tranexamic acid given within three hours. If bleeding persists, options include intrauterine balloon tamponade, repair of lacerations, uterine artery embolization, surgical compression sutures, vessel ligation, and, as a last resort, hysterectomy. Blood transfusion may be required.

Epidemiology

Postpartum hemorrhage affects roughly 1–5% of deliveries depending on definition and setting, and is a leading cause of maternal death worldwide, with the great majority of deaths occurring in low- and middle-income countries where access to uterotonics, transfusion, and surgical care is limited.
