Intermetallic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An intermetallic (also called an intermetallic compound, intermetallic alloy, ordered intermetallic alloy, or long-range-ordered alloy) is a type of metallic alloy that forms an ordered solid-state compound between two or more metallic elements. Intermetallics are generally hard and brittle, with a crystal structure distinct from those of the constituent metals and a fixed or narrow range of composition, often expressible by a simple chemical formula.

Structure and properties

Unlike conventional alloys, which are disordered solid solutions, intermetallics have specific crystallographic sites for each element, giving them definite stoichiometry (for example Ni3Al, TiAl, FeCo, and CuZn, the β-brass phase). The partially ionic or covalent character of their bonding accounts for their hardness, high melting points, and resistance to creep and oxidation at elevated temperatures — but also for their brittleness at room temperature, which has long limited structural applications.

Applications

Nickel aluminide (Ni3Al) is the strengthening phase in nickel-base superalloys used in jet engines and gas turbines. Titanium aluminides are used in low-pressure turbine blades for their light weight. NiTi (nitinol) is a shape-memory alloy used in stents and actuators. Other notable intermetallics include Al2Cu precipitates in age-hardened aluminium alloys, B2-structured aluminides for heating elements and coatings, Heusler alloys studied for spintronics and magnetism, and the superconductor Nb3Sn used in high-field magnets. Intermetallic phases also form when solders react with circuit-board metals, affecting the reliability of electronics.

Related phases

Many intermetallic structures are named after prototype compounds (B2 CsCl-type, L12 Cu3Au-type, D03 Fe3Al-type). Metallic glasses and quasicrystals, such as icosahedral Al–Cu–Fe, are related classes of ordered metallic materials.
