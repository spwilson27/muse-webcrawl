Bézier curve

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Bézier curve is a parametric curve used in computer graphics, computer-aided design and related fields. A set of discrete control points defines a smooth, continuous curve by means of a formula, usually based on Bernstein polynomials. Bézier curves are widely used to model smooth curves that can be scaled indefinitely — for example, the outlines of letters in PostScript and TrueType/OpenType font systems.

History

The underlying mathematics, the Bernstein polynomial basis, dates to 1912. The curves were developed independently around 1959–1962 by Paul de Casteljau at Citroën, who devised the stable evaluation algorithm that bears his name, and by Pierre Bézier at Renault, who publicized them and whose name became attached to the curves.

Properties

A linear Bézier curve is a straight line between two points; quadratic and cubic curves, defined by three and four control points respectively, are the workhorses of graphics software. Bézier curves are invariant under affine transformations, lie within the convex hull of their control points, start and end tangent to the segments joining the end control points, and can be subdivided recursively (de Casteljau's algorithm) — properties that make them easy to manipulate interactively. Chains of segments form Bézier paths, and surfaces generalize the idea to Bézier surfaces.

Applications

Cubic Bézier curves define the glyphs of most digital typefaces, the pen tools of vector drawing programs such as Adobe Illustrator and Inkscape, paths in the SVG web standard, and keyframe interpolation curves in animation software.
