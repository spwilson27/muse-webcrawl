An English country house is a large house or mansion in the English countryside. Such houses were often owned by individuals who also owned a town house, allowing them to spend time in the country with their families, guests, and staff for leisure and political purposes while administering their estates.

History

Country houses developed from the fortified manor houses of the Middle Ages into the great unfortified residences of the Tudor, Jacobean, Georgian, and Victorian eras, built in styles from Palladian to Gothic Revival and set in landscaped parks by designers such as Capability Brown. They were centres of political influence and social life — the setting of the "country house party" — and required large staffs of domestic servants.

Decline

Agricultural depression, death duties, and the loss of heirs and servants in the two World Wars forced the sale or demolition of many houses; over a thousand English country houses were demolished during the twentieth century. Many survivors passed to the National Trust, became hotels or schools, or remain in family ownership and open to the public.
