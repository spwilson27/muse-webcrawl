Grotesque (architecture)

TypeArchitectural ornament — carved stone figure
Found onGothic and Gothic Revival churches, cathedrals, universities
Distinguished fromGargoyle (which is a functional waterspout)
Related termsChimera, boss, corbel head

In architecture, a grotesque is a carved stone figure — usually a fantastic or mythical creature, a distorted animal or a grimacing human form — used as ornament on buildings, especially Gothic churches and cathedrals. Grotesques that also function as waterspouts are properly called gargoyles; a grotesque is purely decorative.

Origin of the term

The word comes from the Italian grottesche, referring to the fanciful images discovered in the buried rooms (grotte) of Nero's Domus Aurea in Rome in the late fifteenth century — hybrids of humans, animals and foliage that Renaissance artists including Raphael adopted into a decorative style. Transferred to architecture, the term came to denote carved stone monsters and distorted figures.

Use in Gothic architecture

Grotesques swarm across the towers, parapets and cornices of medieval cathedrals such as Notre-Dame de Paris, whose famous "chimères" — notably the brooding Stryge — were largely added during Viollet-le-Duc's nineteenth-century restoration. Folklore holds that grotesques ward off evil spirits or embody the sins and demons kept outside the sacred space; art historians more cautiously note their roles as mason's fancy, moral instruction and visual sermon in an age of low literacy.

Revival and modern examples

The Gothic Revival made grotesques ubiquitous on churches, universities and civic buildings. Modern carvers continue the tradition: the National Cathedral in Washington, D.C. famously includes a grotesque of Darth Vader, sculpted after a children's design contest in the 1980s, high on its northwest tower. Oxford and Cambridge colleges commission grotesques of contemporary figures and student caricatures, keeping stonecarving — and gentle mockery — alive.
