Anti-imperialism

Anti-imperialism in political science and international relations is opposition to imperialism — the extension of a nation's authority through territorial acquisition or indirect control over other peoples. Anti-imperialists argue that empire is unjust, exploitative and a cause of war, and they have supported national self-determination and decolonisation.

History

Opposition to empire is as old as empire itself, but organised anti-imperialism took shape in the late nineteenth century: the American Anti-Imperialist League (1898), counting Mark Twain among its vice-presidents, opposed the annexation of the Philippines. In 1916 Lenin's Imperialism, the Highest Stage of Capitalism made anti-imperialism central to Marxist thought, while J. A. Hobson's Imperialism: A Study (1902) offered a liberal critique. After 1945, anti-imperialist movements drove decolonisation across Asia and Africa, and the Non-Aligned Movement sought independence from both Cold War blocs.

Contemporary usage

The term is now applied to criticism of military intervention, economic dominance by great powers and international institutions, and cultural hegemony. Its scope is contested: debates continue over which contemporary states and actions count as imperialist. See also decolonisation and self-determination.
