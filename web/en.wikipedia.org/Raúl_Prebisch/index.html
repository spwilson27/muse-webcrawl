Raúl Prebisch

Raúl Prebisch
Born: 17 April 1901, Tucumán, Argentina
Died: 29 April 1986, Santiago, Chile
Occupation: Economist
Known for: Prebisch–Singer hypothesis, structuralism

Raúl Prebisch (17 April 1901 – 29 April 1986) was an Argentine economist who became one of the most influential development economists of the twentieth century. As head of the UN Economic Commission for Latin America (ECLAC), he argued that the structure of world trade disadvantaged primary-commodity exporters, and he became the intellectual father of Latin American structuralism and dependency thinking.

Career

Prebisch helped found Argentina's central bank in 1935 and served as its general manager until 1943. From 1950 to 1963 he led ECLAC in Santiago, where his 1949 manifesto, The Economic Development of Latin America and Its Principal Problems, set out the centre–periphery model: the industrial "centre" captured the gains from technical progress, while the "periphery" exporting raw materials faced deteriorating terms of trade.

Ideas and influence

With Hans Singer he is associated with the Prebisch–Singer hypothesis of a long-run decline in commodity prices relative to manufactures. His policy prescription — import-substituting industrialisation behind protective tariffs — shaped Latin American policy for decades, though it was later criticised for inefficiency and debt. Prebisch went on to become the first secretary-general of UNCTAD in 1964, championing fairer trading rules for developing countries.
