Humorism

Humorism, or humoralism, is a system of medicine that treats the human body as being governed by four bodily fluids, or humours — blood, phlegm, yellow bile, and black bile. Health was understood as a balance of the humours, and disease as an excess or deficiency of one or more of them. The theory dominated Western medicine for more than two thousand years.

Origins

The doctrine is associated with the Hippocratic corpus (5th–4th centuries BC), particularly the treatise On the Nature of Man, attributed to Polybus, and was systematised by Galen in the 2nd century AD. The humours were linked to the four elements (air, water, fire, earth), the qualities hot, cold, wet, and dry, the seasons, and the organs of the body.

Temperaments

Predominance of a humour was held to shape a person's temperament: blood produced the sanguine temperament, phlegm the phlegmatic, yellow bile the choleric, and black bile the melancholic. These categories influenced ideas about personality well into the modern era.

Treatment and decline

Treatments aimed at restoring balance included bloodletting, purging, emetics, dietary regulation, and exercise. Humorism began to lose ground with the rise of anatomical and cellular pathology in the 19th century — notably Rudolf Virchow's cellular theory (1858) and germ theory — and was abandoned by scientific medicine, though it remains historically important and survives in modified form in traditional systems such as Unani medicine.
