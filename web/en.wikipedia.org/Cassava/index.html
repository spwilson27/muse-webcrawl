Cassava (Manihot esculenta), commonly called yuca, manioc or mandioca, is a woody shrub of the spurge family, Euphorbiaceae, native to South America and cultivated throughout the tropics for its edible, starchy tuberous roots. It is a staple food for hundreds of millions of people in Africa, Asia and Latin America, particularly valued because it grows on poor soils and tolerates drought.

Cultivation and use

Cassava is propagated from stem cuttings and harvested 8–24 months after planting. The roots are processed into flours and meals such as Brazilian farinha and West African gari, and into tapioca starch and pearls. Nigeria, Thailand and Indonesia are among the largest producers. The plant is also used for animal feed, biodegradable products and bioethanol.

Toxicity and processing

Cassava roots and leaves contain cyanogenic glycosides (principally linamarin), which can release hydrogen cyanide. "Bitter" varieties have higher concentrations and must be properly processed — by soaking, fermenting, drying and cooking — before eating; inadequate processing, especially during famine, is associated with the paralytic disease konzo. "Sweet" varieties contain less cyanide and can be eaten after simple cooking. Cassava is also notably poor in protein, so diets dominated by it require supplementation.
