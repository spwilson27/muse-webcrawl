A mountain bike (MTB) is a bicycle designed for off-road cycling. Mountain bikes share some similarities with other bicycles, but incorporate features designed to enhance durability and performance in rough terrain, which often makes them heavier, more complex and less efficient on smooth surfaces. These typically include a suspension fork, large knobby tyres, more durable wheels, more powerful brakes, straight, extra-wide handlebars to improve balance and comfort over rough terrain, and a wider-ratio drivetrain.

Since the development of the sport in the 1970s, many new subtypes of mountain biking have developed, such as cross-country (XC), trail, all-mountain, enduro, downhill (DH), and freeride. Most of these require specific design features, from short-travel lightweight frames for cross-country racing to long-travel, slack-geometry bikes for downhill racing. Wheel sizes have standardised largely around 29-inch and 27.5-inch (650b) wheels, and hydraulic disc brakes are now universal.

History

The modern mountain bike emerged in Marin County, California, in the 1970s, where riders including Gary Fisher, Charlie Kelly, Joe Breeze and Tom Ritchey raced modified cruiser bicycles down Mount Tamalpais. The first mass-produced mountain bike, the Specialized Stumpjumper, appeared in 1981. Mountain biking became an Olympic sport at the 1996 Atlanta Games with the cross-country event.

Design

Mountain bikes commonly use front suspension ("hardtail") or both front and rear suspension ("full suspension"). Frames are built from aluminium alloy, carbon fibre, steel or titanium, with geometry tuned to discipline: endurance cross-country bikes favour steep angles and efficiency, while downhill bikes use slack head angles of about 63 degrees and up to 200 mm of suspension travel. Electric-assist mountain bikes (e-MTBs) have become a major market segment since the 2010s.
