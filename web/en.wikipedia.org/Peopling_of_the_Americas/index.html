Peopling of the Americas

The peopling of the Americas refers to the migration of Paleo-Indians from Siberia into North and South America during the late Pleistocene. Scholarly consensus holds that ancestors of Indigenous peoples of the Americas crossed from Asia via the Beringia land bridge, which connected Siberia and Alaska during the Last Glacial Maximum, and then dispersed southward through the continents.

Models of migration

Two principal routes have been proposed for the movement south of the North American ice sheets: an interior "ice-free corridor" between the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets, and a Pacific coastal route (the "kelp highway") travelled by boat or along the shore. Evidence increasingly favours an early coastal movement. Genetic studies indicate the founding population diverged from Siberian ancestors roughly 20,000–25,000 years ago, possibly after a period of isolation in Beringia (the "Beringian standstill").

Earliest sites

Sites such as Monte Verde in Chile (about 14,500 years old) demonstrated settlement well before the Clovis culture (about 13,000 years ago), ending the "Clovis first" model. Human footprints at White Sands, New Mexico, have been dated to roughly 21,000–23,000 years ago, suggesting a much earlier presence, though aspects of the dating have been debated.

Later migrations

Subsequent movements brought the ancestors of Na-Dene speakers and, much later, Inuit and Yupik peoples across the Arctic. Norse explorers reached Newfoundland around AD 1000, but permanent European colonisation followed only after 1492.
