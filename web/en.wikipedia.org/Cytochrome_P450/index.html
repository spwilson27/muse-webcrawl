Cytochromes P450 (CYPs) are a superfamily of enzymes containing heme as a cofactor that mostly function as monooxygenases. They are found in all domains of life and catalyse a vast range of oxidation reactions.

In mammals, P450s in the liver and other tissues oxidise drugs, toxins and other xenobiotics (phase I metabolism), making them more water-soluble for excretion, and also synthesise and break down endogenous compounds including steroid hormones, fatty acids, bile acids and vitamin D. The name comes from the characteristic absorption peak at 450 nm of the reduced, carbon-monoxide-bound pigment.

Clinical significance

A handful of human isoforms — above all CYP3A4 and CYP2D6 — metabolise most marketed drugs. Genetic polymorphisms and drug–drug interactions that inhibit or induce these enzymes are a leading cause of variable drug response and adverse reactions; for example, grapefruit juice inhibits intestinal CYP3A4, raising levels of many medicines, while codeine depends on CYP2D6 to be converted into morphine. Nomenclature groups sequences into families (e.g. CYP2) and subfamilies (e.g. CYP2D) sharing amino-acid identity.
