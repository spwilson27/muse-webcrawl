Drunk driving in the United States

Drunk driving — driving under the influence (DUI) or driving while intoxicated (DWI) — is the offence of operating a motor vehicle while impaired by alcohol in the United States. It is criminalised in all fifty states, principally on the basis of a driver's blood alcohol concentration (BAC).

Legal standards

In every state except Utah, a BAC of 0.08% or higher is per se illegal for adult drivers; Utah lowered its limit to 0.05% in 2018. Lower limits apply to commercial drivers (0.04%) and to drivers under 21, for whom "zero tolerance" laws typically set limits at 0.00–0.02%. Drivers may also be convicted on evidence of impairment regardless of BAC. Most states operate implied-consent regimes under which holding a licence constitutes consent to chemical testing, with licence suspension for refusal.

Penalties

Penalties escalate with repeat offences and aggravating factors such as causing injury or death, and commonly include fines, licence suspension, jail time, community service and mandatory ignition interlock devices. All states have felony provisions for repeat offenders or fatal crashes.

Statistics and advocacy

Alcohol-impaired driving accounts for roughly ten thousand deaths a year in the United States — around 30% of all traffic fatalities in recent years — down substantially from the early 1980s, when advocacy by organisations such as Mothers Against Drunk Driving (MADD, founded 1980) helped drive reforms including the national minimum drinking age of 21 and the uniform 0.08% standard.
