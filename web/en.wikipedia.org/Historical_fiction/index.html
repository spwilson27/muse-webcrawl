Historical fiction is a literary genre in which a fictional plot unfolds in a real historical setting. Works in the genre combine invented characters and dialogue with documented events, places and social detail, aiming to immerse readers in a past age. By a common rule of thumb the story is set at least a generation — often 50 years — before the author's own time.

Development

Historical tales are ancient, but the modern historical novel begins with Sir Walter Scott's Waverley (1814) and the Waverley novels, which treated history as a force shaping ordinary lives. The 19th century embraced the form: Alexandre Dumas (The Three Musketeers), Victor Hugo, Charles Dickens (A Tale of Two Cities) and Leo Tolstoy, whose War and Peace (1869) blended fiction with essays on history itself.

The modern genre

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century historical fiction ranges from literary works — Hilary Mantel's Wolf Hall trilogy about Thomas Cromwell, a two-time Booker winner — to vast popular markets in romance, mystery, military and alternate-history subgenres. Robert Graves's I, Claudius, Mary Renault's Greek novels and Ken Follett's The Pillars of the Earth are landmarks.

Accuracy and debate

The genre constantly negotiates between fidelity and invention. Authors research material culture, speech and events, yet must imagine the undocumented interior lives that archives omit; critics debate anachronism, the ethics of ventriloquising real people, and how far novels shape public memory of the past more powerfully than scholarship does.
