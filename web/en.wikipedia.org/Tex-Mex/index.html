Tex-Mex

Tex-Mex (a portmanteau of "Texan" and "Mexican") is a regional American cuisine that derives from the culinary creations of the Tejano people of Texas. It has spread from the border states such as Texas and others in the Southwestern United States to the rest of the country, and internationally.

Characteristics

Tex-Mex is characterized by its heavy use of shredded cheese (often yellow cheeses such as Cheddar or Monterey Jack), meat (particularly beef), beans, peppers, spices, and flour tortillas, along with dishes such as chili con carne and nachos. Chili con carne — a stew of beef and chili peppers, the official state dish of Texas — is emblematic. Other signatures include fajitas of grilled skirt steak, chimichangas, chile con queso dip, breakfast tacos, and combination plates served with rice and refried beans. Cumin, an Old World spice introduced through Spanish colonists (including settlers from the Canary Islands), is used far more heavily than in most Mexican regional cooking.

History

The term "Tex-Mex" was first used in print for the Texas Mexican Railway in 1875 before being applied to food in the mid-20th century. The cuisine developed in Tejano home kitchens and in the "chili queens" food stalls of nineteenth-century San Antonio. Restaurants in Texas codified it in the early 1900s; the critic and writer Diana Kennedy's 1972 The Cuisines of Mexico drew a sharp distinction between Mexican food and its Texas offshoot, which boosters such as Robb Walsh later defended as a venerable regional cuisine in its own right. From the 1970s it became the dominant style of "Mexican" restaurant food across the United States.
