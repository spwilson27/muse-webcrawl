Hen Ogledd

The Brittonic kingdoms of the Hen Ogledd in northern Britain around the 6th century.

The Hen Ogledd (Welsh pronunciation: [heːn ˈɔɡlɛð]), meaning the Old North, is the region of northern England and the southern Scottish Lowlands inhabited by the Celtic Britons of sub-Roman and early medieval Britain, and the term used by Welsh tradition for the Brittonic-speaking kingdoms and peoples there. In the post-Roman period this area was divided into several kingdoms, remembered in Welsh poetry and genealogies as the homelands of the Gwŷr y Gogledd, the "Men of the North".

Kingdoms

The kingdoms of the Old North included Rheged (centred on the Solway Firth and the Eden valley), Elmet (around modern Leeds in West Yorkshire), Strathclyde (the Clyde valley, surviving longest of all), Gododdin (the Lothians, with its stronghold at Din Eidyn, modern Edinburgh), Bryneich (the lands around Bamburgh, later Anglian Bernicia), and Deira and Bernicia before their Anglo-Saxon takeover. These kingdoms were gradually conquered or absorbed by the expanding Angles of Northumbria and the Picts and Scots during the 6th and 7th centuries; Strathclyde alone survived into the 11th century.

Culture and sources

The Hen Ogledd is the setting of some of the earliest surviving Welsh literature. The heroic elegies of the Gododdin, attributed to the poet Aneirin and describing a disastrous British raid on Catraeth (probably Catterick) around the year 600, and the poems of Taliesin in praise of kings such as Urien of Rheged, preserve the memory of the northern British heroes. Welsh genealogies and the Historia Brittonum trace the descent of later Welsh dynasties from figures of the North such as Coel Hen ("Old King Cole"), whose name in Welsh — Godebog — reflects the region's history.

Legacy

The term Yr Hen Ogledd remains a powerful element of Welsh national consciousness, representing a lost Brythonic world in what is now England and Scotland. Place-names of Brittonic origin survive widely across the region, in names such as Carlisle, Penrith, Glasgow, and Edinburgh.
