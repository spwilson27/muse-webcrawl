Electric battery

An electric battery is a device consisting of one or more electrochemical cells that convert stored chemical energy into electrical energy. Each cell has two electrodes — a positive cathode and a negative anode — separated by an electrolyte. When connected to an external circuit, chemical reactions at the electrodes drive a flow of electrons through the circuit.

History

Alessandro Volta invented the voltaic pile in 1800, the first true battery. In 1859 Gaston Planté invented the rechargeable lead-acid battery, still used in automobiles. Georges Leclanché's 1866 cell led to the dry cell, and Waldemar Jungner and Thomas Edison developed nickel-based rechargeable batteries around 1900. Lithium-ion batteries, commercialized by Sony in 1991 from work by John Goodenough, M. Stanley Whittingham, and Akira Yoshino (who shared the 2019 Nobel Prize in Chemistry), now dominate portable electronics and electric vehicles.

Types

Primary batteries are single-use: zinc-carbon, alkaline, and lithium metal cells are common. Secondary (rechargeable) batteries include lead-acid, nickel-cadmium, nickel-metal hydride, lithium-ion, and lithium iron phosphate. Batteries are described by their voltage, capacity (ampere-hours), energy density, self-discharge, and cycle life.

Applications and disposal

Batteries power everything from hearing aids and phones to grid-scale energy storage. Spent batteries contain valuable and sometimes toxic materials; recycling recovers lead, lithium, cobalt, and nickel, and many jurisdictions mandate collection schemes.
