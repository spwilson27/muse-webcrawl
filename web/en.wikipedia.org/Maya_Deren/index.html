Maya Deren

Maya Deren (born Eleonora Derenkowska; April 29, 1917 – October 13, 1961) was a Ukrainian-born American experimental filmmaker, theorist, and choreographer. She is widely regarded as the mother of American avant-garde cinema. Her landmark film Meshes of the Afternoon (1943), made with her husband Alexander Hammid, is one of the most influential experimental films of all time.

Life and career

Born in Kyiv, Deren emigrated to the United States with her family in 1922. She studied literature at NYU and Smith College, worked as a social secretary and dance researcher (assisting Katherine Dunham, through whom she became interested in Haitian culture), and began filmmaking in 1943. Her short films — Meshes of the Afternoon (1943), At Land (1944), A Study in Choreography for Camera (1945), and Ritual in Transfigured Time (1946) — pioneered dreamlike, poetic cinema using techniques such as match cuts across space and repeated, looping actions.

Theory and legacy

Deren wrote extensively on film theory, including the monograph An Anagram of Ideas on Art, Form and Film (1946), founded the Creative Film Foundation to support independent filmmakers, and was an early champion of personally made, low-budget cinema ("chamber films"). Her research in Haiti produced the book Divine Horsemen: The Living Gods of Haiti (1953), hours of footage, and initiation into Vodou. She died in New York in 1961 at age 44.
