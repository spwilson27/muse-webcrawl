Herostratus

Died: 356 BC, Ephesus
Known for: Arson of the Temple of Artemis

Herostratus (Ancient Greek: Ἡρόστρατος) was a young man who, in 356 BC, set fire to the Temple of Artemis at Ephesus — one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World — solely in order to become famous. Under torture he confessed his motive, and the Ephesians executed him and attempted a damnatio memoriae, forbidding mention of his name under penalty of death.

The crime

The temple, a vast marble structure about 115 metres long, burned on the night traditionally said to coincide with the birth of Alexander the Great (21 July 356 BC). Plutarch joked that Artemis was too busy attending Alexander's birth to save her shrine. The temple was rebuilt on a grander scale, financed in part by Alexander himself.

"Herostratic fame"

The ban on his name failed: the historian Theopompus recorded it, and it passed into literature through Valerius Maximus, Aelian, and others. The episode gave rise to the expression "Herostratic fame" — fame achieved through destructive or shameful acts. The concept is frequently invoked in discussions of media coverage of crimes committed for notoriety, and Sartre wrote a short story, "Erostratus" (1939), in his collection The Wall, about a man planning a murder for fame.
