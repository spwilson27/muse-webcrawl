Programming language theory

Programming language theory (PLT) is a branch of computer science that deals with the design, implementation, analysis, characterization, and classification of formal languages known as programming languages. It draws heavily on mathematics, particularly logic, algebra, and category theory.

History

The field's roots lie in the lambda calculus developed by Alonzo Church in the 1930s, which profoundly influenced language design. The 1960s saw the development of formal semantics: operational approaches, Christopher Strachey's work, and the denotational semantics of Dana Scott and Strachey. The backus–Naur form, introduced around 1960, gave a standard notation for syntax.

Subfields

Major areas include type theory, which studies type systems that classify expressions by the values they compute; denotational, operational, and axiomatic semantics; domain theory; concurrency theory, including the pi calculus; and the study of program verification and formal methods. The annual ACM symposium Principles of Programming Languages (POPL), founded in 1973, is a leading venue.

Relation to other fields

PLT overlaps with computability theory, compiler construction, and software engineering. Concepts originating in PLT — such as Hindley–Milner type inference, monads in functional programming, and garbage collection — have migrated into mainstream languages.
