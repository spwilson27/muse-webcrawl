Free verse

Free verse is an open form of poetry that does not use consistent metre patterns, rhyme, or any musical pattern. It thus tends to follow the rhythm of natural speech rather than a fixed metrical scheme. Although free verse lacks regular metre, its lines are still arranged deliberately, and the form relies on cadence, line breaks, imagery and the natural rhythms of language for its effects.

History

Antecedents of free verse include the biblical Psalms in translation, the prose poems of the 19th century, and Walt Whitman's Leaves of Grass (1855), whose long, irregular lines broke with convention. The term derives from the French vers libre, practised in the 1880s by poets such as Gustave Kahn, Jules Laforgue and Arthur Rimbaud, whose "Marine" and "Mouvement" are often cited as the first free-verse poems in French.

In English, free verse became the dominant mode of modernist poetry in the early 20th century through T. S. Eliot, Ezra Pound, William Carlos Williams, H.D. and the Imagists. Pound advised poets to "compose in the sequence of the musical phrase, not in sequence of a metronome". By the mid-20th century it had become the prevailing form in English-language poetry.

Characteristics

Free verse is not prose: it is printed in lines, and the line break is one of its principal tools, used to create emphasis, pace and enjambment. Poets such as T. E. Hulme and Eliot argued that the absence of a fixed form did not mean an absence of form at all — Eliot wrote that "no verse is free for the man who wants to do a good job". Critics have sometimes attacked the form as shapeless, a debate that has followed it since its emergence.
