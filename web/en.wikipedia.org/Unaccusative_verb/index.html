Unaccusative verb

An unaccusative verb is an intransitive verb whose single grammatical subject is not an agent but corresponds semantically to the direct object (patient or theme) of a transitive verb. Classic examples include fall, melt, arrive and die: in "the ice melted", the subject the ice undergoes the change rather than causing it. Unaccusatives contrast with unergative verbs (such as run, laugh and work), whose subjects are agent-like.

The Unaccusative Hypothesis

The distinction was formalised by David Perlmutter in 1978 within Relational Grammar: under the Unaccusative Hypothesis, the surface subject of an unaccusative verb originates as the object ("2") in the initial grammatical relations of the clause, whereas the subject of an unergative verb is an original subject ("1"). In later generative accounts, the unaccusative subject is generated inside the verb phrase as the complement of the verb and raises to subject position.

Diagnostics

Languages display many tests for unaccusativity. In Italian, unaccusative verbs select the auxiliary essere ("be") rather than avere ("have") in compound tenses and allow participial absolutes and ne-cliticisation. In Dutch, unaccusatives take zijn and appear in impersonal passive constructions differently from unergatives. In English, unaccusatives participate in the causative–inchoative alternation ("I broke the vase / the vase broke"), allow resultative phrases of the type "the pond froze solid", and allow subjects in locative inversion ("into the room walked a man"). Unergatives, by contrast, allow cognate objects and the fake reflexive resultative ("he laughed himself sick").

Semantic basis

Verb classes that tend to be unaccusative across languages include verbs of change of state (break, open), change of location (arrive, fall), appearance and existence (appear, exist), and uncontrolled processes (tremble). Scholars such as Beth Levin and Malka Rappaport Hovav argue unaccusativity is semantically determined by event structure: verbs whose events lack an external causer and involve a theme undergoing change are unaccusative. Split intransitivity languages such as Basque and Georgian mark the distinction morphologically on case and agreement.
