The Book of Enoch (also 1 Enoch) is an ancient Jewish apocalyptic text ascribed to Enoch, the great-grandfather of Noah. It describes the fall of a group of angels known as the Watchers, Enoch's journeys through heaven and the underworld, and visions of the final judgment. Although it is not part of the biblical canon of most Jewish and Christian traditions, it is regarded as canonical by the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church and the Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church.

Contents and structure

The book is a composite work made up of five distinct sections, composed at different times between roughly the third century BC and the first century BC:

  • The Book of the Watchers (chapters 1–36) recounts how the angels called Watchers descended to earth, took human wives, and fathered the Nephilim, giants whose violence corrupted the earth before the Flood.
  • The Book of Parables (chapters 37–71), also called the Similitudes of Enoch, contains visions of the coming judgment and the figure of the "Son of Man".
  • The Astronomical Book (chapters 72–82) describes the movements of the sun, moon, and stars and a 364-day solar calendar.
  • The Book of Dream Visions (chapters 83–90) presents a symbolic history of Israel from Adam to the Maccabean revolt.
  • The Epistle of Enoch (chapters 91–108) contains exhortations and prophecies, including the "Apocalypse of Weeks".

Textual history

The book was originally written in Aramaic, as fragments found among the Dead Sea Scrolls at Qumran attest. It was translated into Greek, and from Greek into Ge'ez, the classical language of Ethiopia, the only version in which the complete text survives. The text was largely lost to the Western church for over a thousand years until the Scottish explorer James Bruce brought three copies from Ethiopia to Europe in 1773. The first English translation, by Richard Laurence, appeared in 1821.

Influence

The Book of Enoch influenced early Christian thought; the New Testament Epistle of Jude quotes it directly (Jude 1:14–15), and the figure of Enoch appears in Genesis only briefly. Themes from the book — fallen angels, the Watchers, and elaborate angelology — shaped later Jewish and Christian apocalyptic writing. Related works include the Book of Jubilees and the Slavonic 2 Enoch.
