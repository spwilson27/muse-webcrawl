Mir (Russian: Мир, "peace" or "world") was a space station operated by the Soviet Union and later by Russia in low Earth orbit from 1986 to 2001. The first modular space station, assembled in orbit from modules launched separately between 1986 and 1996, Mir served as a long-duration research laboratory in microgravity and supported a near-continuous human presence in space for a decade. It was the largest artificial satellite of its time and the direct forerunner of the International Space Station.

Assembly and structure

The core module, launched on 20 February 1986, provided living quarters, life support, and command facilities. Six further modules were added over the following decade: the astrophysics module Kvant-1 (1987), the airlock and technology module Kvant-2 (1989), the Kristall technological module (1990), the remote-sensing modules Spektr (1995) and Priroda (1996), and a docking module to receive American Space Shuttles during the Shuttle–Mir programme. In its final form the station had a pressurised volume of about 350 cubic metres.

Operations

Crews reached Mir aboard Soyuz spacecraft and were resupplied by uncrewed Progress freighters. Cosmonauts performed thousands of experiments in biology, physics, astronomy, and materials science and set endurance records that still stand for single flights: Valeri Polyakov spent 437 consecutive days aboard Mir in 1994–1995. From 1995 to 1998, under the Phase One programme, American astronauts lived aboard Mir and Space Shuttles docked nine times, giving NASA experience in long-duration operations ahead of the ISS.

Incidents

The station's final years were marked by serious accidents. In February 1997 an oxygen-generator fire filled the cabin with smoke; in June 1997 the Progress M-34 freighter collided with the Spektr module during a manual docking test, puncturing and depressurising it and damaging its solar arrays, permanently reducing station power. Ageing systems, computer failures, and funding shortages plagued the ageing station even as its crews kept it flying.

Deorbit

With Russia committed to the International Space Station, Mir was abandoned in 1999 and deliberately deorbited on 23 March 2001. The station broke up over the South Pacific Ocean near Fiji, with surviving debris falling into a remote ocean area often called the spacecraft cemetery. Mir had hosted 104 people from 12 nations and orbited the Earth more than 86,000 times.
