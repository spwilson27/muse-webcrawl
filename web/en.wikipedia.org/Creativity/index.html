Creativity

DomainPsychology, cognitive science, arts
Standard definitionOriginality plus effectiveness (usefulness)
Key researchersJ. P. Guilford, E. Paul Torrance, Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi, Teresa Amabile

Creativity is the ability to generate ideas, solutions, or products that are both novel and valuable. It is studied across psychology, neuroscience, education, and business, and manifests in domains from the arts and sciences to everyday problem solving. The widely used "standard definition" requires two criteria: originality and effectiveness.

History of the concept

Ancient cultures often attributed creative achievement to divine inspiration — the Muses of Greek tradition — rather than individual ability. The modern conception of creativity as a human faculty developed during the Renaissance and Enlightenment, and scientific study began in earnest with Francis Galton's work on genius and, later, J. P. Guilford's 1950 presidential address to the American Psychological Association, which lamented the neglect of creativity research and promoted the study of divergent thinking.

Models and measurement

Graham Wallas proposed a four-stage model of the creative process: preparation, incubation, illumination, and verification. Tests such as the Torrance Tests of Creative Thinking assess fluency, flexibility, originality, and elaboration. Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi's systems model holds that creativity emerges from the interaction of an individual, a domain of knowledge, and a field of gatekeepers; Teresa Amabile's componential model emphasises domain skills, creativity-relevant processes, and intrinsic motivation.

Neuroscience and enhancement

Neuroimaging research associates creative cognition with interplay between the default mode, executive control, and salience networks. Creativity can be supported by techniques such as brainstorming, and is studied at levels from "mini-c" personal insight to eminent "Big-C" achievement (Margaret Boden's and James Kaufman's frameworks).
