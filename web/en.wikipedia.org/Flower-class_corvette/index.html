Flower-class corvette

The Flower-class corvette (also referred to as the Gladiolus class after the lead ship) was a British class of 294 corvettes used during the Second World War by Allied navies, most notably the Royal Navy and the Royal Canadian Navy. The vessels, named after flowers, were built to a design derived from a commercial whale-catcher so that they could be constructed quickly in small civilian shipyards. They became the workhorses of the Battle of the Atlantic, escorting convoys and hunting U-boats.

Design and construction

In 1939, facing an acute shortage of ocean escorts, the Royal Navy adapted Smiths Dock Company's design for a Southern Pride-type whale catcher. The resulting patrol vessel was cheap, simple and could be built by small yards unused to naval work. Ships were ordered in Britain and Canada; the Royal Canadian Navy ultimately operated over 100. The class was produced in two groups: the original Flower class of 1939–41 orders and the modified Flower class of 1942–44, with a lengthened forecastle improving notoriously wet and uncomfortable seakeeping.

Service

Flower-class corvettes bore the brunt of convoy escort in the early war years. They were slow — barely faster than a surfaced U-boat — and rolled violently, but they were available in numbers, and as radar, HF/DF and ahead-throwing weapons such as Hedgehog were fitted they became effective submarine hunters. Some 36 were lost to enemy action. The last surviving example, HMCS Sackville, is preserved as a museum ship in Halifax, Nova Scotia. The class was immortalised in Nicholas Monsarrat's novel The Cruel Sea, whose fictional HMS Compass Rose was a Flower-class corvette.
