Nuclear pulse propulsion

Nuclear pulse propulsion is a hypothetical method of spacecraft propulsion that uses a series of explosions for thrust. The best-known version, studied under Project Orion in the late 1950s and 1960s, would detonate nuclear bombs behind a massive pusher plate, with shock absorbers cushioning the acceleration of the vehicle.

Project Orion

Physicists Ted Taylor and Freeman Dyson led studies at General Atomics from 1958. Chemical-explosive tests with model vehicles ("putt-putt" experiments) demonstrated the basic principle. Proposed designs ranged from interplanetary freighters to interstellar craft with specific impulses far beyond chemical rockets.

Later concepts

Related ideas include Medusa (which uses a large sail-like canopy), Daedalus-style fusion pulse propulsion, and concepts using small fusion pellets detonated in a magnetic nozzle. The 1963 Partial Test Ban Treaty and concerns about fallout from surface launches ended serious work on the original Orion design.

Performance

Because the energy released per explosion is enormous, nuclear pulse vehicles could in principle combine high thrust with high exhaust velocity, enabling missions impractical for other propulsion systems.
