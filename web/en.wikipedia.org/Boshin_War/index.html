The Boshin War (Japanese: 戊辰戦争, "War of the Year of the Yang Earth Dragon"), sometimes known as the Japanese Revolution or Japanese Civil War, was a civil war fought in Japan from 1868 to 1869 between the ruling Tokugawa shogunate and factions seeking to restore political power to the Imperial Court. Its outcome ended the shogunate and completed the Meiji Restoration.

Background

After Commodore Perry's arrival in 1853 forced Japan open to foreign trade, dissatisfaction with the shogunate's handling of foreign pressure grew, especially in the domains of Satsuma and Chōshū, under the slogan sonnō jōi ("revere the emperor, expel the barbarians"). In November 1867 the last shōgun, Tokugawa Yoshinobu, returned governing authority to Emperor Meiji, hoping to retain influence in a new government — but hardliners in the imperial camp issued a decree abolishing the shogunate outright.

Course of the war

Fighting began at the Battle of Toba–Fushimi outside Kyoto in January 1868, where the smaller but modernised imperial army routed the shogunate's forces. Edo (Tokyo) was surrendered without a major battle in May 1868, through negotiations between Katsu Kaishū and Saigō Takamori. Resistance continued in northern Honshu, where the Ōuetsu Reppan Dōmei alliance of northern domains fought until late 1868, and the last holdouts — the shogunal loyalist fleet under Enomoto Takeaki — established the short-lived Republic of Ezo on Hokkaido before surrendering in June 1869 after the Battle of Hakodate.

Significance

The war was an early example of modern warfare in Japan, with rifled firearms, modern artillery, ironclad warships, and French and British military advisers on both sides. The imperial victory cleared the way for the abolition of the domains (1871), conscription, and the rapid modernisation programme of the Meiji state.
