Superstition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A superstition is any belief or practice considered by non-practitioners to be irrational or supernatural, attributed to fate or magic, perceived supernatural influence, or fear of things unknown. It is commonly applied to beliefs such as astrology, fortune telling, omens, lucky and unlucky objects and days, and the evil eye, as well as to practices dismissed as excessive or misplaced religious devotion.

Common examples

Widespread superstitions include knocking on wood for luck, avoiding walking under ladders, fear of the number 13 (triskaidekaphobia) and of Friday the 13th, four-leaf clovers and rabbit's feet as luck-bringers, breaking a mirror bringing seven years of bad luck, and black cats as omens. Different cultures hold different beliefs: the number 4 is avoided in much of East Asia, while the number 17 is unlucky in Italy.

Psychology

Psychologists explain superstition through pattern-seeking, the illusion of control, and operant conditioning — B. F. Skinner famously induced "superstitious" behaviour in pigeons rewarded at random intervals. Superstitious rituals can reduce anxiety and modestly improve performance through increased confidence, though they can also reinforce irrational fears and, in extreme cases, harmful behaviour such as avoiding medical treatment.

History

The word derives from Latin superstitio, used by Romans for excessive religious fear, and was later applied by churches to rival or folk beliefs. Enlightenment thinkers attacked superstition as an obstacle to reason, while modern usage varies: what one tradition regards as faith, another labels superstition, making the term partly a matter of perspective.
