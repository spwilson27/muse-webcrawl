Capital account

Capital account

In international macroeconomics, the capital account is one of the main components of a country's balance of payments. In the modern presentation used by the International Monetary Fund, the capital account records capital transfers between residents and non-residents — such as debt forgiveness and investment grants — and the acquisition or disposal of non-produced, non-financial assets, such as patents, leases and embassy land. It is usually small compared with the current account (trade in goods and services, income and transfers) and the financial account (investment flows).

Terminology

The term is a frequent source of confusion because older usage — and much journalism — applies "capital account" to what the IMF now calls the financial account: cross-border purchases of stocks, bonds, real estate and direct investment. In accounting for a business, "capital account" has yet another meaning: the equity account recording owners' contributions and retained earnings. In national accounts, by contrast, the capital account shows how saving and capital transfers finance investment in fixed assets.

Balance of payments identity

By construction, the balance of payments balances: the current account balance plus the capital account balance equals the financial account balance (net lending to, or borrowing from, the rest of the world), subject to a statistical discrepancy termed errors and omissions. A country running a current account deficit must, by accounting identity, be a net recipient of capital and financial inflows from abroad.

Capital account liberalisation

Policies that open or restrict cross-border capital movements are often described as capital account liberalisation or capital controls. Economists debate their effects: free capital mobility can channel savings to productive investment but also exposes economies to sudden stops and financial crises, a debate sharpened by the Asian financial crisis of 1997–98.
