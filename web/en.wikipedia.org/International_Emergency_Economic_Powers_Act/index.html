The International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) is a United States federal law, enacted in 1977, that authorises the President to regulate international commerce and block transactions and assets after declaring a national emergency in response to an unusual and extraordinary threat to the United States originating substantially outside the country. It is the legal basis for most US economic sanctions programmes.

Successive presidents have kept dozens of IEEPA emergencies continuously in force.

History

IEEPA was passed as part of a reform of presidential emergency powers following Watergate-era concerns. Its predecessor, the Trading with the Enemy Act of 1917, had been used in peacetime to impose broad controls — most controversially during the Korean War and in sustaining the embargo of Cuba. Congress restricted the Trading with the Enemy Act to wartime and created IEEPA for peacetime emergencies, intending the new powers to be narrower and exercised under the reporting requirements of the National Emergencies Act of 1976.

Powers and procedure

Once an emergency is declared, the President may investigate, regulate, or prohibit foreign-exchange transactions, transfers of credit, imports and exports of currency or securities, and dealings in property in which a foreign country or national has an interest. Emergencies must be renewed annually and may be terminated by the President or by a joint resolution of Congress. The administration of blocking sanctions is principally carried out by the Treasury Department's Office of Foreign Assets Control (OFAC).

Uses

The first IEEPA emergency was declared by President Jimmy Carter in November 1979, freezing Iranian assets during the hostage crisis. The statute has since underpinned sanctions against numerous countries and, increasingly, against non-state targets such as narcotics traffickers, terrorists, cyber actors, and proliferators. In 2019 the threat of IEEPA-based tariffs on Mexico drew legal debate, and in 2025 the use of IEEPA to impose broad tariffs on imports from many countries became the subject of major litigation over whether the statute authorises tariffs at all.
