Cuisine of Quebec

The cuisine of Quebec (French: cuisine québécoise) is the traditional food culture of the Canadian province of Quebec, rooted in the French settler cooking of New France from the 17th century, adapted to harsh winters, Indigenous ingredients, and later British and Irish influences. It is known for hearty, meat-and-potato dishes designed to sustain farmers and lumberjacks through long, cold seasons.

Signature dishes

The most famous creation is poutine — French fries topped with fresh cheese curds and brown gravy — which emerged in rural Quebec in the late 1950s and has become an emblem of Canadian food worldwide. Other classics include tourtière (spiced meat pie, especially at Christmas and New Year), pea soup (soupe aux pois), fèves au lard (baked beans with lard), cretons (a pork spread), pâté chinois (a shepherd's-pie-like dish), and pouding chômeur ("unemployed person's pudding"), a Depression-era cake baked in maple or brown-sugar sauce.

Maple and the cabane à sucre

Quebec produces the great majority of the world's maple syrup. In spring, families gather at sugar shacks (cabanes à sucre) for meals of eggs, ham, beans, and pancakes drenched in syrup, finished with tire sur la neige — hot maple taffy poured over snow.

Modern developments

Quebec is also known for its farm cheeses (such as Oka, originally made by Trappist monks), its bagels in the Montreal style, smoked meat, and a strong fine-dining scene in Montreal. Chefs such as Martin Picard have championed a modern, exuberant take on traditional ingredients including foie gras poutine.
