The Southern and Northern dynasties (Chinese: 南北朝) was a period in Chinese history lasting from 420 to 589 AD, during which China was divided between rival regimes in the south and north following the fall of the Eastern Jin dynasty. It ended when Yang Jian founded the Sui dynasty and reunified China in 589.

The Southern dynasties

Four successive dynasties ruled from Jiankang (modern Nanjing): the Liu Song (420–479), Southern Qi (479–502), Liang (502–557) and Chen (557–589). Southern regimes were dominated by aristocratic great families, fostered a flourishing culture of poetry and Buddhist devotion under emperors such as Wu of Liang, and absorbed large numbers of refugees fleeing the north.

The Northern dynasties

The north was first reunified by the Northern Wei (386–535), founded by the Tuoba clan of the Xianbei. The reforms of Emperor Xiaowen moved the capital to Luoyang and promoted sinicisation. The Wei split into Eastern and Western Wei, succeeded by the Northern Qi and Northern Zhou respectively; the Zhou conquered the Qi in 577, before the Yang family usurped the throne and founded Sui.

Culture and legacy

Despite political division, the era saw major developments in Buddhism, Daoism, mathematics (Zu Chongzhi's calculation of pi), literature and the equal-field land system, which the reunified Sui and Tang dynasties inherited.
