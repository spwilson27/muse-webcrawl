Vipassanā

Vipassanā (Pāli; Sanskrit: vipaśyanā) is a Buddhist practice of insight meditation aimed at seeing reality as it is — in particular the three marks of existence: impermanence (anicca), suffering or unsatisfactoriness (dukkha), and non-self (anattā). Along with samatha (calm-abiding), it is one of the two main categories of Buddhist meditation, and it forms the basis of the modern mindfulness movement.

Meaning and roots

The word combines the prefix vi- ("in a special way", "discerning") with the verbal root passanā, "seeing". Early Buddhist texts present insight as arising from sustained attention to the body, feelings, mind and mental phenomena, as described in the Satipaṭṭhāna Sutta. The classical Theravāda commentary tradition, especially Buddhaghosa's Visuddhimagga, systematised the progress of insight through a series of knowledges (ñāṇa) culminating in enlightenment.

Modern insight movement

In the 19th and 20th centuries a revival in Burma (Myanmar) made vipassanā accessible to laypeople on a mass scale. Teachers such as Ledi Sayadaw, Mahasi Sayadaw — whose "noting" technique labels sensations and thoughts as they arise — and U Ba Khin became influential. U Ba Khin's student S. N. Goenka spread a technique of body-scanning meditation through ten-day residential courses offered worldwide on a donation basis. In the West, teachers including Joseph Goldstein, Sharon Salzberg and Jack Kornfield, founders of the Insight Meditation Society in Massachusetts, adapted the practice for secular audiences, contributing to the development of mindfulness-based therapies such as MBSR.

Practice

Typical vipassanā practice involves sustained, non-reactive awareness of present-moment experience: the breath, bodily sensations, sounds, emotions and thoughts are observed as they arise and pass away, without clinging or aversion. Retreats are usually held in silence, with alternating periods of sitting and walking meditation. The goal is direct experiential insight into impermanence and non-self, leading to liberation from suffering.
