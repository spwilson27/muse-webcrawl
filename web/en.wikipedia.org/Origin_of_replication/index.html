Origin of replication

The origin of replication (also called the replication origin) is a particular sequence in a genome at which replication is initiated. Propagation of the genetic material between generations requires timely and accurate duplication of DNA prior to cell division; replication can proceed from specific locations in the genome known as origins of replication.

Although the molecular details of initiation differ among organisms, a common theme is the binding of initiator proteins to the origin, local unwinding of the double helix, and recruitment of the replication machinery.

In prokaryotes

Bacterial chromosomes are usually circular and typically have a single origin of replication. In Escherichia coli, the origin, called oriC, is about 245 base pairs long and rich in AT sequences. The initiator protein DnaA binds to recognition sites (DnaA boxes) within oriC, melts the DNA unwinding element, and recruits the DnaB helicase, after which DNA polymerase III begins synthesis, producing two replication forks that travel in opposite directions.

In eukaryotes

Eukaryotic chromosomes are linear and much larger, so they contain many origins — tens of thousands in a human genome — which fire at different times during S phase. Origins are recognized by the origin recognition complex (ORC), a six-subunit protein complex that binds DNA and, together with Cdc6 and Cdt1, loads the MCM helicase during the licensing phase of the cell cycle. In budding yeast, origins called autonomously replicating sequences (ARS) have a defined consensus sequence; in metazoans, origin specification is influenced more by chromatin structure and epigenetic features than by strict sequence motifs.

Viruses and plasmids

Many plasmids and viruses carry their own origins, which can be exploited in molecular biology: plasmid vectors replicate in host cells because they carry a bacterial origin such as the pUC or ColE1 origin, determining copy number.
