Iron Age

Preceded byBronze Age (in regions where it occurred)
Datesc. 1200 BC onward in the Near East; varies by region

Three-age systemStone Age → Bronze Age → Iron Age

The Iron Age is the final epoch of the three-age system of prehistory and protohistory, following the Stone Age and the Bronze Age. In this stage, humans in the relevant region make extensive use of iron or steel for cutting tools and weapons. Its timing varies greatly by region: ironworking spread in the Near East and Mediterranean from around 1200 BC, in India and China during the 1st millennium BC, in northern Europe from about 800 BC, and in parts of sub-Saharan Africa by the mid-1st millennium BC, while some regions (notably the Americas and Oceania) never developed indigenous iron smelting before contact with Europeans.

Transition from bronze

Meteoric iron was worked for millennia before smelting (Tutankhamun's dagger is a famous example). Smelted iron became widespread after about 1200 BC — a shift once attributed to the disruptions of the Late Bronze Age collapse and the interruption of tin supplies needed for bronze — through the bloomery process, in which iron ore is reduced with charcoal to a spongy bloom that is hammered into wrought iron. Because iron ores are far more widely available than copper and tin, iron democratised metal tools and weapons. Early iron was not obviously superior to good bronze until carburisation (steelmaking) techniques matured; China's cast-iron blast furnaces, from the mid-1st millennium BC, took a different path from the forge-based traditions elsewhere.

Regional developments

In the Near East, iron-using societies included the Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian empires, Israel and Judah, Urartu and the Phrygians. In Europe, the Iron Age encompasses the Hallstatt and La Tène (Celtic) cultures, Greek and Roman civilisation, and in Britain runs from c. 800 BC to the Roman conquest (AD 43), while in Scandinavia it extends through the Viking Age to about AD 1100. In India the Iron Age saw the second urbanisation of the Ganges plain, the mahajanapadas and the rise of Buddhism and Jainism. In Africa, early iron centres such as Nok (Nigeria) are notable, and iron may have been developed independently there.

Characteristics

Iron Age societies commonly show population growth, agricultural expansion (iron axes and ploughshares clearing heavy soils), fortified settlements and oppida, coinage in many regions, and alphabetic writing's spread in the Mediterranean world. The concept of an "Iron Age" is principally an archaeological periodisation; in literate regions it overlaps entirely with recorded history.
