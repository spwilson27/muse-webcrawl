Modified-release dosage

Modified-release dosage is a mechanism used in drugs — in pills, tablets, or capsules — that dissolves slowly and releases a drug over time. This contrasts with immediate-release formulations, in which the entire dose is available quickly. Modified-release allows a drug to be taken less frequently, improves patient compliance, and can reduce peak-related side effects by smoothing plasma concentration.

Types

Modified-release technologies include:

  • Extended-release (ER/XR/XL/SR/CR) formulations, which release the drug gradually over many hours, through matrix systems, coated pellets, or osmotic pumps (e.g., OROS technology).
  • Delayed-release formulations, which postpone release until the dosage form passes the stomach, typically using enteric coatings that resist gastric acid.
  • Targeted-release formulations, designed to release the drug at a particular site in the gastrointestinal tract or in response to pH or enzymes.
  • Pulsatile-release systems, which release drug in pulses after programmed lag times.

Mechanisms

Common mechanisms are diffusion through polymer matrices or membranes, erosion of the dosage form, ion-exchange resins, and osmotic pressure-driven pumps. Many products should not be crushed or chewed, because destroying the release mechanism can cause "dose dumping" — rapid release of a potentially dangerous amount of drug.

Examples

Well-known examples include extended-release formulations of metformin, bupropion (Wellbutrin XL), methylphenidate (Concerta, using an osmotic system), and opioids such as oxycodone ER, whose abuse potential led to abuse-deterrent reformulations.
