Sleep disorder

A sleep disorder, or somnipathy, is a medical disorder of an individual's sleep patterns. Some sleep disorders are severe enough to interfere with normal physical, mental, social and emotional functioning. Sleep disorders are broadly classified into dyssomnias, parasomnias, circadian rhythm sleep disorders and other conditions, and are diagnosed and treated within the specialty of sleep medicine. Polysomnography and actigraphy are common diagnostic tools.

Classification

The International Classification of Sleep Disorders groups conditions into insomnia disorders; sleep-related breathing disorders, such as obstructive sleep apnea; central disorders of hypersomnolence, including narcolepsy; circadian rhythm sleep-wake disorders, such as delayed sleep phase disorder and shift-work disorder; parasomnias, including sleepwalking, night terrors and REM sleep behavior disorder; and sleep-related movement disorders such as restless legs syndrome and periodic limb movement disorder.

Common disorders

Insomnia, difficulty initiating or maintaining sleep, is the most prevalent sleep disorder, affecting a significant fraction of adults at some point in their lives. Obstructive sleep apnea, in which the upper airway repeatedly collapses during sleep, causes fragmented sleep, loud snoring and daytime sleepiness and is associated with cardiovascular disease. Narcolepsy is a neurological disorder of sleep-wake control characterized by excessive daytime sleepiness and, in type 1, cataplexy triggered by emotion. Parasomnias involve undesirable physical events or experiences during sleep entry, sleep itself or arousal.

Causes and consequences

Causes include genetic predisposition, neurological and psychiatric illness, medication effects, pain and other medical conditions, poor sleep habits, and environmental or occupational disruption of circadian rhythms. Untreated sleep disorders contribute to accidents, cognitive impairment, mood disorders, metabolic and cardiovascular disease, and reduced quality of life, making them a substantial public-health concern.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the disorder: cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is the first-line treatment for chronic insomnia; continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) is standard for moderate-to-severe sleep apnea; wake-promoting medications and scheduled naps are used in narcolepsy; and light therapy and melatonin timing can realign circadian disorders. Sleep hygiene education commonly accompanies specific treatments.
