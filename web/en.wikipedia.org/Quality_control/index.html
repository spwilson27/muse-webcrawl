Quality control

Quality control (QC) is a process by which entities review the quality of all factors involved in production. ISO 9000 defines quality control as "a part of quality management focused on fulfilling quality requirements". This approach places emphasis on three aspects: elements such as controls and job management, competent personnel, and integrity of data; process features including defined standards; and detection of defects through inspection and testing.

History

Formal quality control emerged with the Industrial Revolution and the move from craft production to mass manufacturing. In the 1920s, Walter A. Shewhart at Bell Telephone Laboratories developed statistical process control and the control chart, distinguishing common-cause from special-cause variation. W. Edwards Deming and Joseph M. Juran later promoted statistical quality methods that were widely adopted in post-war Japanese industry and contributed to methods such as total quality management, kaizen and Six Sigma.

Methods

Common quality control techniques include acceptance sampling, in which a sample of items is inspected and a lot accepted or rejected on the results; control charts for monitoring processes over time; failure testing and stress testing of products; and checklists and statistical analysis of defect rates. In software development, quality control includes testing, code review and static analysis. Quality control is distinguished from quality assurance: QC is product-oriented and reactive, focused on detecting defects, while QA is process-oriented and preventive.
