Indemnity

Indemnity is a contractual obligation of one party (the indemnitor) to compensate the loss incurred by another party (the indemnitee) as a result of the indemnitor's own acts or those of a third party. Indemnities form part of many commercial contracts and underlie the principle of insurance, in which the insurer agrees to restore the insured, so far as money can, to the position occupied before an insured loss.

Contractual indemnity

Indemnity clauses allocate risk between contracting parties — for example, a contractor may indemnify a client against claims arising from the contractor's negligence. Their enforceability varies: many jurisdictions restrict indemnities that cover a party's own negligence or wilful misconduct, and consumer law often limits their use against individuals.

Insurance

In insurance law, the principle of indemnity means that a policyholder should not profit from a loss; measures such as deductibles, subrogation and contribution between insurers support this principle. Life insurance is a notable exception, since human life is not valued in money terms.

Other uses

The term also covers payments imposed on a defeated state (war indemnities), the compensation of office-holders for acts done in office, and letters of indemnity used in shipping to obtain delivery of goods without the original bill of lading.
