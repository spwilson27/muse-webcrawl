Creative Commons license

A Creative Commons (CC) license is one of several public copyright licenses that enable the free distribution of an otherwise copyrighted "work". A CC license is used when an author wants to give other people the right to share, use and build upon a work that the author has created. Creative Commons, a non-profit organisation founded by Lawrence Lessig and others in 2001, released the first licenses in December 2002; the current suite is version 4.0 (2013), designed to work internationally.

The building blocks of the CC license suite.

License elements

The main licenses are assembled from four elements:

ElementMeaning
BY — AttributionUsers must credit the creator.
SA — ShareAlikeAdaptations must be shared under the same license (a form of copyleft).
NC — NonCommercialUse is limited to non-commercial purposes.
ND — NoDerivativesOnly unadapted copies may be shared.

Combining these yields six core licenses, from the permissive CC BY to the restrictive CC BY-NC-ND. In addition, the CC0 public-domain dedication lets creators waive all rights to the extent legally possible. Works under licenses allowing derivatives and commercial use — and content marked CC0 — qualify as "free cultural works" under the Definition of Free Cultural Works endorsed by the open-content movement.

Use and impact

CC licenses are expressed in three layers: a human-readable deed, a lawyer-readable legal code, and machine-readable metadata, so search engines and platforms can identify licensed content. Hundreds of millions of works are CC-licensed: Wikipedia and its sister projects use CC BY-SA, as do open-access publishers, government open-data portals, Flickr and YouTube (which offers CC BY as a marking option). The licenses have been criticised for ambiguities — notably the contested meaning of "non-commercial" — and for license proliferation, but they remain the dominant standard for open content.
