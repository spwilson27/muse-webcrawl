Critical rationalism

Critical rationalism is an epistemological philosophy advanced by Karl Popper. Popper wrote about critical rationalism in his works The Open Society and Its Enemies (1945) and Conjectures and Refutations (1963). Critical rationalism is the opposite of classical empiricism and of the classical view of knowledge, which held that knowledge must be justified by evidence. Instead, critical rationalism holds that beliefs cannot be justified, only criticised, and that rationality consists in holding one's views open to criticism and revision.

Falsification and the growth of knowledge

Central to critical rationalism is the principle of falsifiability: a scientific theory must be capable of being shown false by observation. Science progresses not by verifying theories but by proposing bold conjectures and subjecting them to severe attempts at refutation; theories that survive criticism are corroborated, but never proven. This view replaced the logical positivists' criterion of verifiability as the standard of demarcation between science and non-science.

Criticism and discussion rather than justification

Popper argued that the traditional demand for justified true belief is impossible to satisfy without infinite regress. Critical rationalism instead defines rationality as an attitude of readiness to listen to critical arguments and to learn from experience. The approach extends beyond science: in politics and ethics, decisions and institutions should likewise be exposed to criticism, underpinning Popper's defence of the “open society”.

Later development

Popper's students and critics, including Imre Lakatos, William Warren Bartley III (who developed “pancritical rationalism”), Hans Albert and David Miller, extended and debated the doctrine, notably on whether critical rationalism can account for choosing between competing theories and whether it can itself be defended without circularity.
