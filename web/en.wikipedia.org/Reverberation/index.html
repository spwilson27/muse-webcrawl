Reverberation

Reverberation (commonly shortened to reverb) is the persistence of sound in an enclosed space after the original sound source has stopped. It is created when a sound is produced in a space that causes a large number of echoes to build up and then decay slowly as the sound is absorbed by the surfaces of the objects in the space.

Reverberation is a characteristic of a space defined by its physical dimensions and the materials of its surfaces. The most commonly used parameter to describe reverberation is the reverberation time, RT60 — the time it takes for the sound level to decrease by 60 decibels after the source stops.

Reverberation time

The reverberation time was first formalised by Wallace Clement Sabine in the late 1890s. Sabine's formula relates the reverberation time to the volume of the room and the total absorption of its surfaces: RT60 = 0.161 V / A, where V is the volume in cubic metres and A is the total absorption in sabins. Later refinements, such as the Eyring formula, account for rooms with highly absorptive surfaces.

Applications

Reverberation is deliberately controlled in concert halls, recording studios, and places of worship. In audio production, artificial reverberation is created with plate reverbs, spring reverbs, and digital signal processing, and is widely used in music recording and film sound.
