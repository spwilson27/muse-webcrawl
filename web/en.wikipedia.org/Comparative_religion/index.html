Comparative religion

FieldReligious studies
SubjectSystematic comparison of doctrines and practices of world religions
Key figuresMax Müller, James Frazer, Mircea Eliade, Wilfred Cantwell Smith

Comparative religion is the branch of the study of religions concerned with the systematic comparison of the doctrines, rituals, institutions, and narratives of the world's religions. In contrast to confessional theology, the academic discipline generally aims at descriptive and explanatory understanding rather than adjudicating the truth of any tradition, examining both the common patterns that recur across religions and the distinctive features of each.

Origins

Interest in comparing religions is ancient — Herodotus compared Greek and Egyptian gods, and medieval Muslim and Christian scholars wrote about other faiths, often polemically. The modern discipline emerged in the nineteenth century with the growth of philology and colonial-era access to Asian scriptures. Friedrich Max Müller, editor of the Sacred Books of the East series, is often called its founder; he famously wrote that "he who knows one [religion], knows none."

Themes and debates

Comparative work has addressed recurring phenomena such as sacrifice, pilgrimage, monasticism, scripture, meditation, eschatology, and rites of passage. In the twentieth century Mircea Eliade's phenomenology of religion emphasized patterns like the sacred and the profane and the "eternal return." Later scholars, including Wilfred Cantwell Smith and Jonathan Z. Smith, criticized the category of "world religions" itself as a modern construction shaped by European scholarship, urging more historically grounded and self-critical comparison.

Classifications

Textbooks commonly group religions as Abrahamic (Judaism, Christianity, Islam), Dharmic (Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, Sikhism), and Taoic or East Asian (Taoism, Confucianism, Shinto), alongside indigenous and new religious movements — while acknowledging that such taxonomies are heuristic and contested.
