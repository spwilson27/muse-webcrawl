Congee

TypeRice porridge
RegionEast, South and Southeast Asia
Main ingredientRice cooked in abundant water or broth
Chinese粥 (zhōu); Cantonese: juk1

Congee (from the Tamil word kanji) is a type of rice porridge eaten in many Asian countries. Rice is cooked in a large amount of water or broth until it breaks down into a thick, soft porridge. It is often served as a breakfast dish or given to the sick and to infants because it is easy to digest.

Regional varieties

In China, congee (zhou in Mandarin, juk in Cantonese) may be plain or cooked with pork, century egg, fish, offal or peanuts. Korean juk includes varieties such as jeonbok-juk (abalone) and dak-juk (chicken). Japanese okayu is traditionally eaten when ill, and nanakusa-gayu with seven herbs is eaten on 7 January. Variants include Vietnamese cháo, Thai jok, Filipino lugaw and arroz caldo, Indonesian bubur ayam, and South Asian khichdi-adjacent rice gruels. In Portugal-influenced traditions, canja is a chicken-and-rice soup.

Preparation

A typical ratio is one part rice to six to ten parts liquid, simmered for an hour or more, sometimes with the rice pre-soaked or briefly fried. Ginger, white pepper, sesame oil, scallions and fried dough sticks (youtiao) are common accompaniments.

Cultural notes

Congee has a long history in China, recorded since antiquity, and is associated with famine relief and charity kitchens as well as home cooking.
