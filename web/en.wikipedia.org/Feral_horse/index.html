A feral horse is a free-roaming horse of domesticated stock. As such, a feral horse is not a "wild horse" in the sense of an animal without domesticated ancestors; the only truly wild horse to survive into modern times is Przewalski's horse of Central Asia. Feral horse populations exist on every continent except Antarctica — descended from horses that escaped, were released, or were abandoned — and include the mustangs of the American West, the brumbies of Australia, and the ponies of Assateague Island, Dartmoor, and the New Forest.

Behaviour and ecology

Feral horses live in small bands, typically a stallion with several mares and their offspring, with surplus males forming bachelor groups. They graze grasses and browse, often travelling long distances to water. In ecosystems where they evolved recently or not at all, large populations can overgraze fragile rangeland, trample waterholes, and compete with native wildlife and livestock, making their management politically contentious.

Management

In the United States, free-roaming horses on federal land were protected by the Wild and Free-Roaming Horses and Burros Act of 1971 and are managed by the Bureau of Land Management, which periodically rounds up excess animals for adoption or holding facilities and uses fertility control such as the PZP vaccine. Australia manages brumbies through aerial and ground culling and trapping, a practice that has provoked public controversy, particularly in Kosciuszko National Park. Smaller island populations, such as the Chincoteague ponies of Virginia, are managed by regular roundups and auctions that keep numbers in balance with the habitat.
