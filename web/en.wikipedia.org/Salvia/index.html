Salvia

Flowering sage plants are widely grown as ornamentals.

Salvia is the largest genus of plants in the mint family, Lamiaceae, with roughly 1,000 species of shrubs, herbaceous perennials and annuals distributed worldwide. The name derives from the Latin salvere ("to feel well and healthy"), reflecting the medicinal reputation of common sage.

Notable species

Salvia officinalis (common sage) is a classic culinary herb of Mediterranean cooking. Salvia hispanica is the source of chia seeds. Salvia divinorum, a Mexican species traditionally used by Mazatec shamans, contains the potent hallucinogen salvinorin A. Many species, including Salvia splendens (scarlet sage) and the rosmarinus group (rosemary was reclassified as Salvia rosmarinus in 2017), are grown as garden ornamentals valued by pollinators.

Pollination mechanism

Salvia flowers have a distinctive staminal lever mechanism: the two stamens are joined so that a visiting bee or hummingbird pushing into the flower triggers the anthers to swing down and deposit pollen on the pollinator's back, promoting cross-pollination.

Cultivation

Most sages prefer full sun and well-drained soil and are drought-tolerant once established. They are staples of herb gardens, Mediterranean planting schemes and wildlife-friendly borders.
