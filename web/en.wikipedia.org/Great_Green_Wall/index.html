Great Green Wall

Launched2007, by the African Union
RegionThe Sahel, from Senegal (Atlantic) to Djibouti (Red Sea)
Planned extentAbout 8,000 km long, 15 km wide
GoalsRestore 100 million hectares of degraded land by 2030; sequester 250 Mt carbon; create 10 million jobs
Lead countries11 founding Sahelian states, coordinated by the Pan-African Agency of the Great Green Wall

The Great Green Wall (French: Grande Muraille Verte) is an African-led initiative to combat desertification and land degradation in the Sahel, the semi-arid belt south of the Sahara. Launched by the African Union in 2007, it originally envisaged a band of planted trees stretching some 8,000 km from Dakar, Senegal, to Djibouti on the Red Sea coast. The concept descends from earlier proposals, including Thomas Sankara's anti-desertification campaigns in Burkina Faso and the "green wall" ideas of the 1950s, and echoes China's Three-North Shelter Forest Program.

From wall of trees to mosaic

The project has evolved from a literal wall of trees — criticised by scientists as impractical in drylands — into a mosaic of land-restoration interventions: farmer-managed natural regeneration (notably in Niger, where some 200 million trees have been regreened on around 5 million hectares), agroforestry, zaï pits and half-moon bunds for water harvesting, communal gardens such as Senegal's Koyli Alpha projects, and sustainable grazing management. Senegal planted an early tree belt near Widou Thiengoly, with mixed survival rates.

Progress and challenges

Progress has lagged the 2030 targets: by the early 2020s assessments estimated only a fraction (roughly a fifth or less, on some measures) of the restoration goal achieved, with funding shortfalls repeatedly cited. In January 2021 donors pledged about US$19 billion under a "Great Green Wall Accelerator" coordinated with the UN Convention to Combat Desertification. Security crises in the central Sahel, land-tenure disputes and the need to centre local communities remain major obstacles, but the initiative is widely regarded as a flagship of ecosystem restoration and a symbol of African climate adaptation.
