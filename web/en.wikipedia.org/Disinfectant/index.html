Disinfectant

A disinfectant is a chemical substance or compound used to inactivate or destroy microorganisms on inert surfaces. Disinfection does not necessarily kill all microorganisms, especially resistant bacterial spores; it is less effective than sterilization, which is an extreme physical or chemical process that kills all types of life. Disinfectants are generally distinguished from other antimicrobial agents such as antibiotics, which destroy microorganisms within the body, and antiseptics, which destroy microorganisms on living tissue.

Properties

A perfect disinfectant would offer complete and full microbiological sterilization, without harming humans or useful forms of life, be inexpensive, and be noncorrosive. Few disinfectants meet all of these criteria. Effectiveness depends on the concentration of the agent, the contact time, the temperature, the pH, and the presence of organic matter such as blood or soil, which can inactivate many disinfectants.

Types

  • Alcohols — ethanol and isopropyl alcohol at 60–90% concentration are widely used for skin and surface disinfection.
  • Chlorine compounds — sodium hypochlorite (bleach) is a broad-spectrum disinfectant used in households, hospitals and water treatment.
  • Quaternary ammonium compounds ("quats") — cationic detergents effective against many bacteria and enveloped viruses.
  • Peroxides — hydrogen peroxide and peracetic acid act as oxidizers.
  • Phenolics — among the oldest disinfectants, descended from Lister's carbolic acid antisepsis.
  • Aldehydes — glutaraldehyde and formaldehyde are high-level disinfectants used for medical instruments.

Levels of disinfection

Health-care practice classifies disinfection as high-level (kills all microorganisms except large numbers of bacterial spores), intermediate-level (kills mycobacteria, most viruses and bacteria), and low-level (kills most vegetative bacteria and some viruses). Instruments are assigned to critical, semi-critical or non-critical categories according to the risk of infection, following the Spaulding classification.

Disinfectants and the environment

Widespread use of disinfectants, particularly in households and agriculture, raises concerns about toxicity to aquatic organisms and about the possible selection of antimicrobial-resistant bacteria. Regulatory agencies such as the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency register disinfectant products and specify the organisms against which their efficacy has been demonstrated.
