Frankfurt School

The Institute for Social Research, Frankfurt, home of the Frankfurt School

The Frankfurt School refers to a school of thought in sociology and critical philosophy associated with the Institute for Social Research founded at Goethe University Frankfurt in 1923. Its major figures — including Max Horkheimer, Theodor W. Adorno, Herbert Marcuse, Walter Benjamin, Erich Fromm, and later Jürgen Habermas — developed critical theory, an interdisciplinary critique of society and culture drawing on Marx, Freud, Kant, and Hegel.

History

Founded with an endowment from Felix Weil, the Institute turned under Horkheimer's directorship (from 1930) toward an interdisciplinary materialism. Forced into exile by the Nazis in 1933, it relocated via Geneva to Columbia University in New York, returning to Frankfurt after the Second World War. Key works produced in exile include Horkheimer and Adorno's Dialectic of Enlightenment (1947) and The Authoritarian Personality (1950).

Key ideas

Frankfurt School theorists analysed how domination operates through culture as well as economics: the culture industry standardises mass entertainment and pacifies consumers; "instrumental reason" reduces thought to calculation; and one-dimensional society (Marcuse) absorbs opposition. Their critique influenced the New Left, cultural studies, and media theory. Critics have charged the school with elitism and pessimism, and its name is sometimes invoked loosely in polemics about "cultural Marxism".
