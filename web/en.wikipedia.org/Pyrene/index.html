Pyrene

Pyrene is a polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon (PAH) consisting of four fused benzene rings, with the molecular formula C16H10. It is a colourless to pale yellow crystalline solid formed during the incomplete combustion of organic matter, and it occurs in coal tar and in soot, vehicle exhaust, and cigarette smoke. Pyrene is one of the most widely studied PAHs and is used as a fluorescent probe in spectroscopy and biochemistry.

Properties

Pyrene melts at about 150 °C and is nearly insoluble in water but soluble in organic solvents. Its fluorescence is highly sensitive to solvent polarity — the relative intensities of its vibronic emission bands (the "Py scale" of polarity) are used to probe microenvironments such as cell membranes and micelles. At high concentrations, pyrene forms excited-state dimers (excimers) with a characteristic broad blue emission, a phenomenon exploited in studies of molecular crowding and polymer dynamics.

Occurrence and exposure

Like other PAHs, pyrene is ubiquitous in the environment wherever fossil fuels or biomass are burned. It is found in grilled and smoked foods, urban air particulates, and contaminated soils and sediments, and it is one of the 16 priority-pollutant PAHs listed by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency. In interstellar medium studies, pyrene and related PAHs are candidate carriers of some unidentified infrared emission bands, and pyrene has been detected in samples of the carbonaceous meteorite Murchison.

Health and metabolism

Pyrene itself is not classified as a human carcinogen, but it is metabolized in the body by cytochrome P450 enzymes to 1-hydroxypyrene, which is excreted in urine and used as a biomarker of total PAH exposure in occupational health studies of coke-oven, asphalt, and aluminium workers. It co-occurs with the carcinogen benzo(a)pyrene, making it a useful indicator compound for PAH contamination.
