The Mexico–United States border is an international border separating Mexico and the United States, extending from the Pacific Ocean in the west to the Gulf of Mexico in the east. The border traverses a variety of terrains, ranging from urban areas to deserts, and is approximately 3,145 kilometres (1,954 mi) long, making it the tenth-longest international border in the world and the most frequently crossed.

Geography

The border begins at the Pacific Ocean near San Diego, California, and Tijuana, Baja California, proceeds eastward through desert and mountainous terrain, follows the Rio Grande for much of its length through Texas, and ends at the Gulf of Mexico near Brownsville, Texas, and Matamoros, Tamaulipas.

History

The present boundary was largely established by the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848), which ended the Mexican–American War, and the Gadsden Purchase (1853). Earlier boundaries had been set by the Adams–Onís Treaty of 1819 between the United States and Spain.

Security and crossings

The border is patrolled by U.S. Customs and Border Protection. Portions are lined with fencing and vehicle barriers, particularly near urban areas. Hundreds of millions of legal crossings occur annually, and the border region is a major corridor for trade as well as a focus of debate over unauthorized immigration and drug trafficking.
