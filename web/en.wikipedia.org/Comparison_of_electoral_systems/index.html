Comparison of electoral systems

An electoral system is a set of rules that determine how elections and referendums are conducted and how their results are determined — how votes are translated into seats or outcomes. Electoral systems can be compared along criteria such as proportionality, voter choice, local representation, accountability, party system effects, simplicity, and susceptibility to strategic voting and manipulation.

Evaluation criteria

Political scientists evaluate systems against criteria such as those associated with figures like Arend Lijphart: proportionality of seats to votes, effective representation of minorities and women, governability and stable majorities, voter participation, and the ease of holding individual representatives accountable. Social choice theory adds formal criteria — such as the Condorcet criterion and independence of irrelevant alternatives — that no single voting method can fully satisfy, as demonstrated by Arrow's impossibility theorem.

Effects

Duverger's law holds that plurality systems tend toward two-party competition, while proportional systems permit multiparty systems. Electoral system design also affects campaigning, gerrymandering incentives, party discipline, and the frequency of coalition and minority governments.
