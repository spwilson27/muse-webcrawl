The Seven Sages of the Bamboo Grove (Chinese: 竹林七賢) were a group of Chinese scholars, writers, and musicians of the third century AD, in the late Cao Wei and early Jin dynasty period. The seven were Ji Kang, Ruan Ji, Shan Tao, Xiang Xiu, Liu Ling, Wang Rong, and Ruan Xian. They are traditionally depicted gathering in a bamboo grove near Ji Kang's home in Shanyang (in present-day Henan), drinking wine, composing poetry and music, and engaging in "pure conversation" (qīngtán) far from the dangerous politics of the court.

Context and thought

Their gatherings were a response to the violent power struggles between the Cao family and the rising Sima clan, which made conventional Confucian service at court perilous. The Sages are associated with xuanxue ("dark learning" or Neo-Daoism), which reinterpreted Daoist classics such as the Tao Te Ching and Zhuangzi alongside Confucian texts. Their ideal was ziran — naturalness and spontaneity — expressed in deliberate eccentricity: Ruan Ji rolled his eyes at unwelcome guests and wept on dead-end roads; Liu Ling, a notorious drinker, is credited with the "Ode to the Virtue of Wine"; Ji Kang, a master of the qin and author of the essay "On the Absence of Emotions in Music," refused to serve the Sima regime and was executed in 262 or 263 AD after signing a petition in his support by three thousand students failed to save him.

Legacy

The Sages became enduring icons of the free-spirited scholar-recluse in Chinese culture, celebrated in the fifth-century anecdote collection A New Account of the Tales of the World (Shishuo Xinyu). The theme was a favourite of Chinese, Korean, and Japanese painting for well over a millennium, often shown in embossed brick reliefs in Southern Dynasties tombs, and the group furnished a model for later literary gatherings and bohemian circles throughout East Asia.
