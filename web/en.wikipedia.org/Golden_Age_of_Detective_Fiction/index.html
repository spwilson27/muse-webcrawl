Golden Age of Detective Fiction

The Golden Age of Detective Fiction was an era of classic murder mystery novels of similar patterns and styles, predominantly in the 1920s and 1930s, largely in Britain. It is associated with authors such as Agatha Christie, Dorothy L. Sayers, Margery Allingham and Ngaio Marsh — the so-called "Queens of Crime."

Characteristics

The classic Golden Age novel is a "whodunit": a murder occurs, usually in a closed setting such as a country house, village, train or island, and a detective — professional, amateur or gentlemanly — solves it through observation and logical deduction. The reader is given the clues needed to solve the puzzle, and the solution is revealed in a final gathering of suspects. Critics such as Ronald Knox, whose "Ten Commandments" of detective fiction appeared in 1929, and S. S. Van Dine attempted to codify the rules of "fair play" between author and reader.

Major figures

Agatha Christie's Hercule Poirot and Miss Marple novels, including The Murder of Roger Ackroyd (1926) and Murder on the Orient Express (1934), defined the form for millions of readers. Dorothy L. Sayers created Lord Peter Wimsey; G. K. Chesterton wrote the Father Brown stories; Margery Allingham created Albert Campion; and Ngaio Marsh, a New Zealander, created Inspector Alleyn. John Dickson Carr specialised in "locked room" mysteries.

Decline and legacy

The Second World War and the rise of the hardboiled American school of crime writing (Dashiell Hammett, Raymond Chandler) shifted tastes toward realism and psychological depth. Nevertheless, Golden Age conventions — the fair-play puzzle, the closed circle of suspects, the brilliant amateur detective — remain highly influential, and the works of the era continue to be adapted for film and television.
