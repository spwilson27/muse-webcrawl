The pons (Latin for "bridge"; also called the pons Varolii, after the Italian surgeon Costanzo Varolio) is part of the brainstem, lying between the midbrain above and the medulla oblongata below, and in front of the cerebellum. It measures about 2.5 cm in length and appears as a broad transverse band of white matter on the front of the brainstem.

Structure

The pons is divided into a ventral (basilar) part and a dorsal (tegmental) part. The ventral pons contains longitudinal descending fibres — corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts — interrupted by pontine nuclei, whose axons cross to the opposite cerebellar hemisphere as the middle cerebellar peduncle, relaying information from the cerebral cortex to the cerebellum. The tegmentum contains cranial nerve nuclei, the reticular formation and ascending sensory pathways.

Function

The pons relays signals between the cerebrum and cerebellum and helps coordinate movement. Its nuclei give rise to, or are associated with, cranial nerves V (trigeminal), VI (abducens), VII (facial) and VIII (vestibulocochlear). Pontine centres also modulate respiration (the pneumotaxic and apneustic centres act on the medullary respiratory groups), contribute to sleep — particularly REM sleep — taste, and arousal.

Clinical significance

Strokes from occlusion of branches of the basilar artery cause characteristic pontine syndromes, including locked-in syndrome when the ventral pons is extensively damaged: the patient remains conscious but cannot move or speak. Rapid correction of low blood sodium can injure the pons (central pontine myelinolysis, now termed osmotic demyelination). Tumours and demyelinating diseases such as multiple sclerosis may also affect the pons.
