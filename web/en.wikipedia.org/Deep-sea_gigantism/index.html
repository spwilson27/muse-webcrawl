In zoology, deep-sea gigantism (or abyssal gigantism) is the tendency for species of deep-sea dwelling animals, particularly invertebrates, to grow to a larger size than their shallow-water relatives. It is a striking and still incompletely explained feature of life in the ocean's cold, dark depths.

Examples

Familiar examples include the giant isopod Bathynomus giganteus, which reaches 50 cm where coastal isopods measure a few centimetres; the Japanese spider crab, with a leg span approaching 4 m; the giant and colossal squids; the Greenland shark, among the largest and longest-lived vertebrates; giant amphipods and sea spiders; the barreleye and gulper eels; and xenophyophores, single-celled organisms that can exceed 10 cm. Notable cases were documented by expeditions and by deep-diving submersibles such as Alvin.

Proposed explanations

Several factors may favour large size at depth. Near-freezing temperatures slow metabolism and growth, delaying maturity and extending lifespan — conditions that in many ectotherms produce larger eventual body size, consistent with Bergmann's rule. Scarce food rewards efficient, energy-storing bodies and the ability to survive long fasts and to exploit rare, large food falls. Reduced predation pressure, higher dissolved oxygen in cold water, and lower population densities may also contribute. Different explanations likely apply to different lineages, and gigantism is not universal: many deep-sea groups are small.
