The Statue of Liberty (Liberty Enlightening the World) is a colossal neoclassical sculpture on Liberty Island in New York Harbor. A gift from the people of France to the United States, it was designed by sculptor Frédéric Auguste Bartholdi, built in Paris, and dedicated on 28 October 1886. The robed figure represents Libertas, the Roman goddess of liberty; she holds a torch above her head with her right hand and carries a tablet inscribed with the date of American independence, 4 July 1776, while a broken chain lies at her feet.

Conception and construction

The idea is credited to the French historian Édouard René de Laboulaye, who proposed in 1865 that France present a monument celebrating American independence and the friendship of the two nations. Bartholdi built the statue in Paris using repoussé copper sheets over an iron armature engineered by Gustave Eiffel. Fundraising was difficult on both sides of the Atlantic; in the United States, newspaper publisher Joseph Pulitzer's campaign drew more than 120,000 mostly small donations to pay for the pedestal, designed by Richard Morris Hunt. The completed statue was disassembled into 350 pieces, shipped aboard the French frigate Isère in 1885, and reassembled on Bedloe's Island (renamed Liberty Island in 1956).

Symbolism and immigration

Originally a monument to republican liberty, the statue became an emblem of immigration and hope as millions of newcomers passed it en route to the processing station at Ellis Island. Emma Lazarus's 1883 sonnet "The New Colossus" — with its famous lines "Give me your tired, your poor, / Your huddled masses yearning to breathe free" — was placed on a plaque in the pedestal in 1903 and cemented that association.

Later history

The statue's copper skin has weathered to its characteristic green patina. It was administered by the Lighthouse Board, then the War Department, and since 1933 by the National Park Service. A major restoration for its centennial (1982–86) replaced the torch and repaired the iron framework; the original torch is now displayed in the Statue of Liberty Museum, opened in 2019. Access to the crown resumed after post-2001 closures with limited tickets; the torch balcony has been closed since 1916.
