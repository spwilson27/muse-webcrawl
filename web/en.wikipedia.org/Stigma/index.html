Stigma

The stigma of a flower is the receptive tip of the carpel.

Stigma is a word with several meanings. In social science it denotes an attribute that is deeply discrediting — the subject of this article's longest treatment. In botany it is the pollen-receiving part of a flower's carpel, in zoology a type of spiracle or eyespot, in medicine a visible sign of disease, and in Christianity the term stigmata refers to bodily wounds corresponding to those of the crucified Christ.

Social stigma

The sociologist Erving Goffman's 1963 book Stigma: Notes on the Management of Spoiled Identity founded the modern study of the topic. Goffman described stigma as an attribute that reduces its bearer "from a whole and usual person to a tainted, discounted one", and distinguished bodily "abominations", blemishes of character, and tribal stigmas of race, nation and religion. Later researchers describe components of labelling, stereotyping, separation, status loss and discrimination, occurring where power allows them to take effect. Stigma surrounds conditions such as mental illness, HIV/AIDS, disability, addiction and criminal records, and can itself worsen health and social outcomes; campaigns to reduce it are a major strand of public-health work.

Botany

In flowering plants, the stigma is the part of the pistil that receives pollen during pollination; it sits atop the style and is often sticky or feathery to trap pollen grains, which germinate there and grow pollen tubes toward the ovules.

Other senses

In medicine, "stigmata" are characteristic signs associated with a disease. In entomology a stigma is a coloured spot on an insect wing. Sigma (Σ) was historically written with a final form called stigma in Greek numerals, where it had the value 6.
