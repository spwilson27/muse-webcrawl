Democratic Party presidential primaries

The Democratic Party presidential primaries are the process by which voters of the United States Democratic Party choose its nominee for President of the United States in the general election. Through a series of state-level primary elections and caucuses, candidates compete to win delegates to the Democratic National Convention, where the nominee is formally selected. Since reforms adopted after the chaotic 1968 convention, pledged delegates have been allocated in proportion to the vote in each primary or caucus, subject to a 15 percent threshold, and the influence of unpledged "superdelegates" — party leaders free to support any candidate — was sharply reduced by reforms adopted in 2018.

History

For much of American history, Democratic nominees were chosen by party insiders at conventions, epitomised by the "smoke-filled room". Progressive-era reforms introduced presidential primaries in some states, but most delegates remained under the control of state party organisations until the McGovern–Fraser Commission reforms of the early 1970s transferred power to primary voters. Memorable modern contests include the 1984 Hart–Mondale race, the 2008 contest between Barack Obama and Hillary Clinton — the longest and most expensive primary campaign to that date — and the 2016 race between Clinton and Bernie Sanders. In 2024, President Joe Biden won the primaries but withdrew from the race in July, after which Vice President Kamala Harris was nominated at the convention.

Process

The primary calendar traditionally began with the Iowa caucuses and the New Hampshire primary, though changes to the official calendar in the 2020s elevated South Carolina and other states to the start of the schedule; Iowa Democrats moved to a mail-in process. "Super Tuesday", when many states vote simultaneously, often proves decisive. Because delegates are awarded proportionally, Democratic contests tend to last longer than Republican ones, which permit winner-take-all allocation.
