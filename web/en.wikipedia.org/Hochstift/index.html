Hochstift

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The prince-bishops ruled their Hochstifte as secular princes of the Holy Roman Empire.

In the Holy Roman Empire, a Hochstift (plural: Hochstifte) was the territory ruled by a bishop or archbishop in his capacity as a secular prince — that is, the prince-bishopric or prince-archbishopric itself, as distinct from the diocese (Bistum), the larger ecclesiastical jurisdiction over which he exercised spiritual authority.

The distinction arose because a diocese's boundaries rarely matched those of the territory its bishop actually governed. Within their Hochstifte, prince-bishops held imperial immediacy, a seat and vote in the Imperial Diet (Reichstag), and the rights of secular rulers: taxation, jurisdiction, coinage and military levies. The three great Rhenish ecclesiastical electors — the Archbishops of Mainz, Cologne and Trier — ruled substantial Hochstifte known as Kurstifte, and together with the other ecclesiastical princes formed the spiritual bench of the College of Princes.

Other notable Hochstifte included Würzburg, Bamberg, Münster, Liège, Salzburg (an archbishopric), Paderborn, Hildesheim, Osnabrück, Passau and Fulda. Many originated in grants of land and rights by medieval emperors, who relied on celibate, non-hereditary church princes as a counterweight to the hereditary secular nobility.

Nearly all Hochstifte were abolished by the secularizations of the Reichsdeputationshauptschluss of 1803, which mediatized ecclesiastical states and transferred their territories to secular princes as compensation for lands lost to France on the left bank of the Rhine. The term survives in German historiography and in names of some modern foundations and institutions.
