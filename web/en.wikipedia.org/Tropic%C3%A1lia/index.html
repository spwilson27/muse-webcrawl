Tropicália

The 1968 collective album Tropicália: ou Panis et Circencis served as the manifesto of the movement.

Tropicália, also known as tropicalismo, was a Brazilian artistic movement that arose in the late 1960s and encompassed music, theatre, poetry, cinema and the visual arts. Characterised by the fusion of Brazilian traditions with foreign influences — rock and roll, psychedelia, concrete poetry and avant-garde art — it arose in part as a response, both playful and critical, to the military dictatorship that had ruled Brazil since 1964, and to nationalist purism in Brazilian popular music.

Origins and music

The name derived from an installation by the artist Hélio Oiticica exhibited in Rio de Janeiro in 1967. In music, the movement coalesced around the Bahian musicians Caetano Veloso and Gilberto Gil, whose appearances at televised song festivals in 1967 — Veloso performing "Alegria, Alegria" and Gil with "Domingo no Parque", both backed by rock bands — scandalised traditionalists. In 1968 Veloso, Gil, Tom Zé, Gal Costa, Nara Leão and the psychedelic rock band Os Mutantes, with arrangements by Rogério Duprat, released the collective album Tropicália: ou Panis et Circencis, the movement's musical manifesto, mixing samba, bossa nova, baião, bolero, rock and orchestral avant-garde writing.

The tropicalists embraced cultural "cannibalism" (antropofagia), an idea taken from Oswald de Andrade's 1928 Manifesto Antropófago: devouring foreign influences and transforming them into something Brazilian. Their songs combined social critique with irony, wordplay and references to both rural tradition and urban consumer modernity.

Repression and legacy

After the regime hardened with Institutional Act No. 5 in December 1968, Veloso and Gil were arrested, imprisoned and then exiled to London in 1969, effectively ending tropicália as an organised movement. Its influence on Brazilian music and culture proved immense and lasting: it permanently expanded the horizons of música popular brasileira (MPB) and influenced later generations in Brazil and abroad; international interest revived in the 1990s and 2000s with reissues and advocacy by musicians such as David Byrne and Beck.
