Modus tollens

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Modus tollens is a rule of inference in propositional logic and a valid form of deductive argument. It states that if a conditional statement ("if P then Q") is accepted, and the consequent Q is false, then the antecedent P must also be false:

  • Premise 1: P → Q
  • Premise 2: ¬Q
  • Conclusion: ¬P

For example: "If it is raining, the ground is wet. The ground is not wet. Therefore it is not raining." The name derives from the Latin modus tollendo tollens, "the mode that by denying denies".

Rationale

The rule follows from the logic of the material conditional: if P were true, Q would have to be true; since Q is false, P cannot be true. It is equivalent in form to reasoning by contraposition, since "P implies Q" is logically equivalent to "not-Q implies not-P".

Scientific reasoning

Modus tollens underlies the logic of falsification in the philosophy of science: if a hypothesis H implies an observable prediction O, and observation shows not-O, then H is refuted. Karl Popper built his account of scientific method on this asymmetry — hypotheses can be conclusively falsified by a failed prediction (by modus tollens) but never conclusively verified, since inferring H from O would commit the fallacy of affirming the consequent. In practice, the Duhem–Quine thesis notes that predictions depend on auxiliary assumptions, so real-world refutations are less clean than the logical schema.
