Hilbert basis theorem

In mathematics, specifically commutative algebra, the Hilbert basis theorem states that every ideal in a polynomial ring over a Noetherian ring is finitely generated. Equivalently: if R is a Noetherian ring, then the polynomial ring R[x] is also Noetherian. It follows by induction that a polynomial ring in finitely many variables over a field (or over the integers) is Noetherian, so every ideal of such a ring — for instance, the ideal of all polynomials vanishing on an algebraic variety — is generated by finitely many polynomials.

History

David Hilbert proved the theorem in his 1890 paper "Über die Theorie der algebraischen Formen", motivated by invariant theory: Paul Gordan had spent years computing finite bases of invariants case by case, and Hilbert's abstract, nonconstructive proof that a finite basis always exists prompted Gordan's famous (and possibly apocryphal) remark, "This is not mathematics; this is theology." Hilbert later found a constructive proof. Emmy Noether subsequently axiomatized the underlying finiteness property in her theory of Noetherian rings, where the theorem becomes nearly immediate from the ascending chain condition.

Proof idea

Given an ideal I in R[x], one considers, for each degree d, the ideal of leading coefficients of polynomials of degree d in I. These coefficient ideals form an ascending chain in the Noetherian ring R and therefore stabilize. Choosing finitely many generators for each coefficient ideal up to the stabilization point, and finitely many generators for the parts of I of lower degree (by induction on degree), yields a finite set that generates I.

Significance

The theorem is a foundation stone of algebraic geometry: it implies that affine algebraic varieties can be defined by finite systems of polynomial equations, and its corollary — sometimes expressed through Hilbert's Nullstellensatz — connects algebra with geometry. It also underlies Gröbner basis theory, which makes ideal-theoretic computations algorithmic.
