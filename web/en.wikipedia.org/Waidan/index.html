Waidan

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Waidan (Chinese: 外丹, "external alchemy" or "external elixir") is a branch of Chinese alchemy concerned with compounding elixirs of immortality in a laboratory — by heating minerals and metals, especially cinnabar and mercury, in a sealed crucible or furnace. It contrasts with neidan, "internal alchemy", which pursued the same goals through meditation and bodily disciplines.

History

Waidan texts appear from the 2nd century BCE onward and flourished under the Han, Six Dynasties, and Tang periods. The earliest surviving alchemical classic, Ge Hong's Baopuzi (c. 320 CE), describes elixir recipes in detail. Practitioners sought the jindan, the "golden elixir", believing that ingesting substances impervious to decay — gold, cinnabar (mercuric sulfide) — would confer permanence on the body.

Practice and dangers

Elixirs were prepared through repeated cycles of heating and cooling ("nine-times refined cinnabar") and commonly involved mercury, lead, arsenic, and sulfur compounds. Many preparations were lethally toxic: several Tang-dynasty emperors are recorded to have died from elixir poisoning, as did numerous adepts. Despite this, waidan experimentation advanced Chinese knowledge of chemistry — the manipulation of gunpowder precursors is often traced to alchemists seeking elixirs.

Decline and legacy

After the Tang and Song dynasties, external alchemy waned as neidan became dominant within Taoism. Waidan remains a key source for the history of chemistry and medicine in China, and its language was largely absorbed into internal alchemy as metaphor.
