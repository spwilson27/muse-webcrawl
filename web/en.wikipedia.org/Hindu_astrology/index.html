Hindu astrology, also called Jyotiṣa (Sanskrit: ज्योतिष, "science of light") or Vedic astrology, is the traditional Hindu system of astrology, one of the six auxiliary disciplines (Vedāṅgas) connected with the study of the Vedas. Jyotiṣa encompasses the casting and interpretation of horoscopes based on the positions of the Sun, Moon, planets, and lunar mansions at a person's birth, and remains widely practised in India for purposes such as choosing auspicious times (muhūrta), matching marriage partners, and interpreting the course of individual lives.

Principles

Unlike Western astrology, which uses the tropical zodiac, Hindu astrology uses the sidereal zodiac, which is anchored to the fixed stars and slowly diverges from the tropical system by the precession of the equinoxes (the difference, the ayanāṁśa, is roughly 24 degrees). The twelve signs (rāśis) bear Sanskrit names corresponding to the Western signs, from Meṣa (Aries) to Mīna (Pisces). Nine grahas ("seizers") are recognised: the Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn, and the two lunar nodes, Rāhu and Ketu, which have no physical body but govern eclipses and carry great astrological weight.

A distinctive feature is the system of 27 nakṣatras, or lunar mansions, each spanning 13°20′ of the ecliptic. The nakṣatra occupied by the Moon at birth determines, among other things, the starting point of the Vimśottarī daśā, a uniquely Jyotiṣa system of planetary periods totalling 120 years that times the unfolding of karmic results in a native's life. Charts are drawn in characteristic north-Indian (diamond) or south-Indian (fixed-sign grid) formats, and divisional charts (vargas) subdivide signs for detailed analysis of specific life areas.

History

The oldest stratum, the Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa attributed to Lagadha, is a calendrical-astronomical treatise concerned with timing sacrifices. From roughly the early centuries CE, Hellenistic astrology reached India through Greco-Indian contact—the Sanskrit Yavanajātaka ("Greek horoscopy") is a key witness—and Indian astrologers fused Greek horoscopic techniques with indigenous nakṣatra lore. Varāhamihira (6th century CE) summarised the tradition in works such as the Bṛhat Saṁhitā and Bṛhaj Jātaka. The system most practised today is called Parāśarī, after the Bṛhat Parāśara Horā Śāstra attributed to the sage Parāśara; a second major system derives from the sage Jaimini. Jyotiṣa continues to be culturally significant across South Asia, and is taught in some Indian universities, though its predictive claims are regarded as pseudoscientific by the scientific community.
