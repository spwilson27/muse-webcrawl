Battle of Passchendaele

Date31 July – 10 November 1917
LocationPasschendaele, Ypres Salient, Belgium
ResultAllied victory (limited territorial gains)
BelligerentsBritish Empire, France, Belgium vs. German Empire
CommandersDouglas Haig; Hubert Gough; Herbert Plumer; Arthur Currie — Erich Ludendorff; Friedrich Sixt von Armin

The Battle of Passchendaele, also known as the Third Battle of Ypres, was a campaign of the First World War, fought by the Allies against the German Empire. The battle took place on the Western Front, from July to November 1917, for control of the ridges south and east of the Belgian city of Ypres in West Flanders, as part of a strategy decided by the Allies at conferences in November 1916 and May 1917.

Passchendaele lay on the last ridge east of Ypres, 5 mi (8.0 km) from a railway junction at Roeselare, which was an important supply route for the German 4th Army. Having probed the German defences in June 1917 with an attack at Messines, the British commander Sir Douglas Haig launched the main offensive on 31 July 1917.

Course of the battle

The opening attack made modest gains, but in August the heaviest rains in decades turned the battlefield — low-lying reclaimed land whose drainage had been destroyed by millions of artillery shells — into a sea of mud in which men and horses drowned and movement became almost impossible. General Plumer's Second Army took over the main effort in September, using "bite and hold" tactics that won a series of limited victories at Menin Road, Polygon Wood and Broodseinde. In October the weather and German resistance slowed the advance again; the Canadian Corps under Arthur Currie finally captured the ruined village of Passchendaele on 6–10 November, ending the offensive.

Casualties and legacy

Casualty figures remain disputed; estimates commonly place British and Commonwealth losses around 240,000–275,000 and German losses in a similar range of roughly 200,000 or more, for an advance of about 5 miles (8 km). Passchendaele became synonymous with the futility and horror of trench warfare and remains a central case in debates over Haig's generalship, though some historians argue the battle also wore down the German army and achieved its broader strategic purpose of keeping pressure on Germany while its allies faltered.
