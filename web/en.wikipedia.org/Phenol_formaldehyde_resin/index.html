Phenol formaldehyde resin

TypeThermosetting polymer
InventorLeo Baekeland (1907)
Trade nameBakelite
ClassesNovolacs, resoles

Phenol formaldehyde resins (PF) are synthetic polymers obtained by the reaction of phenol or substituted phenol with formaldehyde. They were the first wholly synthetic plastics: Leo Baekeland's Bakelite, patented in 1907 and marketed from 1909, launched the modern plastics industry. Phenolic resins remain in wide use as adhesives, moulding compounds, coatings and laminates because of their heat resistance, dimensional stability and low cost.

Two classes are distinguished. Novolacs are made with an excess of phenol under acid catalysis; they are thermoplastic until cured with a crosslinking agent such as hexamethylenetetramine. Resoles are made with excess formaldehyde under alkaline conditions and crosslink on heating. Curing converts the fusible resin into an infusible, insoluble three-dimensional network.

Applications

Phenolic resins bind plywood and oriented strand board, impregnate the paper and fabric of electrical laminates, form brake linings and abrasive wheels, and provide heat-resistant mouldings for appliance handles and automotive parts. Phenolic foam is used for insulation, and carbonised phenolic ablators protected early spacecraft heat shields. Their dark colour limits use in decorative goods.
