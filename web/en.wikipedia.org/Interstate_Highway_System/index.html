Interstate Highway System

Dwight D. Eisenhower National System of Interstate and Defense Highways
FormedJune 29, 1956
LengthAbout 46,876 miles (75,440 km)
Maintained byState departments of transportation

The Interstate Highway System — formally the Dwight D. Eisenhower National System of Interstate and Defense Highways — is a network of controlled-access highways that forms part of the National Highway System in the United States. Authorized by the Federal-Aid Highway Act of 1956, it is one of the largest public works projects in history.

History

President Dwight D. Eisenhower was influenced by his experiences with the 1919 Army transcontinental motor convoy and by the Autobahn network he saw in Germany during the Second World War. The 1956 act committed the federal government to pay 90% of construction costs from a new Highway Trust Fund financed by fuel taxes. The system was officially declared complete in 1992, although individual segments, such as the Glenwood Canyon section of I-70 in Colorado, finished later.

Standards and numbering

Interstate highways have controlled access, divided carriageways, minimum lane widths, design speeds of 50–70 mph, and grade-separated interchanges. Primary routes have one- or two-digit numbers: even numbers run generally east–west (increasing northward), odd numbers north–south (increasing eastward), and low numbers lie in the south and west. Three-digit numbers denote auxiliary routes around or into cities.

Impact

The system transformed American commerce, suburbanization and culture, while also drawing criticism for displacing urban neighbourhoods, encouraging car dependence, and accelerating the decline of rail travel. Although it covers little more than 1% of U.S. road miles, it carries roughly a quarter of all vehicle traffic.
