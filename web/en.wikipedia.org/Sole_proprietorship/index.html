Sole proprietorship

A sole proprietorship, also known as a sole tradership, individual entrepreneurship or proprietorship, is a type of enterprise owned and run by one person in which there is no legal distinction between the owner and the business entity. The owner receives all profits (subject to taxation) and bears unlimited liability for all losses and debts.

Characteristics

A sole proprietorship is the simplest and most common form of business organization. Because the business is not a separate legal person, it does not produce a separate business tax return in jurisdictions such as the United States; income and losses are reported on the owner's personal tax return. The owner is personally liable for the business's obligations, meaning personal assets may be used to satisfy business debts.

Formation

Starting a sole proprietorship generally requires no formal incorporation. The owner may still need local business licenses, permits, and — if trading under a name other than their own legal name — a fictitious name ("doing business as," DBA) registration. In the United Kingdom, a sole trader registers with HM Revenue and Customs for self-assessment.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include ease and low cost of formation, complete managerial control, and simple taxation. Disadvantages include unlimited personal liability, difficulty raising capital (the owner cannot sell shares), and the fact that the business's continuity depends on the owner. As businesses grow, owners often convert to structures such as a limited liability company (LLC) or corporation.

Examples and prevalence

Freelancers, independent contractors, market traders, small retailers, and many tradespeople operate as sole proprietors. In the United States, sole proprietorships constitute the large majority of all businesses by number, though they account for a smaller share of total revenue than corporations.
