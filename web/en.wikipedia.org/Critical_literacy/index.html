Critical literacy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Critical literacy is an educational approach in which readers actively analyze texts to understand how they construct meaning, whose voices and perspectives they privilege or marginalize, and how they relate to power. Rather than treating a text as a neutral container of information, critical literacy encourages readers to question the author's purposes, the social context of production, and the ideologies embedded in language.

Origins

The approach draws heavily on the work of Brazilian educator Paulo Freire, whose Pedagogy of the Oppressed (1968) framed literacy as a means of liberation and critical consciousness (conscientização). It also incorporates insights from critical theory, poststructuralism, and discourse analysis, and was developed in literacy education by scholars such as Allan Luke, Peter Freebody, Barbara Comber, and Hilary Janks.

Classroom practice

Critical literacy teaching typically involves comparing multiple texts on the same topic, rewriting texts from alternative perspectives, examining how images and layout shape meaning, and producing counter-texts. It is applied not only to print but also to media, advertising, digital texts, and everyday documents. Supporters argue it equips students for democratic participation; critics sometimes contend that it can introduce political bias into reading instruction or crowd out basic skills.
