God is dead

"God is dead" (German: Gott ist tot) is a widely quoted statement by the German philosopher Friedrich Nietzsche. It first appears in his book The Gay Science (1882) and recurs in Thus Spoke Zarathustra. The phrase is not meant literally as a claim about a deity's physical death, but as a diagnosis of Western culture: the Christian God, and with Him the absolute values He guaranteed, has lost His power to structure belief and moral life in the wake of the Enlightenment and the rise of modern science.

The madman parable

In section 125 of The Gay Science, Nietzsche tells of a madman who lights a lantern in the morning and runs into the marketplace crying "I seek God!":

"Whither is God?" he cried; "I will tell you. We have killed him — you and I. All of us are his murderers. ... God is dead. God remains dead. And we have killed him."

The bystanders, who do not believe in God, laugh at the madman, who concludes that he has come too early: the event has happened, but humanity has not yet grasped its consequences.

Interpretation

For Nietzsche, the death of God threatens nihilism: if the highest values lose their foundation, life risks losing all meaning. His response was a call to revalue values and to affirm life without appeal to a transcendent guarantor, embodied in figures such as the Übermensch. In the 1960s the phrase became associated with the radical "death of God theology" of thinkers such as Thomas J. J. Altizer, and it reached a wide public when Time magazine put "Is God Dead?" on its cover in April 1966. The idea had precursors in Hegel and in earlier German literature, and it remains one of the most debated slogans in modern philosophy and theology.
