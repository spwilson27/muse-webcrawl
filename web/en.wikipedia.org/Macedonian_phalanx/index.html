The Macedonian phalanx (Greek: Μακεδονικὴ φάλαγξ) was an infantry formation developed by Philip II of Macedon and used by his son Alexander the Great to conquer the Achaemenid Persian Empire. It was built around the pezhetairoi ("foot companions"), professional heavy infantry armed with the sarissa, a pike far longer than the spears of Greek hoplites, deployed in dense blocks typically sixteen ranks deep.

The phalanx was one component of a combined-arms system: light troops screened it, the elite hypaspists linked it to the Companion cavalry, and the cavalry delivered the decisive blow while the phalanx pinned the enemy front. In Hellenistic armies after Alexander, the phalanx grew larger and heavier while the supporting arms declined, leaving it increasingly vulnerable.

Equipment and organisation

The sarissa was a two-handed pike of cornel wood, originally about 4 m and later extended to 5.5–6.5 m (the length is debated), with an iron leaf-shaped head, a butt spike for counterweight and for planting in the ground against cavalry, and a bronze coupling sleeve joining its two halves. Because both hands held the pike, the phalangite carried a small shield (~60 cm) slung from the neck and shoulder, a short sword (xiphos or kopis) as a secondary weapon, and lighter armour than a hoplite — a linen or leather cuirass for most ranks, bronze for officers and front-rankers.

The file (lochos) of 16 men was led by a dekadarch with the most experienced soldier closing the rear; 16 files formed a syntagma of 256 men, the smallest tactical unit manoeuvring independently, and syntagmata combined into taxeis of about 1,500 men under regionally recruited brigades. In battle the first five ranks levelled their pikes, presenting five points before each enemy soldier, while rear ranks held theirs upright or sloped to deflect missiles.

Tactics and limitations

Drilled to march and wheel in formation, to open and close order, and to form the defensive synaspismos (locked shields), the phalanx advanced as a near-irresistible steamroller on flat ground — provided its flanks held. Its weaknesses were structural: it needed level terrain, and broken ground or a successful flank attack could disorder the files, inside which the long sarissa became useless; individual phalangites were poorly equipped for hand-to-hand fighting. Alexander mitigated these flaws with aggressive cavalry and flexible light troops.

Decline

Successor kingdoms fielded ever-larger phalanxes (up to 30,000+ men) but with less training and weaker combined arms. Roman commanders learned to exploit terrain and gaps: at Cynoscephalae (197 BC) a Roman tribune's improvised flank attack routed the Macedonian phalanx, and at Pydna (168 BC) the legion's flexibility on uneven ground destroyed the army of Perseus, ending the Antigonid kingdom. Polybius's comparison of phalanx and legion (Histories 18.28–32) remains the classic ancient analysis of the two systems.
