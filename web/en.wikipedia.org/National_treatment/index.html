National treatment

National treatment is a principle in international law, especially international trade and intellectual property law, under which a state must treat foreigners and foreign goods, services, and rights holders no less favourably than its own nationals in like circumstances.

In trade law

Article III of the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT 1947), carried into the WTO agreements, prohibits internal taxes and regulations that discriminate against imported products once they have entered the market. Similar obligations appear in the GATS (services, subject to scheduled commitments) and in bilateral investment treaties, where national treatment protects foreign investors alongside the most-favoured-nation standard.

In intellectual property

The Paris Convention (1883) and Berne Convention (1886), and later TRIPS, require member states to grant nationals of other members the same protection of patents, trademarks, and copyrights as their own nationals, subject to limited exceptions.

Exceptions and disputes

General exceptions (GATT Article XX) permit measures for health, public morals, and conservation if not applied discriminatorily. Famous disputes include the EC–Asbestos and US–Clove Cigarettes cases on whether products are "like" and whether treatment is "less favourable".
