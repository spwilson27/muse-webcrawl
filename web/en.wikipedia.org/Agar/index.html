Agar

Agar (pronounced /ˈeɪɡɑːr/ or /ˈɑːɡər/), or agar-agar, is a jelly-like substance consisting of polysaccharides obtained from the cell walls of some species of red algae, primarily from the genera Gelidium and Gracilaria. It is a mixture of two components: the linear polysaccharide agarose and a heterogeneous mixture of smaller molecules called agaropectin.

Uses

Agar has been used since the 17th century in Japan, where it is known as kanten. Its principal modern use is as a solid growth medium in microbiology: agar plates provide a stable, largely indigestible surface on which bacteria and fungi can be cultured. Agar melts at about 85 °C and solidifies at 32–40 °C, a hysteresis that makes it convenient for laboratory work.

In food, agar serves as a vegetarian gelling agent in desserts, jellies, and confectionery, and as a thickener in soups and ice cream. It is also used in dentistry, electrochemistry, and as a laxative.

History

Agar was reportedly discovered in Japan around 1658, when an innkeeper discarded leftover seaweed soup that gelled overnight. Its microbiological use was popularised in the 1880s through the laboratory of Robert Koch, where Fanny Hesse suggested agar as a superior alternative to gelatin, which melted at incubation temperatures and was digested by many microbes.
