Decimation (signal processing)

In digital signal processing, decimation is the process of reducing the sampling rate of a signal. It normally consists of two steps: low-pass filtering to limit the signal's bandwidth, followed by downsampling, which keeps only every M-th sample. The integer M is the decimation factor. Decimation is used in multirate systems to reduce data rates and computational load while preserving the information in the band of interest.

Aliasing and filtering

By the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem, reducing the sample rate folds any frequency components above the new Nyquist frequency back into the band, a distortion called aliasing. An anti-aliasing filter is therefore applied before downsampling, removing energy above half the output sampling rate. Practical implementations often use polyphase filter structures that compute only the samples that will be retained.

Applications

Decimation is ubiquitous: digital audio converters decimate heavily oversampled sigma-delta modulator outputs to standard rates such as 48 kHz; software-defined radios decimate wideband digitized spectra to isolate a channel; and image processing uses equivalent operations when reducing image resolution. The complementary operation of increasing the sample rate is called interpolation or upsampling.
