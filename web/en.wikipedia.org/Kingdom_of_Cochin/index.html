Kingdom of Cochin

The Kingdom of Cochin (Malayalam: കൊച്ചി രാജ്യം), named after its capital and port city of Cochin (Kochi), was a late-medieval Hindu kingdom on the Malabar Coast of southwestern India, later a princely state of British India. Ruled by the Cochin royal family (the Perumpadappu Swarupam), it was one of the principal pepper ports of the Indian Ocean and the first point of sustained European contact with India after 1500.

Early history

Cochin emerged after the disintegration of the Later Chera (Kulasekhara) empire in the 12th century, growing rich on the spice trade through its harbour — created, tradition says, by the great flood of 1341 that silted up Kodungallur. The rajas styled themselves heirs of the Cheras and protected famous trading communities, including the Jews of Cochin (recipients of the Jewish copper plates) and the St Thomas Christians.

European era

Pedro Álvares Cabral reached Cochin in 1500 and Vasco da Gama died there in 1524. The Portuguese, welcomed as allies against the Zamorin of Calicut, built Fort Emmanuel (1503) — the first European fort in India. The Dutch captured Cochin in 1663, reducing it to a protectorate. The kingdom resisted Travancore's expansion under Sakthan Thampuran's able administration, accepted British subsidiary alliance in 1791, and became a princely state known for progressive governance: it pioneered railways, cochin port development and education in Kerala, and held a Legislative Council from 1925.

Integration

Maharaja Kerala Varma acceded to India in 1947; in 1949 Cochin merged with Travancore to form the state of Travancore-Cochin, and in 1956 its Malayalam districts joined the new state of Kerala. The royal family retains a ceremonial role, and Thrippunithura remains its seat.
