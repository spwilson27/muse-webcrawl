SN1 reaction

The SN1 reaction (also written as SN1, with the "S" standing for substitution, "N" for nucleophilic, and "1" for unimolecular) is a substitution reaction in organic chemistry. It is a type of nucleophilic substitution in which the rate-determining step does not involve the nucleophile. The reaction commonly involves an alkyl halide reacting under strongly acidic or strongly basic conditions, proceeding through a planar carbocation intermediate.

The two-step mechanism of the SN1 reaction.

Mechanism

The SN1 reaction proceeds in two steps. In the first, rate-determining step, the carbon–leaving group bond breaks heterolytically and the leaving group departs, forming a carbocation. In the second step, the nucleophile attacks the planar carbocation from either face, which means that if the reaction centre is a stereocentre, the product is formed as a racemic mixture. The rate law is first order overall: rate = k[RX], depending only on the concentration of the substrate.

Reactivity

Because the rate-determining step generates a carbocation, the reaction is favoured for tertiary substrates, whose carbocations are stabilized by hyperconjugation and inductive effects, and disfavoured for primary and methyl substrates. Polar protic solvents accelerate the reaction by stabilizing the ionic intermediates, and good leaving groups such as iodide and bromide react fastest. Competing E1 elimination is common, particularly on heating.

Comparison with SN2

In contrast to the bimolecular SN2 reaction, which proceeds in a single concerted step with inversion of configuration, the SN1 pathway requires formation of a carbocation and is subject to rearrangement when a hydride or alkyl shift can produce a more stable cation.
