Amylopectin

Amylopectin is a water-insoluble polysaccharide and a highly branched polymer of α-glucose units found in plants. It is one of the two components of starch, the other being amylose, and typically makes up about 70–80% of starch by weight. Its animal counterpart is glycogen, which has a similar composition but is even more extensively branched.

Structure

Amylopectin consists of chains of glucose units joined by α(1→4) glycosidic bonds, with branch points formed by α(1→6) bonds occurring roughly every 24 to 30 glucose units. A single molecule may contain two thousand to two hundred thousand glucose units, making amylopectin one of the largest molecules in nature. The clustered branching pattern gives starch granules their semi-crystalline structure and influences how they gelatinise in cooking.

Digestion and uses

Human digestive enzymes (amylases) hydrolyse the α(1→4) links, while debranching enzymes handle the α(1→6) branch points. Starches rich in amylopectin, such as waxy maize starch, form clear, cohesive pastes and are used as thickeners and stabilisers in food processing, paper manufacture and textiles.
