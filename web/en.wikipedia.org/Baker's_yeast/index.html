Baker's yeast

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
SpeciesSaccharomyces cerevisiae
UsesBread leavening, fermentation
FormsFresh (compressed), active dry, instant

Baker's yeast is the common name for strains of yeast commonly used in baking bread and other bakery products, where it acts as a leavening agent causing the dough to rise by converting fermentable sugars into carbon dioxide and ethanol. The species almost always used is Saccharomyces cerevisiae.

History

The use of yeast in baking dates to ancient Egypt. In the 19th century bakers obtained yeast from brewers; dedicated yeast production followed, with Charles Fleischmann patenting a compressed yeast process in the 1860s and active dry yeast developed during the Second World War.

Types

Baker's yeast is sold as fresh compressed (cake) yeast, cream yeast for industrial use, active dry yeast, and instant yeast, which can be mixed directly with flour. Instant and active dry forms differ in granule size and rehydration requirements.

Use in baking

Typical usage is around 1–2% of flour weight for fresh yeast. Activity is retarded by cold, high salt, and high sugar or fat concentrations, which is why enriched doughs use osmotolerant strains.
