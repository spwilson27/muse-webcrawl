Protein–energy malnutrition (PEM), also called protein–energy undernutrition, is a form of malnutrition caused by an inadequate intake of protein and/or dietary energy (calories). It ranges from mild undernutrition that retards children's growth to life-threatening severe acute malnutrition. It is most prevalent in low-income countries among children under five, and undernutrition is estimated to underlie nearly half of all deaths in that age group worldwide.

Forms

The three clinical syndromes of severe PEM are traditionally distinguished as: kwashiorkor, dominated by protein deficiency, characterised by oedema (swelling), an enlarged fatty liver, skin and hair changes and apathy; marasmus, dominated by overall energy deficiency, characterised by severe wasting ("skin and bones"), loss of muscle and fat, and no oedema; and marasmic kwashiorkor, combining wasting with oedema. Chronic, less severe undernutrition produces stunting — impaired growth and development from poor nutrition, repeated infection and inadequate psychosocial stimulation.

Causes and effects

PEM arises where food insecurity, poverty, poor weaning practices and infectious diseases (diarrhoea, measles, malaria, HIV) reinforce one another: malnutrition weakens immunity, and infection increases nutrient losses. Consequences include impaired cognitive development, reduced adult earning capacity and, in pregnancy, low birth weight in the next generation.

Management

Severe acute malnutrition is treated following WHO protocols: stabilisation with cautious therapeutic milks (F-75), antibiotics and correction of electrolytes and micronutrients, followed by rehabilitation with energy-dense feeds (F-100) or ready-to-use therapeutic food such as Plumpy'Nut, which enables community-based treatment. Refeeding must be gradual to avoid refeeding syndrome. Prevention rests on food security, breastfeeding promotion, sanitation and treatment of childhood infections.
