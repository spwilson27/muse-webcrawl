Interferometric synthetic-aperture radar

Interferometric synthetic aperture radar, abbreviated InSAR (or IfSAR), is a radar technique used in geodesy and remote sensing. This geodetic method uses two or more synthetic aperture radar (SAR) images to generate maps of surface deformation or digital elevation, using differences in the phase of the waves returning to the sensor. The technique can potentially measure centimetre-scale changes in deformation over time spans of days to years.

Principle

A SAR instrument transmits microwave pulses and records the amplitude and phase of echoes. When the same area is imaged from slightly different positions (a "baseline") or at different times, the phase difference between the two images — an interferogram — contains information about ground range to sub-wavelength precision. Topographic phase can be removed using a reference digital elevation model, leaving differential interferograms (DInSAR) that reveal surface motion along the satellite line of sight. Persistent scatterer and SBAS (small baseline subset) time-series methods track slow deformation over many images.

Applications

  • Measuring co-seismic and post-seismic ground deformation from earthquakes
  • Monitoring volcanic inflation and deflation
  • Land subsidence from groundwater extraction, mining and tunnelling
  • Glacier and ice-sheet velocity mapping
  • Creating digital elevation models — the Shuttle Radar Topography Mission (2000) used single-pass interferometry to map most of Earth's land surface

Satellites

Key InSAR data sources include ESA's ERS-1/2, Envisat and Sentinel-1 missions, Canada's RADARSAT series, Germany's TerraSAR-X/TanDEM-X, Italy's COSMO-SkyMed and Japan's ALOS PALSAR series, as well as NASA's forthcoming NISAR mission (jointly with ISRO). Limitations include decorrelation over vegetation and snow, atmospheric phase delays, and sensitivity only to the line-of-sight component of motion.
