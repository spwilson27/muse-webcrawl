Character theory

In mathematics, more specifically in group theory, character theory is the study of characters: functions defined on a group that arise as the trace of a representation of the group by matrices. Characters distil much of the information contained in a representation into a class function (constant on conjugacy classes), and the characters of the irreducible representations of a finite group satisfy powerful orthogonality relations that make them an exceptionally effective tool for investigating group structure.

Definition

If ρ : G → GL(V) is a finite-dimensional representation of a group G, its character is the function χρ(g) = Tr(ρ(g)), the trace of the matrix representing g. Because the trace is invariant under conjugation, χ is a class function. The character of the trivial representation is the constant function 1; the degree of a character is its value at the identity, equal to the dimension of V.

Orthogonality relations

For a finite group G, the irreducible characters form an orthonormal set under the inner product

⟨χ, ψ⟩ = (1/|G|) Σg∈G χ(g) ψ(g⁻¹)

as a consequence of Schur's lemma and the great orthogonality theorem. It follows that a representation is determined up to isomorphism by its character, that the number of irreducible characters equals the number of conjugacy classes, and that the sum of the squares of the irreducible degrees equals |G|.

Character tables

The character table of a finite group is the square array whose rows are the irreducible characters and whose columns are the conjugacy classes. Column orthogonality, integrality properties of character values (they are algebraic integers), and degree divisibility theorems (Frobenius) let one deduce structural facts — such as the existence of normal subgroups and the simplicity or solvability of the group — directly from the table.

Applications and history

Ferdinand Georg Frobenius founded character theory in 1896, initially by purely group-theoretic means before adopting Dedekind's matrix viewpoint; Issai Schur systematised the theory and William Burnside applied it spectacularly, proving the paqb theorem that groups of order divisible by only two primes are solvable. Character-theoretic methods remain central to the classification of finite simple groups and to modular representation theory, where Brauer characters play the analogous role in positive characteristic.
