American System (economic plan)

Henry Clay (1777–1852), the plan's leading champion in Congress.

The American System was an economic plan championed in the early 19th century by Kentucky congressman and later Secretary of State Henry Clay. It was one of the most historically significant examples of a government-sponsored program to harmonize and balance the nation's agriculture, commerce, and industry, and it rested on three mutually reinforcing elements:

  • A protective tariff to shield American manufacturers from foreign (especially British) competition;
  • A national bank to foster commerce and provide a stable national currency; and
  • Federal subsidies for internal improvements — roads, canals, and other infrastructure — to develop profitable markets for agriculture.

History

Clay first used the term "American System" in 1824, contrasting it with what he called the "British System" of free trade and limited government. The idea drew on the earlier economic thought of Alexander Hamilton, whose Report on Manufactures (1791) had argued for federal support of infant industries. After the War of 1812 exposed the dangers of dependence on British manufactured goods, support for economic nationalism grew.

The program was partially implemented: the Second Bank of the United States was chartered in 1816, protective tariffs were enacted in 1816, 1824, and 1828, and the federal government funded projects such as the National Road. However, internal improvements were repeatedly frustrated by presidential vetoes — James Madison vetoed the Bonus Bill of 1817, and Andrew Jackson vetoed the Maysville Road bill in 1830 — and Jackson's destruction of the Second Bank in the 1830s dismantled a central pillar of the system.

Influence

The American System was the dominant economic program of the National Republican and later Whig parties. Its ideas influenced later Republican economic policy, including Abraham Lincoln's support for tariffs, a national banking system, and federally aided railroad construction during the Civil War era. Historians regard it as an early American example of developmental economic policy, and the term is sometimes invoked in modern debates over industrial policy.
