Underground mining (hard rock)

Underground hard-rock mining refers to the extraction of minerals and metals from ore bodies beneath the surface where the host rock is hard, typically igneous or metamorphic rock, as opposed to soft-rock mining of coal and evaporites. It is used for gold, copper, nickel, zinc, lead, uranium, platinum-group metals, and diamonds, among other commodities.

Access is gained through vertical shafts, declines (ramps) for rubber-tyred equipment, and adits. Development headings (drifts, crosscuts, and raises) reach the ore body, which is mined in excavations called stopes. Principal methods include cut-and-fill stoping, sublevel open stoping, room-and-pillar, shrinkage stoping, and block caving, chosen according to the ore body's geometry, grade, and ground conditions. Broken ore is hauled by loaders (LHDs) and trucks or skipped to the surface.

Hard-rock underground mines require extensive ground support (rock bolts, shotcrete), ventilation to remove diesel particulates, heat, and blasting fumes, and dewatering. The world's deepest mines, such as South Africa's Mponeng gold mine at nearly 4 km deep, are hard-rock underground mines, where rock temperatures require industrial refrigeration.
