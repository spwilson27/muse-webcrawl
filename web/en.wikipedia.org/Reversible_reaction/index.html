Reversible reaction

A reversible reaction is a chemical reaction in which the reactants form products that, in turn, react together to give the reactants back. Such reactions are written with a double arrow (⇌) and, in a closed system, proceed to a state of chemical equilibrium in which forward and reverse reactions occur at equal rates and the concentrations of reactants and products remain constant.

Examples

Classic examples include the synthesis of ammonia in the Haber process (N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3), the dissociation of dinitrogen tetroxide (N2O4 ⇌ 2NO2), and weak acid dissociation in water. Le Chatelier's principle predicts how an equilibrium system responds when conditions such as temperature, pressure, or concentration are changed.

Irreversibility

In practice many reactions are effectively irreversible because a product escapes as a gas, precipitates, or is consumed in a subsequent step. Strictly, all reactions are microscopically reversible, a statement known as the principle of microscopic reversibility.
