Collective behavior

Collective behavior is a term used in sociology to describe the processes and events that emerge in relatively spontaneous, unstructured and short-lived group behaviour, outside of established social institutions and norms. It includes crowds, mobs, panics, riots, fads, crazes, rumours, mass hysteria, disasters and the early stages of social movements.

History of the field

Early work by Gustave Le Bon portrayed crowds as irrational and suggestible. Later theorists, such as Herbert Blumer, developed more analytical approaches: Blumer distinguished crowds, publics and masses, and described "circular reaction" and social contagion. Ralph Turner and Lewis Killian's emergent norm theory argued that new norms arise within crowds to govern behaviour. Neil Smelser's value-added theory identified conditions — structural conduciveness, strain, a generalised belief, precipitating factors, mobilisation and the operation of social control — under which collective episodes occur.

Types

Sociologists commonly distinguish casual, conventional, expressive and acting crowds; panics and mass hysteria; fads and fashions; rumour; and disaster behaviour, which research often finds to be more orderly and pro-social than popular stereotypes suggest. Collective behavior is usually contrasted with institutionalised group behaviour and studied as a stepping stone to the study of social movements.
