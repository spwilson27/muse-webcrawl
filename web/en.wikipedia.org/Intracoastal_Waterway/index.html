Intracoastal Waterway

The Intracoastal Waterway (ICW) is a 3,000-mile (4,800 km) inland waterway system along the Atlantic and Gulf of Mexico coasts of the United States, running from Norfolk, Virginia, around Florida, and west to Brownsville, Texas. It consists of natural inlets, rivers, bays and sounds connected by artificial canals, providing a sheltered, toll-free navigable route inside the barrier islands.

History

Plans for an inland coastal route date to the early republic, but construction began in earnest in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, with canals such as the Albemarle and Chesapeake Canal (1859) and the Cape Cod Canal (1914). The federal government progressively bought up private canals, and the Atlantic and Gulf sections were substantially complete by the 1940s. The waterway proved strategically important during the Second World War, when barges could move oil and cargo safely away from U-boats offshore.

Use

The ICW carries heavy commercial barge traffic — petroleum, chemicals, coal, grain and building materials, especially on the Gulf section — and is equally famous among recreational boaters. Thousands of cruisers travel its Atlantic section each spring and autumn in the seasonal migration nicknamed "snowbird" traffic, and it forms the eastern leg of the Great Loop cruising route. The authorised channel depth is nominally 12 feet, though shoaling in some stretches requires constant dredging by the Army Corps of Engineers.
