Edward Teller

Edward Teller (Hungarian: Teller Ede; 15 January 1908 – 9 September 2003) was a Hungarian-American theoretical physicist known colloquially as "the father of the hydrogen bomb" and one of the most controversial scientific figures of the Cold War. He worked on the Manhattan Project, championed the development of thermonuclear weapons, and helped found Lawrence Livermore National Laboratory.

Teller, theoretical physicist and advocate of the hydrogen bomb.

Early life

Born in Budapest, Teller studied chemical engineering and physics in Germany, working with Werner Heisenberg at Leipzig. Fleeing the Nazis in 1934, he emigrated to the United States and became a professor at George Washington University, where he collaborated with George Gamow.

Wartime and hydrogen bomb work

Teller joined the Manhattan Project at Los Alamos, working on the atomic bomb while pressing for a thermonuclear "Super". After the Soviet Union's first atomic test in 1949, President Truman ordered a crash programme; in 1951 Teller and Stanisław Ulam devised the radiation-implosion design that made a practical hydrogen bomb possible, first tested in the Ivy Mike shot of 1952.

Controversy and later career

Teller's testimony at the 1954 security hearing of J. Robert Oppenheimer alienated many colleagues. He remained a prominent adviser on defence policy, advocating missile defence under the Strategic Defense Initiative, and was associated with the Hoover Institution. Early in his career he had also made lasting contributions to molecular physics, including the Jahn–Teller effect and the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller theory of surface adsorption.
