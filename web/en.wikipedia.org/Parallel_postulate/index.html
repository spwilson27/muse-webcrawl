Parallel postulate

The parallel postulate is the fifth postulate in Euclid's Elements (c. 300 BC). In the form given by Proclus, it states: if a straight line falling on two straight lines makes the interior angles on the same side less than two right angles, the two straight lines, if produced indefinitely, meet on that side.

Playfair's axiom

An equivalent and more familiar formulation, named after John Playfair, states: through a point not on a given line there is exactly one line parallel to the given line. Other equivalents include the statement that the angles of a triangle sum to 180 degrees, the existence of rectangles, and the Pythagorean theorem.

Attempts at proof

For two thousand years mathematicians tried to prove the postulate from Euclid's other axioms, feeling it less self-evident than the rest. Medieval Islamic mathematicians such as Ibn al-Haytham and Omar Khayyam, and later Giovanni Girolamo Saccheri (1733), derived theorems from its negation without finding a contradiction.

Non-Euclidean geometry

In the 1820s–1830s, Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky, János Bolyai and (unpublished) Carl Friedrich Gauss showed that denying the postulate yields consistent geometries — hyperbolic geometry, in which many parallels pass through a point, and elliptic geometry, in which there are none. Bernhard Riemann generalised the framework in 1854. The independence of the postulate was thus established: it cannot be proved from the other axioms. Non-Euclidean geometry later became essential to Einstein's general theory of relativity.
