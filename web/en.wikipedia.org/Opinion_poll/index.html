An opinion poll, often simply called a poll, is a human research survey of public opinion from a particular sample. Opinion polls are usually designed to represent the opinions of a population by asking a sample of people a series of questions and then extrapolating the results in a broader population, with a stated margin of error.

History

Straw polls of voter intentions date to the 1820s in the United States. Scientific polling began in the 1930s: in 1936 George Gallup correctly predicted Franklin D. Roosevelt's victory using a small quota sample, while the Literary Digest, using millions of unrepresentative ballots, wrongly forecast a landslide for Alf Landon. Gallup's success established the modern polling industry, though pollsters famously failed in 1948 when they stopped interviewing early and predicted Thomas Dewey would defeat Harry Truman.

Methodology

Polls are conducted by telephone (landline and mobile), online panels, mail, and in-person interviews. Results depend on probability sampling, question wording and order, weighting responses to match population demographics, and modelling likely voters. Reported accuracy is typically expressed through a margin of error (for a sample of ~1,000, about ±3 percentage points at the 95% confidence level). Sources of error include non-response bias, social desirability bias, late shifts in opinion, and the "shy voter" effect.

Types and uses

Common types include benchmark polls, tracking polls, exit polls, and push polls (a form of campaigning disguised as research). Polls inform election coverage, government policy, and market research, though critics argue they can influence voters through bandwagon and underdog effects; some countries ban publishing polls shortly before elections.
