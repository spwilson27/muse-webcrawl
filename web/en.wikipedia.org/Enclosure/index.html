Enclosure

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Enclosure or inclosure is a term, used in English landownership, that refers to the appropriation of "waste" or "common land" enclosing it and by doing so depriving commoners of their rights of access and privilege. Agreements to enclose land could be either through a formal or informal process. The process could normally be accomplished in three ways. First there was the creation of "closes", taken out of larger common arable fields by estate owners. The second method involved enclosure by proprietors, owners who acted together, usually small farmers or squires, leading to the enclosure of whole parishes. The last method was by Acts of Parliament.

The primary reason for enclosure was to improve the efficiency of agriculture. However, there were other motives too, one example being that the value of the enclosed land would substantially rise. There were social consequences to the policy, including protests and revolts; the effects upon the poor were particularly severe.

History

Enclosure proceeded piecemeal from the later Middle Ages, accelerated during the Tudor period, when landowners enclosed common land for sheep pasture to supply the lucrative wool trade, and reached its height between about 1750 and 1850, when more than five thousand Inclosure Acts were passed by Parliament covering roughly a fifth of England. Parliamentary enclosure typically consolidated scattered strips in open fields into compact hedged or walled farms.

Social effects

Critics, both contemporary and modern, have argued that enclosure dispossessed many smallholders and cottagers who depended on common rights for grazing, fuel and gleaning, swelling the ranks of landless labourers and driving migration to industrial towns. Defenders argued that it raised productivity and was a precondition of the agricultural improvements feeding a growing population. The poet John Clare lamented the transformation of his native Northamptonshire landscape.
