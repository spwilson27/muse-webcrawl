Plessy v. Ferguson, 163 U.S. 537 (1896), was a landmark decision of the United States Supreme Court that upheld the constitutionality of racial segregation under the "separate but equal" doctrine. The case stemmed from an 1892 incident in which Homer Plessy, a mixed-race passenger who was seven-eighths white, refused to sit in a car for black people on the East Louisiana Railroad, as required by Louisiana's Separate Car Act of 1890. The case had been deliberately arranged by the Comité des Citoyens of New Orleans to test the law.

Decision

By a vote of 7–1, the Court rejected Plessy's argument that segregated railway cars violated the Thirteenth and Fourteenth Amendments. Justice Henry Billings Brown's majority opinion held that enforced separation did not stamp one race with a badge of inferiority, and that legislation could not abolish social prejudices or enforce social, as distinct from political, equality. The ruling provided constitutional cover for the Jim Crow laws that segregated schools, transport, housing and public facilities across the American South for more than half a century.

Harlan's dissent

Justice John Marshall Harlan wrote the lone dissent, declaring that "our Constitution is color-blind, and neither knows nor tolerates classes among citizens." He warned that the decision would prove as pernicious as Dred Scott and that it would embolden aggressive state legislation against black citizens. His dissent later became a foundational text of the civil rights movement.

Legacy

The "separate but equal" doctrine was eroded by a series of NAACP cases and repudiated in public education by Brown v. Board of Education (1954), where the Court held that separate educational facilities are inherently unequal. Plessy is now widely regarded as one of the worst decisions in Supreme Court history. In 2022, Louisiana Governor John Bel Edwards formally pardoned Homer Plessy under a state law enacted for that purpose.
