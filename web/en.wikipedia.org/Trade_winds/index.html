The trade winds are the prevailing pattern of easterly surface winds found in the tropics, within the lower portion of the Earth's atmosphere, in the lower section of the troposphere near the Earth's equator. They blow predominantly from the northeast in the Northern Hemisphere and from the southeast in the Southern Hemisphere.

Mechanism

Air heated at the equator rises in the Hadley cell and flows poleward aloft, descending in the subtropics around 30° latitude. The returning surface flow is deflected westward by the Coriolis effect, producing the steady easterlies. The two trade-wind systems converge near the equator in the Intertropical Convergence Zone (ITCZ), a belt of calms and thunderstorms historically known to sailors as the doldrums.

History and navigation

Sailing ships of the age of exploration depended on the trades for westward passages across the Atlantic and Pacific; the name comes from the Middle English "trade", meaning a regular course or track. Columbus's first voyage and the Manila galleon route both rode the trade winds.

Effects

The trades drive westward ocean currents, pile warm water into western ocean basins, and shape tropical climates — producing windward rainfall on island and coastal slopes and arid leeward zones. They also steer tropical cyclones westward.
