Assimilation (sociology)

FieldSociology, migration studies
Related conceptsAcculturation, integration, multiculturalism, melting pot

Cultural assimilation is the process by which a minority group or culture adopts the customs, language, values, and behaviours of a dominant or host society, coming to resemble it over time. It is most often discussed in relation to immigrant communities and indigenous peoples.

Dimensions

Sociologists commonly distinguish stages or dimensions of assimilation, including cultural or behavioural change (acculturation), structural assimilation (entry into the institutions and social networks of the host society), marital assimilation (intermarriage), and identificational assimilation (adopting the host society's identity). Milton Gordon's mid-20th-century framework proposed that structural assimilation was the key stage from which others followed.

Debates

Assimilation may occur naturally across generations or be compelled by state policy, as in historical forced-assimilation programmes directed at indigenous peoples and minority languages — policies now widely condemned. Critics contrast assimilation with multiculturalism and pluralism, in which groups retain distinct cultures within a shared framework, and with the "melting pot" metaphor sometimes used for the United States. Later scholarship on "segmented assimilation" notes that immigrant groups may integrate into different segments of the host society, with divergent outcomes.
