Fab region

The fragment antigen-binding (Fab) region is the portion of an antibody that binds to antigens. It is composed of one constant and one variable domain from each heavy and light chain of the antibody molecule. The variable domains at the tips of the two Fab arms contain the paratope — the antigen-binding site formed by the complementarity-determining regions (CDRs) — which gives each antibody its specificity for a particular epitope.

Structure

An antibody of the immunoglobulin G (IgG) class is roughly Y-shaped. Each arm of the Y is a Fab region, consisting of the light chain (one variable domain, VL, and one constant domain, CL) paired with the variable and first constant domains of the heavy chain (VH and CH1). The two arms are joined to the stem — the Fc region — by a flexible hinge. Proteolytic cleavage with the enzyme papain separates the two Fab fragments from the Fc fragment, a classic experiment that revealed the modular structure of antibodies.

Function and applications

Because binding specificity resides in the Fab, isolated Fab fragments are widely used in research and medicine. They retain the antigen-binding activity of the parent antibody but lack the effector functions mediated by the Fc region, such as complement activation and binding to Fc receptors. Therapeutic Fab fragments include abciximab, ranibizumab and certolizumab pegol (a PEGylated Fab). In structural biology, Fabs are commonly used to stabilise proteins for crystallography and cryo-electron microscopy.
