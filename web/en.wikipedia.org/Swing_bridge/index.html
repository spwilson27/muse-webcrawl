Swing bridge

Swing bridge
Type: Movable bridge
Carries: Road, rail, or pedestrian traffic
Span: Short to medium
Mechanism: Rotates horizontally on a pivot pier

A swing bridge (or swing span bridge) is a movable bridge that has its primary structural support on a vertical locating pin and support ring, usually at or near the center of the span, about which the turning span can swing horizontally. Unlike lift or bascule bridges, the swing span rotates in a horizontal plane to open a navigation channel for passing vessels.

Design

The movable span is balanced on a circular pier in the middle of the waterway. When closed, the bridge carries road or rail traffic across the channel; when a ship needs to pass, the span rotates roughly 90 degrees so that it lies parallel to the channel, opening passages on both sides of the pivot pier. The turning mechanism may be driven hydraulically or by electric motors, and rim-bearing and center-bearing designs are both common.

History

Swing bridges became widespread in the nineteenth century, particularly on railways crossing navigable rivers in North America and Europe. Famous examples include swing spans on the Mississippi River and in the Netherlands, where many canal bridges are of the swing type. Although largely superseded for major crossings by vertical-lift and bascule bridges, which require a smaller footprint, swing bridges remain in use where traffic is light.

Advantages and disadvantages

Swing bridges require no counterweights and can span longer openings than many other movable types, but the central pivot pier obstructs the waterway and the protected island around it occupies considerable space. They also open and close relatively slowly.
