Candide

Candide, ou l'Optimisme (Candide, or Optimism) is a French satirical novella by Voltaire, published in January 1759. It follows the young Candide, raised in a Westphalian castle under the philosopher Pangloss, who teaches that "all is for the best in the best of all possible worlds." Expelled from the castle, Candide endures a picaresque sequence of disasters — war, the Lisbon earthquake, the Inquisition, shipwreck, slavery — that steadily refutes Pangloss's optimism. The work concludes with the famous injunction that "we must cultivate our garden."

Publication

Voltaire published the book pseudonymously, attributing it to a "Docteur Ralph," and arranged near-simultaneous editions in Geneva, Paris, London, and Amsterdam. It was quickly banned in several places for blasphemy and sedition — which only increased its sales; it became the best-selling work of Voltaire's lifetime.

Plot summary

Candide is expelled from the castle of Thunder-ten-Tronckh for kissing the baron's daughter, Cunégonde. Press-ganged into the Bulgar army, he deserts, survives the 1755 Lisbon earthquake and an auto-da-fé, and reunites with Cunégonde in Lisbon. Pursued after killing two men, he flees to South America, finds the utopian land of Eldorado (which he leaves, laden with treasure, out of love), loses most of his fortune through swindlers, and eventually settles with his companions on a small farm near Constantinople, where work, not metaphysics, becomes their answer to suffering.

Themes

The novella satirizes Leibnizian optimism, organized religion, war, colonialism, and philosophical system-building. Its rapid, deadpan narration treats atrocity and absurdity alike, and its ending — practical labour over abstract theodicy — remains one of the most quoted conclusions in literature. The character Martin voices a pessimistic Manichaean counterweight to Pangloss.

Influence

Candide is the most widely read of Voltaire's works and a cornerstone of the Enlightenment. Leonard Bernstein's 1956 operetta Candide, with contributions from Lillian Hellman and Stephen Sondheim, adapted it for the stage; the term "Panglossian" entered English for naive optimism.
