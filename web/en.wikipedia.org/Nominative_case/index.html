Nominative case

In grammar, the nominative case (abbreviated NOM) is one of the grammatical cases of a noun or other part of speech, which generally marks the subject of a verb or the predicate noun or adjective, as opposed to its object or other verb arguments. Generally, the noun "that is doing" something is in the nominative, and the nominative is often the form listed in dictionaries.

Characteristics

The nominative case is the citation form of nouns in many inflected languages, including Latin, Greek, German, Russian, Icelandic and Sanskrit. In nominative–accusative languages, the nominative marks both the subject of an intransitive verb and the agent of a transitive verb, while the object of a transitive verb takes the accusative case. In ergative–absolutive languages, the corresponding citation and subject role is usually associated with the absolutive case instead.

Examples

In Latin, the sentence Puella nautam videt ("The girl sees the sailor") has puella in the nominative singular and nautam in the accusative. In English, full nouns do not distinguish nominative from accusative, but personal pronouns do: he, she, I, we and they are nominative forms, contrasting with him, her, me, us and them.

Predicate nominative

After a linking verb (copula), a noun or pronoun that renames the subject is also in the nominative in many languages; this is the predicate nominative, as in Latin Cicero est orator ("Cicero is an orator").
