Alcoholics Anonymous

Founded: 10 June 1935, Akron, Ohio
Founders: Bill Wilson (Bill W.) and Bob Smith (Dr. Bob)
Type: Mutual aid fellowship
Membership: approx. 2 million in 180 countries

Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) is an international mutual aid fellowship with the purpose of enabling its members to "stay sober and help other alcoholics achieve sobriety". It was founded in 1935 by Bill Wilson (known as Bill W.) and Robert Smith (known as Dr. Bob). It is the progenitor of the twelve-step program used by many other recovery organizations.

History

Wilson and Smith met in Akron, Ohio, in 1935 through the Oxford Group, a Christian fellowship. Finding that one alcoholic helping another sustained sobriety, they began working with alcoholics at Akron's City Hospital; the date of Dr. Bob's last drink, 10 June 1935, is regarded as AA's founding. Their program was set out in the book Alcoholics Anonymous (1939), nicknamed the "Big Book", which presented the Twelve Steps of recovery. Publicity in the early 1940s, including a 1941 Saturday Evening Post article, brought rapid growth.

Program and organization

AA's recovery program centres on the Twelve Steps — a set of spiritual practices including admitting powerlessness over alcohol, making amends, and carrying the message to other alcoholics. Members typically attend meetings, work with a sponsor, and practise anonymity, which AA considers its spiritual foundation. The fellowship has no dues or fees, is self-supporting, and is organized loosely through autonomous local groups guided by the Twelve Traditions. AA states that its only membership requirement is a desire to stop drinking.

Influence

AA's model spawned numerous fellowships, including Narcotics Anonymous and Al-Anon (for families), and twelve-step facilitation remains a common component of addiction treatment, though its effectiveness and spiritual emphasis continue to be studied and debated.
