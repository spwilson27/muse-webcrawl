A terrace in agriculture and earthworks is a piece of sloped plane that has been cut into a series of successively receding flat surfaces or platforms — resembling steps — in order to farm sloping land more effectively. Terracing slows water runoff, reduces soil erosion, conserves moisture, and creates level planting beds on hillsides that would otherwise be uncultivable.

Types and history

Bench terraces are formed by cutting and filling to make level platforms separated by steep risers, often retained with stone walls. Contour terraces follow the natural contours of the slope and are used at larger scales for soil conservation, while flooded paddy terraces are carefully levelled and bunded to impound water for wet rice.

Terracing is ancient and arose independently in many mountainous regions. The Inca and their predecessors built elaborate stone terraces (andenes) in the Andes; the Ifugao rice terraces of the Philippine Cordilleras, including Banaue, were built over centuries and are a UNESCO World Heritage Site, as are the Hani terraces of Yuanyang in Yunnan. Terracing also shaped landscapes in Yemen, the Mediterranean basin (for vines and olives), Japan, and prehistoric Britain, where "strip lynchets" mark old hillside fields.

Modern use

Modern soil-conservation practice employs engineered terraces — broad-base, grassed waterway, and parallel terraces — on farmland worldwide, and terracing remains standard in landscape architecture for gardens, vineyards, and erosion-prone construction sites. Abandonment of maintained terraces can, however, accelerate erosion and landslides, since the structures concentrate water when drainage fails.
