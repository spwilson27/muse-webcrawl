Brent's method

In numerical analysis, Brent's method is a root-finding algorithm that combines the robustness of the bisection method with the speed of the secant method and inverse quadratic interpolation. It was developed by Australian mathematician Richard Peirson Brent in 1973, building on an earlier algorithm by Dutch mathematician Theodorus Dekker (1969), and is therefore sometimes called the Brent–Dekker method. Brent's method is the standard bracketing root-finder in many numerical libraries and is the basis of the widely used fzero routine in MATLAB and of implementations in SciPy, the GNU Scientific Library and Numerical Recipes.

How it works

Like bisection, Brent's method maintains an interval [a, b] that brackets a root — the function values at the endpoints have opposite signs. At each step it proposes a new point by inverse quadratic interpolation through the three most recent points (or the secant method if only two distinct points are available). The proposal is accepted only if it lies safely inside the bracket and is making adequate progress; otherwise the algorithm falls back to a bisection step. Acceptance tests involving the size of the previous step — the heart of Brent's refinement of Dekker's method — guarantee convergence not much slower than bisection in the worst case, while retaining fast, often superlinear, convergence on smooth functions.

Comparison with other methods

Newton's method converges quadratically but requires derivatives and can diverge without a good starting guess; the secant method avoids derivatives but can also leave the bracket. Brent's method needs no derivatives, never loses the bracket, and typically needs only a handful more evaluations than the secant method. These properties made it the default general-purpose scalar root-finder: Numerical Recipes calls it the method of choice for functions that can be bracketed. Related later algorithms, such as Chandrupatla's method (1997) and the ITP method (2020), aim to improve worst-case guarantees further.

The zbrent implementation

The best-known implementation, zbrent from Numerical Recipes, encodes the algorithm in a few dozen lines and has been ported to most scientific languages; Brent's original ALGOL 60 and Fortran versions appear in his 1973 book Algorithms for Minimization Without Derivatives, which treats the companion problem of minimising a function of one variable by a related method also named after Brent.
