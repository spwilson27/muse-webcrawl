Low-ball

The low-ball technique is a persuasion and compliance method in which an item or service is offered at a lower price than is actually intended to be charged, after which the price is raised. Because a person has already committed to the item, and feels a connection to it, the person tends to accept the higher price; it is related to the foot-in-the-door technique and to commitment and consistency effects in social psychology.

Experimental evidence

The technique was first described experimentally by Robert Cialdini and colleagues in 1978. In their studies, university students who first agreed to participate in an experiment were much more likely to still take part after being told it would begin at 7 a.m. — an unattractive condition revealed only after commitment — than students told about the early start before deciding. The act of making an initial, freely chosen commitment generated resistance to abandoning it when conditions worsened.

Applications and countermeasures

Low-balling is a classic tactic in retail sales, notably car sales, where a salesperson secures agreement on a low price that a manager later "cannot approve", adding fees or charges. Consumer-protection guidance typically advises obtaining full written prices before committing, walking away when terms change, and recognising that the sense of obligation created by the initial agreement is a deliberate feature of the technique.
