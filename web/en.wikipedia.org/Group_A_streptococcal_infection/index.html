Group A streptococcal infection

Group A streptococcal infection is any infection caused by Streptococcus pyogenes, a species of Gram-positive bacteria in the genus Streptococcus. These bacteria are beta-haemolytic and belong to Lancefield group A. S. pyogenes is responsible for a wide range of diseases, from mild superficial infections such as pharyngitis ("strep throat") and impetigo to severe, life-threatening invasive diseases including necrotizing fasciitis and streptococcal toxic shock syndrome.

Types of infection

Group A streptococci commonly cause pharyngitis, tonsillitis, scarlet fever, impetigo, erysipelas and cellulitis. Invasive infections occur when the bacteria enter normally sterile sites, producing bacteraemia, pneumonia, necrotizing fasciitis and toxic shock syndrome. Post-infectious complications include acute rheumatic fever and post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis, which are thought to arise from immune cross-reactivity rather than direct bacterial invasion.

Virulence factors

The M protein on the bacterial surface is a major virulence factor and the basis of strain typing; more than 200 emm types are recognised. Other factors include the hyaluronic acid capsule, streptolysins O and S, streptococcal pyrogenic exotoxins (which act as superantigens), and enzymes such as streptokinase and hyaluronidase that promote spread through tissues.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis of pharyngitis is by rapid antigen detection testing or throat culture. Group A streptococci remain universally susceptible to penicillin, which is the treatment of choice; macrolides or clindamycin are used in patients with penicillin allergy and as adjunctive therapy in severe invasive disease. Intravenous immunoglobulin is sometimes used in toxic shock syndrome.

Epidemiology

Streptococcal pharyngitis is most common in children aged 5 to 15 years. The World Health Organization estimates that severe group A streptococcal diseases cause several hundred thousand deaths each year worldwide, many of them from rheumatic heart disease.
