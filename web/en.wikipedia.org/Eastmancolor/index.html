Eastmancolor

Eastmancolor is a trade name used by Eastman Kodak for a family of colour motion picture film processes and stocks introduced in 1950. Unlike the earlier three-strip Technicolor process, Eastmancolor used a single strip of film with multiple emulsion layers (a monopack), which could be shot in ordinary cameras and processed in standard laboratory equipment.

History and use

The first feature film shot in Eastmancolor was released in 1951, and through the 1950s the process was rapidly adopted worldwide because it was far cheaper and more flexible than Technicolor's beam-splitting cameras and dye-transfer printing. Films shot on it were often credited simply as "Color by Eastmancolor" or under local trade names used by licensees.

Fading and preservation

Early Eastmancolor stocks are notorious among archivists for their unstable dyes: prints often fade toward magenta or pink with age. This problem spurred the development of improved low-fade stocks in the late 1970s and 1980s and made Eastmancolor a central concern of film preservation and restoration.
