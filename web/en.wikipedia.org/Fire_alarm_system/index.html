Fire alarm system

Fire alarm system
PurposeDetection of fire and alerting of occupants
Key componentsControl panel, detectors, manual call points, sounders, strobes
StandardsNFPA 72 (US), EN 54 (EU), BS 5839 (UK)

A fire alarm system is a building system designed to detect the presence of fire or its effects — smoke, heat, carbon monoxide, or flames — and to alert occupants and emergency services so that evacuation and firefighting can begin. Modern systems range from a single smoke detector in a home to networked, addressable installations covering entire campuses.

Components

The fire alarm control panel is the hub of the system, receiving signals from initiating devices and supervising the wiring for faults. Automatic detectors include smoke detectors (photoelectric and ionisation), heat detectors (fixed-temperature and rate-of-rise), flame detectors, and aspirating systems that sample air through pipes. Manual call points (pull stations or break-glass units) let occupants raise the alarm directly. Notification appliances — bells, sounders, speakers for voice evacuation, and strobe lights for the hearing-impaired — warn occupants to evacuate.

Types of system

In a conventional system, detectors are wired in zones, and the panel indicates only the zone in alarm. In an addressable system, each device has a unique address, so the panel pinpoints the exact detector that triggered, and the devices can report their own condition. Systems may be monitored by a central station that summons the fire brigade, and they commonly interface with sprinklers, smoke control, lifts, and access control to support the building's fire strategy.

History

The first electric fire alarm telegraph was patented in Boston in 1852 by William Channing and Moses Farmer; street alarm boxes transmitted coded signals to a central office. Automatic heat detectors followed later in the nineteenth century, and Francis Robin Upton, an associate of Thomas Edison, patented an early electric fire alarm in 1890. Smoke detection became widespread in the twentieth century, and battery smoke alarms transformed domestic fire safety from the 1970s onward.

Regulation

Design, installation, and maintenance are governed by codes such as NFPA 72 in the United States, the EN 54 series in Europe, and BS 5839 in the United Kingdom. These specify detector spacing, alarm sound levels, standby battery capacity, and inspection intervals. False alarms — from cooking fumes, dust, or faulty detectors — remain the largest operational burden for fire services.
