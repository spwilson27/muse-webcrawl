A reverse transcriptase (RT) is an enzyme used to convert RNA into complementary DNA (cDNA) in a process called reverse transcription. Reverse transcriptases have been identified in many organisms, including viruses, bacteria, animals and plants, and are essential to the replication of retroviruses such as HIV, which copy their RNA genome into DNA that integrates into the host genome.

Reverse transcriptase was discovered independently by Howard Temin and David Baltimore in 1970, overturning the assumption that genetic information flows only from DNA to RNA to protein (the "central dogma"). Temin and Baltimore shared part of the 1975 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery.

HIV-1 reverse transcriptase, the target of many antiretroviral drugs.

Function and structure

Retroviral reverse transcriptases have three enzymatic activities: RNA-dependent DNA polymerase activity, which synthesises a DNA strand from the RNA template; ribonuclease H (RNase H) activity, which degrades the RNA of the RNA–DNA hybrid; and DNA-dependent DNA polymerase activity, which completes the double-stranded DNA. Unlike cellular DNA polymerases, retroviral RTs lack 3′→5′ exonuclease proofreading, making them highly error-prone — a major source of viral mutation and drug resistance.

Other occurrences

Reverse transcriptase activity also occurs in retrotransposons, in group II introns, and in cellular enzymes such as telomerase, whose catalytic subunit TERT is a specialised reverse transcriptase that extends chromosome ends using its own internal RNA template.

Applications and medicine

In the laboratory, reverse transcriptases from avian and murine retroviruses are workhorse reagents for RT-PCR and cDNA library construction. In medicine, RT is a principal target of antiretroviral therapy: nucleoside/nucleotide RT inhibitors (NRTIs) and non-nucleoside RT inhibitors (NNRTIs) are mainstays of HIV treatment.
