Human–computer interaction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Human–computer interaction (HCI) is research in the design and use of computer technology, focused on the interfaces between people (users) and computers. HCI researchers observe the ways humans interact with computers and design technologies that let humans interact with computers in novel ways. The field emerged in the 1980s with the advent of personal computing, though it has roots in earlier work on ergonomics, human factors, and the pioneering systems of researchers such as Ivan Sutherland, Douglas Engelbart, and Alan Kay.

Principles

HCI draws on computer science, behavioural sciences, cognitive psychology, design, and media studies. Core concepts include usability, accessibility, user experience (UX), affordances, and mental models. Design methods range from user-centred design and participatory design to usability testing, heuristic evaluation, and ethnographic field studies.

Interfaces

Interaction styles studied by HCI include command-line interfaces, graphical user interfaces (GUIs), direct manipulation, touch interfaces, speech recognition, gesture interaction, virtual and augmented reality, and wearable computing. The field also examines social and organisational aspects of computing, including computer-supported cooperative work.

Goals

A central goal is to design systems that are effective, efficient, safe, and satisfying to use. HCI informs practical design guidelines — from Fitts's law for pointing tasks to Nielsen's usability heuristics — and has become an essential part of software and product development worldwide.
