Ligand (biochemistry)

Ligand (biochemistry)
DefinitionA molecule that binds to a target biomolecule, usually a protein, to serve a biological purpose
Typical targetsReceptors, enzymes, transport proteins, nucleic acids
BindingUsually reversible, non-covalent; characterised by affinity (dissociation constant Kd)
Effect typesAgonist, antagonist, inverse agonist, allosteric modulator

In biochemistry and pharmacology, a ligand is a substance that forms a complex with a biomolecule to serve a biological purpose. In protein–ligand binding, the ligand is usually a molecule which produces a signal by binding to a site on a target protein. The binding typically occurs through intermolecular forces such as ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds and van der Waals forces, and is usually reversible.

Binding and affinity

The strength of binding is called affinity and is quantified by the dissociation constant Kd: the lower the Kd, the tighter the binding. High-affinity ligand binding implies that a relatively low concentration of ligand is sufficient to occupy the binding site and trigger a physiological response. Ligands display selectivity when they preferentially bind one target over similar ones — a property of central importance in drug design.

Functional types

  • Agonists bind a receptor and activate it, mimicking the endogenous ligand.
  • Antagonists bind without activating and block agonists from binding.
  • Inverse agonists suppress a receptor's constitutive (baseline) activity.
  • Allosteric modulators bind at a site distinct from the main (orthosteric) site and alter the receptor's response.
  • Enzyme substrates, inhibitors and activators are ligands of enzymes; cofactors are ligands essential to activity.

Examples

Hormones such as insulin and adrenaline, neurotransmitters such as dopamine and acetylcholine, oxygen binding to haemoglobin, and antigens binding to antibodies are all ligand interactions. Most drugs act as ligands of receptors, enzymes, ion channels or transporters, and ligand-based methods — from radioligand binding assays to computer-aided docking — are mainstays of pharmacological research.
