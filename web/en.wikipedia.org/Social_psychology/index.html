Social psychology is the scientific study of how the thoughts, feelings, and behaviours of individuals are influenced by the actual, imagined, or implied presence of others. It bridges psychology and sociology, examining topics such as social perception, attitude change, conformity, obedience to authority, interpersonal attraction, and group processes. This article concerns the academic discipline; for the emotional state of fear in social situations, see social anxiety.

History

The field is often dated to Kurt Lewin, whose formula B = f(P, E) (behaviour is a function of the person and the environment) defined its approach. After the Second World War, landmark experiments shaped its identity: Asch's conformity studies (1951), Festinger's theory of cognitive dissonance (1957), Milgram's obedience experiments (1963), and Zimbardo's Stanford prison study (1971).

Major topics

Core areas include attribution theory (how people explain causes of behaviour), persuasion and attitude change, social cognition (schemas, stereotypes, and biases such as the fundamental attribution error), group dynamics (cohesion, social loafing, groupthink), prejudice and intergroup relations, and close relationships. The 2010s brought a replication crisis that prompted reforms in methodology and preregistration across the field.

Methods

Social psychologists rely primarily on laboratory and field experiments, supplemented by surveys, observational studies, and archival research. A parallel sociological tradition studies similar phenomena at the level of social interaction and identity.
