Catechumen

A catechumen is a person receiving instruction in the Christian faith in preparation for baptism. The term comes from the Greek katēkhoumenos, "one being taught orally". In the early Church, catechumens underwent a lengthy catechumenate — sometimes lasting up to three years — of teaching, prayer, fasting and moral examination, before being baptised, usually at the Easter Vigil.

History

Early catechumens attended the first part of the liturgy (the Liturgy of the Word) but were dismissed before the Eucharist; the partly secret teaching reserved for the baptised was called the disciplina arcani. As infant baptism became universal in late antiquity, the adult catechumenate largely fell into disuse.

Modern practice

The Second Vatican Council restored the adult catechumenate, formalised in the Rite of Christian Initiation of Adults (RCIA, now OCIA), a staged process of enquiry, catechesis, election and initiation through baptism, confirmation and first communion, typically at Easter. Eastern Orthodox churches likewise receive adult converts through a period as catechumens.
