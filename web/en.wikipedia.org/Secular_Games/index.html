Secular Games

The Secular Games (Latin: Ludi Saeculares) were a religious celebration held in ancient Rome marking the end of a saeculum — the longest span of a human lifetime, reckoned at 100 or 110 years — and the beginning of a new one. The festival combined theatrical performances, sacrifices and prayers for the renewal and prosperity of Rome, and was held only a handful of times in Roman history.

Origins

Republican tradition traced the games to the Valerian gens and to expiatory rites offered to Dis Pater and Proserpina at the Tarentum in the Campus Martius. Ancient sources placed celebrations in the fifth and third centuries BC, though the dating is uncertain and the Sibylline Books were invoked to justify the intervals chosen.

The Augustan games of 17 BC

The most famous celebration was staged by Augustus in 17 BC, presented as the dawn of a new golden age after the civil wars. Over three days and nights, sacrifices were offered — unusually, to Greek deities including the Fates, Ilithyia, Terra Mater, Apollo and Diana rather than the underworld gods of the old rite. The poet Horace composed the Carmen Saeculare, sung by a choir of twenty-seven boys and twenty-seven girls, and an inscribed record of the proceedings, the Acta Ludorum Saecularium, survives in fragments. Free citizens received purification materials, and theatrical shows were mounted across the city.

Later celebrations

Subsequent emperors held the games on rival calculations of the saeculum. Claudius celebrated in AD 47, the 800th anniversary of Rome's founding. Domitian held games in AD 88, Septimius Severus in AD 204, and Philip the Arab in AD 248, marking the millennium of Rome. A final celebration appears to have taken place around AD 304 under Diocletian's era before the tradition lapsed with Christianisation.
