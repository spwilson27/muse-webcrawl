The Eighth Army was a field army formation of the British Army during the Second World War, formed in Egypt in September 1941 to take control of all Allied forces during the Western Desert Campaign. It fought in the North African campaign, the Tunisian campaign, and later in the Italian campaign, and became one of the most famous British formations of the war.

History

The Eighth Army was formed from the Western Desert Force, with Lieutenant-General Alan Cunningham as its first commander. It was a multinational force built around British, Commonwealth and imperial troops, including soldiers from Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, India, Canada and Greece, alongside units of the Free French and Poles.

Its first major operation, Operation Crusader in November 1941, relieved the siege of Tobruk. After setbacks in 1942, including the fall of Tobruk during the Gazala battles, command passed to Lieutenant-General Neil Ritchie and then, in August 1942, to Lieutenant-General Bernard Montgomery. Under Montgomery the Eighth Army won the decisive Second Battle of El Alamein in October–November 1942, a turning point of the war in North Africa.

Later campaigns

The army pursued the Axis forces across Libya and into Tunisia, fighting at the Mareth Line and Wadi Akarit before the Axis surrender in North Africa in May 1943. It then landed in Sicily in July 1943 as part of Operation Husky and fought up the Italian mainland, including the bitter fighting at the Gothic Line in 1944, before the campaign ended in May 1945.

Legacy

The Eighth Army's veterans, nicknamed the "Desert Rats" (a title properly belonging to the 7th Armoured Division), formed a strong regimental association after the war, and the army's red, white and gold crusader shield insignia remains one of the most recognisable British symbols of the Second World War.
