Animal husbandry

Livestock grazing is one of the oldest forms of animal husbandry

Animal husbandry is the branch of agriculture concerned with animals that are raised for meat, fibre, milk, eggs, or other products. It includes day-to-day care, management, and selective breeding of livestock. Practices vary widely depending on the species, climate, culture and level of technology available.

History

Animal husbandry began during the Neolithic Revolution, when humans transitioned from hunting and gathering to settled agriculture. Dogs were domesticated earliest; sheep, goats, cattle and pigs followed between roughly 11,000 and 8,000 years ago in the Fertile Crescent and elsewhere. Selective breeding, systematised in the 18th century by figures such as Robert Bakewell, greatly increased productivity.

Practices

Modern husbandry encompasses housing, feeding, breeding, health care, and waste management. Systems range from extensive pastoralism — herds grazing large areas — to intensive "factory farming" in which animals are kept at high density in controlled environments. Poultry, pigs and dairy cattle are frequently raised intensively; sheep and beef cattle more often extensively.

Issues

The industry is debated for its environmental impact (greenhouse gas emissions, land and water use), animal welfare, and public health (zoonotic diseases, antimicrobial resistance). Alternatives and reforms include improved welfare standards, precision livestock farming with sensors, and breeding for disease resistance and lower emissions.
