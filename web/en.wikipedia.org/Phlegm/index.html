Phlegm

Phlegm (from Ancient Greek phlégma, "inflammation", "humour caused by heat") is mucus produced by the respiratory system, excluding that produced by the nose passages. It often refers to respiratory mucus expelled by coughing, otherwise known as sputum. The composition of phlegm may vary depending on the climate and the state of the immune system, and color may vary from clear to pale or dark yellow and green, from light to dark brown, and even dark grey depending on the constituents.

Distinction between phlegm and mucus

Contrary to popular misconceptions, the color of phlegm does not reliably indicate whether an infection is bacterial or viral. Mucus is a normal protective layering in various parts of the body; phlegm specifically refers to mucus produced in the respiratory system. When mucus builds up or becomes infected, its color and consistency can change.

Causes

Excess phlegm production can result from respiratory infections such as the common cold, influenza, and bronchitis, as well as allergies, asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), gastroesophageal reflux, and environmental irritants such as smoke. The body produces phlegm to trap dust, bacteria, and other inhaled particles, which are then expelled by coughing or moved by cilia.

Humorism

In ancient and medieval medicine, phlegm was one of the four humours, alongside blood, yellow bile, and black bile. Phlegm was associated with the element water, with cold and wet qualities, and with the "phlegmatic" temperament — calm, thoughtful, and sometimes sluggish. The theory of humourism, systematized in the Hippocratic corpus and by Galen, dominated Western medicine until the 19th century.

Treatment

Management typically targets the underlying cause. Adequate hydration can keep mucus thinner; expectorants such as guaifenesin are used to loosen phlegm, while mucolytics such as acetylcysteine break down thick mucus. Persistent, blood-stained, or otherwise concerning sputum warrants medical evaluation.
