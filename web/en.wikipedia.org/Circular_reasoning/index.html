Circular reasoning

Circular reasoning (Latin: circulus in probando, "circle in proving"; also circular argument) is a logical fallacy in which the reasoner begins with what they are trying to end with: the components of a circular argument are often logically valid because if the premises are true, the conclusion must be true, but the argument proves nothing because the conclusion is already assumed in the premises.

Relation to begging the question

Circular reasoning is closely related to begging the question (petitio principii), in which a proposition is assumed in the course of trying to prove it. In practice, circular arguments often take a subtler, reworded form, so that the premise restates the conclusion in different words: "This policy is right because it is the correct thing to do." Classic theological and everyday examples include "The Bible is true because it says so in the Bible."

Detection and use

A circular argument cannot establish its conclusion for anyone who does not already accept that conclusion, and it fails to provide independent evidence. Nevertheless, circular formulations are common in rhetoric and persuasion, and a distinction is sometimes drawn between vicious circularity and coherent systems of mutually supporting beliefs, such as in coherentist epistemology, where the circularity is debated rather than simply dismissed.
