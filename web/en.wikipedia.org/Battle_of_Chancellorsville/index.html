The Battle of Chancellorsville (April 30 – May 6, 1863) was a major battle of the American Civil War and the principal engagement of the Chancellorsville campaign. It is widely considered Confederate general Robert E. Lee's "perfect battle": facing an army roughly twice his size, Lee divided his forces in the presence of the enemy and won — but at the cost of his ablest lieutenant, Thomas "Stonewall" Jackson, who was mortally wounded by friendly fire.

Background

Union Maj. Gen. Joseph Hooker planned to envelop Lee's Army of Northern Virginia, crossing the Rappahannock and Rapidan rivers above Fredericksburg while part of his army (under John Sedgwick) threatened Lee from the east. Hooker had about 133,000 men against Lee's 60,000. The Union army concentrated around the crossroads hamlet of Chancellorsville in the dense thickets of the Wilderness.

Battle

After early success, Hooker lost his nerve on May 1 and withdrew into a defensive position. Lee audaciously split his army twice: leaving Jubal Early with a small force facing Sedgwick at Fredericksburg, he met Hooker with part of his army while Jackson marched some 30,000 men on a 12-mile flank march across the Union front on May 2. Late that afternoon Jackson crashed into the exposed Union XI Corps, routing it. That night, scouting ahead of his lines, Jackson was shot by his own men; he died of pneumonia on May 10 after his left arm was amputated.

On May 3 the Confederates, now under J.E.B. Stuart's temporary command of Jackson's corps, fought their way to reunite Lee's army, including fierce fighting around Hazel Grove and Fairview. Lee then turned east to defeat Sedgwick (Second Fredericksburg and Salem Church, May 3–4). Hooker withdrew across the Rappahannock on the night of May 5–6.

Casualties and aftermath

Union casualties were about 17,000; Confederate about 13,000 — proportionally heavier for the smaller army, and irreplaceable in Jackson. Emboldened, Lee undertook his second invasion of the North, which ended at Gettysburg two months later.
