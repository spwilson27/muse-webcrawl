Suffrage

Suffrage, political franchise, or simply franchise, is the right to vote in public, political elections and referendums. The question of who holds suffrage — by age, sex, property, citizenship, or criminal record — has been central to the development of democracy.

History

In classical Athens, voting in the assembly was restricted to adult male citizens. Early modern democracies typically limited the vote by property and sex; the nineteenth and twentieth centuries saw its progressive extension. Working-class men gained the vote through reform acts in Britain (1867, 1884) and similar movements elsewhere. Women's suffrage was won first in New Zealand in 1893, followed by Australia (1902), Finland (1906), and after the First World War in many countries; the United States adopted the Nineteenth Amendment in 1920, and Switzerland completed the process at federal level only in 1971. Universal suffrage — for all adult citizens regardless of sex, race, or wealth — became the norm after the Second World War; in the United States, the Voting Rights Act of 1965 dismantled racial barriers in practice.

Current debates

Modern controversies include the voting age (lowered to 18 in most countries; some jurisdictions allow 16), the disenfranchisement of prisoners and people convicted of felonies, voting rights for non-citizen residents, and compulsory voting, which is practised in Australia and Belgium.
