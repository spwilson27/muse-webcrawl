Robert Bloch (1917–1994)

Robert Albert Bloch (/blɒk/; April 5, 1917 – September 23, 1994) was an American fiction writer, primarily of crime, psychological horror and fantasy, much of which has been dramatised for radio, cinema and television. He is best known as the writer of Psycho (1959), the basis for Alfred Hitchcock's film of the same name.

Career

Born in Chicago, Bloch was a teenage protégé of H. P. Lovecraft and published his first stories in Weird Tales in the 1930s, contributing to the Cthulhu Mythos. Over a sixty-year career he wrote hundreds of short stories and more than thirty novels, along with scripts for Star Trek and Alfred Hitchcock Presents.

Psycho

Bloch's novel Psycho drew partly on the case of Wisconsin murderer Ed Gein to create Norman Bates, one of fiction's most enduring killers. Hitchcock's 1960 film adaptation made the character iconic; Bloch later wrote two sequel novels, Psycho II (1982) and Psycho House (1990).

Awards and legacy

Bloch won the Hugo Award for his story "That Hell-Bound Train" (1959) and received the Bram Stoker Award for Lifetime Achievement. He was a guest of honour at the 1948 World Science Fiction Convention and remained a celebrated raconteur on the convention circuit.
