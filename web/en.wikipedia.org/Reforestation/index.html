Reforestation

Reforestation is the re-establishment of forest cover on land that previously had forest but was converted to another use, such as agriculture, grazing, or settlement, or that lost its forest through logging, fire, or other disturbance. It may occur naturally through regeneration from seed and rootstock, or artificially through direct seeding and the planting of seedlings. Reforestation differs from afforestation, which establishes forest on land that has not recently been forested.

Purposes and methods

Forests are restored to produce timber and other forest products, to protect watersheds and soils against erosion, to restore habitat and biodiversity, to provide recreation, and increasingly to sequester carbon from the atmosphere as a climate-change mitigation measure. Methods range from simply protecting regenerating areas from grazing and fire (assisted natural regeneration) to intensive plantation forestry with site preparation, nursery-grown seedlings, and tending. The choice of species — native mixed stands versus fast-growing monocultures of pine or eucalyptus — strongly affects ecological outcomes.

Programmes and debate

Large-scale reforestation efforts include China's Grain for Green programme and the shelterbelt plantings against desertification, India's national missions, the Bonn Challenge pledge to restore degraded land, and corporate and governmental tree-planting campaigns worldwide. Scientists caution that tree planting benefits the climate only when the right trees are planted in the right places and survive to maturity, and that protecting existing old-growth forest generally stores more carbon, sooner, than new plantings.
