Metatheria is a mammalian clade that includes all marsupials (the crown group Marsupialia) and a variety of extinct groups more closely related to marsupials than to placental mammals (Eutheria). The name was coined by Thomas Henry Huxley in 1880. Metatherians are distinguished from eutherians by features of the skull, dentition, and reproduction.

Evolutionary history

The oldest generally accepted metatherian is Sinodelphys szalayi from the Early Cretaceous (~125 million years ago) of China, a small scansorial animal. Metatherians diversified widely in the Late Cretaceous of North America, including the large stagodontids and the abundant herpetotheriids. After the end-Cretaceous extinction, they spread through South America — where they included the carnivorous sparassodonts — Antarctica, and Australia, where marsupials underwent their greatest radiation. Molecular evidence places the origin of crown marsupials in the Late Cretaceous or earliest Paleogene.

Characteristics

Metatherians typically have a dental formula with more incisors than eutherians, a relatively small braincase, and characteristic features of the palate and ear region. Reproduction in marsupials involves a short gestation, birth of highly altricial young, and a prolonged period of lactation, often in a pouch (marsupium), though stem metatherians presumably lacked a true pouch. Epipubic bones project from the pelvis in both sexes in most living marsupials.

Classification

Living metatherians fall into seven orders: Didelphimorphia (opossums), Paucituberculata (shrew opossums), Microbiotheria (the monito del monte), Dasyuromorphia, Peramelemorphia (bandicoots and bilbies), Notoryctemorphia (marsupial moles), and Diprotodontia (kangaroos, koalas, wombats, possums). Extinct groups include the Sparassodonta of South America and numerous Cretaceous North American lineages.
