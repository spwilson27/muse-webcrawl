Romanesque architecture is an architectural style of medieval Europe characterised by semi-circular arches. There is no consensus for when the style began, with proposals ranging from the 6th to the 11th century; the later part of the range is most commonly accepted. In the 12th century it developed into the Gothic style, marked by pointed arches. Examples of Romanesque architecture can be found across the continent, making it the first pan-European architectural style since Imperial Roman architecture.

Characteristics

Romanesque buildings are known for their massive quality, thick walls, round arches, sturdy piers, groin vaults and barrel vaults, large towers, and decorative arcading. Small windows and heavy masonry give many churches a fortress-like appearance. Plans were typically regular and symmetrical, with cruciform layouts for churches.

Regional forms

The style took distinctive regional forms: Norman Romanesque in England (after 1066) and Sicily; the pilgrims' road churches of France and Spain, such as Saint-Sernin at Toulouse and Santiago de Compostela Cathedral, built to accommodate crowds visiting relics; and the imperial cathedrals of the Rhineland, including Speyer, Mainz and Worms. In Italy, Romanesque churches such as Pisa Cathedral combined marble revetment with classical influences.

Transition to Gothic

Romanesque masons experimented with rib vaults and pointed arches, notably at Durham Cathedral, paving the way for the Gothic architecture that emerged in northern France in the 1140s.
