Epicondyle

An epicondyle is a rounded eminence on a bone that lies upon a condyle (epi-, "upon" + condyle, from the Greek kondylos, "knuckle"). Epicondyles serve as attachment points (entheses) for muscles, tendons and ligaments. There are various epicondyles in the human skeleton, each named for its location on its host bone.

In the upper limb

The distal humerus bears two prominent epicondyles. The medial epicondyle of the humerus, the larger and more prominent of the two, gives attachment to the flexor muscles of the forearm and to the ulnar collateral ligament of the elbow; the ulnar nerve passes in a groove behind it, where it is vulnerable to blows — the "funny bone." The lateral epicondyle of the humerus is smaller and serves as the common origin of the extensor muscles of the forearm.

In the lower limb

The distal femur has a medial and a lateral epicondyle. The medial epicondyle of the femur is a large convex eminence to which the tibial (medial) collateral ligament of the knee is attached; just above it lies the adductor tubercle. The lateral epicondyle, smaller and less prominent, gives attachment to the fibular (lateral) collateral ligament.

Clinical significance

Overuse disorders of the epicondylar entheses are common: lateral epicondylitis ("tennis elbow") and medial epicondylitis ("golfer's elbow") are painful enthesopathies of the humeral epicondyles. Fractures of the medial epicondyle are a recognised injury in children and adolescents, sometimes associated with elbow dislocation.
