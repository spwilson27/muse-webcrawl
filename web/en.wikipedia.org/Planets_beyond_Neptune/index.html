Planets beyond Neptune

The existence of planets beyond Neptune has been hypothesised since the discovery of Neptune in 1846. Astronomers have repeatedly proposed unseen planets in the outer Solar System to explain apparent irregularities in the orbits of Uranus and Neptune, or features of the Kuiper belt and the population of extreme trans-Neptunian objects.

Historical hypotheses

In the early 20th century, Percival Lowell predicted a "Planet X" beyond Neptune, and the search he inspired led Clyde Tombaugh to discover Pluto in 1930. Pluto proved far too small to explain the supposed perturbations, which were later shown to result from an overestimate of Neptune's mass. Other historical proposals include multiple trans-Neptunian planets suggested to explain the orbits of comets.

Planet Nine

In 2016, astronomers Konstantin Batygin and Michael E. Brown proposed Planet Nine, a hypothetical giant planet of roughly five to ten Earth masses on a wide, eccentric orbit, to explain the clustering of orbits among extreme trans-Neptunian objects such as Sedna and 2012 VP113. Extensive searches have not yet found such a planet, and alternative explanations involving the collective gravity of the Kuiper belt itself or observational bias remain under debate.

Known dwarf planets

Beyond Neptune lie the known dwarf planets Pluto, Eris, Haumea, and Makemake, along with a large population of trans-Neptunian objects. Whether any of the hypothesised additional giant planets exists remains an open question in planetary science.
