Boat lift

A boat lift, also called a ship lift or lift lock, is a machine for transporting boats between water bodies at different elevations on a river or canal. It is an alternative to a canal lock and can move vessels vertically (or, in some historical designs, along an inclined plane) while they float in a water-filled chamber called a caisson, or rest on cradles.

Operation

A boat enters the caisson through gates, which are then sealed; the chamber is raised or lowered by hydraulic, mechanical, or balanced counterweight systems, and the gates at the other level are opened. Because the vessel displaces its own weight of water, the load on the machinery changes little whether the caisson carries a boat or not. Lifts use far less water than a flight of locks and traverse height differences much faster.

Notable examples

Historic lifts include the Anderton Boat Lift in England (1875) and the boat lifts of the Canal du Centre in Belgium, now a World Heritage Site. The Falkirk Wheel in Scotland (2002) is a rotating boat lift connecting the Forth and Clyde Canal with the Union Canal. The largest ship lifts in the world are at Chinese dams such as the Three Gorges, where a lift raises vessels of up to 3,000 tonnes over a height of more than 100 metres.
