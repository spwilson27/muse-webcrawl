Classicism

Classicism, in the arts, refers generally to a high regard for a classical period, classical antiquity in the Western tradition, as setting standards for taste which the classicists seek to emulate. In its purest form, classicism is an aesthetic attitude dependent on principles based in the culture, art and literature of ancient Greece and Rome, with the emphasis on form, simplicity, proportion, clarity of structure, perfection, and restrained emotion, as well as explicit appeal to tradition.

In the visual arts

The art of classicism typically seeks to be formal and restrained. Classicism flourished in the Italian Renaissance, when artists and architects such as Brunelleschi, Alberti, Raphael, and Michelangelo revived antique forms and ideals. It re-emerged in the seventeenth century — Nicolas Poussin's ordered landscapes and history paintings, and French classical tragedy in literature — and again in the later eighteenth century as Neoclassicism, a self-conscious reaction against the Rococo, led by painters like Jacques-Louis David and sculptors like Antonio Canova, inspired partly by the excavations at Herculaneum and Pompeii.

In literature and architecture

Literary classicism prizes clarity, order, decorum, and adherence to established genres and unities; it dominated French letters under Boileau and the Académie française and English letters in the Augustan age of Pope and Johnson. In architecture, classicism means the use of the classical orders, symmetry, and proportion — from Renaissance palazzi through Palladian country houses to the neoclassical civic buildings of the nineteenth century and the stripped classicism of some twentieth-century regimes.

Classicism and romanticism

Classicism is conventionally contrasted with romanticism: reason, restraint, universality, and rule-following against emotion, individuality, the sublime, and spontaneity. The pairing structures much art-historical writing about the decades around 1800, though historians caution that the two modes interpenetrate in practice.
