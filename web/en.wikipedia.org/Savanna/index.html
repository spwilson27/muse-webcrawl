Savanna

TypeTropical and subtropical grassland biome
ClimateStrongly seasonal rainfall, pronounced dry season
Major regionsEast and southern Africa (Serengeti), Brazilian cerrado, Venezuelan llanos, northern Australia
Typical vegetationGrasses with scattered trees (acacia, baobab, eucalyptus)
Key processesFire, herbivory, seasonal drought

A savanna or savannah is a mixed woodland–grassland ecosystem characterised by an open tree canopy above a continuous ground layer of grasses. Savannas occur in tropical and subtropical regions with strongly seasonal rainfall, and cover roughly a fifth of the Earth's land surface, including large parts of Africa, South America, Asia and Australia.

Climate and formation

Savanna climates alternate between wet and dry seasons: most rain falls in a few summer months, followed by drought. Rainfall alone does not determine where savanna forms. Frequent fires — set by lightning and, for millennia, by human burning — kill young tree saplings while grasses, which regrow from protected buds at or below ground level, recover quickly. Large herbivores, from elephants that topple trees to herds of grazing ungulates, further hold the landscape open. The balance among rainfall, fire and herbivory keeps savannas poised between grassland and forest.

Vegetation and fauna

Savanna grasses are mostly C4 species adapted to heat and bright light. Trees such as umbrella-thorn acacias, baobabs and palms are spaced widely enough that the canopy never closes, allowing a continuous grass layer. African savannas support the world's richest assemblage of large mammals: wildebeest, zebras, giraffes, elephants, rhinoceroses, lions, leopards, cheetahs and hyenas. The Serengeti–Mara ecosystem hosts an annual migration of some 1.5 million wildebeest, the largest terrestrial mammal migration on Earth. The Brazilian cerrado — the world's most biodiverse savanna — and the Australian tropical savannas each support distinctive endemic floras and faunas.

Human history

Savannas are widely considered the environment in which early hominins evolved bipedalism and many other human traits as forests shrank in Africa. Pastoralist peoples such as the Maasai of Kenya and Tanzania have grazed cattle on East African savannas for centuries. Today savannas are under pressure from conversion to cropland and ranching, commercial charcoal production, altered fire regimes and the spread of woody shrubs ("bush encroachment"), sometimes driven by rising atmospheric carbon dioxide that favours trees over grasses.
