Lime (material)

Lime is produced by burning limestone in a kiln.

Lime is a calcium-containing inorganic material composed primarily of oxides and hydroxide of calcium. The principal forms are quicklime (calcium oxide, CaO), produced by calcining limestone (calcium carbonate), and slaked lime (calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)2), produced by reacting quicklime with water in an exothermic process called slaking.

Production

Limestone is heated to around 900–1000 °C in a lime kiln, driving off carbon dioxide and leaving quicklime — the basis of one of humanity's oldest chemical industries. When water is added, quicklime slakes to calcium hydroxide with considerable heat; further water gives lime putty or milk of lime. Hardened lime works by slowly reabsorbing carbon dioxide from the air and reverting to calcium carbonate (carbonation).

Uses

  • Construction — lime mortar, lime plaster and limewash have been used for millennia; lime remains valued in the conservation of historic masonry for its breathability and flexibility. It is also used in soil stabilisation and in producing calcium silicate bricks.
  • Agriculture — agricultural lime raises the pH of acidic soils and supplies calcium.
  • Industry — lime is used in steelmaking as a flux, in water and sewage treatment, in flue-gas desulfurisation, in paper manufacture, in sugar refining, and in the production of chemicals such as calcium carbide and soda ash.
  • Food — food-grade lime (calcium hydroxide, additive E526) is used in nixtamalisation of maize and in pickling.

Safety

Quicklime is caustic and reacts vigorously with water; slaked lime is a strong alkali that can irritate skin and eyes. Both require protective handling.
