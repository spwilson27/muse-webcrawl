Digital television transition

The digital television transition, also called the digital switchover (DSO), the analogue switch/sign-off (ASO), the digital migration or the analogue shutdown, is the process in which older analogue television broadcasting technology is converted to and replaced by digital television. Conducted by individual nations on different schedules, this primarily involves the conversion of analogue terrestrial television broadcasting infrastructure to digital terrestrial (DTT), a major benefit being extra frequencies on the radio spectrum and lower broadcasting costs, as well as improved picture quality for consumers.

Background

Digital television standards — DVB-T in most of Europe and many other regions, ATSC in North America, ISDB-T in Japan and much of Latin America, and DTMB in China — compress video so that several programme channels ("multiplexes") fit in the bandwidth of a single analogue channel, and enable high-definition broadcasts. Governments promoted switchover to free valuable UHF spectrum, the so-called digital dividend, for mobile broadband, and to modernise broadcasting.

Implementation

Transitions typically proceeded through years of simulcasting, in which analogue and digital signals were broadcast in parallel, followed by region-by-region switch-off dates. Viewers with analogue sets needed a digital tuner or a converter box; governments often subsidised converters for low-income or elderly households, as with the US coupon programme administered by the NTIA in 2008–2009. Public information campaigns warned of the "snow" that would appear on unprepared sets.

By country

The Netherlands and Luxembourg were among the first to complete terrestrial switch-off in 2006. Germany completed in 2008, the Nordic countries around 2007–2009, and Spain in 2010. The United States ended full-power analogue broadcasts on 12 June 2009, a date delayed from February 2009 to allow more households to prepare; low-power and translator stations continued longer. The United Kingdom switched region by region from 2007 to 2012, culminating in Northern Ireland. Japan switched off in 2011 (delayed in three prefectures affected by the Tōhoku earthquake), Australia in 2013, and many developing countries followed during the 2010s and 2020s, with some analogue services persisting into the mid-2020s.

Consequences

Switchover expanded channel choice in free-to-air television, improved reception quality, and released spectrum auctioned for billions for 4G and 5G services. It also rendered millions of analogue receivers obsolete without converters, raised e-waste concerns, and in some markets reduced coverage at the fringes of transmitter areas because digital signals exhibit a "cliff effect" rather than graceful degradation.
