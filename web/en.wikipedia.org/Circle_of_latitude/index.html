Circle of latitude

A circle of latitude or line of latitude on Earth is an abstract east–west small circle connecting all locations around Earth (ignoring elevation) at a given latitude coordinate line. Circles of latitude are often called parallels because they are parallel to each other; that is, planes that contain any of these circles never intersect. A location's position along a circle of latitude is given by its longitude.

Major circles of latitude

There are five major circles of latitude, listed below from north to south:

  • The Arctic Circle (66°33′ N) — the southernmost latitude at which the midnight sun and polar night can occur.
  • The Tropic of Cancer (23°26′ N) — the northernmost latitude at which the Sun can be directly overhead.
  • The Equator (0°) — the only circle of latitude that is also a great circle; it divides Earth into Northern and Southern Hemispheres.
  • The Tropic of Capricorn (23°26′ S) — the southernmost latitude at which the Sun can be directly overhead.
  • The Antarctic Circle (66°33′ S) — the northernmost latitude in the Southern Hemisphere at which the midnight sun and polar night can occur.

Length

The circumference of a circle of latitude decreases with distance from the equator, since the circle at latitude φ has radius R cos φ, where R is Earth's radius. At the equator the circle is about 40,075 km long; at the poles the circles shrink to points. One degree of longitude along a parallel therefore spans a distance that decreases from about 111.3 km at the equator toward zero at the poles, a fact of importance in navigation and map-making.

Use

Together with lines of longitude, circles of latitude form the geographic coordinate system by which every location on Earth can be specified. Many national and subnational borders are defined along parallels, including much of the border between the United States and Canada along the 49th parallel north.
