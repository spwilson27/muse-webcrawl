A toll bridge is a bridge where a fee (toll) is charged for passage, typically to pay for the bridge's construction, maintenance and operation. Tolls may be levied only until construction costs are recovered — as was famously the case with Britain's Severn Bridge, whose tolls were abolished in 2018 after the crossing reverted to public ownership — or indefinitely to raise revenue and manage traffic. Historically, toll points were staffed booths at one or both ends of the bridge; increasingly, electronic toll collection using transponders or automatic number-plate recognition allows free-flow charging without stopping.

History

Charging for crossings is ancient: ferries and pontoon bridges levied tolls in classical times, and medieval Europe's bridges were often financed by toll charters and bridge chapels. In 18th- and 19th-century Britain, turnpike trusts and private companies built toll bridges across rivers such as the Thames and Severn; many small privately owned toll bridges survive today. In the United States, great Depression-era and post-war crossings — including the Golden Gate Bridge (1937) and the bridges and tunnels operated by authorities such as the Port Authority of New York and New Jersey — were financed by toll-backed bonds issued by public authorities.

Operations and economics

Toll rates usually vary by vehicle class and sometimes by direction or time of day. Because bridges are natural bottlenecks with few alternatives, toll revenue is relatively predictable, making toll bridges attractive for bond financing and public-private partnerships. Controversies include the persistence of tolls after construction debts are repaid, diversion of traffic onto untolled local roads, and privacy questions raised by electronic collection.

Notable examples

Famous toll bridges include the Golden Gate Bridge and San Francisco–Oakland Bay Bridge in California, the George Washington Bridge between New York and New Jersey (among the world's busiest), the Chesapeake Bay Bridge in Maryland, the Øresund Bridge between Denmark and Sweden, and the Confederation Bridge in Canada. The Humber Bridge in England long retained tolls partly to service its construction debt.
