Passive smoking

Passive smoking, also called secondhand smoke exposure or involuntary smoking, is the inhalation of tobacco smoke by persons other than the smoker. It consists of sidestream smoke from the burning tip of a cigarette and mainstream smoke exhaled by the smoker. Secondhand smoke contains thousands of chemicals, including at least 250 known to be harmful and about 70 that can cause cancer.

Health effects

The scientific consensus, summarised in reports by the US Surgeon General (notably 1986 and 2006), the International Agency for Research on Cancer, and the World Health Organization, is that secondhand smoke causes disease in non-smokers. In adults it increases the risk of lung cancer (by roughly 20–30% for those living with a smoker) and coronary heart disease, and it can trigger asthma attacks and worsen respiratory symptoms. In children, exposure is linked to sudden infant death syndrome, lower respiratory infections, middle-ear disease, and more severe asthma. The 2006 Surgeon General's report concluded there is no risk-free level of exposure.

Policy

Evidence about passive smoking drove smoke-free legislation. Ireland enacted the first comprehensive national workplace smoking ban (including bars and restaurants) in 2004; many jurisdictions followed, and Article 8 of the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control obliges parties to protect people from tobacco smoke in indoor public places and workplaces. Studies after bans recorded rapid falls in secondhand-smoke exposure biomarkers and in hospital admissions for acute coronary events. The tobacco industry historically funded campaigns to dispute the evidence and promote ventilation as an alternative, strategies documented in internal industry records released through litigation.

Thirdhand smoke

Researchers also describe "thirdhand smoke": residual nicotine and other chemicals deposited on surfaces and dust that can react to form carcinogens and expose occupants, especially crawling infants, after smoking has ceased.
