Liberal Christianity

Liberal Christianity, also known as theological liberalism, is a movement within Protestant Christianity that interprets and reforms Christian teaching in light of modern knowledge, science, and ethics. It emphasizes the authority of human reason and experience over doctrinal authority.

Origins

The movement emerged in the late 18th and 19th centuries, shaped by Enlightenment thought and the historical-critical study of the Bible. Friedrich Schleiermacher, often called the father of modern liberal theology, grounded religion in the feeling of absolute dependence. Later figures such as Albrecht Ritschl and Adolf von Harnack stressed the ethical teaching of Jesus and the fatherhood of God.

Beliefs

Liberal Christians typically view the Bible as a human witness to religious experience rather than an inerrant text, accept evolutionary science, and emphasize social justice, inclusivity, and the immanence of God. In the early 20th century, liberal theology was challenged by neo-orthodoxy, associated with Karl Barth, which reasserted divine transcendence and revelation.

Influence

Liberal Christianity shaped the mainline Protestant denominations, the Social Gospel movement, and the ecumenical movement. Its critics, including fundamentalist and evangelical movements, formed partly in reaction against its doctrines and methods.
