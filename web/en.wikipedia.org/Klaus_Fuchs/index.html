Klaus Fuchs


Klaus Emil Julius Fuchs (29 December 1911 – 28 January 1988), German-born theoretical physicist and atomic spy.

Klaus Emil Julius Fuchs (29 December 1911 – 28 January 1988) was a German theoretical physicist and atomic spy who supplied information from the American, British and Canadian Manhattan Project to the Soviet Union during and shortly after the Second World War. His disclosures are widely credited with accelerating the Soviet atomic bomb programme.

Early life and work

Fuchs was born in Rüsselsheim, Germany. A member first of the Social Democratic Party and then the Communist Party of Germany, he fled to Britain after the Nazi seizure of power in 1933. He studied at the University of Bristol and took his doctorate at the University of Edinburgh under Max Born. Interned briefly as an enemy alien, he joined Rudolf Peierls's team at Birmingham working on the British atomic bomb project ("Tube Alloys") and became a British citizen in 1942.

Espionage

In 1943 Fuchs travelled to the United States as part of the British mission to the Manhattan Project, working at Columbia University and then at Los Alamos Laboratory, where he contributed to the design of the implosion-type plutonium bomb. From 1941 onward he passed classified information to Soviet intelligence, including details of the plutonium bomb tested at Alamogordo and used at Nagasaki.

After the war Fuchs became head of the Theoretical Physics Division at the Atomic Energy Research Establishment, Harwell. In late 1949, following the decryption of Soviet cables by the Venona project, he was interrogated by MI5 officer William Skardon and confessed in January 1950. He was convicted of espionage and sentenced to fourteen years' imprisonment, the maximum for the offence.

Later life

Released in 1959, Fuchs emigrated to East Germany, where he resumed scientific work at the Central Institute for Nuclear Research at Rossendorf near Dresden and received state honours. He died in East Berlin in 1988.
