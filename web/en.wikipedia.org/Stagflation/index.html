Stagflation

Inflation and unemployment in the 1970s, the classic stagflation episode.

Stagflation is an economic condition characterized by slow economic growth and relatively high unemployment—economic stagnation—occurring at the same time as rising prices, or inflation. The term, a portmanteau of stagnation and inflation, was popularized by British politician Iain Macleod in 1965 and became widely used during the economic difficulties of the 1970s.

The 1970s episode

Stagflation struck the United States and other Western economies in the 1970s, particularly following the 1973 oil crisis, when OPEC's embargo quadrupled oil prices, and again after the 1979 energy crisis. Inflation in the US reached double digits while unemployment rose, confounding the Keynesian expectation, embodied in the Phillips curve, that inflation and unemployment moved in opposite directions.

Explanations

Economists explain stagflation mainly through adverse supply shocks, which raise production costs and prices while reducing output, and through monetary policy that accommodates the shock. Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps argued that once workers and firms adjust their expectations of inflation upward, attempts to hold unemployment below its natural rate produce accelerating inflation.

Policy response

Stagflation was largely ended in the early 1980s when US Federal Reserve chairman Paul Volcker raised interest rates sharply, inducing a deep recession but breaking inflation expectations. The episode reshaped macroeconomic policy toward central bank credibility and inflation targeting, themes also central to broader debates about the responsibilities of institutions in this period.
