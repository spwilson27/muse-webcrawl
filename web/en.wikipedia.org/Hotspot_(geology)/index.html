Hotspot (geology)

In geology, a hotspot is a volcanic region thought to be fed by an underlying mantle that is anomalously hot compared with the surrounding mantle. Hotspots may lie far from tectonic plate boundaries, and their volcanoes can form chains as a plate moves over a relatively stationary heat source in the mantle.

Mantle plume hypothesis

The hotspot concept was proposed by J. Tuzo Wilson in 1963 to explain chains of Pacific islands, such as the Hawaiian–Emperor seamount chain, which grow older in a consistent direction away from the currently active volcanoes. In 1971 W. Jason Morgan proposed that hotspots are fed by mantle plumes — narrow columns of hot rock rising from deep within the Earth, possibly from the core–mantle boundary. As the Pacific Plate moved over the Hawaiian hotspot, it produced the Hawaiian islands; a bend in the chain records a change in plate direction about 47 million years ago.

Examples and debate

Some 40–50 hotspots are commonly recognised, including Hawaii, Iceland, Yellowstone, Réunion, and the Galápagos. The onset of a plume is associated in some cases with large igneous provinces — enormous flood-basalt eruptions such as the Deccan and Siberian Traps. The plume hypothesis remains debated: some geologists argue that certain "hotspots" can be explained by shallow plate-tectonic processes and fertile patches in the upper mantle rather than deep plumes, and the question is an active area of geophysical research using seismic tomography.
