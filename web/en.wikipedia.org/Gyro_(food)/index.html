Gyro (food)

Gyro

A gyro or gyros (Greek: γύρος, romanized: gýros, lit. 'turn') is a Greek dish made of meat cooked on a vertical rotisserie, then sliced and wrapped or served in pita bread, usually with tomato, onion, lettuce, tzatziki and sometimes fried potatoes. The meat is traditionally pork or chicken in Greece, though lamb, beef and veal are also used; in the United States a seasoned blend of lamb and beef became the standard.

History

Grilling a vertical stack of seasoned meat slices and cutting off pieces as it cooks was developed in Bursa in the 19th-century Ottoman Empire in the form of döner kebab. After the Greco-Turkish War (1919–1922) and the population exchange between Greece and Turkey, refugees from Anatolia brought the dish to Greece, where it evolved into gyros. Greek immigrants introduced it to the United States in the mid-20th century, and by the 1970s it had been industrialized: factories in Chicago mass-produced gyro cones, and the dish spread nationwide.

Preparation

Slices of meat are stacked on a tall vertical spit, seasoned with spices such as cumin, oregano, thyme, rosemary, marjoram and ground black pepper, and pressed into an inverted cone shape. The spit rotates slowly in front of a heat source; as the outer layer cooks, it is shaved off with a long knife or an electric slicer. The shavings are served in a warmed pita with accompaniments. In Greece, pork and chicken gyros predominate, and french fries are commonly included inside the wrap.

Name

The name comes from the Greek word γύρος ('circle' or 'turn'), a calque of the Turkish döner, which also means 'turning'. In Greece the dish was originally called döner; it was Hellenized to gyros in the mid-1970s.

Related dishes

Gyros is closely related to the Turkish döner kebab, the Levantine shawarma, Canadian donair, and Mexican tacos al pastor, the latter adapted by Lebanese immigrants using pork and serving it on corn tortillas with pineapple.
