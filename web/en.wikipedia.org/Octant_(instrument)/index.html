The octant, also called a reflecting quadrant, is a measuring instrument used primarily in navigation to determine the angle between a celestial body and the horizon, from which a ship's latitude could be calculated. It was developed around 1730, independently by John Hadley in England and Thomas Godfrey in colonial America.

Design

The octant's frame forms a sector of one-eighth of a circle, hence its name. A movable index arm carries a mirror; light from a star or the Sun reflects from the index mirror into a half-silvered horizon glass, where the observer aligns the reflected image with the horizon seen directly. Because of the double reflection, the 45° arc reads angles up to 90° (and, on later instruments, beyond), each degree on the limb representing two degrees of measured angle. Early octants were made of wood (often mahogany or ebony) with ivory scales; later ones were brass.

Use and significance

Superseding the cross-staff and backstaff, the octant greatly improved the accuracy of celestial navigation because the observer viewed the body and the horizon simultaneously and the reading was largely unaffected by the ship's motion. It was used with the Nautical Almanac to find latitude from the Sun's noon altitude, and for lunar-distance observations to determine longitude.

Successor

The octant was in turn largely replaced by the sextant, whose wider arc (one-sixth of a circle) permits measuring the large angles needed for lunar distances and star sights. Octants remained in use on some vessels into the nineteenth century.
