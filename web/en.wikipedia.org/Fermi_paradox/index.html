Fermi paradox

The Fermi paradox is the discrepancy between the lack of conclusive evidence of advanced extraterrestrial life and the apparently high likelihood of its existence. Given the age and size of the Milky Way — hundreds of billions of stars, many with planets, over billions of years — even slow interstellar colonisation should have filled the galaxy long ago, and signs of extraterrestrial civilisations might be expected to be visible. The paradox takes its name from a remark attributed to physicist Enrico Fermi, who, during a 1950 lunchtime conversation at Los Alamos about flying saucers and interstellar travel, asked: "Where is everybody?"

The Milky Way contains hundreds of billions of stars.

Background

The argument was elaborated by astronomer Michael H. Hart in 1975 and is related to the Drake equation, which estimates the number of communicative civilisations in the galaxy. Earth-like planets appear to be common — exoplanet surveys suggest billions in the galaxy — so explanations for the "Great Silence" tend to target other factors: the rarity of life or of intelligence, the brevity of technological civilisations, the difficulty of interstellar travel, or the possibility that we have not searched adequately or correctly.

Proposed explanations

Explanations fall into several families. Some hold that extraterrestrial intelligence is genuinely rare: the "Rare Earth" hypothesis argues that the conditions for complex life are improbable, while the "Great Filter" argument locates an extremely unlikely step either behind us (for example, abiogenesis or the evolution of eukaryotes) or ahead of us (self-destruction of technological civilisations). Others propose that civilisations exist but are unobserved — because interstellar colonisation is uneconomical, because they deliberately avoid contact (the "zoo hypothesis"), because they communicate in ways we cannot detect, or because our search, conducted for only a few decades over a narrow range of frequencies and sky, is far too limited to justify strong conclusions. A further family suggests they have visited or left artefacts, but no claim has withstood scientific scrutiny.
