The Megarian school of philosophy flourished in ancient Greece in the 4th century BCE. It was founded by Euclid of Megara, a pupil of Socrates, who combined Socratic ethics with the Eleatic doctrine of the unity of being: Euclid taught that the good is one, though called by many names, and that its opposite does not exist. The Megarians became famous — and controversial — for their devotion to dialectic and logical argument, earning comparison with the eristic disputants Plato criticized.

Members

Euclid's successors included Eubulides of Miletus, famed for paradoxes that are still discussed today: the liar paradox ("a man says he is lying"), the sorites or heap paradox, the horned man, and the bald man. Diodorus Cronus developed influential accounts of possibility and the conditional, defending a form of fatalism through his "Master Argument," which argued that only what is or will be true is possible. Stilpo, the last prominent Megarian, attracted large audiences, taught Zeno of Citium (the founder of Stoicism), and emphasized self-sufficiency and the rejection of universal predication.

Doctrine

The Megarians apparently denied the reality of potentiality: Diodorus held that a thing is possible only if it is or will be actual, against which Aristotle argued at length in Book Theta of the Metaphysics. Stilpo argued that one thing cannot be predicated of another — that "the horse runs" does not express an identity — an attack on Platonic forms and ordinary predication reported by Plato in the Sophist and by later doxographers.

Influence

Through the Stoics, who inherited Megarian logic via Zeno's studies with Stilpo and Diodorus, the school's work on conditionals, modality, and paradox shaped Hellenistic logic and, through it, the later logical tradition. After the 3rd century BCE the school faded, merging into the broader Dialectical school with which ancient sources sometimes identify it.
