In Greek mythology, Sisyphus (Ancient Greek: Σίσυφος) was the founder and king of Ephyra (later Corinth), famed for his cunning and deceit. He is best known for his punishment in Tartarus: to roll an immense boulder up a hill, only for it to roll back down every time it neared the top — a task he must repeat for eternity.

Mythology

Sisyphus promoted navigation and commerce but was a devious tyrant who killed guests and travellers, violating the sacred laws of hospitality. He outwitted death twice: he captured Thanatos (Death) and chained him, so that no one could die; and after his own death he persuaded Persephone to let him return to the living world to scold his wife for not burying him — then refused to go back. Hermes finally dragged him to the underworld.

Interpretations

From antiquity Sisyphus symbolised futile labour and punishment for hubris. In modern thought, Albert Camus's essay The Myth of Sisyphus (1942) made him the emblem of the absurd: humanity's search for meaning in a meaningless world. Camus concludes that "one must imagine Sisyphus happy," as he embraces his fate. The adjective Sisyphean describes tasks that are both laborious and futile.
