Vasoconstriction

Vasoconstriction is the narrowing of blood vessels resulting from contraction of the muscular wall of the vessels, particularly the large arteries and small arterioles. It is the opposite of vasodilation, the widening of blood vessels. Vasoconstriction raises blood pressure and is a normal mechanism for regulating blood flow and body temperature.

Mechanism

Contraction of vascular smooth muscle is driven by increased intracellular calcium. Vasoconstrictor signals include the sympathetic nervous system acting through alpha-adrenergic receptors, hormones such as angiotensin II, vasopressin and endothelin, and local factors such as cold. Substances that induce vasoconstriction are called vasoconstrictors or vasopressors.

Physiological roles

Vasoconstriction helps maintain arterial pressure, redirects blood toward vital organs during haemorrhage or exercise, and reduces heat loss from the skin in cold environments. Excessive or inappropriate vasoconstriction contributes to conditions such as Raynaud phenomenon, and vasospasm of coronary arteries can cause angina.

Clinical use

Vasoconstrictor drugs such as noradrenaline and phenylephrine are used to treat shock and hypotension; local anaesthetics are often combined with adrenaline to prolong their effect and reduce bleeding. Conversely, vasodilators are used to treat hypertension and heart failure.
