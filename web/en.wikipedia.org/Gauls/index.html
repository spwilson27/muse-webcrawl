Gauls

The Gauls (Latin: Galli) were a group of Celtic peoples of mainland Europe in the Iron Age and the Roman period, roughly the 5th century BC to the 5th century AD. Their territory, called Gaul by the Romans, corresponded roughly to present-day France, Belgium, Luxembourg and parts of Switzerland, Italy, the Netherlands and Germany. They spoke Gaulish, a Continental Celtic language.

History

Gallic culture developed out of the La Tène culture of the European Iron Age. Gallic tribes were never politically unified, though they shared language, religion — overseen by the priestly class of druids — and similar social structures, and could form large coalitions in wartime.

Gauls raided and settled widely: they sacked Rome around 390 BC, invaded the Balkans and Anatolia in the 3rd century BC (where the Galatians settled), and established Cisalpine Gaul in northern Italy. Julius Caesar conquered Gaul for Rome in the Gallic Wars of 58–50 BC, culminating in the defeat of the Arverni chieftain Vercingetorix at Alesia in 52 BC. Under Roman rule Gaul was heavily Romanised: Gaulish gradually died out in favour of Vulgar Latin, surviving longest in remote areas, and Gallo-Roman culture blended Celtic and Roman elements.

Society and culture

Classical writers portray Gallic society as divided between druids, a warrior aristocracy, and commoners. The Gauls were accomplished metalworkers, farmers and traders, known for inventions including the rotating millstone, chainmail and soap. Human sacrifice was alleged by Roman authors, though the extent of the practice is debated. Gallic deities included Taranis, Esus, Teutates, Lugus and the horse goddess Epona.

Legacy

The Gauls occupy a central place in French national identity — "our ancestors the Gauls" — and in popular culture, notably the Asterix comics, whose indomitable village resists Roman occupation.
