Western Desert campaign

Western Desert campaign
Dates: 11 June 1940 – 4 February 1943
Location: Libya and Egypt
Part of: North African campaign, Second World War
Result: Allied victory

The Western Desert campaign (or Desert War) took place in the deserts of Egypt and Libya and was the main theatre of the North African campaign of the Second World War. The campaign began in June 1940 with the Italian invasion of Egypt and see-sawed across the desert for nearly three years before ending in Allied victory in February 1943.

Course of the campaign

British and Commonwealth forces of the Western Desert Force, later the Eighth Army, first routed the Italian Tenth Army in Operation Compass (December 1940 – February 1941), advancing some 800 km and capturing about 130,000 prisoners. The arrival of the German Afrika Korps under Erwin Rommel in early 1941 reversed the position, and the fighting swung back and forth across Cyrenaica, punctuated by the sieges of Tobruk, the battles at Gazala and the First Battle of El Alamein (July 1942), which halted the Axis advance on Egypt.

Under General Bernard Montgomery, the Eighth Army won the decisive Second Battle of El Alamein (23 October – 11 November 1942), forcing the Axis into a long retreat westward through Libya. Combined with the Operation Torch landings in French North Africa, the campaign ended with the Axis trapped in Tunisia, where they surrendered in May 1943.

Character of the war

The desert war was notable for its mobility, the dominance of armour and anti-tank guns, long and vulnerable supply lines dependent on the ports of Tripoli, Benghazi, Tobruk and Alexandria, and the importance of air power and of Malta as a base for interdicting Axis convoys. Because it was fought in sparsely populated territory away from cities, it acquired a reputation as a comparatively "clean" war without much of the ideological brutality seen on the Eastern Front.
