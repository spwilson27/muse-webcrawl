Demosthenes (Greek: Δημοσθένης; 384–322 BCE) was a Greek statesman and orator of ancient Athens, generally considered the greatest of the Attic orators. His speeches constitute a major source for the political and social history of 4th-century Greece and reveal an unrelenting campaign to rouse Athens against the expansion of Philip II of Macedon.

Early life and training

Demosthenes' father, a wealthy sword-maker, died when he was seven, and his guardians squandered the estate. On coming of age he sued them (in the speeches Against Aphobus) and recovered part of the property, training himself as a speechwriter (logographer) in the process. Later tradition — not wholly reliable — told that he overcame a weak voice and a speech impediment by declaiming with pebbles in his mouth and shouting against the sound of the waves. He studied under Isaeus and possibly Plato.

Political career

From the 340s BCE Demosthenes dominated Athenian foreign policy debate, warning in the Olynthiacs (349 BCE) and the Philippics (351 BCE onward) that Philip intended to subjugate Greece. The word philippic still means a fiery denunciation. His efforts culminated in the alliance of Athens and Thebes, defeated by Philip at the Battle of Chaeronea in 338 BCE. In 336 BCE Ctesiphon proposed a golden crown for Demosthenes' services; Aeschines prosecuted the proposal, and Demosthenes' reply, On the Crown (330 BCE), is often judged the greatest speech of antiquity.

Exile and death

Convicted in the Harpalus bribery scandal (323 BCE), Demosthenes was fined and exiled, but recalled after Alexander the Great's death, when he helped organise the Lamian War against Macedon. After the Greek defeat at Crannon (322 BCE), the Macedonian general Antipater demanded the orators be surrendered; Demosthenes fled to the temple of Poseidon on Calauria and took poison rather than be captured.

Legacy

Some sixty speeches survive under his name (a few are by other hands). Cicero modelled his own Philippics against Mark Antony on them, and Quintilian held Demosthenes the supreme exemplar of oratorical force. His career remains the classic case of rhetoric deployed in defence of a free city against a rising monarchy.
