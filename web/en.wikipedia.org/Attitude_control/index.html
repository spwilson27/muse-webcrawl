Attitude control

Attitude control is the process of controlling the orientation of an aircraft, spacecraft or other vehicle with respect to an inertial frame of reference or another entity such as a celestial body or a target. For spacecraft, the attitude determination and control system (ADCS) senses the vehicle's orientation and applies torques to achieve and maintain the desired pointing, for example to aim instruments, solar arrays and antennas.

Spacecraft orientation is described about the pitch, yaw and roll axes.

Sensors

Attitude is determined using sensors such as Sun sensors, Earth (horizon) sensors, star trackers, magnetometers, and inertial sensors including gyroscopes and accelerometers. Sensor measurements are combined, often with a Kalman filter, to estimate the spacecraft's orientation and angular velocity.

Actuators

Torques are generated by reaction wheels and control moment gyroscopes, which exchange angular momentum with the spacecraft; by magnetic torquers (magnetorquers), which interact with the Earth's magnetic field; and by small thrusters. Reaction wheels accumulate momentum from external disturbance torques and must periodically be "desaturated" using thrusters or magnetorquers.

Passive methods

Some spacecraft use passive stabilisation: spin stabilisation keeps an axis fixed by gyroscopic stiffness; gravity-gradient stabilisation exploits the tidal torque on an elongated body to align it with the local vertical; and aerodynamic or solar-pressure torques can be used in some orbits.

Disturbances

External torques arise from gravity gradients, atmospheric drag, solar radiation pressure and the Earth's magnetic field; internal disturbances include moving parts, fuel slosh and flexible appendages. Control laws such as PID control and quaternion feedback are used to reject these disturbances and steer the craft between orientations (slews).
