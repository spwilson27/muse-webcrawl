Pruning

Pruning fruit trees improves air flow, light penetration and crop quality.

Pruning is a horticultural and silvicultural practice involving the selective removal of parts of a plant, such as branches, buds or roots. Reasons for pruning include removing deadwood, shaping the plant (for instance in hedging, topiary and bonsai), improving health by increasing airflow and sunlight penetration, and directing growth to increase the yield and quality of flowers and fruit.

Techniques

Common operations include deadheading spent flowers, thinning crowded branches, heading back shoots to encourage bushiness, and removing suckers and water sprouts. Good practice cuts just outside the branch collar so the tree can seal the wound, and avoids leaving stubs or tearing bark. The correct season depends on the species: many deciduous trees are pruned in winter dormancy, spring-flowering shrubs just after blooming, and stone fruits in summer to reduce disease risk. Tools range from secateurs and loppers to pruning saws and pole saws.

In viticulture and forestry

Annual winter pruning of grapevines — cane or spur pruning — is the principal means of controlling yield and vine balance. In forestry, pruning the lower branches of timber trees produces knot-free "clear" wood of higher value. Excessive pruning, however, stresses plants, invites infection and sunscald, and topping trees is widely condemned by arborists.

Other meanings

By analogy, "pruning" is used in neuroscience for synaptic pruning, the developmental elimination of synapses, and in computing for the removal of branches from search trees and of weights from neural networks.
