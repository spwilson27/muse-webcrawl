Andrew Cecil Bradley (26 March 1851 – 2 September 1935) was an English literary scholar, best known for his book Shakespearean Tragedy (1904), one of the most influential works of Shakespeare criticism of the twentieth century.

Life and career

Bradley was educated at Cheltenham College and Balliol College, Oxford, where he became a fellow. He was appointed the first professor of literature at the new University College, Liverpool (1882), then professor of language and literature at Glasgow (1889), and in 1901 was elected Professor of Poetry at Oxford, the chair from which his celebrated lectures on Shakespeare's tragedies were delivered. He was the younger brother of the idealist philosopher F. H. Bradley.

Shakespearean Tragedy

In Shakespearean Tragedy Bradley analysed Hamlet, Othello, King Lear, and Macbeth, treating the protagonists as rounded psychological characters whose inner conflicts drive the catastrophe, in a broadly Hegelian reading of tragedy. Later critics — notably L. C. Knights in "How Many Children Had Lady Macbeth?" (1933) — attacked this character-based method, yet Bradley's close attention to the plays' moral and emotional structure has kept the book continuously in print and central to Shakespeare studies. His other works include Oxford Lectures on Poetry (1909) and A Miscellany (1929).
