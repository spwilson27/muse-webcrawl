Motion planning

[Image: Motion planning for a robot arm among obstacles]

Motion planning, also known as the piano mover's problem, is a computational problem in robotics and computer science: given a start and a goal configuration of an object or robot, find a sequence of valid configurations that moves it to the goal while avoiding collisions with obstacles. The term was coined in this context by Tomas Lozano-Perez. The problem is usually formulated in the configuration space (C-space), which parameterizes all possible positions of the system; obstacles map to forbidden regions of C-space, and planning reduces to finding a path through free space.

Algorithms

Exact methods include cell decomposition, roadmaps such as visibility graphs and Voronoi diagrams, and potential field methods. Because exact planning is computationally hard (the generalized mover's problem is PSPACE-hard), practice relies heavily on sampling-based planners: probabilistic roadmaps (PRM) sample many configurations and connect them into a graph, while rapidly exploring random trees (RRT) and their optimal variants (RRT*) grow trees from the start or goal. Grid-based search (A*) is common for low-dimensional problems, and trajectory optimization smooths the resulting paths.

Applications

Motion planning underlies robot manipulation, self-driving vehicles, automated warehouse systems, computer-aided design and assembly verification, animation of digital characters, and computational biology (for example, modelling protein folding and ligand docking). Variants address dynamic environments, moving obstacles, kinodynamic constraints, and multi-robot systems.
