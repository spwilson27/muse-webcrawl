Electron shell

An electron shell is the set of atomic orbitals that share the same principal quantum number n, forming a group of electrons at a roughly similar distance and energy around an atomic nucleus. Shells are conventionally labelled K, L, M, N, ... corresponding to n = 1, 2, 3, 4, ...; shell n can hold at most 2n² electrons.

Subshells

Each shell is divided into subshells labelled s, p, d, and f according to the azimuthal quantum number. The K shell has one (1s); the L shell has 2s and 2p; the M shell adds 3d; and so on. Electrons fill orbitals approximately in order of increasing energy (the Aufbau principle), which explains the structure of the periodic table and the chemical behaviour of elements.

History

The shell concept grew out of Niels Bohr's 1913 model of the atom and Charles Glover Barkla's earlier naming of X-ray absorption shells (K and L) in 1911. Arnold Sommerfeld, Edmund Stoner, and Wolfgang Pauli refined the scheme in the 1920s: Pauli's exclusion principle (1925) fixed each orbital's capacity at two electrons of opposite spin, giving the 2n² rule.

Chemical significance

The electrons in the outermost shell — the valence shell — largely determine an element's chemical properties and bonding. Atoms with complete valence shells, like the noble gases, are notably unreactive, while atoms tend to lose, gain, or share electrons to approach such configurations (the octet rule).
