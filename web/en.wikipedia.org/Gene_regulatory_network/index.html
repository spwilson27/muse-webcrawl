Gene regulatory network

A gene regulatory network (GRN) is a collection of molecular regulators that interact with each other and with other substances in the cell to govern the gene expression levels of mRNA and proteins. These regulators are mainly transcription factors — proteins that bind to DNA near genes and increase or decrease their transcription — but also include signalling molecules, microRNAs, and chromatin-modifying factors.

Structure

A GRN is conventionally drawn as a directed graph in which nodes represent genes (or their protein products) and edges represent regulatory influences, either activation or repression. Recurring patterns of connection, called network motifs, appear far more often than chance would predict; common motifs include the feed-forward loop and negative autoregulation. Networks are hierarchical and modular: small subcircuits controlling particular developmental decisions, such as the patterning of the sea urchin embryo, are embedded in larger networks that coordinate the whole organism.

Function in development

Gene regulatory networks are central to development and evolution. During embryogenesis, cascades of transcription factors progressively restrict cell fates, turning broad regions of the embryo into specialised tissues. Because the architecture of these networks constrains what changes mutation can produce, evolutionary biologists study GRNs to understand how body plans evolve; changes in regulatory DNA, rather than in protein-coding sequence, are thought to account for much morphological evolution.

Inference and modelling

Experimentally, GRNs are mapped using gene expression profiling, chromatin immunoprecipitation, and perturbation experiments, and are modelled mathematically with Boolean networks, ordinary differential equations, and Bayesian methods. Inferring complete, accurate networks from data remains a major challenge in systems biology because of the scale of the interactions involved.
