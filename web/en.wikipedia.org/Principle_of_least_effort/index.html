Principle of least effort

A "desire path" — a physical trace of least-effort behaviour.

The principle of least effort is a broad theory in information science, linguistics, and sociology proposing that humans and other animals, when choosing between alternative courses of action, tend to select the path that requires the least perceived effort or work. The principle was articulated most fully by the linguist George Kingsley Zipf in his 1949 book Human Behavior and the Principle of Least Effort.

Formulation

Zipf proposed that individuals strive to minimise the probable average rate of work over the future, balancing immediate effort against expected later effort. The theory was used to explain a range of regularities, including Zipf's law for word frequencies — speakers economise by reusing a small vocabulary of short, frequent words, while listeners' need for precision pushes in the opposite direction, producing the observed power-law distributions. Earlier statements of related ideas include the "law of least effort" in the writings of Guillaume Ferrero and the economist-of-motion ideas in scientific management.

Information behaviour

In library and information science the principle is a cornerstone of research on information seeking: users generally prefer convenient, easily accessible sources over higher-quality but harder-to-reach ones, consult people before documents, and stop searching once a "good enough" answer is found. This behaviour underlies design principles for catalogues and search interfaces, and is related to Mooers' law and to Herbert Simon's concept of satisficing.

Related phenomena

Similar minimisation principles appear throughout the sciences: the principle of least action in physics, least-effort tendencies in animal foraging (optimal foraging theory), and the worn "desire paths" that pedestrians create across lawns. Critics note that effort is context-dependent and that people sometimes deliberately choose effortful routes for signalling, learning, or enjoyment.
