Diptych

A diptych (from the Greek δίπτυχον, diptychon, "two-fold") is any object with two flat plates which form a pair, often attached by a hinge. In art, a diptych is a work of art consisting of two pieces or panels that together create a single art piece; the individual pieces can be joined (e.g. by hinges) or presented together. In ancient times, diptychs were writing tablets: two wooden or ivory tablets joined so that the inner waxed surfaces could be written on and protected.

History

Late-antique consular diptychs — elaborately carved ivory panels distributed by Roman consuls to mark their term of office — are among the finest surviving examples of late Roman art; famous examples celebrated consuls such as Anicius Faustus Albinus Basilius (541 CE). The Church adopted the term for lists of names of the living and the dead commemorated in the liturgy, inscribed on folding tablets.

In medieval and early Renaissance art, small hinged diptychs served as portable devotional altarpieces for private prayer and travel. Panel pairs often showed the Virgin and Child on one wing and a Crucifixion, saint, or kneeling donor portrait on the other. Notable examples include the Wilton Diptych (c. 1395–1399), showing Richard II of England presented to the Virgin and Child, and works by Jan van Eyck and Rogier van der Weyden.

Modern usage

Contemporary artists use the diptych format to place two images in dialogue — contrasting times of day, viewpoints, or states — while photographers speak of diptychs as paired prints displayed together. The word is also used figuratively for any two-part work, such as paired poems, films or essays. Larger multi-panel formats are the triptych (three panels) and polyptych (many panels).
