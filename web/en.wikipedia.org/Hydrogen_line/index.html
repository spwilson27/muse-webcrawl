The hydrogen line, 21-centimeter line, or H I line is the electromagnetic radiation spectral line created by a change in the energy state of neutral hydrogen atoms. It is produced by the hyperfine spin-flip transition in which the relative spin of the proton and electron in a hydrogen atom changes from parallel to antiparallel. The radiation has a frequency of about 1420.4 MHz and a wavelength of 21.1 cm, in the microwave region of the spectrum.

Discovery and astronomical use

The line was predicted in 1944 by the Dutch astronomer Hendrik van de Hulst and was first detected in 1951 independently by Harold Ewen and Edward Purcell at Harvard and by Dutch and Australian groups. Because interstellar dust does not obscure it, the 21 cm line allows astronomers to map neutral hydrogen throughout the Milky Way and other galaxies, revealing spiral structure and galactic rotation curves; the discrepancy between observed rotation and visible mass was early evidence for dark matter. Redshifted 21 cm radiation from the early universe is a target for cosmology experiments studying the cosmic dark ages and reionization.

Role in SETI and the Pioneer plaque

The hydrogen line is regarded as a natural "water hole" frequency for interstellar communication searches. The transition was used as a unit of length and time on the Pioneer plaques and in the design of the Voyager Golden Record, since any scientifically literate civilisation could be expected to know it.
