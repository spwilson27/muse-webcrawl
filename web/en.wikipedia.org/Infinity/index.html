Infinity

Symbol∞ (lemniscate)
FieldsMathematics, philosophy, physics, theology
Key figuresAristotle, Cantor, Hilbert, Dedekind

Infinity is something that is boundless, endless, or larger than any natural number. It is often denoted by the infinity symbol ∞, introduced by John Wallis in 1655. Since the time of the ancient Greeks, the philosophical study of infinity has raised questions about whether an actual, completed infinity can exist, or only a potential infinity of processes that can always be extended.

History

Aristotle distinguished potential infinity — such as counting numbers, which can always be continued — from actual infinity, which he denied could exist in nature. Zeno's paradoxes probed the infinite divisibility of space and motion. In the 19th century, Bernard Bolzano and especially Georg Cantor transformed the subject: Cantor's set theory showed that infinite sets come in different sizes, or cardinalities, with the set of real numbers strictly larger than the set of natural numbers, and his diagonal argument became a cornerstone of modern mathematics.

In mathematics

Infinity appears throughout mathematics: in calculus as the limit concept, in the extended real number line with its two infinities, in projective geometry as points at infinity, and in set theory as the transfinite cardinal and ordinal numbers ℵ0, ℵ1, and beyond. David Hilbert's "hotel" thought experiment illustrates the counterintuitive arithmetic of infinite sets: a fully occupied hotel with infinitely many rooms can still accommodate new guests.

In physics and philosophy

Physical theories usually treat infinities — such as the infinite density of a classical black-hole singularity — as signs that a theory is incomplete. Philosophers and theologians have debated infinity in connection with the nature of God, the eternity of the universe, and the possibility of an infinite past, as in Kant's first antinomy and in medieval cosmological arguments.
