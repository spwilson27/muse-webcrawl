Emptiness (Sanskrit: śūnyatā; Pali: suññatā) is a central concept in Buddhism, particularly in the Mahayana tradition, denoting the absence of inherent, independent existence (svabhāva) in all phenomena. According to the doctrine, things do not exist by themselves but arise dependently, through causes, conditions and conceptual designation — a teaching closely connected to dependent origination (pratītyasamutpāda) and the doctrine of no-self (anattā).

Development

In early Buddhism, emptiness referred chiefly to the absence of a permanent self in persons and phenomena. The Perfection of Wisdom (Prajñāpāramitā) sutras, composed from around the 1st century BCE, radicalised the teaching: the Heart Sutra famously declares "form is emptiness, emptiness is form". The philosopher Nāgārjuna (c. 150–250 CE), founder of the Madhyamaka ("Middle Way") school, systematised emptiness in his Mūlamadhyamakakārikā, arguing that emptiness itself must also be empty, lest it become another absolute.

Interpretations

In Madhyamaka, emptiness is a negation of intrinsic existence, not a claim that nothing exists at all: conventionally, phenomena function and can be experienced, but ultimately they lack essence. The Tibetan tradition distinguishes the Prāsaṅgika and Svātantrika readings of Nāgārjuna. In East Asian Buddhism, emptiness interacts with the Tiantai doctrine of the three truths and with Zen practice, where direct insight into emptiness is cultivated through meditation and kōans.

Emptiness and nihilism

Buddhist teachers emphasise that emptiness is not nihilism. Nāgārjuna warned that emptiness wrongly grasped — "like a snake grasped by the wrong end" — destroys the one who misunderstands it. Properly understood, emptiness supports compassion, since the lack of fixed boundaries between self and others undercuts selfish attachment.
