Voronoi diagram

In mathematics, a Voronoi diagram is a partition of a plane into regions close to each of a given set of objects. Each region, or Voronoi cell, consists of all points closer to one particular object (a seed or site) than to any other. The diagram is named after the Ukrainian mathematician Georgy Voronoi, who defined and studied the general n-dimensional case in 1908; equivalent constructions had been used by Descartes and by Dirichlet, whose name attaches to the 2D case ("Dirichlet tessellation").

Properties

The dual graph of the Voronoi diagram is the Delaunay triangulation: connecting the seeds whose cells share an edge gives the triangulation that maximises the minimum angle of its triangles. In the plane, a Voronoi diagram of n sites has at most 2n − 5 vertices and 3n − 6 edges. Algorithms such as Fortune's sweep-line method compute it in O(n log n) time.

Applications

Voronoi diagrams appear across science: nearest-neighbour search and facility placement in computational geometry; modelling crystal grains and cell territories in physics and biology; John Snow's 1854 analysis of cholera cases around the Broad Street pump; rainfall areas in climatology (Thiessen polygons); robot path planning; and art and architecture. In crystallography, the related Wigner–Seitz cell is the Voronoi cell of a lattice point.
