Living wage

A living wage is defined as the minimum income necessary for a worker to meet their basic needs — food, housing, clothing, healthcare, transport, and a modest margin for contingencies — in the place where they live. It differs from a statutory minimum wage, which is set by law and may fall below the cost of living, and from subsistence levels, which cover only survival.

History

The idea has roots in ancient and medieval ideas of a just wage and was articulated by thinkers from Adam Smith to Pope Leo XIII's encyclical Rerum novarum (1891). In the United States, living wage ordinances began in Baltimore in 1994, requiring city contractors to pay wages above the federal minimum; hundreds of municipalities followed. In the United Kingdom, the Living Wage Foundation (growing out of a 2001 Citizens UK campaign in London) accredits employers paying a voluntary rate calculated from actual living costs, distinct from the government's statutory "National Living Wage" introduced in 2016.

Calculation

Methodologies such as the Anker methodology estimate a local living wage from the cost of a nutritious diet, decent housing, other essentials, and a small margin (often 5%) for unforeseen events. Rates therefore vary sharply by place: living wages in major metropolitan areas are far higher than in rural districts, and family composition matters — most calculations assume one or two earners supporting children.

Debate

Supporters argue that a living wage reduces dependence on public benefits, turnover, and in-work poverty. Critics contend that mandates may raise costs, reduce employment or hours, and that anti-poverty goals are better pursued through tax credits and transfers. Evidence from minimum-wage research informs the debate, though living wage campaigns have largely proceeded voluntarily or through procurement rules.
