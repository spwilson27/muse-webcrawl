Modernist poetry


Period: late 19th – mid 20th century
Key figures: T. S. Eliot, Ezra Pound, Wallace Stevens, W. B. Yeats, Marianne Moore
Key works: The Waste Land (1922), The Cantos

Modernist poetry refers to poetry written in Europe and North America between roughly 1890 and 1950, characterized by a self-conscious break with traditional ways of writing in both form and content. Modernist poets experimented with free verse, collage, allusion, and fragmentation, and often addressed themes of disillusionment, urbanization, and the collapse of inherited certainties after the First World War.

Origins

The movement grew out of late-19th-century developments including French symbolism, the work of poets such as Stéphane Mallarmé and Arthur Rimbaud, and the influence of Walt Whitman and Emily Dickinson in America. Ezra Pound's Imagist movement, launched around 1912, with its insistence on direct treatment of the "thing" and economy of language, was an important early current.

Major works and poets

T. S. Eliot's The Waste Land (1922) became the emblematic modernist long poem, combining multiple voices, quotations in several languages, and extensive scholarly notes. Other central figures include W. B. Yeats, Wallace Stevens, William Carlos Williams, H.D. (Hilda Doolittle), Marianne Moore, and Hart Crane. In other languages, modernist poetry flourished in the work of poets such as Fernando Pessoa, Rainer Maria Rilke, and Federico García Lorca.

Characteristics

Common features include the use of free verse, juxtaposition and montage, densely allusive language, irony, the persona or mask, and a concern with the difficulty of meaning in the modern world. Many modernist poems demand active interpretation from the reader, and the movement generated a large body of accompanying criticism and manifesto writing.
