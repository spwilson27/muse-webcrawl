Silenus, companion and tutor of Dionysus, in classical art.

In Greek mythology, Silenus was a companion and tutor to the wine god Dionysus. He is typically older than the satyrs of the Dionysian retinue (the thiasos) and is shown as a jovial old man, fat, bald, and snub-nosed, often riding a donkey or supported by attendants because he is perpetually drunk. His kind, the sileni, were wild woodland beings, part horse and part man.

Myths

Silenus was the foster father and tutor of Dionysus, accompanying the god on his travels. In one famous tale, Silenus wandered drunk into the rose gardens of King Midas of Phrygia, who treated him hospitably; in gratitude, Dionysus granted Midas the golden touch. Another tradition held that when captured and forced to speak, Silenus uttered words of bleak wisdom — most famously the saying that the best thing for a human being is never to have been born, and the second best is to die soon — a pessimism echoed in Sophocles and discussed by Aristotle.

The "wisdom of Silenus"

The phrase became a byword in Greek thought. In The Birth of Tragedy, Friedrich Nietzsche contrasted the grim wisdom of Silenus with Apollo's beautiful illusions, arguing that the Greeks transformed this terror into art.

In art and drama

Silenus was a stock character in satyr plays, where he appeared as the father of the satyr chorus. He features prominently in Euripides' Cyclops, the only complete surviving satyr play, and was a favourite subject of vase painters and sculptors.
