Fog

Fog is a visible aerosol consisting of tiny water droplets or ice crystals suspended in the air at or near the Earth's surface. It can be considered a low-lying type of cloud usually characterised by misty or murky conditions. Fog is distinguished from mist when visibility drops below 1 kilometre (0.62 mi); otherwise the same phenomenon is called mist or haze.

Formation

Fog forms when the difference between air temperature and dew point is less than about 2.5 °C and the air becomes saturated, causing water vapour to condense on microscopic condensation nuclei. It commonly occurs in humid air near water bodies, after rain, or in low valleys on clear calm nights.

Types

Radiation fog forms overnight as the ground cools. Advection fog forms when warm moist air moves over a cold surface — famously along the Californian coast, where it streams through San Francisco's Golden Gate. Steam fog rises when cold air passes over warmer water. Other varieties include upslope fog, valley fog, freezing fog, and ice fog at very low temperatures.

Effects

Fog is a major hazard for road, air, and marine transport; dense fog has contributed to many multi-vehicle collisions and to aviation accidents, and it delays flights because take-off and landing minima require minimum visibility. Conversely, fog drip is an important water source for some ecosystems such as coastal redwood forests, and fog harvesting with mesh nets supplies drinking water in arid coastal regions.

Dispersal

Fog usually "burns off" as the sun warms the ground, or is swept away by rising wind. Artificial dispersal by seeding or heating has been attempted at airports with limited success.
