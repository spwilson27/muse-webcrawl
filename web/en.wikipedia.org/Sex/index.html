Sex

Gametes: sperm and egg

Sex is the trait that determines whether a sexually reproducing organism produces male or female gametes. During sexual reproduction, a male and a female gamete fuse to form a zygote, which develops into an offspring that inherits genetic characteristics from both parents. By convention, the larger gamete (an ovum or egg) and the organism producing it are called female; the smaller gamete (a sperm cell) and its producer are called male.

Sexual reproduction

Sexual reproduction involves the formation of haploid gametes by meiosis, followed by fertilisation, in which two gametes fuse to restore the diploid chromosome number. Because offspring receive half their genes from each parent, sex creates genetic variation through recombination and independent assortment — variation thought to accelerate adaptation and help purge harmful mutations, offsetting the "twofold cost of sex" (only half of an asexual population's per-capita reproductive output in the form of childbearing individuals).

Systems of sex determination

Sex may be determined genetically — by sex chromosomes (the XY system of most mammals, the ZW system of birds), by chromosome number (haplodiploidy in bees and ants), or by single genes — or environmentally, as in the temperature-dependent sex determination of many reptiles. Many organisms are hermaphrodites, producing both kinds of gametes, simultaneously or sequentially. In anisogamous species the two sexes usually differ in secondary sexual characteristics (sexual dimorphism), shaped in part by sexual selection.

Across the tree of life

Sexual processes occur in nearly all eukaryotes; bacteria and archaea exchange genes by other means such as conjugation. Many plants are predominantly hermaphroditic, while some animals, fungi, and algae have more than two mating types. A minority of species reproduce only asexually, though wholly asexual lineages tend to be short-lived on evolutionary timescales.
