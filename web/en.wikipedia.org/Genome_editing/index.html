Genome editing

Genome editing (or gene editing) is a group of technologies that allow scientists to change an organism's DNA — inserting, deleting or replacing genetic material at targeted positions in the genome. Unlike earlier genetic engineering, which inserted genes at random locations, genome editing directs changes to specific sequences using engineered nucleases: "molecular scissors" that cut DNA at a chosen site and harness the cell's own repair machinery.

Technologies

Four main families of nucleases have been used: meganucleases, zinc finger nucleases (ZFNs), transcription activator-like effector nucleases (TALENs), and the CRISPR–Cas systems. CRISPR–Cas9, adapted from a bacterial immune system and described as a programmable editing tool in 2012 by Emmanuelle Charpentier, Jennifer Doudna and colleagues (who received the 2020 Nobel Prize in Chemistry), became dominant because its target is specified by an easily synthesised guide RNA. Newer methods — base editing (2016) and prime editing (2019) — change single letters or small stretches of DNA without making double-strand breaks.

Applications and ethics

Genome editing is used in basic research, crop improvement and medicine. In December 2023 regulators approved the first CRISPR-based therapy, Casgevy (exagamglogene autotemcel), for sickle cell disease and beta thalassaemia. Editing of the human germline remains widely prohibited or restricted; the 2018 announcement by He Jiankui that he had created gene-edited babies (the "CRISPR babies") drew international condemnation and led to his imprisonment.
