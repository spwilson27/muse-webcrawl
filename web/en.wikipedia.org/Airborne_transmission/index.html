Airborne transmission

Airborne transmission is the spread of an infectious agent through the air by dissemination of droplet nuclei (aerosols) that remain infectious when suspended in air over long distances and time. Airborne transmission can be obligate, preferential, or opportunistic depending on the pathogen.

Overview

Diseases capable of airborne transmission include tuberculosis, measles, and chickenpox. The particles involved are generally small — often defined as aerosols of less than about 5 micrometres in earlier conventions, though later research during and after the COVID-19 pandemic emphasized that larger particles can also remain suspended and that the droplet–aerosol distinction is a continuum rather than a sharp cutoff. Because aerosols can accumulate in poorly ventilated indoor spaces, ventilation, filtration, and respirator masks are important control measures.

History

The concept developed from the miasma theory of disease and was formalized in the 1930s by William F. Wells, who described droplet nuclei and the Wells curve relating droplet size to settling and evaporation. For much of the twentieth century, airborne transmission was accepted mainly for a small set of diseases, while droplet and contact routes were emphasized for respiratory viruses. The COVID-19 pandemic prompted extensive re-evaluation, and by 2021 public health bodies acknowledged that inhalation of aerosols is a major route of SARS-CoV-2 transmission.

Control measures

Measures against airborne transmission include improving indoor ventilation, using high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filtration, upper-room ultraviolet germicidal irradiation, airborne infection isolation rooms with negative pressure, and well-fitting respirators such as N95 masks.
