Parasitoid wasp

Parasitoid wasps are a very large group of hymenopteran insects whose larvae develop on or inside other arthropods, eventually killing the host. They are estimated to number well over 100,000 species across families such as the Ichneumonidae, Braconidae, and Chalcidoidea, making them among the most diverse of all insect groups.

Life cycle

A female wasp uses her ovipositor to lay eggs in, on, or near a host — often a caterpillar, aphid, or beetle larva. The wasp larvae consume the host from within, typically sparing vital organs until late in development, then pupate and emerge as adults, killing the host. Many species inject polydnaviruses that suppress the host's immune system.

Ecological and economic importance

Parasitoid wasps regulate populations of herbivorous insects and are widely used in biological pest control, for example Encarsia formosa against greenhouse whitefly. Their natural history famously troubled Charles Darwin, who wrote that the ichneumons' habits shook his belief in a beneficent designer.

Diversity

Parasitoidism has evolved many times within the Hymenoptera; some lineages are hyperparasitoids, parasitising other parasitoids.
