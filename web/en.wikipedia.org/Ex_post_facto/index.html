Ex post facto law

An ex post facto law is a law that retroactively changes the legal consequences of actions committed before the law's enactment. In criminal law, it may criminalize an act that was legal when committed, increase the punishment for an existing crime, or alter the rules of evidence to make conviction easier. The Latin phrase means "from a thing done afterward."

United States

The Constitution of the United States forbids ex post facto laws at both the federal level (Article I, Section 9) and the state level (Article I, Section 10). In Calder v. Bull (1798), Justice Samuel Chase described four categories of laws violating the prohibition: those criminalizing previously innocent acts, aggravating a crime, increasing its punishment, or changing rules of evidence to convict on less proof. The prohibition applies only to criminal or punitive laws, not to retroactive civil legislation, though disputes arise over whether measures such as sex-offender registration laws are punitive in effect, as in Stogner v. California (2003), which struck down a law reviving prosecutions after the statute of limitations had expired.

International law

The principle nulla poena sine lege (no punishment without law) is enshrined in the European Convention on Human Rights (Article 7) and the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights (Article 15), subject to an exception for acts criminal under general principles of law recognized by civilized nations — the basis on which the Nuremberg trials were defended against ex post facto objections.
