Ising model

The Ising model (German pronunciation: [ˈiːzɪŋ]) is a mathematical model of ferromagnetism in statistical mechanics. The model consists of discrete variables that represent magnetic dipole moments of atomic "spins" that can be in one of two states (+1 or −1) arranged on a lattice, allowing each spin to interact with its neighbours.

History and solution

The model was invented by the physicist Wilhelm Lenz in 1920 and studied by his student Ernst Ising, who in his 1924 thesis showed that the one-dimensional model has no phase transition. The two-dimensional square-lattice model in zero field was solved exactly by Lars Onsager in 1944, a landmark result revealing a continuous phase transition at a critical temperature Tc = 2J/(kB ln(1+√2)) ≈ 2.269 J/kB, and the first exact calculation of non-classical critical exponents.

Significance

Though no exact solution exists in three dimensions, the Ising model is the most studied model of phase transitions and a cornerstone of statistical physics. Its mathematics has been applied far beyond magnetism — to neural networks, opinion dynamics, image processing, and lattice gases.
