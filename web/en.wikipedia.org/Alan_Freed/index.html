Alan Freed

Albert James "Alan" Freed (December 15, 1921 – January 20, 1965) was an American disc jockey who became internationally known for promoting African-American rhythm and blues music on the radio in the United States and Europe under the name "rock and roll." Broadcasting from Cleveland and later New York, Freed introduced white teenage audiences to Black artists such as Chuck Berry, Little Richard, and Fats Domino, helping to launch the rock and roll era before his career was destroyed by the payola scandal.

Career

Freed began in radio in Ohio and, encouraged by record-store owner Leo Mintz, started playing rhythm and blues on WJW Cleveland in 1951, billing himself as "Moondog" and his programme the Moondog House. In March 1952 he staged the Moondog Coronation Ball, often called the first rock and roll concert; it was shut down by authorities after the arena overflowed. Moving to New York in 1954, his shows on WINS and WABC and his stage revues at the Brooklyn Paramount made him the most powerful DJ in America.

Payola scandal and death

Freed refused to sign an affidavit denying he took payola and was fired in 1959; in 1962 he pleaded guilty to commercial bribery, and federal tax charges followed. Unemployable and alcoholic, he died of uraemia and cirrhosis in 1965, aged 43. He was among the first inductees into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame in 1986.
