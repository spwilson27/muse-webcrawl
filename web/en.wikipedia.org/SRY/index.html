SRY

Full nameSex-determining region Y protein
LocationY chromosome (Yp11.2)
FunctionTranscription factor initiating male sex determination
Also known asTestis-determining factor (TDF)

SRY (sex-determining region Y) is a gene on the Y chromosome of therian mammals that encodes a transcription factor responsible for initiating male sex determination in the embryo. The SRY protein is also known as the testis-determining factor.

Function

In early development the gonads are bipotential, able to become either testes or ovaries. Around the sixth to seventh week of human gestation, SRY is expressed in the supporting cell precursors of the genital ridge. Its protein binds DNA through a high-mobility-group (HMG) box domain, bending the DNA and upregulating expression of the related gene SOX9. SOX9 drives differentiation of Sertoli cells, testis cord formation, and production of anti-Müllerian hormone, committing the embryo to male development.

Discovery

The gene was identified in 1990 by Peter Goodfellow's team, after the analysis of rare XX males and XY females localised the testis-determining factor to a small region of the Y chromosome. In 1991, experiments showing that adding Sry to XX mouse embryos caused them to develop as males confirmed its role as the master switch of sex determination.

Clinical significance

Mutations or deletions of SRY cause XY gonadal dysgenesis (Swyer syndrome), in which individuals with a Y chromosome develop as females with non-functional streak gonads. Translocation of SRY onto an X chromosome, usually through unequal crossing-over in paternal meiosis, produces XX males, who are typically sterile. Because of its role, SRY testing has historically been used in sex verification in sport, a practice now largely abandoned as scientifically and ethically problematic.

Evolution

SRY is thought to have evolved from the X-linked gene SOX3 roughly 180–200 million years ago in the ancestors of therian mammals. It is present in marsupials and placental mammals but absent from monotremes, which use a different sex-determining system.
