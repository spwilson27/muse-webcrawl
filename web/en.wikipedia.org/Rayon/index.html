Rayon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Rayon is a semi-synthetic fibre made from regenerated cellulose, typically derived from wood pulp or cotton waste. It was developed in the late 19th century as "artificial silk" and became the first commercially successful manufactured fibre, preceding fully synthetic fibres such as nylon by decades. Because it is made from a natural polymer but is not itself naturally occurring, it is classed as a regenerated or semi-synthetic fibre; in much of the world the generic name viscose is used for rayon made by the viscose process. Rayon is soft, smooth, moisture-absorbent and drapes well, and is used in clothing, furnishings, tyre cord, surgical products and nonwoven fabrics.

History

George Audemars patented a nitrocellulose fibre in 1855, and Count Hilaire de Chardonnet industrialised "Chardonnet silk" in France in 1884–1889. The decisive viscose process — treating cellulose with caustic soda and carbon disulfide to make a spinnable solution — was patented in Britain in 1892 by Charles Cross, Edward Bevan and Clayton Beadle, and commercial production began in 1905; the American Viscose Company coined the name "rayon" in 1924 to replace "artificial silk" in the United States. Production peaked in the 1940s, then lost share to synthetics, but continues at several million tonnes per year, mainly in Asia.

Production and properties

In the viscose process, purified cellulose is steeped in sodium hydroxide, shredded, aged, reacted with carbon disulfide to form cellulose xanthate, dissolved in dilute alkali to form the viscous solution (hence "viscose"), and extruded through spinnerets into an acid bath that regenerates the cellulose as filaments. Variants produce high-tenacity tyre yarn, high-wet-modulus modal, and — by a different, solvent-based route — lyocell (sold as Tencel). Rayon breathes and absorbs moisture better than polyester, feels cool against the skin and takes dyes brilliantly, but it is weaker when wet, wrinkles easily, and may shrink. The traditional process is polluting — carbon disulfide emissions endanger workers and air and water quality — which has driven factory closures in the West and research into closed-loop and ionic-liquid processes.
