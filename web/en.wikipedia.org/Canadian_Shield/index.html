Canadian Shield

The Canadian Shield (French: Bouclier canadien), also called the Laurentian Plateau, is a geologic shield — a large area of exposed Precambrian igneous and high-grade metamorphic rocks — that forms the ancient geological core of North America. It extends over about 8 million square kilometres, covering eastern and central Canada and parts of the northern United States, from the Great Lakes to the Arctic Ocean and Hudson Bay.

Geology

The Shield is composed of some of the oldest rocks on Earth, including the Acasta Gneiss of the Northwest Territories (about 4 billion years old) and the Nuvvuagittuq Greenstone Belt in Quebec. It was assembled from several Archean cratons and Proterozoic mountain belts welded together during collisions of ancient continents, then worn down over billions of years. Repeated glaciations during the Pleistocene scraped away most soil, leaving thin soils, exposed bedrock, and a deranged drainage pattern of countless lakes, swamps, and rivers.

Geography and ecology

The Shield surrounds Hudson Bay in a giant horseshoe. Its southern margins support the boreal forest, one of the world's largest tracts of coniferous woodland, grading northward into tundra beyond the tree line. The region is sparsely populated but rich in resources: metallic minerals (nickel at Sudbury, iron, gold, uranium, diamonds), timber, and hydroelectric power from its many rivers.

History

Indigenous peoples have inhabited the Shield for millennia. From the seventeenth century, the fur trade drew European traders along its rivers and lakes, using canoe routes developed by First Nations. Mining and forestry later brought small industrial towns, but much of the Shield remains wilderness, accessible mainly by floatplane or winter road.
