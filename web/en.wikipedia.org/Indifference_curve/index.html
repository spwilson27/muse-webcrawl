Indifference curve

In economics, an indifference curve connects combinations of two goods or commodities that give a consumer the same level of satisfaction or utility, so that the consumer is indifferent between any of them. Indifference curves are a central device of modern consumer theory: together with the budget constraint, they determine the consumer's optimal choice without requiring utility to be measured in absolute ("cardinal") units — only ranked ("ordinal").

Properties

Standard indifference curves are drawn under conventional assumptions about preferences:

  • Downward sloping — if a bundle has less of one good it must have more of the other to keep utility constant.
  • Convex to the origin — reflecting a diminishing marginal rate of substitution (MRS): the more of a good a consumer already has, the less of the other good they will give up to obtain still more of it.
  • Non-intersecting — curves cannot cross, since that would contradict transitivity of preferences.
  • Higher curves are preferred — under monotonicity ("more is better"), bundles on curves further from the origin yield greater utility.

The slope of the curve at any point is the (negative of the) marginal rate of substitution, equal to the ratio of the marginal utilities of the two goods. The set of all indifference curves for a consumer is called an indifference map.

Consumer optimum

A consumer maximises utility by reaching the highest indifference curve attainable within the budget constraint, whose slope equals the (negative of the) price ratio. At an interior optimum the two are tangent: the MRS equals the ratio of prices. Special cases include perfect substitutes (straight-line curves), perfect complements (L-shaped curves, as with left and right shoes), and "bad" goods, which give curves of positive slope.

History

The concept was introduced by Francis Ysidro Edgeworth in Mathematical Psychics (1881) and developed by Vilfredo Pareto, who showed that only ordinal rankings were needed. John Hicks and R. G. D. Allen's 1934 paper "A Reconsideration of the Theory of Value" made indifference-curve analysis the standard presentation in textbooks, and it underlies the Slutsky and Hicksian decompositions of price changes into substitution and income effects.
