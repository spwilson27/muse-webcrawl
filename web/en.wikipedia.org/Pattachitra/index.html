Pattachitra is a traditional cloth-based scroll painting originating in the eastern Indian states of Odisha and West Bengal. The name derives from the Sanskrit words patta (canvas/cloth) and chitra (picture). It is known for its intricate details, mythological narratives, rich colours and creative motifs, and is one of the oldest and most popular art forms of Odisha, closely linked with the cult of Jagannath of Puri.

Origins and tradition

Pattachitra is believed to date back to around the 12th century, associated with the construction of the Jagannath Temple at Puri. The hereditary painters, called chitrakaras, traditionally served the temple; during the Anasara period, when the deity images were withdrawn for repainting, painted pattachitra surrogates (Anasara patti) were worshipped in their place. The village of Raghurajpur near Puri is a celebrated centre of the craft and has been designated a heritage village.

Technique

The canvas is prepared by coating cotton cloth with a paste of chalk powder and tamarind-seed gum, then rubbing it smooth with stones. Colours are traditionally natural: white from conch shells, black from lamp soot, yellow from orpiment (haritala), red from cinnabar or hingula, and blue from indigo. Fine brushes of animal hair are used. Finished paintings are often given a lacquer coating for sheen and protection. Subjects are drawn with bold outlines and flat colour application, framed by decorative floral borders.

Themes and variants

Odisha Pattachitra depicts Jagannath, Balabhadra and Subhadra, episodes from the Ramayana and Mahabharata, Krishna Lila, and the ten avatars of Vishnu (Dasavatara). Related forms include palm-leaf painting (talapattachitra) and the Bengal patachitra scrolls of the itinerant Patua painters, who unroll and sing their painted narratives. The art form received a Geographical Indication (GI) tag for Odisha.
