Heartland theory

Proposed byHalford Mackinder
First presented1904, "The Geographical Pivot of History"
FieldGeopolitics, geostrategy

The Heartland theory is a geopolitical concept proposed by the British geographer Sir Halford Mackinder in his 1904 paper "The Geographical Pivot of History" and developed in his 1919 book Democratic Ideals and Reality. It holds that control of the vast interior of Eurasia — the "Heartland" — is the key to world domination.

The theory

Mackinder divided the world into the "World-Island" (the combined continents of Europe, Asia and Africa), the offshore islands, and the outlying islands including the Americas and Australia. The Heartland, roughly the region drained by rivers flowing to the Arctic or to landlocked seas, was inaccessible to sea power and rich in resources. His famous summary ran: "Who rules East Europe commands the Heartland; who rules the Heartland commands the World-Island; who rules the World-Island commands the world."

Influence and criticism

The theory influenced strategic thinking during the Second World War and the Cold War, and was taken up — and modified — by thinkers including Nicholas Spykman, whose rival Rimland theory emphasised the coastal fringes of Eurasia. Critics argue that Mackinder underestimated air power, technology and the economic integration of coastal regions.
