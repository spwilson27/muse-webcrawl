Google LLC v. Oracle America, Inc., 593 U.S. 1 (2021), was a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States concerning the copyrightability of application programming interfaces (APIs) and fair use. The Court held that Google's copying of about 11,500 lines of "declaring code" from the Java SE API in building the Android platform constituted fair use as a matter of law, resolving an eleven-year legal battle between the two technology companies.

Background

When developing Android in the 2000s, Google — after licensing talks with Sun Microsystems failed — wrote its own implementation of the Java language environment but copied the names and declarations of 37 Java API packages (about 11,500 lines of declaring code) so that programmers familiar with Java could easily write for Android. Oracle, which acquired Sun in 2010, sued Google in August 2010 for copyright and patent infringement, seeking billions of dollars in damages.

Procedural history

In 2012, Judge William Alsup ruled that APIs were not copyrightable, but the Federal Circuit reversed in 2014, holding the declaring code and its structure, sequence, and organization copyrightable. A second jury in 2016 found Google's use to be fair use; the Federal Circuit again reversed in 2018, holding the use was not fair as a matter of law. Google petitioned the Supreme Court, which heard arguments (by telephone, owing to the COVID-19 pandemic) in October 2020.

Decision

By a 6–2 majority, Justice Breyer's opinion assumed without deciding that the code was copyrightable, but held Google's copying was fair use. The Court weighed the nature of the work (functional, declaring code at the boundary of copyrightable expression), the purpose and character of the use (reimplementation enabling programmers to use their skills on a new platform), the amount used (0.4% of the API, only what was needed for interoperability), and market effects. Justice Thomas, joined by Justice Alito, dissented; Justice Barrett did not participate.

Significance

The decision was widely seen as a major victory for software interoperability and developers, preserving the common industry practice of reimplementing APIs, though the Court deliberately left the broader copyrightability of APIs unresolved.
