Gaussian process

In probability theory and statistics, a Gaussian process (GP) is a stochastic process — a collection of random variables indexed by time, space, or another set — such that every finite collection of those random variables has a multivariate normal distribution. A Gaussian process is completely specified by its mean function and its covariance function (or kernel). Gaussian processes are named after Carl Friedrich Gauss because of their reliance on the Gaussian (normal) distribution.

Gaussian process regression

In machine learning, Gaussian processes are used as a prior over functions for nonparametric Bayesian regression and classification, a technique also known in spatial statistics as kriging. Observing noisy data updates the prior to a posterior process whose mean serves as a prediction and whose variance quantifies uncertainty, growing away from observed points. The choice of kernel — such as the squared exponential (radial basis function) kernel or the Matérn family — encodes assumptions about smoothness and length scale, and kernel hyperparameters are commonly learned by maximising the marginal likelihood.

Applications and limitations

Gaussian processes are applied in geostatistics, computer experiments and surrogate modelling, Bayesian optimisation for tuning expensive black-box functions, time-series analysis, and robotics. Their main drawback is computational cost: exact inference requires inverting an n × n covariance matrix, an O(n3) operation, which has motivated sparse and inducing-point approximations for large datasets.
