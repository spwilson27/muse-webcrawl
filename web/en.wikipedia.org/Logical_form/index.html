Logical form

In logic, the logical form of a statement or argument is a precisely specified semantic version of that statement in a formal system, isolating its structure from its content. Informally, logical form is the way statements and arguments are represented so that features relevant to validity can be assessed.

In formal logic

Translating a natural-language sentence into a formal language such as propositional or first-order logic replaces names with variables and predicates, and connectives with symbols like ¬, ∧, ∨ and quantifiers ∀ and ∃. Two statements with the same logical form share their validity properties: an argument is valid in virtue of its form if every interpretation making the premises true also makes the conclusion true.

In linguistics and philosophy

In philosophy of language and generative linguistics, logical form (LF) also denotes a level of syntactic representation that interfaces with semantics, invoked by philosophers such as Bertrand Russell — whose theory of descriptions analysed sentences like "The present King of France is bald" as quantificational statements — to explain scope, binding and other phenomena not evident in surface grammar.
