Marie-Georges-Jean Méliès (8 December 1861 – 21 January 1938) was a French magician, actor, and film director. He led many technical and narrative developments in the earliest days of cinema, primarily in the fantasy and science fiction genres. Méliès is credited with pioneering the use of special effects in film, including substitution splices, multiple exposures, time-lapse photography, dissolves, and hand-painted colour.

Career

Born into a wealthy family of shoemakers, Méliès worked as a stage magician at the Théâtre Robert-Houdin in Paris before attending one of the Lumière brothers' first film screenings in 1895. Unable to buy their camera, he built his own and began making films in 1896, eventually producing over 500 of them at his Star Film Company and his glass studio in Montreuil — one of the first film studios.

A Trip to the Moon

His most famous film, A Trip to the Moon (Le Voyage dans la Lune, 1902), loosely based on works by Jules Verne and H. G. Wells, depicts a group of astronomers travelling to the Moon in a cannon-fired capsule. Its image of the capsule landing in the Moon's eye remains one of the most iconic in cinema history. Other notable works include The Impossible Voyage (1904) and The Conquest of the Pole (1912).

Later years and legacy

Business difficulties and competition drove Méliès from filmmaking in 1913, and he was largely forgotten, reduced to selling toys at a kiosk in the Gare Montparnasse. He was rediscovered by film enthusiasts in the late 1920s and awarded the Legion of Honour in 1931. His innovations laid the groundwork for narrative and special-effects filmmaking.
