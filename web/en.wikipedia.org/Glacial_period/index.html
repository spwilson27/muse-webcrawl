Glacial period

A glacial period (or simply glacial) is an interval of time within an ice age that is marked by colder temperatures and glacier advances. The warmer intervals between glacials are called interglacials. Earth has experienced a series of major ice ages, most recently the Quaternary glaciation, which began 2.58 million years ago and continues today — the present Holocene epoch is an interglacial within it.

The last glacial period

The last glacial period, informally the "last ice age", lasted from about 115,000 to about 11,700 years ago. It reached its maximum extent, the Last Glacial Maximum, around 26,500–19,000 years ago, when ice sheets covered much of North America (the Laurentide Ice Sheet), northern Europe (the Fennoscandian Ice Sheet), and parts of Asia, and global sea level was roughly 120 metres lower than today. Regional names for this glaciation include the Wisconsin glaciation in North America, the Weichselian in northern Europe, the Würm in the Alps, and the Devensian in Britain.

Causes

Glacial cycles of the past million years have a dominant periodicity of about 100,000 years, paced by variations in Earth's orbit and axial tilt known as Milankovitch cycles, which alter the distribution of solar radiation. Feedbacks involving greenhouse gas concentrations, ice-albedo effects, and ocean circulation amplify these small forcings into large climate swings.

Evidence

Glacial periods are reconstructed from ice cores, deep-sea sediment cores (through oxygen isotope ratios in foraminifera), glacial deposits such as till and moraines, and erratics. See glacial erratic.
