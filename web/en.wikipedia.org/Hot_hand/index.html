Hot hand

The hot hand (also known as the hot hand phenomenon or hot hand fallacy) is the purported phenomenon that a person who experiences a successful outcome has a greater chance of success in further attempts. The concept is applied primarily to sports, notably basketball, where a player who has made several shots in a row is believed to be "on fire" and more likely to make the next one. A famous 1985 paper by Thomas Gilovich, Robert Vallone and Amos Tversky concluded that apparent hot hands in basketball shooting were consistent with random chance, and the term "hot-hand fallacy" entered psychology as the mistaken belief in streaks.

Subsequent research

Later research complicated the original conclusion. A 2015 paper by Joshua Miller and Adam Sanjurjo showed that Gilovich, Vallone and Tversky's analysis contained a subtle selection bias: in a finite sequence of random shots, the proportion of hits following a hit is, on average, lower than the base rate. Correcting for this bias reversed the finding—re-analysis of the original data suggested a modest but real hot hand effect. Subsequent analyses of richer data sets have generally found small positive streak effects, though the size and practical significance of the effect remain debated.

Related concepts

The hot hand is often contrasted with the gambler's fallacy, the mistaken belief that a random event is more likely after a run of opposite outcomes (e.g., that tails is "due" after a run of heads). Interestingly, the same person may hold both beliefs in different contexts: believing that skill-based streaks continue while chance-based streaks reverse. Belief in the hot hand also appears in financial markets, where investors chase fund managers with streaks of past outperformance.
