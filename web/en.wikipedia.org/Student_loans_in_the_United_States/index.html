Student loans in the United States

In the United States, student loans are a form of financial aid used to help students access higher education. Total outstanding student loan debt exceeds $1.6 trillion, held by more than 43 million borrowers, making it the second-largest category of household debt after mortgages. Loans are made both by the federal government — through the William D. Ford Federal Direct Loan Program — and by private lenders such as banks and state agencies.

History

Federally guaranteed student lending began with the Higher Education Act of 1965. For decades most federal loans were made by private lenders but guaranteed by the government under the Federal Family Education Loan (FFEL) program; since 2010, all new federal student loans have been made directly by the Department of Education.

Types of federal loans

  • Direct Subsidized Loans — for undergraduates with financial need; the government pays interest while the borrower is in school.
  • Direct Unsubsidized Loans — available to undergraduate and graduate students regardless of need; interest accrues from disbursement.
  • Direct PLUS Loans — for graduate students and parents of undergraduates, subject to a credit check.

Repayment

Standard repayment runs for ten years, but borrowers may choose extended, graduated, or income-driven repayment plans that cap payments at a percentage of discretionary income and forgive remaining balances after 20–25 years. The Public Service Loan Forgiveness program forgives balances after ten years of qualifying public-sector employment. Federal student loans are exceptionally difficult to discharge in bankruptcy.

Debate

Rising tuition, stagnant wages, and the size of the debt stock have made student loans a major political issue, with proposals ranging from targeted cancellation to broad-based debt forgiveness, including executive actions attempted in the 2020s.
