Cahokia was a pre-Columbian Native American city situated in present-day Illinois, across the Mississippi River from St. Louis, Missouri. The largest city ever built in North America north of Mexico before the late 18th century, it flourished during the Mississippian culture period, reaching its peak around 1100 CE with a population comparable to that of contemporary London.

History

The site was settled by around 600 CE, but the city underwent a rapid transformation — sometimes called the "big bang" — around 1050 CE, with massive construction of plazas, temples and mounds, and a sudden influx of population. Cahokia's influence spread along the Mississippi and its tributaries through trade and religion. The city declined from the 13th century, possibly owing to environmental pressure, flooding, resource depletion and social unrest, and was abandoned by about 1350 CE, before European contact.

Layout and mounds

The city contained as many as 120 earthen mounds arranged around plazas and protected by a wooden palisade. The largest, Monks Mound, rises about 30 metres (100 ft) and covers over 5 hectares (13 acres), making it the largest earthen structure in the Americas. Woodhenge, a circle of timber posts, served as a solar calendar. Mound 72 contained elite burials, including the "Birdman" burial and evidence of retainer sacrifice.

Society

Cahokia was supported by extensive maize agriculture in the fertile American Bottom floodplain, supplemented by hunting, fishing and trade reaching from the Great Lakes to the Gulf Coast. Archaeologists debate whether it was a chiefdom or an early state; either way it was the dominant centre of the Mississippian world.

Preservation

The Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site preserves the core of the city and was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1982. The name "Cahokia" derives from an unrelated Illiniwek clan that lived in the area when French explorers arrived.
