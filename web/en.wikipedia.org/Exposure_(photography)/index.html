Exposure (photography)

In photography, exposure is the amount of light per unit area reaching a frame of photographic film or the surface of an electronic image sensor. It is determined by the combination of the shutter speed (exposure time), the lens aperture (f-number), and the sensitivity of the medium (film speed or ISO setting). Exposure is measured in lux seconds and is given by the exposure value (EV) system for a given scene luminance.

The exposure triangle

The three parameters of exposure trade off against one another: doubling the exposure time, opening the aperture by one stop, or doubling the ISO sensitivity each increases exposure by one EV. However, each choice has side effects. Aperture controls depth of field; shutter speed controls motion blur; sensitivity controls image noise. A "correct" exposure is one that renders the scene with the brightness the photographer intends, measured with a light meter in the camera or a handheld meter, or estimated with rules such as the Sunny 16 rule.

Overexposure and underexposure

Too much light produces overexposure, in which highlight detail is lost ("blown highlights"); too little produces underexposure, in which shadows become murky and noise increases. Photographers deliberately depart from nominal exposure for creative effect — high-key and low-key styles — or to preserve detail, as in the maxim "expose for the highlights." Digital histograms and film latitude guide these decisions.

Reciprocity and techniques

The reciprocity law states that exposure depends on the product of illuminance and time; at extremely long or short exposures, film exhibits reciprocity failure. Long-exposure techniques are used in night and astrophotography, light painting, and neutral-density-filter work; exposure bracketing and high-dynamic-range imaging combine multiple exposures to cover scenes exceeding the sensor's dynamic range.
