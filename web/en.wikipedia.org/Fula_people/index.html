Fula people

The Fula people (also called Fulani, Fulɓe, Peul, or Fulbe) are one of the largest ethnic groups in Africa, numbering an estimated 38–40 million people, spread across many countries of West and Central Africa. They are traditionally nomadic or semi-nomadic cattle herders and traders, and the majority are Muslim.

History

The Fula are believed to have originated in the Senegal River valley region, migrating eastward and southward over many centuries with their zebu cattle. Between the 18th and 19th centuries, Fula-led Islamic jihads established states across the region, most notably the Sokoto Caliphate founded by Usman dan Fodio in 1804 in present-day Nigeria, the Imamate of Futa Jallon in Guinea, and the Macina Empire in Mali under Seku Amadu.

Culture

Fula society is guided by pulaaku, a code of conduct emphasizing patience and self-control (munyal), wisdom (hakkilo), modesty and reserve (semteende), and courage (tiinaande). Cattle hold deep economic and symbolic importance. Groups include the nomadic Wodaabe (Mbororo), famous for the Gerewol festival in Niger and Chad, as well as settled farming and urban Fula communities.

Distribution

The largest Fula populations are in Nigeria (roughly 15 million, often counted together with the Hausa as Hausa-Fulani), Guinea (where they form the largest ethnic group, around 40% of the population), Cameroon, Senegal, Mali, and Niger.

Religion

Islam spread among the Fula from around the 11th century and is today the near-universal faith. Fula scholars and clerics historically played a central role in spreading Islam across West Africa.
