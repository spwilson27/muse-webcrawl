Esotericism

An alchemical emblem in the tradition of Western esotericism

Esotericism is a term for a body of knowledge, practices, and traditions characterised by teachings that are held to be secret, hidden, or accessible only to initiates, or by claims to knowledge of hidden realities beyond ordinary perception. The word derives from the Greek esōterikós ("inner"), as contrasted with "exoteric" knowledge intended for the general public.

History

Ideas later classed as esoteric appear in late antiquity in Hermeticism, Gnosticism, and Neoplatonism. During the Renaissance, the translation of the Corpus Hermeticum and the influence of Kabbalah on thinkers such as Giovanni Pico della Mirandola gave rise to Christian Kabbalah and to the natural magic of figures such as Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa and Paracelsus. Early modern esoteric currents included alchemy, astrology, Rosicrucianism, and Freemasonry.

In the nineteenth century, movements such as Theosophy, founded by Helena Blavatsky, and the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn systematised occult teachings and spread them widely. In the twentieth century, esotericism interacted with the counterculture and the New Age movement, while academic study of the field developed from the later twentieth century onward, notably through the work of Antoine Faivre and Wouter Hanegraaff.

Approaches

Scholars debate whether "esotericism" names a coherent tradition or a scholarly category imposed on disparate phenomena. Faivre proposed characteristics such as belief in universal correspondences, a "living nature," the mediating role of imagination, and the experience of transmutation. Hanegraaff influentially described Western esotericism as "rejected knowledge" — bodies of thought marginalised by mainstream religion and rationalist science.
