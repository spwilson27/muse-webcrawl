Mycenaean Greek

Region: Southern Balkan peninsula, Crete
Era: 1450–1100 BC
Family: Indo-European → Hellenic → Greek
Writing system: Linear B
Status: Oldest attested form of Greek

Mycenaean Greek is the most ancient attested form of the Greek language, spoken on mainland Greece and Crete during the late Bronze Age (roughly 1450–1100 BC) and preserved in inscriptions in the Linear B script. It is the language of the palace administrations of Mycenaean Greece — of Knossos, Pylos, Mycenae, Tiryns and Thebes.

Decipherment

Linear B tablets, excavated beginning in 1900 by Arthur Evans at Knossos, remained unread for half a century. In 1952 the English architect and linguist Michael Ventris, building on the sign-grid work of Alice Kober and assisted by John Chadwick, demonstrated that the underlying language was an early form of Greek. The decipherment revealed that the tablets are administrative records — inventories of livestock, grain, oil, textiles, chariots and personnel — not literature.

Linguistic features

Mycenaean retains archaic features lost in later Greek dialects: the labiovelar consonants written with the q-series (later becoming p, t, or k sounds), the consonant w (digamma), and instrumental and locative case endings. It predates characteristic later developments such as the loss of initial s in many contexts and the full Attic-Ionic vowel shifts. Personal names, place names (such as Ko-no-so, Knossos) and divine names on the tablets — including forms of Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Athena and Dionysus — show striking continuity with classical Greek.

Legacy

With the collapse of the palaces around 1200 BC, Linear B writing disappeared, and Greek was not written again until the adoption of the alphabet centuries later. Mycenaean is generally considered most closely related to the later Arcadocypriot dialect group, whose speakers in Arcadia and Cyprus may descend from Bronze Age populations that escaped the worst of the collapse.
