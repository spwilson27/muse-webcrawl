Affix

Affix
TypeBound morpheme
Main typesPrefix, suffix, infix, circumfix
FunctionsDerivation, inflection

In linguistics, an affix is a morpheme that is attached to a word stem to form a new word or to mark a grammatical function. Affixes are bound morphemes: they cannot normally stand alone as words. The process of adding affixes is called affixation, one of the principal methods of word formation across the world's languages, and languages that rely heavily on it are described as agglutinative.

Types by position

  • Prefix — attached before the stem: un-happy, re-do, pre-view.
  • Suffix — attached after the stem: happi-ness, walk-ed, nation-al.
  • Infix — inserted inside the stem, rare in English (mostly in expletive insertion such as "abso-bloody-lutely") but common in languages such as Tagalog, where -um- appears in sumulat ("wrote") from sulat.
  • Circumfix — a two-part affix placed around the stem, as in German past participles such as ge-spiel-t ("played").
  • Interfix — a linking element joining compounds, and transfix — the discontinuous vowel patterns of Semitic root-and-pattern morphology.

Derivational and inflectional affixes

Derivational affixes create new lexemes, often changing word class or meaning: teach → teacher (verb to noun), happy → unhappy (reversal of meaning). Inflectional affixes mark grammatical categories such as tense, number, case or comparison without creating a new word: plural -s, past-tense -ed, comparative -er. English has only a handful of inflectional suffixes, a remnant of the much richer system of Old English.

Affixes versus other bound forms

Affixes differ from clitics, which are grammatically independent words that phonologically lean on a host (such as English possessive 's in "the Queen of England's hat"), and from combining forms of classical origin. Affixes may be productive — freely applicable to new words, like -ness or un- — or fossilised in a closed set of words, like the -th of warmth and growth.
