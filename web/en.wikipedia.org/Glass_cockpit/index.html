Glass cockpit

A glass cockpit is an aircraft cockpit featuring electronic (digital) flight instrument displays, typically large liquid-crystal displays (LCDs), instead of traditional mechanical gauges and indicators. Whereas a conventional cockpit relies on numerous individual dials, a glass cockpit consolidates information into a few integrated displays, principally the primary flight display (PFD) and the multifunction display (MFD).

History

Glass cockpits originated in military and research aircraft. NASA research in the 1970s informed the first commercial applications, and the Boeing 767 and Airbus A310 of the early 1980s introduced cathode-ray-tube displays into airline service. By the 2000s systems such as the Garmin G1000 brought glass panels to light general-aviation aircraft, and most new airliners are now built with fully integrated glass flight decks.

Advantages and concerns

Digital displays reduce pilot workload, present information flexibly, and improve reliability and maintainability. Critics note potential drawbacks: over-reliance on automation, "mode confusion", and the need for training in managing display failures. Modern systems add synthetic vision, integrated weather, traffic, and terrain information.
