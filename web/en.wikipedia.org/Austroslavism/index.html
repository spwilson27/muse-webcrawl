Austroslavism was a political concept and programme, developed mainly by Czech liberals in the mid-nineteenth century, which sought to achieve equality and autonomy for the Slavic peoples of the Habsburg Monarchy within a federalised Austrian state, rather than through independence or union with Russia.

Origins

The idea emerged during the revolutions of 1848. The historian František Palacký famously declined an invitation to the Frankfurt Parliament, arguing that the Austrian state was a necessary bulwark for the small nations of Central Europe: if Austria did not exist, it would have to be invented. The Prague Slav Congress of 1848 brought the programme to wider attention.

Development

Austroslavism contrasted with both German centralism in Vienna and with Pan-Slavism oriented toward the Russian Empire. Its adherents pressed for a federal reorganisation of the monarchy in which Slavic crown lands would enjoy equal status. The movement declined as national movements radicalised in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries.
