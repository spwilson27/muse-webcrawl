A caliph (Arabic: خَلِيفَة, khalīfa) is the chief religious and political leader of an Islamic state known as a caliphate. The title means "successor" or "deputy", signifying succession to the Islamic prophet Muhammad as head of the Muslim community (ummah), though not as a prophet. Following Muhammad's death in 632 CE, Abu Bakr became the first caliph.

Historical caliphates

The first four caliphs — Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman and Ali — are known to Sunni Muslims as the Rashidun ("rightly guided") caliphs. The institution then became hereditary under the Umayyad Caliphate (661–750), ruling from Damascus, and the Abbasid Caliphate (750–1258), ruling from Baghdad. Rival caliphates later arose, including the Fatimids in Egypt and the Umayyads of Córdoba. After the Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258, a shadow Abbasid line continued in Cairo under Mamluk protection.

The Ottoman caliphate and abolition

From the early modern period the Ottoman sultans claimed the caliphate, and the title acquired renewed political significance in the 19th and early 20th centuries. Following the Turkish War of Independence, the Grand National Assembly of Turkey abolished the sultanate in 1922 and the caliphate itself on 3 March 1924, exiling the last caliph, Abdülmecid II. The abolition provoked movements abroad, notably the Indian Khilafat Movement, which had campaigned to preserve it.

Theology and qualifications

Classical Sunni jurists, such as al-Mawardi, set out qualifications for the caliph — including descent from Quraysh, sound knowledge, and capacity to govern and defend the community — and debated whether the office should be singular worldwide. Shia Muslims hold that leadership rightly belonged to the Imams of Muhammad's family, beginning with Ali, and do not recognise the historical caliphs in the same way. In the modern era, various movements have sought to restore a caliphate, most notoriously the Islamic State group, whose 2014 declaration was widely rejected by Muslim scholars and governments.
