Oolite

Oolite or oölite (from Greek ᾠόν ōión, "egg") is a sedimentary rock formed from ooids — spherical grains composed of concentric layers of calcium carbonate (calcite or aragonite) accreted around a nucleus such as a sand grain or shell fragment. Oolites are typically a form of limestone, known as oolitic limestone. Ooids are usually 0.25–2 millimetres in diameter; rocks composed of larger grains (over 2 mm) are called pisolites.

Formation

Ooids form today in warm, shallow, wave-agitated marine waters supersaturated with calcium carbonate, notably on the Bahama Banks (also called Bahamian oolite), in the Persian Gulf, and in hypersaline lakes such as the Great Salt Lake in Utah. As grains are rolled about by currents, they acquire concentric coatings of carbonate precipitated from the water; continued agitation keeps them rounded and polished and prevents cementation until they are deposited.

Geological occurrence and use

The Jurassic oolitic limestones of England are famous building stones: Bath stone and the honey-coloured stone of the Cotswolds are oolites, as is Portland stone, used in St Paul's Cathedral and Buckingham Palace. In the United States, Indiana limestone (Salem Limestone), widely used in monumental buildings, is an oolitic limestone composed largely of fossil fragments and ooids. Oolites also occur in the geological record in association with some iron ores, and oolitic ironstones were historically mined in parts of England and continental Europe.

The term "oolite" has also been applied in stratigraphy to major divisions of the Jurassic System in older literature (such as the "Inferior Oolite" and "Great Oolite" series of England), reflecting the abundance of such rocks in that system.
