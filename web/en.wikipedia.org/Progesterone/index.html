Progesterone (P4) is an endogenous steroid and progestogen sex hormone involved in the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and embryogenesis of humans and other species. It belongs to a group of steroid hormones called the progestogens and is the major progestogen in the body. Progesterone has a variety of important functions: it is also a crucial metabolic intermediate in the production of other endogenous steroids, including the sex hormones and the corticosteroids, and it plays an important role in brain function as a neurosteroid.

Biological activity

Progesterone is produced principally by the corpus luteum in the ovary after ovulation, and in much larger amounts by the placenta during pregnancy; smaller quantities are secreted by the adrenal glands, and it is also produced in the testes in males. In the menstrual cycle, rising progesterone during the luteal phase transforms the endometrium into a secretory state receptive to implantation, raises basal body temperature, and reduces uterine contractility. If pregnancy does not occur, progesterone levels fall and menstruation follows. During pregnancy, progesterone maintains the endometrium, suppresses uterine contractions, supports the development of the mammary glands in preparation for lactation, and modulates the maternal immune response.

Progesterone acts mainly through the intracellular progesterone receptor, a ligand-activated transcription factor, and also through membrane progesterone receptors and as a precursor to neuroactive metabolites such as allopregnanolone, which modulates GABAA receptors and affects mood, sleep, and seizure susceptibility.

Chemistry and synthesis

Progesterone is a pregnane steroid with the formula C21H30O2, characterised by ketone groups at positions 3 and 20 and a double bond between carbons 4 and 5. In the body it is synthesised from cholesterol via pregnenolone. The hormone was first isolated and named in 1934; its name reflects its role as a "pro-gestational" steroid. Industrial synthesis was revolutionised in the 1940s by Russell Marker's degradation process, which produced progesterone cheaply from diosgenin extracted from Mexican yams, enabling the development of hormonal contraceptives.

Medical use

Progesterone and synthetic progestogens (progestins) are used in hormonal contraception, hormone replacement therapy — where a progestogen is given alongside estrogen to protect the endometrium — prevention of preterm birth in certain pregnancies, treatment of secondary amenorrhoea and abnormal uterine bleeding, and in assisted reproduction to support the luteal phase. Natural progesterone for medical use is usually micronised to improve oral bioavailability; it is also administered vaginally or by injection.

Levels and measurement

Serum progesterone is low before ovulation (typically below 1 ng/mL), rises to roughly 5–20 ng/mL in the mid-luteal phase, and reaches much higher levels in pregnancy. Measurement of a mid-luteal progesterone level is commonly used to confirm that ovulation has occurred in fertility assessment.
