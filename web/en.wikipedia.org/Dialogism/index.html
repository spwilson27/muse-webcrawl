Dialogism

Mikhail Bakhtin developed the theory of dialogism.

Dialogism is a theory of language and literature developed by the Russian philosopher and literary critic Mikhail Bakhtin. It holds that meaning never resides in a single word, text, or consciousness, but arises in the interaction — the dialogue — between voices. Every utterance is addressed to someone, shaped by previous utterances, and anticipates a response.

Core concepts

In Problems of Dostoevsky's Poetics (1929, revised 1963), Bakhtin argued that Dostoevsky's novels are polyphonic: their characters are not mouthpieces for the author but autonomous consciousnesses whose voices interact on equal terms. Related ideas include heteroglossia, the multiplicity of social languages and speech styles within a single national language, and unfinalizability, the principle that a person or meaning can never be fully summed up or completed.

Dialogism and monologism

Bakhtin contrasted dialogic discourse with monologism, in which a single authoritative voice subordinates all others to one truth. Epic poetry and official rhetoric are monologic; the novel, in his account, is the dialogic genre par excellence, forever staging contests among worldviews, classes, and generations.

Influence

Dialogism became central to late twentieth-century literary theory, influencing narratology, cultural studies, and discourse analysis, and it has been taken up in education theory, psychotherapy, and the philosophy of communication, where the self itself is understood as formed through dialogue with others.
