French Resistance

Résistants and the Cross of Lorraine

PeriodJune 1940 – October 1944
OpponentsNazi German occupation forces; Vichy regime

The French Resistance (French: La Résistance) was a collection of French movements that fought the Nazi occupation of France and the collaborationist Vichy regime during the Second World War. Resistance cells carried out guerrilla warfare, sabotage, and intelligence gathering, published clandestine newspapers, and ran escape lines for Allied airmen.

Origins

Resistance began after the armistice of June 1940, spurred by Charles de Gaulle's Appeal of 18 June from London. Early groups were small and uncoordinated: the Musée de l'Homme network, Combat, Libération, Franc-Tireur, and the communist Francs-Tireurs et Partisans, who joined the struggle after Germany invaded the USSR in June 1941. Foreigners and Spanish Republican exiles played significant roles, as in the Manouchian group.

Unification and liberation

In 1943 Jean Moulin, de Gaulle's emissary, united the main movements in the National Council of the Resistance (CNR), which adopted a programme promising post-war social reforms. Moulin was arrested by the Gestapo under Klaus Barbie and died under torture in July 1943. From early 1944 the armed groups were merged into the French Forces of the Interior (FFI). The maquis — rural guerrilla bands, many formed by young men evading forced labour in Germany (STO) — sabotaged railways and ambushed convoys around the Normandy and Provence landings, and Paris rose in insurrection in August 1944.

Legacy

After the war, the "résistancialiste" myth of a France united in resistance, promoted by Gaullism, was later complicated by historical work on collaboration and indifference. Resistance veterans and deportees remain central to French national memory, commemorated in monuments, the Panthéon burials of figures such as Jean Moulin (1964) and Josephine Baker (2021), and the Order of the Liberation.
