The Sorrows of Young Werther (German: Die Leiden des jungen Werthers) is an epistolary novel by Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, published in 1774 when its author was twenty-four. It made Goethe an overnight literary celebrity across Europe and is considered a defining work of the Sturm und Drang movement and a forerunner of Romanticism.

Plot

The novel consists largely of letters from the sensitive young artist Werther to his friend Wilhelm. Traveling to the village of Wahlheim, Werther falls hopelessly in love with Charlotte ("Lotte"), who is engaged to the solid, dependable Albert and marries him. Werther's happiness in nature and society curdles into despair as he recognizes the hopelessness of his passion; humiliated in aristocratic company and unable to free himself, he borrows pistols from Albert under a pretext and shoots himself at his desk, dying after twelve hours of agony. An "editor" narrates the final sequence, since the letters fail.

Origins

Goethe drew on his own unrequited love for Charlotte Buff during his legal internship at Wetzlar in 1772, and on the suicide of his acquaintance Karl Wilhelm Jerusalem, who shot himself over an unhappy love affair using borrowed pistols. The book was written in a burst of about four weeks in early 1774.

Reception: "Werther fever"

The novel triggered a Europe-wide sensation — "Werther fever": young men dressed in Werther's blue frock coat and yellow waistcoat, Werther merchandise flooded markets, and the book was blamed (on debated evidence) for a wave of copycat suicides, the phenomenon later named the Werther effect. Authorities in Leipzig, Copenhagen, and Milan banned it. Napoleon later told Goethe he had read the novel seven times. The work remains a classic of German literature and a founding text of the modern literature of sensibility.
