Apartheid (from Afrikaans apartheid, "separateness") was a system of institutionalised racial segregation that existed in South Africa and South West Africa (now Namibia) from 1948 until the early 1990s. It was characterised by an authoritarian political culture based on white supremacy, which ensured that South Africa's majority black population was dominated politically, socially and economically by the nation's minority white population.

Background and establishment

Racial discrimination in South Africa long predated 1948, with segregationist laws such as the Natives Land Act of 1913 restricting black land ownership. In the 1948 general election, the National Party under D. F. Malan campaigned explicitly on apartheid and won power, then proceeded to legislate a comprehensive system of racial classification and separation. South Africa's population was classified into racial groups — white, black, Coloured and Indian/Asian — with rights and opportunities determined by classification.

The system

Under laws such as the Population Registration Act (1950) and Group Areas Act (1950), residence, education, employment, marriage and even access to beaches, buses and park benches were segregated by race. The Prohibition of Mixed Marriages Act (1949) and Immorality Amendment Act criminalised interracial marriage and sexual relations. Black South Africans were required to carry pass books controlling their movement. From the 1960s the government pursued "separate development," stripping black South Africans of citizenship and assigning them to nominally self-governing homelands, or Bantustans, while the Bantu Education Act provided deliberately inferior schooling.

Resistance

Opposition was led by the African National Congress (ANC) and later the Pan Africanist Congress (PAC). The Defiance Campaign of 1952, the Sharpeville massacre of 1960 — in which police killed 69 anti-pass-law protesters — and the Soweto uprising of 1976 were landmark episodes. After Sharpeville the ANC and PAC were banned, and their leaders, including Nelson Mandela, were imprisoned or exiled following the Rivonia Trial (1963–64). The Black Consciousness movement of Steve Biko, trade unions, and township uprisings of the 1980s intensified internal pressure.

International response

Apartheid was condemned internationally; the United Nations imposed arms embargoes and many countries applied economic sanctions and boycotts, including sporting isolation. The UN General Assembly declared apartheid a crime against humanity, a status later codified in international law.

End of apartheid

In 1990 President F. W. de Klerk announced the unbanning of the ANC and other organisations and the release of Nelson Mandela after 27 years in prison. Apartheid legislation was repealed in 1990–91, and negotiations produced a new constitution. In April 1994 South Africa held its first elections with universal suffrage; the ANC won and Mandela became president. The post-apartheid Truth and Reconciliation Commission (1996–98) investigated human rights abuses committed during the era.
