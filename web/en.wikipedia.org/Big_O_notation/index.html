Big O notation

Big O notation
FieldMathematics, computer science
PurposeDescribing the limiting (asymptotic) behavior of a function
Introduced byPaul Bachmann (1894); popularized by Edmund Landau
FamilyO (big O), Ω (big Omega), Θ (big Theta), o (little o), ω (little omega)

Big O notation is a mathematical notation that describes the limiting behavior of a function when its argument tends towards a particular value or infinity. In computer science, it is used to classify algorithms according to how their run time or space requirements grow as the input size grows — the core tool of the analysis of algorithms. A function f(n) is said to be O(g(n)) if, for all sufficiently large n, |f(n)| is at most a constant multiple of g(n): formally, f(n) = O(g(n)) if there exist constants C > 0 and n₀ such that |f(n)| ≤ C·g(n) for all n ≥ n₀.

Growth rates: logarithmic, linear, n log n, quadratic and exponential

History

The notation was introduced by the German mathematician Paul Bachmann in the second volume of his number theory treatise Die Analytische Zahlentheorie (1894), and was spread by his student Edmund Landau, after whom the family is sometimes called Landau symbols or Bachmann–Landau notation. Donald Knuth's The Art of Computer Programming (from 1968) and his 1976 article "Big Omicron and Big Omega and Big Theta" standardized the notation — and its Omega and Theta companions — throughout computer science.

Related notations

Big O gives an asymptotic upper bound. Its companions complete the family: big Omega, Ω(g(n)), gives a lower bound; big Theta, Θ(g(n)), means the function is bounded both above and below by g(n) and is thus the exact growth rate; little o denotes a strictly smaller order (f grows more slowly than g, so the ratio tends to zero); and little omega, ω, denotes a strictly larger order. In algorithm analysis, statements are usually understood as worst-case unless an average-case or amortized analysis is specified; sloppily using O where Θ is meant is common in informal writing but discouraged in formal work.

Properties and use

Big O discards constant factors and lower-order terms, which is what makes it robust across machines and implementations but also means it does not by itself predict actual running times for small inputs. The notation is used well beyond time complexity: for memory consumption, communication complexity, error terms in analytic number theory (where it originated — for example in estimates of the prime number theorem), and approximations in physics. Formally, O(g) denotes a set of functions, so the conventional expression f = O(g) is an abuse of the equals sign, read as "f is big-O of g".
