Quantum chromodynamics

In theoretical physics, quantum chromodynamics (QCD) is the study of the strong interaction between quarks mediated by gluons, the fundamental particles that make up protons, neutrons, and other hadrons. QCD is a type of quantum field theory called a non-Abelian gauge theory with symmetry group SU(3). The QCD analog of electric charge is a property called color — hence the name chromodynamics.

Key features

QCD has two peculiar properties:

  • Color confinement: the force between quarks does not diminish as they are separated. The energy of the gluon field between a color charge and an anticharge grows approximately linearly with distance, so unlike electrons and positrons it would theoretically take an infinite amount of energy to separate a quark from a hadron. Free quarks and gluons are never observed directly; instead they hadronise into jets of color-neutral particles.
  • Asymptotic freedom: a very weak interaction between quarks and gluons at short distances, discovered in 1973 by David Gross, Frank Wilczek and David Politzer, who shared the 2004 Nobel Prize in Physics for it. At high energies quarks behave almost as free particles, permitting perturbative calculations.

Theory

QCD describes six flavours of quarks (up, down, charm, strange, top, bottom), each carrying one of three color charges, interacting via eight massless gluons which themselves carry color and so interact among themselves — a feature distinguishing QCD from quantum electrodynamics. At low energies the coupling becomes strong and perturbative methods fail, so techniques such as lattice QCD, which discretises spacetime, are used to compute hadron masses and other properties numerically.

Experimental tests

Deep inelastic scattering experiments at SLAC in the late 1960s revealed point-like constituents inside the proton. Later experiments at CERN, DESY and the Large Hadron Collider, including studies of three-jet events and quark–gluon plasma formed in heavy-ion collisions, have confirmed QCD's predictions with high precision.
