Yeasts are eukaryotic, single-celled microorganisms classified as members of the fungus kingdom. About 1,500 species are currently described. The principal species, Saccharomyces cerevisiae, converts carbohydrates to carbon dioxide and ethanol through fermentation and has been used in baking and brewing since ancient times.

Saccharomyces cerevisiae cells reproducing by budding.

Biology

Yeasts are chemoorganotrophs, obtaining energy from organic compounds. They reproduce asexually by budding or fission and sexually by forming spores. Most are facultative anaerobes, fermenting sugars even in the presence of oxygen when sugar is abundant (the Crabtree effect).

Uses

Besides food and drink production, yeasts are used to make bioethanol, as nutritional supplements, and as model organisms in genetics and cell biology — S. cerevisiae was the first eukaryote to have its genome fully sequenced (1996). Some species, such as Candida albicans, are opportunistic human pathogens.

History

Louis Pasteur's studies of fermentation in the 1850s–60s established yeast as a living organism responsible for alcoholic fermentation; Emil Christian Hansen later isolated pure brewing cultures at the Carlsberg laboratory.
