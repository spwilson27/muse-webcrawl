Social inclusion


Inclusive community activities bring together people of different backgrounds and abilities

Social inclusion is the process of improving the terms on which individuals and groups take part in society—improving the ability, opportunity, and dignity of those disadvantaged on the basis of their identity. It is the counterpart of social exclusion, in which individuals or whole communities are prevented from full participation in economic, social, and political life. The concept is central to the policies of the European Union, the World Bank, and the United Nations, whose 2030 Agenda pledges to "leave no one behind."

Concept and origins

The term gained currency in French social policy in the 1970s and 1980s, referring to people excluded from mainstream systems of work, housing, and welfare. It subsequently broadened into a multidimensional framework: people may be excluded not only from income and employment but also from education, healthcare, housing, digital access, social networks, and civic participation. Social inclusion policies therefore aim at removing barriers—legal, institutional, economic, physical, and attitudinal—that produce exclusion.

Applications

In education, inclusion refers to teaching students with disabilities and special needs in mainstream classrooms wherever possible, as reflected in the 1994 Salamanca Statement and the UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities. In urban policy, inclusion concerns affordable housing, accessible transport, and mixed-income neighbourhoods. In the digital sphere, digital inclusion addresses gaps in broadband access, affordability, devices, and digital literacy. Financial inclusion—the delivery of affordable financial services to underserved populations—is measured by the World Bank's Global Findex.

Measurement and criticism

Multidimensional indices attempt to measure inclusion through indicators of poverty, employment, education, health, and participation. Critics note that "inclusion" can be a vague policy slogan, and that inclusion into existing structures may be less transformative than changing those structures themselves; others caution that the concept can mask persistent inequality behind participatory rhetoric.
