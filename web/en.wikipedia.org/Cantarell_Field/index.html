The Cantarell Field is a supergiant offshore oil field in the Bay of Campeche in the Gulf of Mexico, about 80 kilometres off the coast of the Mexican state of Campeche. Operated by the state oil company Pemex, it was for many years Mexico's largest oil field and one of the largest in the world, at its peak supplying a majority of the country's crude production and a substantial share of government revenue.

Discovery and geology

The field is named after the fisherman Rudesindo Cantarell, who in 1971 reported an oil seep in the bay to Pemex; exploration confirmed a major discovery in 1976. Unusually, the reservoir is a fractured carbonate breccia formed from debris of the Chicxulub impact — the asteroid strike that ended the age of the dinosaurs 66 million years ago — which created a highly permeable rock that allowed exceptional flow rates from individual wells.

Production and decline

Production rose steadily and, aided from 2000 by one of the world's largest nitrogen-injection projects to maintain reservoir pressure, peaked at roughly 2.1 million barrels per day in 2004, making Cantarell briefly the second most productive oil field on Earth. Output then entered a steep decline as pressure fell and water and gas breakthrough increased, dropping to a small fraction of peak within a decade. Cantarell's rise and fall is a widely cited case study of peak oil and of the fiscal risks of dependence on a single giant field.
