Short story

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: a volume of collected short stories]

A short story is a piece of prose fiction, typically shorter than a novel and usually dealing with a small cast of characters and a single unified effect. Its length is as short as a word, but typically under 7,500 words — the threshold used by the Hugo and Nebula Awards, with longer works classed as novelettes or novellas. Edgar Allan Poe, in his 1842 review of Nathaniel Hawthorne's Twice-Told Tales, argued that a short story should be readable in one sitting and aim at a single pre-designed effect.

The form has roots in oral storytelling, fables, and exempla, and emerged as a distinct literary genre in the 19th century alongside the growth of magazines. Masters of the form include Anton Chekhov, Guy de Maupassant, O. Henry (famed for twist endings), Katherine Mansfield, James Joyce, Ernest Hemingway, Jorge Luis Borges, Shirley Jackson, and Alice Munro, who won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 2013 as a "master of the contemporary short story".

Characteristics

Short stories tend toward economy: a restricted setting, few characters, and an emphasis on mood, image, or a moment of revelation (Joyce's "epiphany"). Modern variations include flash fiction, the short short, and micro-fiction produced for digital media.

Prestigious awards dedicated to the form include the O. Henry Award and the Sunday Times Short Story Award.
