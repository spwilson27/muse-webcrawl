Golden Age

The term Golden Age comes from Greek mythology and was created by the poet Hesiod in his work Works and Days, referring to the period when the Golden Race of humans lived. It was a time of peace, harmony, stability and prosperity: people lived to a very old age while retaining a youthful appearance, the earth provided food in abundance without agriculture, and humans lived like gods, free from sorrow, toil and old age's misery.

Greek and Roman tradition

Hesiod described five successive ages of man — Golden, Silver, Bronze, Heroic and Iron — each worse than the one before. Roman poets, notably Ovid in the Metamorphoses, elaborated the Golden Age as a reign of Saturn marked by eternal spring, spontaneous abundance and the absence of laws, property and war. The myth is an early example of a widespread cultural theme: an idealised lost past against which the present is judged corrupt or diminished.

General usage

By extension, "golden age" now denotes any period of great achievement, flourishing or classic quality in a field, nation or medium — the Golden Age of Athens under Pericles, the Dutch Golden Age of painting and trade in the 17th century, the Islamic Golden Age of science and philosophy, the Golden Age of Hollywood, of radio, of television, of comic books, of science fiction, or of piracy. Historians note that the label is often applied retrospectively and can reflect nostalgia as much as analysis, but it remains a durable shorthand for a peak era.
