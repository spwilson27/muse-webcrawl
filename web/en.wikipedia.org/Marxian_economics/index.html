Marxian economics

Marxian economics, or the Marxian school of economics, is a school of economic thought based on the work of Karl Marx, above all his critique of political economy in Das Kapital (1867). It analyses capitalism as a historically specific mode of production driven by the extraction of surplus value from wage labour.

Core concepts

Central ideas include the labour theory of value, in which the value of a commodity is determined by the socially necessary labour time required to produce it; surplus value, the difference between the value labour creates and the wages paid for labour power, which forms the source of profit; and the tendency of the rate of profit to fall, arising from a rising organic composition of capital as production mechanises.

Analysis of capitalism

Marxian theory holds that competition compels capitalists to accumulate and cheapen production, generating periodic crises of overproduction, concentration of capital, class conflict between the bourgeoisie and the proletariat, and eventually conditions for a transition beyond capitalism. Later Marxian economists have developed the theory in directions such as monopoly capital, dependency theory and analytical Marxism.

Criticism

Mainstream economists have criticised the labour theory of value and the transformation problem — the difficulty of reconciling values with prices of production — while Marxian approaches remain influential in economic sociology, geography and heterodox economics.
