Culture of the Soviet Union

The culture of the Soviet Union developed over the USSR's 69-year history (1922–1991), shaped by Marxist-Leninist ideology, state control of the arts, and the diverse traditions of its many nationalities. It passed through phases of revolutionary experimentation, Stalinist socialist realism, the Khrushchev Thaw, stagnation, and glasnost.

Culture of the Soviet Union
Period: 1922–1991
Official doctrine: Socialist realism (from 1934)
Languages: Russian and republic languages

Early Soviet culture

The 1920s saw avant-garde flourishing — constructivism, the films of Sergei Eisenstein and Dziga Vertov, and mass literacy campaigns (likbez). The state promoted a new Soviet person through education, youth organisations such as the Komsomol and Pioneers, and anti-religious propaganda.

Socialist realism

From 1934 socialist realism became the mandatory style in literature, art, film, and music, demanding optimistic depictions of socialist construction. Censorship under Glavlit and periodic campaigns — notably the Zhdanovshchina — suppressed deviation, though works of lasting value appeared within and against the system.

Thaw, stagnation, and glasnost

After Stalin's death in 1953, the Thaw loosened controls; poetry, cinema, and bard song flourished, alongside unofficial samizdat literature. Under glasnost in the late 1980s, previously banned works were published openly, contributing to the intellectual ferment preceding the USSR's dissolution in 1991.
