Luxor Temple (Arabic: معبد الأقصر) is a large ancient Egyptian temple complex on the east bank of the Nile at the city of Luxor (ancient Thebes). Unlike most Egyptian temples it was not dedicated to a single god or a deified king, but to the rejuvenation of kingship itself: it was the focus of the annual Opet Festival, in which the cult images of Amun, Mut and Khonsu travelled from Karnak to Luxor, and where pharaohs were symbolically (and, some argue, actually) crowned. The temple was built principally under Amenhotep III (r. c. 1390–1352 BC) and Ramesses II (r. 1279–1213 BC), and enlarged by many later rulers over two millennia.

Layout and construction

The complex runs about 260 m along a north–south axis parallel to the river. From the north, an avenue of sphinxes — the restored Avenue of Sphinxes, reopened in 2021 — leads from Karnak, 3 km away. The massive first pylon of Ramesses II is fronted by seated colossi of the king and a single remaining obelisk of pink granite; its twin was removed in the 1830s and now stands in the Place de la Concorde in Paris. Behind the pylon lies Ramesses' peristyle court, then the great colonnade of Amenhotep III — seven pairs of 16 m papyrus-bundle columns — leading to the Sun Court of Amenhotep III, the hypostyle hall, and the sanctuary complex, where a shrine rebuilt by Alexander the Great bears his image as pharaoh. A "birth room" depicts the divine birth of Amenhotep III.

The Opet Festival

During the second month of the inundation season, the statues of the Theban Triad were carried in barques from Karnak to Luxor along the sphinx avenue (or by river), amid feasting in which the populace shared — an unusually public rite, celebrated with bread, beer and offerings distributed to the crowds. Reliefs in the colonnade, carved under Tutankhamun and Horemheb, depict the procession in detail. The festival, which could last nearly a month by the late New Kingdom, renewed the king's divine ka and his right to rule.

Later history

The temple remained in use through the Roman period — a sanctuary of the imperial cult was installed in the inner rooms, with frescoes of emperors — and a Roman legionary fortress surrounded it. In the Christian era a church occupied the first court; remarkably, the mosque of Abu al-Haggag, first built in the medieval period, still stands atop the temple's courts and remains in use, making Luxor Temple a place of continuous worship for some 3,400 years. Excavation of the buried temple proceeded from the 1880s under Gaston Maspero; today it is a highlight of Egypt's tourist circuit and a UNESCO World Heritage Site as part of "Ancient Thebes with its Necropolis".
