The Big Four are the four largest international professional services networks in the world: Deloitte, PricewaterhouseCoopers (PwC), Ernst & Young (EY), and KPMG. Together they audit the great majority of publicly traded companies worldwide, including nearly all constituents of major stock indices such as the S&P 500 and the FTSE 100, and they provide extensive tax, consulting, and advisory services alongside their traditional audit work.

The group was known as the "Big Eight" through most of the 20th century, reduced to the "Big Six" in 1989, the "Big Five" in 1998 after the merger of Price Waterhouse and Coopers & Lybrand, and finally the Big Four in 2002 when Arthur Andersen collapsed following the Enron scandal.

Each firm is not a single company but a network of legally independent member firms, coordinated by a global umbrella entity. This structure limits liability and reflects the national regulation of the accounting profession.

History

The roots of the firms stretch back to 19th-century accountancy practices in Britain and the United States. Samuel Lowell Price founded Price Waterhouse in London in 1849; Deloitte traces its origin to William Welch Deloitte's practice of 1845; KPMG descends from firms founded by William Barclay Peat, James Marwick, Piet Klynveld, and William Henry Michael Goerdeler; EY combines the legacies of Alwin C. Ernst and Arthur Young. A series of mergers during the 1980s and 1990s created vast global networks. The collapse of Arthur Andersen in 2002, after its conviction (later overturned) over the destruction of Enron-related documents, distributed its practices among the surviving four.

Services

  • Audit and assurance — statutory audits of financial statements, the historical core of the firms.
  • Tax — compliance, transfer pricing, and international tax planning.
  • Consulting and advisory — technology implementation, strategy, risk management, and deals advice; by the 2010s non-audit services generated the majority of revenue for most of the firms.

Criticism and regulation

The concentration of the audit market in four firms has drawn persistent criticism from regulators and competition authorities, who note the limited choice available to large multinational companies and the perceived conflicts of interest arising when firms sell consulting services to audit clients. The Big Four audited companies involved in major accounting scandals, including Enron (Andersen), Satyam, Wirecard (EY), and Carillion (KPMG), prompting reforms such as mandatory audit firm rotation in the European Union, restrictions on non-audit services, and debate in the United Kingdom about splitting audit and consulting practices. In 2020 the UK Financial Reporting Council asked the firms to operationally separate their audit arms.
