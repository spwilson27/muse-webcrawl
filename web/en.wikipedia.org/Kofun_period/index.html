Kofun period

A keyhole-shaped kofun mound, seen from the air.

The Kofun period (Japanese: 古墳時代, Kofun jidai) is an era in Japanese history, conventionally dated from about 250 or 300 CE to 538 CE, named for the enormous earthen burial mounds (kofun, "old tumuli") built for the ruling elite. It follows the Yayoi period and precedes the Asuka period, and together with the Asuka period forms the Yamato period of protohistoric Japan.

The mounds

The characteristic kofun is the zenpō-kōen-fun, a mound shaped in plan like a keyhole — a square front joined to a circular rear — surrounded by one or more moats and covered with haniwa, terracotta cylinders and figures of houses, animals, warriors and boats. The largest, the Daisen Kofun in Sakai, Osaka, traditionally attributed to Emperor Nintoku, is about 486 metres long, among the largest tombs in the world by area; the Mozu–Furuichi Kofun Group was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2019.

Society and contacts

The period saw the consolidation of the Yamato state under great kings (ōkimi), the spread of iron tools and weapons, horse trappings and continental technologies from the Korean peninsula, and the immigration of skilled lineages (toraijin). Writing arrived from China, and diplomatic missions exchanged embassies with Chinese dynasties and the Korean kingdoms of Baekje, Silla and Goguryeo. The era ends conventionally with the introduction of Buddhism in 538 (or 552), ushering in the Asuka period and the cessation of mound building.
