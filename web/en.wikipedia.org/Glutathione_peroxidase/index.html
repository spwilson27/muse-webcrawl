Glutathione peroxidase

Glutathione peroxidase is a family of selenium-containing antioxidant enzymes.

Glutathione peroxidase (GPx) is the general name of an enzyme family with peroxidase activity whose main biological role is to protect the organism from oxidative damage. The biochemical function of glutathione peroxidase is to reduce lipid hydroperoxides to their corresponding alcohols and to reduce free hydrogen peroxide to water, using the tripeptide glutathione (GSH) as the reducing agent.

Types

Eight different glutathione peroxidases (GPx1–GPx8) have been identified in humans. Most are selenoproteins, containing the amino acid selenocysteine at their active site; GPx4 is notable for reducing phospholipid hydroperoxides directly within membranes, a role central to preventing ferroptosis, an iron-dependent form of cell death. GPx1 is the most abundant form and is found in the cytoplasm of nearly all mammalian tissues; GPx3 is secreted into plasma.

Reaction

The overall reaction is: 2 GSH + H2O2 → GSSG + 2 H2O, and similarly for organic hydroperoxides. The oxidized glutathione (GSSG) is recycled by glutathione reductase using NADPH. The enzymes were discovered in 1957 by Gordon C. Mills as activities protecting red blood cells against oxidative hemolysis.

Clinical significance

Because several GPx enzymes depend on selenium, their activity is a functional marker of selenium status. Mutations and polymorphisms in GPx genes have been studied in relation to cancer, cardiovascular disease, and Keshan disease, a cardiomyopathy associated with severe selenium deficiency.
