Tritium

A tritium atom: one proton and two neutrons

Symbol3H or T
Neutrons2
Half-life12.32 years
Decay modeBeta emission to helium-3

Tritium (from Ancient Greek τρίτος trítos 'third') or hydrogen-3 (symbol T or 3H) is a rare and radioactive isotope of hydrogen with a nucleus (a "triton") containing one proton and two neutrons. The nucleus of the most common hydrogen isotope, protium, contains one proton and no neutrons, while deuterium contains one proton and one neutron.

Decay

Tritium decays by beta emission into helium-3 with a half-life of 12.32 years, releasing a low-energy beta particle (average 5.7 keV). Because the emitted electron is so weak, tritium is a relatively low-energy radioisotope, though its incorporation into water makes it a radiological concern inside the body.

Production and occurrence

Tritium occurs naturally in trace amounts from the interaction of cosmic rays with atmospheric gases — the global natural inventory is only a few kilograms. It is produced industrially by neutron irradiation of lithium-6 in nuclear reactors, and it was released in larger quantities by atmospheric nuclear weapons testing, which briefly raised environmental levels (the "bomb pulse" now used in hydrology dating). Tritiated water is used as a tracer in groundwater and ocean circulation studies.

Uses

Tritium is an essential fuel for thermonuclear weapons and for experimental nuclear fusion, in which it is fused with deuterium in the D–T reaction powering projects such as ITER. Mixed with a phosphor, its beta radiation produces self-powered illumination — the glowing markers in watch dials, compasses, gun sights, and emergency exit signs. Tritium is also used as an isotopic label in drug development and life-science research.
