Shakespeare bibliography

The First Folio (1623)

This article lists the plays, poems, and other works attributed to William Shakespeare (1564–1616), together with notes on their early publication and the apocryphal works once ascribed to him.

Plays

The canonical plays — conventionally 37 to 39 in number — were first collected in the First Folio (1623), compiled by John Heminges and Henry Condell, which printed 36 plays, 18 of them for the first time. Some plays had earlier appeared in individual quarto editions. Modern canons generally add Pericles, Prince of Tyre and The Two Noble Kinsmen (both collaborations, the latter with John Fletcher), and many scholars credit Shakespeare with a hand in Sir Thomas More and the lost Cardenio.

  • Comedies: All's Well That Ends Well, As You Like It, The Comedy of Errors, Love's Labour's Lost, Measure for Measure, The Merchant of Venice, The Merry Wives of Windsor, A Midsummer Night's Dream, Much Ado About Nothing, Pericles, The Taming of the Shrew, The Tempest, Twelfth Night, The Two Gentlemen of Verona, The Two Noble Kinsmen, The Winter's Tale, plus Cymbeline (printed among the tragedies in the Folio).
  • Histories: King John, Richard II, Henry IV, Part 1 and Part 2, Henry V, Henry VI, Parts 1–3, Richard III, Henry VIII.
  • Tragedies: Romeo and Juliet, Coriolanus, Titus Andronicus, Timon of Athens, Julius Caesar, Macbeth, Hamlet, Troilus and Cressida, King Lear, Othello, Antony and Cleopatra.

Poems

Shakespeare's narrative poems are Venus and Adonis (1593), his first published work and a bestseller in his lifetime, and The Rape of Lucrece (1594), both dedicated to the Earl of Southampton. The Sonnets — 154 in number — were published in 1609 by Thomas Thorpe, possibly without authorisation. Other poems include A Lover's Complaint, The Passionate Pilgrim (1599, an anthology only partly by Shakespeare), and The Phoenix and the Turtle (1601).

Apocrypha

The "Shakespeare apocrypha" comprises plays once attributed to him, including The Birth of Merlin, Locrine, The London Prodigal, Sir John Oldcastle, and Edward III — the last now accepted by many scholars as partly Shakespeare's work and included in some modern editions.
