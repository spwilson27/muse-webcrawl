In differential geometry, the Ricci curvature tensor, named after Gregorio Ricci-Curbastro (1853–1925), represents the amount by which the volume of a narrow conical piece of a small geodesic ball in a curved Riemannian manifold deviates from that of the standard ball in Euclidean space. As such, it provides a way of measuring the degree to which the geometry determined by a given Riemannian metric might differ from that of ordinary Euclidean n-space.

The Ricci tensor is obtained by taking a trace (contraction) of the Riemann curvature tensor: Ric(X, Y) = tr(Z ↦ R(Z, X)Y). In coordinates, Rij = Rkikj. Its further trace is the scalar curvature. In dimensions 2 and 3 the Ricci tensor determines the full curvature; in higher dimensions it captures only part of it, the remainder being the Weyl tensor.

Positive Ricci curvature makes geodesic balls smaller than in Euclidean space; negative curvature makes them larger.

Geometric meaning

If one transports a small volume element around a point by geodesics in random directions, the average distortion of volume is governed by the Ricci curvature in each direction: positive Ricci curvature means geodesics tend to converge (as on a sphere), negative means they diverge (as on a saddle). This is expressed precisely by the volume comparison theorems of Bishop and Gromov and underlies Myers' theorem, which bounds the diameter of a manifold with Ricci curvature bounded below by a positive constant.

Einstein manifolds and general relativity

A Riemannian manifold is an Einstein manifold if Ric = λg for a constant λ; such metrics are the Riemannian analogues of solutions of Einstein's vacuum equations. In general relativity, the Ricci tensor of the Lorentzian spacetime metric is directly related by the Einstein field equations to the energy and momentum of matter: Gμν = Ricμν − ½Rgμν = (8πG/c4)Tμν.

Ricci flow

The Ricci tensor drives the Ricci flow, the geometric evolution equation ∂g/∂t = −2Ric introduced by Richard Hamilton in 1982, which deforms a metric in the direction of smoothing its curvature. Grigori Perelman's completion of this programme (2002–2003) proved the Poincaré conjecture.
