Foreign policy of the United States

The foreign policy of the United States comprises the strategies by which the U.S. government interacts with foreign nations and sets standards for its organizations, corporations and citizens. It is conducted principally by the President, advised by the Secretary of State and executed through the Department of State, the Department of Defense and the intelligence community.

Historical themes

Early American policy favored avoiding permanent alliances while expanding across the continent. The Monroe Doctrine, the Open Door Policy, and interventions in Latin America marked a growing hemispheric role; the two world wars drew the United States into global leadership, formalized after 1945 in the United Nations, NATO and the Bretton Woods institutions.

Cold War and after

Containment of the Soviet Union structured policy for four decades, including the Korean and Vietnam Wars. After 1991 the United States stood as the sole superpower, intervening in the Gulf War, the Balkans and, after the September 11 attacks, Afghanistan and Iraq. More recent policy has emphasized strategic competition with China and Russia, alongside alliances and trade frameworks such as the USMCA.

Institutions

The National Security Council coordinates policy in the White House; USAID administers foreign assistance; and the intelligence agencies and the military's geographic combatant commands support policy worldwide.
