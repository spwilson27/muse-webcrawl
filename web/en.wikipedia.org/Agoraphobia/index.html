Agoraphobia

SpecialtyPsychiatry, clinical psychology
SymptomsAnxiety in situations perceived as unsafe or hard to escape; panic attacks
Usual onsetTeens to mid-30s
TreatmentCognitive behavioural therapy, exposure therapy, SSRIs
ICD-10F40.0

Agoraphobia is an anxiety disorder characterised by symptoms of anxiety in situations where the person perceives their environment to be unsafe with no easy way to escape. These situations can include public transit, shopping centres, crowds and queues, cinemas, and open or enclosed spaces. In severe cases, people may become completely homebound. The name derives from the Greek agora, a public assembly place or market.

Signs and symptoms

People with agoraphobia fear situations where help might not be available or escape might be difficult if they develop panic-like symptoms or other embarrassing or incapacitating symptoms. Many develop the condition after having one or more panic attacks, and then fear further attacks — a "fear of fear" cycle. Avoidance behaviour is the hallmark: the person avoids feared places, endures them with dread, or requires a companion.

Causes and diagnosis

Agoraphobia is believed to arise from a combination of genetic and environmental factors, including stressful or traumatic events, substance use, and a family history of anxiety disorders. Under the DSM-5, diagnosis requires marked fear or anxiety about at least two of five situation types (public transportation, open spaces, enclosed places, crowds or queues, and being outside the home alone) lasting at least six months and causing clinically significant distress or impairment.

Treatment

The most effective treatment is cognitive behavioural therapy with graded exposure, in which the person gradually and repeatedly confronts feared situations until anxiety diminishes. Systematic desensitisation and virtual reality exposure are variants. Medications, particularly selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs), may help, especially where panic disorder coexists. With treatment, many people substantially reduce avoidance and regain functioning.
