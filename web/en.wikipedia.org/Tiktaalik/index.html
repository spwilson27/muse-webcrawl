Tiktaalik

Tiktaalik is a monospecific genus of extinct sarcopterygian (lobe-finned fish) from the Late Devonian Period, about 375 million years ago, with many features common to tetrapods (four-limbed animals). Described in 2006 from fossils found on Ellesmere Island in Nunavut, Canada, Tiktaalik is one of the best-known transitional fossils, illustrating the evolutionary transition between swimming fish and walking land vertebrates. Its name comes from an Inuktitut word for a large freshwater fish that lives in the shallows.

Discovery

The fossils were discovered in 2004 in the Fram Formation of southern Ellesmere Island, in stream deposits laid down near the equator during the Devonian. The team led by Neil Shubin, Edward Daeschler and Farish Jenkins had deliberately targeted rocks of the right age and environment to find an intermediate between fish such as Panderichthys and early tetrapods such as Acanthostega. The formal description, published in Nature in 2006, named the species Tiktaalik roseae, the species epithet honouring an anonymous benefactor. Well-preserved specimens range from about 1.25 to 2.75 metres in length.

Anatomy

Tiktaalik had a broad, flattened, crocodile-like skull with eyes on top, a distinct neck — its pectoral girdle was detached from the skull, unlike in fish — and overlapping rib bones that could support the body. Its pectoral fins contained bones corresponding to a humerus, radius and ulna, a wrist-like joint, and simple digits, allowing the fin to flex and bear weight in a push-up posture. Yet it retained fish features: scales, fin rays, and internal gills. The shape of its ear region (spiracle) also suggested it could breathe air at the surface. Its pelvis, described later, was surprisingly large and robust.

Significance

Because of its mosaic of fish and tetrapod traits, Tiktaalik is frequently cited in science education as an icon of evolution and a confirmed prediction: palaeontologists predicted where such an intermediate should occur and then found it there. Later discoveries, including tetrapod trackways from Poland dated older than Tiktaalik, have complicated a simple linear picture, but Tiktaalik remains a key datum on how fins became limbs and how vertebrates colonised land.
