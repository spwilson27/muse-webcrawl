Polygenic trait

A polygenic trait is a characteristic influenced by two or more genes, typically many, each contributing a small effect. Unlike Mendelian traits determined by a single locus, polygenic traits usually show continuous variation in a population—such as height, skin colour, body weight and intelligence—and their distribution often approximates a normal (bell-shaped) curve.

Quantitative genetics

The study of polygenic traits is the subject of quantitative genetics. R. A. Fisher's 1918 paper reconciled Mendelian inheritance with the continuous variation observed by biometricians by showing that many loci of small effect, combined with environmental influences, generate approximately normal distributions. Genetic effects are commonly partitioned into additive, dominance and interaction (epistatic) components, and heritability estimates measure the proportion of phenotypic variance attributable to genetic differences in a given population.

Modern genomics

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified thousands of variants associated with traits such as height, for which studies of millions of people have found more than ten thousand contributing loci, mostly of very small effect. Summing these effects yields polygenic scores, which can predict a modest fraction of individual variation and are used in research and, controversially, in some clinical and commercial settings. The tendency of identified loci to explain only part of the heritability estimated from family studies is known as the "missing heritability" problem.
