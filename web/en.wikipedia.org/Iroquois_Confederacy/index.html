Iroquois Confederacy

The Iroquois Confederacy, also known by the endonym Haudenosaunee ("People of the Longhouse"), is a historically powerful confederacy of Iroquoian-speaking Native American nations in northeast North America. It originally comprised five nations — the Mohawk, Oneida, Onondaga, Cayuga, and Seneca — united under the Great Law of Peace, traditionally credited to the prophet Deganawida and his spokesman Hiawatha. A sixth nation, the Tuscarora, joined in 1722, forming the Six Nations.

Government

The confederacy was governed by a Grand Council of fifty sachems meeting at Onondaga, with clan mothers holding the power to nominate and remove chiefs. Its consensus-based federal structure — frequently cited as one of the earliest participatory democracies — has been argued, controversially, to have influenced the framers of the United States Constitution.

History

Dates for the founding of the League range from the 12th to the 16th century. In the 17th century the Haudenosaunee grew powerful through the fur trade and the Beaver Wars, playing French and British empires against each other. The American Revolution split the confederacy, and the Sullivan Expedition of 1779 devastated Iroquois towns. Today the Six Nations maintain governments in New York, Ontario, and elsewhere, and Haudenosaunee passports and sovereignty remain live political questions.
