The asymptotic giant branch (AGB) is a region of the Hertzsprung–Russell diagram populated by evolved, cool, luminous stars. It is a period of stellar evolution undertaken by all low- to intermediate-mass stars (roughly 0.6–10 solar masses) late in their lives, after core helium exhaustion.

Structure and evolution

An AGB star consists of an inert carbon–oxygen core surrounded by a shell of burning helium, a shell of burning hydrogen, and a vast, tenuous hydrogen envelope. The helium shell is thermally unstable, producing periodic "thermal pulses" (helium shell flashes) every tens of thousands of years. Convective "dredge-up" episodes carry carbon and s-process elements to the surface, creating carbon stars and enriching the interstellar medium.

Mass loss and end state

Pulsation and radiation pressure on dust drive powerful stellar winds that strip the envelope — a "superwind" in the final phase. When the envelope is lost, the hot core is exposed as the central star of a planetary nebula and then cools as a white dwarf. Mira variables are well-known AGB stars. AGB stars are major cosmic sources of carbon, nitrogen and dust.
