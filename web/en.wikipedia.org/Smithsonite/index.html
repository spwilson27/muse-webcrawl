Smithsonite

Smithsonite, also known as zinc spar, is a mineral composed largely of zinc carbonate (ZnCO3). The mineral was named in 1832 by François Sulpice Beudant in honor of the English chemist and mineralogist James Smithson (c. 1765–1829), who first identified it as distinct from hemimorphite and who founded the Smithsonian Institution.

Occurrence

Smithsonite is a secondary mineral found in the oxidized zones of zinc ore deposits, where it forms by alteration of primary sulfide minerals such as sphalerite. It occurs in botryoidal, stalactitic, and honeycombed masses, and as rhombohedral crystals. Notable localities include the Kelly Mine in New Mexico, Tsumeb in Namibia, Broken Hill in Australia, and Laurium in Greece.

Uses

Historically an important ore of zinc, smithsonite was long grouped with hemimorphite under the name "calamine", which was used in the production of brass. Well-colored translucent specimens — green, blue, and pink — are cut as collector gemstones, though the mineral's softness limits its use in jewelry.
