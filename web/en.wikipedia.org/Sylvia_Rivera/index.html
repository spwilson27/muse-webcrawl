Sylvia Rivera (July 2, 1951 – February 19, 2002) was an American gay liberation and transgender rights activist who was also a noted community worker in New York. Rivera, who identified as a drag queen, participated in demonstrations with the Gay Liberation Front.

With close friend Marsha P. Johnson, Rivera co-founded the Street Transvestite Action Revolutionaries (STAR), a group dedicated to helping homeless young drag queens, gay youth, and trans women. Rivera was a participant in the Stonewall riots of June 1969, an event widely regarded as a turning point in the modern LGBTQ rights movement.

Early life

Rivera was born in New York City to a Puerto Rican father and a Venezuelan mother. Her father abandoned the family, and her mother died by suicide when Rivera was three, after which she was raised by her Venezuelan grandmother, who disapproved of her effeminate behavior. By age eleven, Rivera had left home and was living on the streets of New York, where she was taken in by a community of drag queens.

Activism

Rivera's activism began in the civil rights and antiwar movements before expanding to gay liberation following the Stonewall uprising. Following the riots, she worked with the Gay Liberation Front and the Gay Activists Alliance, but clashed with more mainstream elements of the movement over the exclusion of transgender people and drag queens from the political agenda.

In 1970 she and Marsha P. Johnson founded STAR, which opened STAR House, a shelter for homeless gay and trans youth—one of the first organizations of its kind, funded by the founders' own street work. In 1973, Rivera famously took the stage at the Christopher Street Liberation Day rally to denounce the movement's marginalization of transgender people, a speech remembered as an iconic moment in LGBTQ history.

Later life and legacy

Rivera spent periods of her later life homeless and struggling with substance abuse, while continuing to advocate for those at the margins of the LGBTQ community, including homeless youth, sex workers, and prisoners. She died of liver disease in 2002. The Sylvia Rivera Law Project, founded the year of her death, works for gender self-determination. In 2019, Rivera and Johnson were announced as the subjects of a monument in New York, among the first monuments in the world honoring transgender activists.
