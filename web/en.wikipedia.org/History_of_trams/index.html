History of trams

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Electric trams became the backbone of urban transport in the late nineteenth century.

The history of trams begins with horse-drawn street railways. The Swansea and Mumbles Railway, opened in 1807, carried the world's first fare-paying railway passengers, and from the 1830s horse-drawn tramcars appeared on the streets of New York and then in cities across Europe and the Americas. Running on rails set into the street, horse trams offered a smoother ride than horse buses.

Steam, cable and electric traction

From the 1870s, steam trams and cable cars (most famously in San Francisco, from 1873) supplemented horses. The decisive change came with electric traction: Werner von Siemens demonstrated an electric railway in Berlin in 1879, and Frank J. Sprague's successful system in Richmond, Virginia (1888) proved large-scale electric streetcar operation. Electric trams spread explosively, and by the early twentieth century they formed the principal urban transport of most large cities in the industrialized world.

Decline

After the Second World War, trams were widely displaced by buses and private cars. In the United States, most streetcar systems were dismantled by the 1960s; Britain closed nearly all its systems (Blackpool alone surviving in continuous service), and France largely abandoned trams. Systems survived and were modernized in much of continental Europe, the Soviet Union, Japan and in cities such as Toronto, Melbourne and San Francisco.

Revival

From the 1970s and 1980s, new light rail and modern tram systems opened in North America and Europe, and France launched a renaissance of city tramways beginning with Nantes in 1985. Low-floor vehicles, segregated tracks, tram-trains and sleek modern designs have made the tram a standard part of contemporary urban transport policy, with hundreds of systems now operating or under construction worldwide.
