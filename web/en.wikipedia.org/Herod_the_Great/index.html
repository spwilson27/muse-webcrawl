Herod the Great


The Herodium, one of Herod's building projects

Herod I (c. 72 BCE – c. 4 BCE), also known as Herod the Great, was a Roman client king of Judea, known for his colossal building projects throughout the region and for his role in the events of the New Testament, where he is depicted ordering the Massacre of the Innocents in an attempt to kill the infant Jesus. Aided by his father Antipater the Idumaean, Herod rose through Roman politics, was appointed king by the Roman Senate in 40 BCE, and conquered his kingdom by 37 BCE, ruling until his death.

Building programme

Herod rebuilt the Second Temple in Jerusalem on a vast scale—the retaining walls of its platform, including the Western Wall, survive today. He founded the port city of Caesarea Maritima with a great artificial harbour, built fortresses and palaces at Masada, Herodium, Jericho and Machaerus, and constructed temples and public works in cities across the eastern Mediterranean, earning a reputation as one of antiquity's great builders. His projects were financed by heavy taxation that bred resentment among his Jewish subjects, many of whom also distrusted his Idumaean origins.

Tyranny and family

Herod's later reign was marked by paranoia and familial bloodshed: he executed his wife Mariamne I, her brother and grandfather, three of his own sons (including his heir Antipater days before his own death), and numerous courtiers. Emperor Augustus is said to have remarked that it was better to be Herod's pig than his son—a Greek pun on hus (pig) and huios (son).

Death and succession

Herod died at Jericho around 4 BCE after a painful final illness (the date anchors scholarly discussions of the chronology of Jesus's birth). His kingdom was divided among his sons Archelaus, Antipas, and Philip, and his sister Salome, as the Herodian tetrarchy.
