Toba catastrophe theory

The Toba catastrophe theory holds that a supervolcanic eruption at Lake Toba on the island of Sumatra, Indonesia, about 74,000 years ago caused a global volcanic winter lasting six to ten years, sharply reduced the worldwide human population to perhaps 3,000–10,000 individuals, and thereby created a genetic bottleneck whose effects are still visible in the low genetic diversity of modern humans.


Lake Toba fills the caldera of the supervolcano

The eruption

The Youngest Toba eruption, rated VEI 8, is the largest known explosive eruption of the last 25 million years. It expelled roughly 2,800 km³ of material — hundreds of times more than the 1980 eruption of Mount St. Helens — and injected vast quantities of sulfur aerosols into the stratosphere. Ice-core records show a sharp sulfate spike and a cold interval of roughly a millennium coinciding with the onset of a glacial period.

The bottleneck hypothesis

In 1993 journalist Ann Gibbons popularised the theory, and in 1998 anthropologist Stanley Ambrose developed it formally: the volcanic winter devastated ecosystems, and the human population crashed to a few thousand breeding individuals in equatorial African refuges. Proponents linked the bottleneck to the relatively shallow genetic variation among living humans and to the divergence dates of human populations and of body lice. Subsequent studies have both supported and questioned the severity of the crash: fossil and archaeological evidence from India and East Africa suggests some human and animal populations weathered the eruption better than the strongest version of the theory predicts, and later modelling indicates regional climate effects varied considerably. The eruption's major climatic impact is widely accepted; the size of the human bottleneck remains debated.
