Preamble

A preamble is an introductory and expressionary statement in a document that explains the document's purpose and underlying philosophy. When applied to the opening paragraphs of a statute, it may recite historical facts pertinent to the subject of the statute. It is distinct from the long title or enacting formula of a law.

In parliamentary drafting, the preamble of a bill gives its reasons and intended effects, while the clauses enact its provisions. In legal interpretation, courts may consult a preamble to resolve ambiguity in the operative text, although the preamble itself does not confer substantive powers.

Constitutional preambles

Many constitutions open with a preamble setting out the source of authority and the aims of the state. The United States Constitution begins "We the People", listing purposes including to "form a more perfect Union" and "secure the Blessings of Liberty". The preamble to the Constitution of India declares India a sovereign, socialist, secular and democratic republic and promises justice, liberty, equality and fraternity to all citizens. The French Constitution of 1958 incorporates by reference the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen of 1789.

Legal effect

Traditionally a preamble is not itself enforceable, but courts have treated constitutional preambles as guides to interpretation. The Supreme Court of India held in the Kesavananda Bharati case (1973) that the preamble is part of the Constitution and reflects its basic structure.
