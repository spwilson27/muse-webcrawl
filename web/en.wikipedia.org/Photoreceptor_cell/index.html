Photoreceptor cell

A photoreceptor cell is a specialized type of neuroepithelial cell found in the retina that is capable of visual phototransduction. The great biological importance of photoreceptors is that they convert light (visible electromagnetic radiation) into signals that can stimulate biological processes. To be more specific, photoreceptor proteins in the cell absorb photons, triggering a change in the cell's membrane potential.

Types

The mature human retina contains about 7 million cones and 75 to 150 million rods. Cones are concentrated in the fovea, provide high-acuity colour vision in bright light, and come in three types sensitive to short, medium, and long wavelengths. Rods dominate the peripheral retina, mediate vision in dim light, and contain the pigment rhodopsin. A third class, the intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells, containing melanopsin, contributes mainly to circadian rhythms and the pupillary light reflex rather than image formation.

Phototransduction

When a photon is absorbed by the photopigment (an opsin bound to 11-cis-retinal), the retinal isomerizes, activating a G-protein cascade that closes cyclic-GMP-gated sodium channels. Unusually, photoreceptors hyperpolarize in response to light and release less glutamate onto bipolar cells. The stacked membrane discs of the outer segment, packed with pigment, maximise photon capture, and are continually shed and renewed by the retinal pigment epithelium.

Development and disease

Photoreceptors differentiate under transcription factors such as CRX and NRL. Inherited degenerations such as retinitis pigmentosa and age-related macular degeneration cause photoreceptor loss and blindness; gene therapy for RPE65-associated Leber congenital amaurosis was the first approved treatment targeting an inherited retinal disease.
