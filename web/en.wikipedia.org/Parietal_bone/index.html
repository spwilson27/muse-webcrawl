The parietal bones (from Latin paries, "wall") are two bones in the human skull which, when joined at the sagittal suture, form the sides and roof of the cranium. They are quadrilateral, curved plates situated behind the frontal bone, above the temporal bones, and in front of the occipital bone, protecting the parietal lobes of the brain beneath them.

Structure

Each parietal bone has an external surface, smooth and convex, marked near its centre by the parietal eminence (tuber parietale) and crossed by the superior and inferior temporal lines for attachment of the temporalis muscle and its fascia. A small parietal foramen near the sagittal border sometimes transmits an emissary vein. The internal surface is concave, bearing impressions of the cerebral gyri and grooves for the middle meningeal vessels; along the sagittal margin runs a groove for the superior sagittal sinus. Like other flat bones of the vault, the parietal consists of two layers of compact bone (tables) enclosing cancellous diploë.

Borders and sutures

  • Sagittal (superior) border — meets the opposite parietal at the sagittal suture; the bregma lies at its junction with the coronal suture and the lambda with the lambdoid suture.
  • Frontal (anterior) border — forms the coronal suture with the frontal bone.
  • Occipital (posterior) border — forms the lambdoid suture.
  • Squamosal (inferior) border — articulates with the temporal bone and the greater wing of the sphenoid.

Development

The parietal bone develops by intramembranous ossification from two centres appearing around the eighth week of fetal life near the parietal eminence. At birth the bones are separated by fontanelles, and the four corners remain incompletely ossified; the sagittal and other sutures typically begin to fuse in adulthood, a feature used (with caution) in forensic age estimation.
