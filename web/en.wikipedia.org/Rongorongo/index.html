Rongorongo

Rongorongo is a system of glyphs discovered in the 19th century on Easter Island (Rapa Nui) that appears to be writing or proto-writing. It is one of the very few independent inventions of writing, and it remains undeciphered.

Texts and medium

Rongorongo texts are found on about two dozen wooden objects — tablets, staffs, statuettes, and ornaments — preserved in museums and collections around the world. The glyphs, carved with shark teeth or obsidian flakes, depict anthropomorphic figures, animals, plants, and geometric shapes. The texts are read in reverse boustrophedon: the reader reads one line, then rotates the tablet 180 degrees to read the next.

History

The script was reported to the outside world in 1864 by the missionary Eugène Eyraud. By then, Peruvian slave raids (1862–63) and disease had devastated Rapa Nui society, and with the deportation and death of the island's learned class (the ma'ori rongorongo), knowledge of the script was lost. Oral tradition held that the script was brought by the legendary founder Hotu Matu'a.

Decipherment attempts

Numerous decipherment claims have been made, none accepted by mainstream scholarship. Structural analysis by Jacques Guy, Konstantin Pozdniakov and others suggests the script is logosyllabic with a core inventory of roughly 55–120 glyphs. A barkcloth tablet studied in the 2020s was radiocarbon-dated to the 15th century, suggesting the script predates European contact, though debate continues.
