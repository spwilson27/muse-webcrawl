The Yoruba people (Yoruba: Àwọn ọmọ Yorùbá) are a West African ethnic group inhabiting southwestern and central Nigeria, Benin and Togo — the region known as Yorubaland — with large diaspora communities in the Americas and Britain. Numbering roughly 50 million, they are one of Africa's largest ethnic groups, noted for a long tradition of urban civilisation, art and religion.

History

Yoruba tradition centres on Ile-Ife, regarded as the sacred city where the world was created and where, from about the 11th–15th centuries, artists produced celebrated naturalistic bronze and terracotta heads. The Oyo Empire dominated much of Yorubaland in the 17th–18th centuries with its cavalry and constitutional monarchy of the Alaafin and Oyo Mesi council. In the 19th century civil wars and the rise of Ibadan reshaped the region before British colonisation.

Culture and religion

Yoruba religion (Isese) venerates a supreme god, Olodumare, and a pantheon of orishas such as Shango, Ogun and Ifa divination, recognised by UNESCO as intangible heritage. Yoruba art includes the Gelede and Egungun festivals, beaded regalia, and woodcarving; Yoruba diasporic religions — Santería/Lucumí, Candomblé and Trinidad Orisha — carried these traditions to the Americas. Notable Yoruba figures include Nobel laureate Wole Soyinka and musician Fela Kuti.

Language

Yoruba is a tonal language of the Yoruboid branch, written in a Latin-based orthography with underdots, and spoken by tens of millions as a first or second language.
