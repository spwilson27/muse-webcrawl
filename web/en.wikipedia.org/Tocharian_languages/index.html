The Tocharian languages are an extinct branch of the Indo-European language family, known from manuscripts dating from roughly the 5th to the 9th century CE found in the northern Tarim Basin, in what is now Xinjiang, China. Two languages are attested: Tocharian A (also called Agnean or East Tocharian), used mainly for Buddhist religious texts around Turfan, and Tocharian B (Kuchean, or West Tocharian), used for both religious and everyday purposes around Kucha. Their discovery in the early 20th century astonished scholars, because they were the easternmost Indo-European languages known, yet were "centum" languages more closely resembling western branches than their geographic neighbours.

Discovery

Expeditions by Aurel Stein and others recovered bundles of manuscripts in an unknown language from sites such as Turfan and Kucha. In 1908 Emil Sieg and Wilhelm Siegling identified the language as Indo-European and named it "Tocharian", linking its speakers to the Tokharoi of Greek sources and to peoples known in Chinese records — a naming that remains conventional though its exact historical identification is debated.

Features

Tocharian was written in a script derived from Indian Brahmi, supplemented with special characters. The languages show thoroughgoing restructuring of the Indo-European inheritance: the three-way stop system collapsed, nominal cases were reshaped — a small set of primary cases supplemented by agglutinative secondary cases built on an oblique stem — and the verb system was heavily rebuilt. Tocharian B preserves more archaic features and was a living vernacular; Tocharian A may already have been a purely liturgical language when the texts were written.

Texts and extinction

The surviving corpus consists largely of Buddhist literature — translations of Sanskrit texts, monastic records, monastery accounts, caravan passes and love poetry, including a Tocharian B love poem among the earliest secular texts. The languages died out around the 9th century as their speakers were absorbed into the Turkic-speaking Uyghur population of the region. Tocharian is often associated with the Afanasievo culture of the Bronze Age steppe and with the strikingly preserved mummies of the Tarim Basin, though these connections remain subjects of research.
