Cross-cultural communication

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cross-cultural communication is the study of how people from differing cultural backgrounds communicate, in similar and different ways among themselves, and how they endeavour to communicate across cultures. It is closely related to intercultural communication, a field that draws on anthropology, psychology, communication studies, and linguistics.

Origins

The field is commonly traced to anthropologist Edward T. Hall, whose book The Silent Language (1959) argued that much of communication is shaped by unspoken cultural rules governing time, space, and context. Hall's distinction between high-context and low-context cultures remains widely cited. Later frameworks include Geert Hofstede's cultural dimensions theory, which compares national cultures along dimensions such as individualism, power distance, and uncertainty avoidance.

Topics

Research addresses verbal and nonverbal communication, including gesture, eye contact, personal space, and attitudes to silence; negotiation and management in multinational organizations; language barriers and translation; and the adjustment of migrants, students, and expatriates to new cultural environments, sometimes described as culture shock. The field also examines how globalization and digital media create shared communicative spaces while preserving local norms.

Criticism

Scholars have warned against treating national cultures as uniform or static, noting that dimensions-based models can encourage stereotyping and neglect variation within countries, as well as the effects of profession, generation, and individual personality.
