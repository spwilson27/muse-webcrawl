Argument

In logic and philosophy, an argument is a series of statements (called premises) intended to determine the degree of truth of another statement, the conclusion. The logical form of an argument in a natural language can be represented symbolically or in ordinary language. Arguments are studied in logic, rhetoric, mathematics, and informal reasoning.

Types of argument

A deductive argument is one in which the premises are intended to provide complete support for the conclusion, so that if the premises are true, the conclusion must be true; such an argument is called valid if it succeeds in this, and sound if it is valid and its premises are actually true. An inductive argument is one in which the premises are intended to provide only probable support for the conclusion. Abductive arguments infer the best explanation for a set of observations.

Argumentation

The study of how arguments are constructed, exchanged, and evaluated in dialogue is called argumentation theory. Informal logic examines arguments in natural language, including the identification of fallacies — errors in reasoning such as ad hominem attacks, straw man arguments, and appeals to ignorance. In everyday language, "argument" can also mean a heated disagreement, a sense distinct from the logical one.

Formal and mathematical usage

In mathematics and computing, "argument" also refers to the input values of a function or procedure, and in complex analysis it denotes the angle (phase) of a complex number.
