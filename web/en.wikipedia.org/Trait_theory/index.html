Trait theory seeks a taxonomy of stable personality dimensions, such as the Big Five.

In psychology, trait theory (also called dispositional theory) is an approach to the study of human personality. Trait theorists are primarily interested in the measurement of traits, which can be defined as habitual patterns of behavior, thought, and emotion. According to this perspective, traits are relatively stable over time, differ across individuals, and influence behavior across situations. Trait theory is the dominant paradigm in modern personality psychology.

History and major models

Gordon Allport pioneered the approach, combing an English dictionary for some 4,500 trait-like words and organizing traits into cardinal, central, and secondary dispositions; he argued for the idiographic study of the individual. Raymond Cattell reduced Allport's list statistically, using factor analysis to propose 16 personality factors measured by the 16PF questionnaire. Hans Eysenck proposed a biologically grounded model of three superfactors — psychoticism, extraversion, and neuroticism (the PEN model) — linked to cortical arousal and conditioning. Work by Warren Norman, Lewis Goldberg, and others converged on five broad factors, formalized by Robert McCrae and Paul Costa as the Big Five: openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism — measured by instruments such as the NEO-PI-R. The six-factor HEXACO model, derived from cross-linguistic lexical studies, adds an honesty–humility dimension.

The person–situation debate

In 1968 Walter Mischel's Personality and Assessment challenged the field, noting that trait measures rarely correlated with behavior in a given situation above about r = .30 (the "personality coefficient"), and arguing that behavior is driven more by situations than traits. The resulting person–situation debate was largely resolved by interactionism: traits predict behavioral aggregates and long-run trends well, situations shape moment-to-moment behavior, and strong versus weak situations moderate trait expression. Later work on whole trait theory treats traits as distributions of states.

Assessment and findings

Traits are assessed mainly by self-report inventories and observer ratings, showing moderate heritability (roughly 40–60% in twin studies), mean-level change across the lifespan (the maturity principle), and predictive validity for outcomes including job performance (especially conscientiousness), health, longevity, and relationship stability. Cross-cultural studies find the Big Five structure replicates broadly, though not perfectly, across languages and societies.
