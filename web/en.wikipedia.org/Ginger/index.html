Ginger

Ginger (Zingiber officinale) is a flowering plant whose rhizome, known as ginger root, is widely used as a spice and in traditional medicine. It belongs to the family Zingiberaceae, which also includes turmeric, cardamom, and galangal.

Origin and cultivation

Ginger originated in the tropical rainforests of South and Southeast Asia and is one of the oldest known spices, cultivated for thousands of years in India and China. It does not grow wild and is propagated entirely by planting pieces of rhizome. From Asia it spread along trade routes to the Middle East, the Mediterranean, Africa, and the Caribbean. Today the largest producers include India, Nigeria, China, and Indonesia. The plant grows to about a metre tall, with narrow leaves and pale yellow flowers with purple markings.

Culinary use

Fresh ginger is pungent and aromatic, owing mainly to compounds called gingerols; dried ginger is hotter, as gingerols convert to shogaols. Ginger is a staple of Asian cuisines, used in curries, stir-fries, pickles, and teas, and in the West it flavours gingerbread, biscuits, ginger ale, and ginger beer. Young rhizomes are tender and mild; mature ones are fibrous and stronger in flavour.

Traditional and medicinal use

Ginger has been used in Ayurvedic and Chinese medicine for digestive complaints. Modern studies support some effectiveness against nausea, including nausea in pregnancy and chemotherapy, and it is commonly consumed as a tea or supplement for colds and indigestion, though evidence for many claimed benefits remains limited.
