Digital cinema

Digital cinema is the digital technology used within the film industry to distribute or project motion pictures as opposed to the historical use of reels of motion picture film, such as 35 mm film. Whereas traditional film reels are shipped to movie theaters, digital films may be distributed to theaters via satellite, optical disks, or through the public or a private network. A movie theater shows movies using a digital projector instead of a conventional film projector.

History

The transition to digital began in earnest in the late 1990s. The Digital Cinema Initiatives (DCI), a joint venture of major motion picture studios, was formed in March 2002 to establish and document specifications for a digital cinema system. In 1999, George Lucas's Star Wars: Episode I – The Phantom Menace became one of the first widely distributed films to be screened digitally in a handful of theaters. By the mid-2010s, the vast majority of cinemas worldwide had converted to digital projection, driven partly by the release of James Cameron's Avatar in 2009, which encouraged exhibitors to install digital and 3D-capable equipment.

Technology

Digital movies are distributed as Digital Cinema Packages (DCPs), which conform to specifications set by the DCI. A DCP typically contains the picture track, compressed using the JPEG 2000 standard, and uncompressed audio tracks. Projectors use either DLP (Digital Light Processing) technology developed by Texas Instruments or LCoS (liquid crystal on silicon) technology. Resolutions are commonly 2K (2048×1080) or 4K (4096×2160).

Advantages and disadvantages

Digital distribution eliminates the cost of printing and shipping heavy film reels, allows for consistent image quality that does not degrade with repeated screenings, and enables alternative content such as live broadcasts of concerts and sporting events. Critics have raised concerns about long-term archival stability of digital formats, higher equipment costs for small exhibitors, and the aesthetic differences between film grain and digital images.
