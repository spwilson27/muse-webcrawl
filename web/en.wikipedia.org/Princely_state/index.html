Princely state

A princely state (also called a native state or Indian state) was a nominally sovereign entity of British India that was ruled by an indigenous ruler in a subsidiary alliance or other indirect relationship with the British Crown. At the time of Indian independence in 1947 there were 565 princely states, ranging from vast territories such as Hyderabad and Jammu and Kashmir, each comparable to a European country, to tiny estates of a few villages.

Relationship with the British Crown

The princely states were not British territory and were not directly administered by the British, but their rulers owed allegiance to the Crown and their foreign affairs, defence and communications were controlled by the British under treaties and the doctrine of paramountcy. A British Resident or Political Agent was stationed at most of the larger courts. Rulers held titles such as Maharaja, Raja, Nizam, Nawab and Maharana, and the more important states received gun salutes of between 9 and 21 guns.

Major states

The largest and most important states included Hyderabad (ruled by the Nizam), Mysore, Travancore, Baroda, Gwalior, Udaipur, Jaipur, Jodhpur, Indore, Bhopal and Jammu and Kashmir. Together the princely states covered about two-fifths of the area of the Indian subcontinent and about a quarter of its population.

Integration after 1947

Under the Indian Independence Act 1947, British paramountcy lapsed and the rulers were technically free to accede to India or Pakistan or remain independent. Through the diplomacy of Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel and V. P. Menon, nearly all rulers signed Instruments of Accession to one dominion or the other; a few cases — Hyderabad, Junagadh and Kashmir — were resolved by police action, plebiscite or became the subject of a lasting dispute. The states were subsequently merged into the unions of India and Pakistan, and the rulers' remaining privileges, including privy purses, were abolished in India by constitutional amendment in 1971.
