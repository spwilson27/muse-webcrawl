Ocean observatory

An ocean observatory is a long-term, fixed or mobile installation of instruments in the ocean that continuously measures physical, chemical, biological and geological properties and transmits the data to shore, usually in real time. Ocean observatories support research into climate, ecosystems, earthquakes and tsunamis, and the deep ocean environment.

Types

Cabled observatories, such as Canada's Ocean Networks Canada installations and the United States' Ocean Observatories Initiative, are connected to shore by fibre-optic cables that supply power and data bandwidth to seafloor nodes. Uncabled systems include moored buoys, profiling floats such as the Argo array, and autonomous underwater vehicles that surface periodically to transmit data by satellite.

Measurements

Instruments routinely record temperature, salinity, currents, dissolved oxygen, chlorophyll, turbidity, hydroacoustic signals and seismic activity, together with video and still imagery of deep-sea life. Long continuous time series allow scientists to detect events such as marine heatwaves, harmful algal blooms and submarine volcanic eruptions as they happen.

History

Permanent deep-ocean observation grew out of Cold War hydrophone arrays and early moorings. Dedicated cabled observatories began operating in the 1990s in Japan and off North America, and large regional systems came online in the 2000s and 2010s, transforming oceanography from an expedition-based science toward continuous monitoring.
