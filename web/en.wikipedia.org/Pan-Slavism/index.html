Pan-Slavism

Pan-Slavism is a political and cultural ideology and movement that emerged in the 19th century, advocating the political and cultural unity of all Slavic peoples. Inspired by other pan-nationalist movements such as Pan-Germanism, it developed among intellectuals in the Slavic lands of the Austrian and Ottoman empires, where many Slavs lived under foreign rule.

Origins

The movement's intellectual roots lie with figures such as the Slovak scholar Ján Kollár, whose poem Slávy dcera (1824) celebrated Slavic reciprocity, and Pavel Jozef Šafárik, who documented Slavic languages and antiquities. The First Pan-Slav Congress met in Prague in 1848, during the revolutionary year, under the chairmanship of František Palacký; it sought equality for Slavs within a federalised Austria (Austro-Slavism) rather than union with Russia.

Russian Pan-Slavism

In Russia, Pan-Slavism took a more expansionist form in the later 19th century, associated with thinkers such as Nikolay Danilevsky, whose Russia and Europe (1869) argued for a Slavic federation led by Russia. Russian Pan-Slav sentiment influenced policy during the Russo-Turkish War of 1877–78 and the Balkan crises, and contributed to the alliance system that preceded the First World War, when Russia posed as protector of Serbia.

Later developments

Competing movements included Illyrianism and Yugoslavism, which sought South Slav unity, and Neo-Slavism in the early 20th century, which promoted cooperation among Slavic nations on equal terms. After 1918, the creation of Yugoslavia, Czechoslovakia, and an independent Poland realised some Pan-Slav aims in national form, though often amid conflict between Slavs themselves. The ideology declined in the 20th century, and it retains only marginal political significance today.
