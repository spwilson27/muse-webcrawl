SARS

Severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) is a viral respiratory disease of zoonotic origin caused by the virus SARS-CoV-1, the first identified strain of the SARS-related coronavirus. The disease first emerged in Foshan, Guangdong, China, in November 2002 and spread to more than two dozen countries in the 2003 outbreak, infecting 8,096 people and killing 774 before it was contained in July 2003.

The SARS coronavirus (SARS-CoV-1), the virus that causes SARS.

Signs and symptoms

SARS typically begins with a high fever above 38 °C (100.4 °F), headache, body aches and an overall feeling of discomfort. After 2–7 days, patients develop a dry cough and breathing difficulties; most cases progress to pneumonia. The incubation period is typically 4–6 days. The case fatality rate was around 9.6%, rising sharply among patients over 65.

Transmission and origin

SARS-CoV-1 spreads mainly through close person-to-person contact via respiratory droplets. The virus is believed to have originated in horseshoe bats and passed to humans through intermediate hosts such as masked palm civets sold in live animal markets in Guangdong. Superspreading events — notably at the Metropole Hotel and the Amoy Gardens housing estate in Hong Kong, and in hospitals in Toronto, Singapore and Hanoi — drove much of the outbreak.

Containment

With no vaccine or specific treatment, the outbreak was stopped by classical public-health measures: case isolation, contact tracing, quarantine and hospital infection control, coordinated by the World Health Organization's global alert of March 2003. No cases have been reported since 2004, apart from laboratory-acquired infections. SARS was the first severe, readily transmissible new disease of the 21st century and a precursor of the later COVID-19 pandemic, caused by the related virus SARS-CoV-2.
