Traditional Chinese characters

Traditional characters in calligraphy

Traditional Chinese characters are a standardised set of Chinese characters used in the writing of the Chinese language before the character simplification movements of the 20th century. They are the principal script in Taiwan, Hong Kong and Macau, and remain widely used among overseas Chinese communities and in formal, scholarly and artistic contexts throughout the Chinese-speaking world.

History

Chinese characters developed over more than three thousand years, from oracle bone script through seal and clerical scripts to the regular script (kaishu), which stabilised in its essentials by the Tang dynasty and remained the standard for printing for over a millennium. In the 1950s and 1960s the government of the People's Republic of China promulgated simplified Chinese characters to promote literacy, and Singapore and Malaysia later adopted the simplified system as well. Japan's kanji underwent a separate, more limited standardisation (shinjitai), and Korean hanja retained traditional forms.

Usage and debate

Traditional characters are the official script of Taiwan and the standard in Hong Kong and Macau, where they are used in government, education and media. Supporters of traditional characters argue for their historical continuity, their visual differentiation of words that simplified forms merge, and their role in preserving the internal logic and aesthetics of the script. Critics counter that the greater stroke counts impose learning burdens. In mainland China, traditional characters persist in calligraphy, seals, signage, classical scholarship and reprinted pre-modern texts, and many mainland readers can read them with modest effort, since the majority of common characters are identical in both systems.
