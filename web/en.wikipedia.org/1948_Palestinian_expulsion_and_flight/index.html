The 1948 Palestinian expulsion and flight, known in Arabic as the Nakba ("catastrophe"), was the mass displacement of Palestinian Arabs during the 1948 Palestine war. Estimates generally hold that more than 700,000 Palestinian Arabs — over half of the Arab population of Mandatory Palestine — fled or were expelled from their homes, and that upwards of 400 Arab towns and villages were depopulated.

Background

In November 1947 the United Nations General Assembly adopted Resolution 181, the Partition Plan for Palestine, proposing separate Jewish and Arab states. Fighting between Jewish and Arab forces in Mandatory Palestine escalated into civil war, and following the Israeli Declaration of Independence on 14 May 1948, neighbouring Arab states invaded, beginning the first Arab–Israeli war.

Course of the exodus

The displacement occurred in several waves. In the early months of 1948, many urban middle- and upper-class families left cities such as Haifa, Jaffa, and Jerusalem. Later waves accompanied major military operations by the Haganah and, after May 1948, the Israel Defense Forces. Some depopulations resulted from direct expulsion orders — most notably in the twin cities of Lydda and Ramle in July 1948, where tens of thousands of inhabitants were forced to leave — while in other places residents fled fighting, fear of attack, or the effects of psychological warfare. Massacres such as that at Deir Yassin in April 1948, in which over one hundred villagers were killed, contributed to panic and flight elsewhere.

Aftermath

Most refugees settled in the Gaza Strip, the West Bank, Jordan, Lebanon, and Syria, where the United Nations established the relief agency UNRWA in 1949 to assist them. Israel declined to permit a general return of refugees after the war, and the new state absorbed the abandoned property under the Absentees' Property Law of 1950. The status of the refugees and their descendants' claimed "right of return" remains one of the central unresolved issues of the Israeli–Palestinian conflict.

Historiography

The causes of the exodus have been debated by historians for decades. Traditional Israeli narratives emphasised voluntary flight encouraged by Arab leaders, while Palestinian narratives emphasised deliberate expulsion. Research by Israel's so-called New Historians from the 1980s onward, drawing on declassified archives, documented a mixture of flight, fear, and explicit expulsions, and the debate continues.
