JAK-STAT signaling pathway

TypeCell signalling pathway
Key componentsCytokine receptor, JAK kinases, STAT transcription factors
SignalsInterferons, interleukins, growth factors

The JAK-STAT signalling pathway is a chain of interactions between proteins in a cell, used to transmit information from extracellular chemical signals — especially cytokines and growth factors — directly to the cell nucleus, resulting in the activation of genes in a process called transcription. It is one of the most direct routes from the cell surface to the genome and is central to immunity, cell division, cell death, and tumour formation.

Mechanism

Signalling begins when a ligand such as an interferon or interleukin binds to a cell-surface cytokine receptor. The receptor is associated intracellularly with Janus kinase (JAK) enzymes. Ligand binding brings the JAKs together, and they phosphorylate and activate one another and the receptor, creating docking sites for STAT proteins (signal transducers and activators of transcription).

Recruited STATs are themselves phosphorylated by the JAKs, whereupon they dimerise, enter the nucleus, bind to specific DNA sequences, and switch on target genes. The pathway is switched off by suppressor of cytokine signalling (SOCS) proteins, protein tyrosine phosphatases, and protein inhibitors of activated STAT (PIAS).

Components

Mammals have four JAK family members — JAK1, JAK2, JAK3, and TYK2 — and seven STAT proteins, STAT1 through STAT6 (including STAT5A and STAT5B). Different receptor–JAK–STAT combinations transduce signals for more than 50 cytokines and hormones, including erythropoietin, growth hormone, and prolactin.

Clinical significance

Disruption of JAK-STAT signalling causes disease: loss-of-function mutations in JAK3 produce severe combined immunodeficiency, while the gain-of-function JAK2 V617F mutation drives most cases of polycythaemia vera and other myeloproliferative neoplasms. Small-molecule JAK inhibitors ("jakinibs") such as tofacitinib and ruxolitinib are used to treat rheumatoid arthritis, myelofibrosis, and other inflammatory and haematological conditions.

History

The pathway was worked out in the late 1980s and early 1990s through studies of interferon signalling by James Darnell, George Stark, Ian Kerr and colleagues, who identified the STAT proteins and showed that JAK kinases — named Janus kinases after the two-faced Roman god, reflecting their tandem kinase domains — link receptors to STATs.
