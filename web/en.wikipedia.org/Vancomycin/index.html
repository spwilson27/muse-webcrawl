Vancomycin

Vancomycin
Class: Glycopeptide antibiotic
Routes: Intravenous, oral
First isolated: 1953
Trade names: Vancocin, others

Vancomycin is a glycopeptide antibiotic used to treat a number of bacterial infections, particularly serious infections caused by Gram-positive bacteria resistant to other antibiotics, including methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). It is recommended intravenously for most infections and by mouth for infections of the intestines such as Clostridioides difficile colitis.

History

Vancomycin was first isolated in 1953 from the bacterium Amycolatopsis orientalis found in a soil sample from Borneo supplied to Eli Lilly. The name derives from "vanco", from vanquish. It was approved for use in 1958 but was initially sidelined by less toxic agents until the spread of MRSA in the 1980s made it a mainstay of hospital medicine.

Mechanism

Vancomycin inhibits bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding to the D-alanyl-D-alanine terminus of peptidoglycan precursors, preventing their incorporation into the cell wall. Resistance, as in vancomycin-resistant enterococci (VRE), arises when bacteria remodel this target to D-alanyl-D-lactate.

Adverse effects

Common side effects include infusion-related flushing ("red man syndrome"), kidney damage (nephrotoxicity) and hearing damage (ototoxicity), especially at high doses or with prolonged use. Blood levels are commonly monitored to balance efficacy and toxicity.
