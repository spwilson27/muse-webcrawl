Policy ineffectiveness proposition

The policy ineffectiveness proposition (PIP) is a new classical economics theory proposed in 1975 by Thomas J. Sargent and Neil Wallace, based on the theory of rational expectations. It holds that systematic, anticipated monetary and fiscal policy cannot affect real output or employment, even in the short run; only unanticipated policy "surprises" can move real variables, and even then only randomly.

Anticipated policy is neutralized by rational agents adjusting expectations.

Logic

If workers and firms form expectations rationally, they incorporate announced policy changes into their wage and price setting. A central bank that predictably expands the money supply to reduce unemployment will simply raise expected inflation; nominal wages and prices adjust in advance, leaving real output at its natural rate. Systematic demand management therefore produces only inflation, not growth. Random, unsystematic policy could affect output, but such policy is neither predictable nor desirable as a stabilization tool.

Relation to other ideas

The proposition builds on Robert Lucas's work on rational expectations and is closely related to the Lucas critique, which argues that econometric models estimated on historical data cannot reliably predict the effects of new policy regimes because private behavior changes with the regime. It also echoes the classical dichotomy and long-run monetary neutrality, extended to the short run under rational expectations and flexible prices.

Criticism

New Keynesian economists object that nominal rigidities — sticky wages, menu costs, and long-term contracts, as emphasized by Stanley Fischer and John B. Taylor — restore short-run effectiveness to anticipated policy. Empirical evidence on money's real effects has also been widely read as inconsistent with the strongest form of the proposition, though its emphasis on expectations became central to modern central banking practice.
