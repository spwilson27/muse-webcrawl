Eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 AD

The eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 AD was one of the most lethal and best-documented volcanic eruptions in European history. Over about two days the volcano buried the Roman cities of Pompeii and Herculaneum, along with the settlements of Oplontis and Stabiae, under metres of volcanic ash and pumice, killing thousands of inhabitants. The eruption ejected a cloud of super-heated gases and tephra to a height of some 33 kilometres and released around a hundred thousand times the thermal energy of the Hiroshima bombing.

Course of the eruption

The eruption began around midday — traditionally dated 24 August 79 AD, though some evidence suggests an autumn date — with a towering eruptive column of the kind now called a Plinian eruption, named after Pliny the Younger, whose two letters to the historian Tacitus provide the only surviving eyewitness account. Pumice fall rained on Pompeii for some 18 hours, collapsing roofs and driving survivors to flee. In the early hours of the following day the column collapsed, sending pyroclastic surges and flows down the flanks: Herculaneum, to the west, was overwhelmed by super-heated surges that killed its remaining population instantly, and a surge reached Pompeii in the morning, killing those still there. Pliny the Elder, commanding the Roman fleet at Misenum, crossed the bay to investigate and to rescue survivors, and died at Stabiae, apparently from asphyxiation.

Victims and rediscovery

Of a combined population of perhaps 20,000 in the affected towns, the remains of over 1,500 people have been found at Pompeii and Herculaneum; the true death toll is unknown but likely in the thousands. The buried cities were lost until their accidental rediscovery in the eighteenth century — Herculaneum in 1738 and Pompeii in 1748 — after which systematic excavations, and Giuseppe Fiorelli's plaster casts of the voids left by decomposed bodies, made the eruption the most famous of the ancient world and founded modern volcanology.
