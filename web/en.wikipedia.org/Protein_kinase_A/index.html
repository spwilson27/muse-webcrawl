Protein kinase A

In cell biology, protein kinase A (PKA) is a family of enzymes whose activity depends on cellular levels of cyclic AMP (cAMP); it is also known as cAMP-dependent protein kinase. PKA is one of the most widely studied protein kinases and a central effector of G protein-coupled receptor signalling.

Structure and activation

In its inactive form, PKA is a tetramer of two regulatory subunits and two catalytic subunits. When hormones such as adrenaline activate adenylyl cyclase and raise cAMP, four cAMP molecules bind the regulatory subunits, releasing the catalytic subunits to phosphorylate target proteins on serine and threonine residues.

Functions

PKA targets include metabolic enzymes controlling glycogen breakdown and synthesis, ion channels, and the transcription factor CREB, through which PKA influences gene expression, memory formation, and many other processes. Anchoring proteins (AKAPs) localize PKA within the cell so that its broad specificity can produce precise, compartmentalized responses.

History

The cAMP–PKA pathway emerged from the work of Earl Sutherland on second messengers, and the kinase itself was characterized in the late 1960s by Edwin Krebs, Edmond Fischer, and colleagues — work later recognized by the Nobel Prize.
