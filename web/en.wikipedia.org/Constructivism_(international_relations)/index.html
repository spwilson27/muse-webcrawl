Constructivism is a theory of international relations which holds that the fundamental structures of world politics are social rather than strictly material, and that the identities and interests of states are shaped by shared ideas, norms and interactions rather than given by nature. It emerged in the late 1980s and 1990s as a challenge to the dominant neorealist and neoliberal schools.

Origins

The term was coined by Nicholas Onuf in his 1989 book World of Our Making. Alexander Wendt's 1992 article "Anarchy is what states make of it" and his 1999 book Social Theory of International Politics became the school's most influential statements, arguing that self-help and power politics are institutions produced by practice, not inevitable features of anarchy.

Key concepts

Constructivists emphasise norms — shared expectations about appropriate behaviour — and study how they spread through processes such as the "norm life cycle" described by Martha Finnemore and Kathryn Sikkink. They also stress identity: states act toward enemies, rivals and friends differently, and their interests follow from who they understand themselves to be. Agents and structures are held to be mutually constituted.

Varieties and criticism

The school ranges from "conventional" constructivists who work within mainstream social science to critical and poststructuralist variants. Realists criticise constructivism for underplaying material power; constructivists reply that material forces acquire meaning only through ideas — five hundred British nuclear weapons, as Wendt noted, are less threatening to the United States than five North Korean ones.
