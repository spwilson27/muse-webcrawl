Detached object

A detached object is a dynamical class of minor planets in the outer reaches of the Solar System, beyond the orbit of Neptune. Detached objects have perihelia sufficiently distant from Neptune that they are not significantly perturbed by Neptune's gravity, which makes them appear "detached" from the rest of the Solar System.

Definition

In this way, detached objects differ substantially from most of the known trans-Neptunian objects (TNOs), which form a loosely defined set of populations that have been perturbed to varying degrees onto their current orbits by gravitational encounters with the gas giants, primarily Neptune. A commonly used threshold is a perihelion distance greater than about 40 astronomical units. The large dwarf planet Sedna, with a perihelion of 76 AU, is the archetypal detached object; others include 2012 VP113 and Leleākūhonua.

Origins

Because Neptune's gravity alone cannot lift perihelia so far from the Sun on the planets' present orbits, detached orbits are a puzzle. Proposed explanations include a passing star in the Sun's birth cluster, a rogue planet, and perturbations from a hypothesised distant massive planet ("Planet Nine"). Detached objects with extremely large semi-major axes are grouped as extreme trans-Neptunian objects, and the apparent clustering of their orbital angles has been cited as evidence for Planet Nine.

Classification

Detached objects overlap with the scattered disc: some catalogues treat them as an extension ("scattered-extended") of the scattered disc population, while others, following the deep ecliptic surveys, regard detachment as a distinct dynamical property. The inner boundary of the detached population near 40 AU corresponds to the point where Neptune scattering ceases to be effective over the age of the Solar System.
