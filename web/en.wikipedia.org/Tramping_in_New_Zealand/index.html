Tramping in New Zealand

Trampers crossing alpine tussock in the Southern Alps.

Tramping, known in other countries as bushwalking, hiking, or trekking, is a popular recreational activity in New Zealand. Tramping is defined as a recreational walk through forested, mountainous, or otherwise natural terrain, typically over one or more days. New Zealand's extensive network of tracks and backcountry huts—maintained by the Department of Conservation (DOC)—makes the country one of the world's most accessible destinations for multi-day walking.

Tracks and huts

New Zealand has more than 950 backcountry huts, the largest network of its kind in the world, ranging from small bivouacs to large serviced lodges. Tracks are graded from short walks to challenging routes requiring alpine skills and river-crossing experience.

Great Walks

DOC's Great Walks are nine (formerly ten, including the Paparoa Track) premier multi-day tracks, including the Milford Track, Routeburn Track, Kepler Track, Tongariro Northern Circuit, and the Abel Tasman Coast Track. Bookings are required for huts and campsites on these tracks during the Great Walks season.

Te Araroa

Te Araroa, "the long pathway," is a 3,000 km trail stretching from Cape Reinga to Bluff, opened in 2011. Through-walking the full length typically takes four to five months.

History

Organised tramping grew in the early 20th century with the founding of clubs such as the Tararua Tramping Club (1919). Māori had long travelled the country's interior on foot for food gathering, trade, and greenstone (pounamu) routes across the Southern Alps. European explorers, surveyors, and gold prospectors extended the network of routes, and deer cullers and government work parties built many of the huts and tracks still in use.

Safety

New Zealand weather is notoriously changeable, and river crossings are a leading cause of tramping fatalities. The New Zealand Mountain Safety Council promotes the Land Safety Code: plan your trip, tell someone your plans, be aware of the weather, know your limits, and take sufficient supplies. Trampers increasingly carry personal locator beacons in remote areas.
