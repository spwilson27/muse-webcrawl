Hepatocellular carcinoma

Specialty: Oncology, hepatology
Symptoms: Abdominal pain, weight loss, jaundice
Risk factors: Hepatitis B and C, cirrhosis, aflatoxin

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common type of primary liver cancer in adults and is currently the most common cause of death in people with cirrhosis. It is also the third leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide. It occurs predominantly in patients with underlying chronic liver disease and cirrhosis, though a growing number of cases arise in non-cirrhotic livers in the setting of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease.

Causes and risk factors

The major risk factors include chronic infection with hepatitis B virus (HBV) or hepatitis C virus (HCV), alcoholic liver disease, aflatoxin exposure, and metabolic syndrome. HBV can be directly oncogenic through integration of viral DNA into the host genome, so HCC may develop in HBV carriers without cirrhosis. Hepatitis B vaccination programmes have markedly reduced HCC incidence in endemic regions such as Taiwan.

Signs and symptoms

Presenting features often reflect both the tumour and decompensating liver disease: right upper quadrant pain, a palpable mass, unintentional weight loss, worsening ascites, jaundice, and hepatic encephalopathy. Serum alpha-fetoprotein (AFP) is elevated in a majority of cases and is used, together with imaging, in surveillance of high-risk patients.

Diagnosis and staging

Diagnosis is usually made by multiphasic CT or MRI showing the characteristic pattern of arterial phase hyperenhancement with washout in the portal venous phase (the basis of the LI-RADS system); biopsy is often unnecessary in cirrhotic patients. Staging systems such as the Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) classification guide therapy.

Treatment

Curative options include surgical resection, liver transplantation, and thermal ablation for small tumours. Intermediate disease may be treated with transarterial chemoembolization (TACE). Advanced HCC is treated with systemic therapy; the combination of atezolizumab and bevacizumab, and tyrosine kinase inhibitors such as sorafenib and lenvatinib, have improved survival.
