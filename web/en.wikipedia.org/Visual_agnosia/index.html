Visual agnosia

A patient's copy of an object they could not identify by sight

Field: Neurology, neuropsychology

Cause: Damage to the ventral visual stream

Visual agnosia is an impairment in recognition of visually presented objects. It is not due to a deficit in vision, language, memory, or intellect, but to damage in the higher visual processing areas of the brain, particularly the ventral ("what") stream of the occipital and temporal lobes. A person with visual agnosia may see an object and be able to describe or copy it, yet be unable to identify it — while recognizing it immediately by touch or sound.

Types

In apperceptive agnosia, basic visual processing is disrupted so that the patient cannot form a coherent percept; they fail at copying and matching shapes. In associative agnosia, perception is relatively intact — the patient can copy drawings accurately — but the percept cannot be linked to stored knowledge, so recognition fails. Related forms include prosopagnosia (inability to recognize faces), pure alexia (inability to read), akinetopsia (motion blindness), and simultanagnosia (inability to see more than one object at a time).

Causes and history

Visual agnosia most often results from stroke, head injury, or carbon monoxide poisoning affecting the posterior cerebral arteries. The term was introduced by Sigmund Freud in 1891, and Heinrich Lissauer had described the apperceptive/associative distinction in 1890. Oliver Sacks popularized the condition in his case study "The Man Who Mistook His Wife for a Hat" (1985).
