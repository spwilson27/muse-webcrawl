Diversity jurisdiction

Diversity jurisdiction
Legal basisU.S. Const. art. III, § 2; 28 U.S.C. § 1332
Amount in controversyMore than $75,000
RequirementComplete diversity of citizenship (generally)

In the law of the United States, diversity jurisdiction is a form of subject-matter jurisdiction that allows a United States federal court to hear a civil case when the parties are citizens of different states (or a citizen and a foreign national) and the amount in controversy exceeds $75,000. It is authorised by Article III of the Constitution and codified at 28 U.S.C. § 1332. Its traditional rationale is to protect out-of-state litigants from the possible local bias of a home-state court.

Requirements

Under the rule of Strawbridge v. Curtiss (1806), diversity must ordinarily be complete: no plaintiff may share state citizenship with any defendant. A person's citizenship for diversity purposes is their domicile. A corporation is a citizen both of its state of incorporation and of the state of its principal place of business (its "nerve centre", per Hertz Corp. v. Friend, 2010). Unincorporated associations such as partnerships and LLCs take the citizenship of all their members. The Class Action Fairness Act of 2005 relaxes complete diversity for large class actions under a "minimal diversity" standard.

Applicable law

A federal court sitting in diversity applies state substantive law and federal procedural law, under the doctrine of Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins (1938), which abolished the federal general common law of Swift v. Tyson. Diversity cases may be filed in federal court directly or removed there from state court by the defendant, subject to limits such as the forum-defendant rule.
