Frontier justice

Frontier justice is the exercise of self-help, vigilantism, or informal punishment in regions where formal legal authority is absent, distant, or distrusted — classically on the American frontier of the nineteenth century, but applied by extension to any weakly governed borderland.

Practice

On the American frontier, miners' courts, vigilance committees, and posses tried and punished accused thieves and murderers, sometimes by hanging, in the absence of established courts and law enforcement. San Francisco's Committees of Vigilance of 1851 and 1856 and the Montana Vigilantes of 1863–65 are well-known examples. Such bodies sometimes restored order where none existed, but they also dispensed punishment without due process and were used to settle private grudges.

Cultural legacy

Frontier justice became a staple of the Western genre in literature and film, romanticising the lone lawman or avenger. Historians debate how violent the frontier actually was compared with settled regions, and how much of its reputation is myth-making after the fact.
