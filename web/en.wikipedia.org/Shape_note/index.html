Shape note

Sacred Harp shape-note notation

Shape notes are a music notation system designed to facilitate congregational and social singing. In shape-note notation, the note heads of a scale are given distinct shapes — triangle, oval, square, diamond, and others — each corresponding to a syllable of a solmisation system (fa, sol, la, mi). Singers learn to sight-read by shape, so that a community with little musical training can sing harmonised hymns in parts.

Systems

Most shape-note systems use either four shapes (the "patent note" or buckwheat system, using fa–sol–la–mi syllables, descended from the Guidonian hexachord) or seven shapes (one per degree of the major scale, using do–re–mi syllables). The four-shape system is used in the Sacred Harp tradition; the seven-shape system, promoted by publishers such as Aldine Kieffer and the Ruebush-Kieffer company, predominates in Southern gospel music.

History

Shape notes arose in early-19th-century America, building on John Tufts's earlier introduction of letters on noteheads and the singing schools of New England. Little and Smith's The Easy Instructor (1801) is generally credited with introducing shaped noteheads. Itinerant singing masters carried the system west and south, and tunebooks such as Southern Harmony (1835) and The Sacred Harp (1844, compiled by B. F. White and E. J. King) became its classic repositories. Spurned by urban reformers who favoured European-style notation, shape-note singing survived in the rural South.

Living tradition

Sacred Harp singing — participatory, unaccompanied, full-voiced singing from the tunebook seated in a hollow square — has experienced a marked revival since the late 20th century, spreading beyond the South to conventions across the United States and internationally. Seven-shape traditions continue in gospel quartets and conventions, and shape-note hymnals remain in use in some Primitive Baptist and Church of Christ congregations.
