The Metaphysics (Greek: τὰ μετὰ τὰ φυσικά, "the works after the Physics") is one of the principal works of Aristotle and the first major work of the branch of philosophy with the same name. The principal subject is "being qua being", or being insofar as it is being: the study of the most general attributes of what there is. It also includes his accounts of substance, form and matter, actuality and potentiality, and the unmoved mover.

Contents

The work comprises fourteen books, conventionally labelled with Greek letters. Book Alpha surveys earlier philosophy; Beta lists puzzles (aporiai); Gamma defends the principle of non-contradiction; Zeta, Eta, and Theta investigate substance, form, potentiality, and actuality; Lambda presents Aristotle's theology of the prime mover — a purely actual, immaterial intellect that moves the cosmos as a final cause; and Mu and Nu discuss the philosophy of mathematics.

Transmission and influence

The text was assembled by later editors, traditionally Andronicus of Rhodes in the 1st century BC, from Aristotle's lecture treatises; its name likely reflects its placement after the Physics in the corpus. Through the commentaries of Alexander of Aphrodisias, the Arabic tradition (Al-Farabi, Avicenna, Averroes), and scholastic thinkers such as Thomas Aquinas, the Metaphysics shaped Western and Islamic philosophy for two millennia.
