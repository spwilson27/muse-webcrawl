Regional geography

The world divided into regions for systematic study.

Regional geography is a branch of geography that studies the world's regions of all sizes. It has been described as a descriptive, rather than purely analytical, tradition: the regional geographer synthesises knowledge of the physical environment, settlement, economy, culture and history of a delimited area in order to characterise what makes that place distinctive.

History

The tradition descends from the chorology of classical writers such as Strabo and Ptolemy, and was formalised in the nineteenth century by German geographers including Carl Ritter and Alfred Hettner, who treated the region (Landschaft) as the central object of geographical science. In France, Paul Vidal de la Blache emphasised the pays and the distinctive way of life (genre de vie) it supported. Richard Hartshorne's The Nature of Geography (1939) codified the regional approach as "areal differentiation" for the English-speaking world.

After the Second World War the quantitative revolution displaced regional geography in favour of systematic, model-based geography, and regional description came to be seen as unscientific. Interest revived from the 1980s with the "new regional geography", which treated regions as socially constructed and historically contingent rather than as natural units.

Approaches

Regional studies typically distinguish formal regions (defined by uniformity of some attribute, such as climate or language), functional regions (defined by flows and interactions, such as a city's hinterland) and vernacular regions (defined by popular perception, such as "the Midwest"). Modern regional geography draws on physical geography, historical geography, cultural geography and political economy in varying proportions.
