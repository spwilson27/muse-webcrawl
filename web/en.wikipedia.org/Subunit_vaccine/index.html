Subunit vaccine

A subunit vaccine is a vaccine that contains purified parts of a pathogen — typically a surface protein or a capsular polysaccharide — rather than the whole organism. Because it contains no live components, a subunit vaccine cannot cause the disease it protects against, and it can be given to people with weakened immune systems.

Types

Protein subunit vaccines contain a specific isolated protein from a virus or bacterium; the hepatitis B vaccine, made of the virus's surface antigen (HBsAg) produced in yeast, was the first recombinant subunit vaccine. The acellular pertussis (aP) component of DTaP vaccines is another example. Polysaccharide vaccines use the sugar coats of encapsulated bacteria, as in the pneumococcal polysaccharide vaccine; because polysaccharides alone elicit weak responses in young children, they are often chemically linked to carrier proteins in conjugate vaccines, such as the Hib and meningococcal conjugate vaccines. Virus-like particle (VLP) vaccines, such as the HPV vaccine, use self-assembling viral structural proteins that mimic the virus without genetic material.

Advantages and limitations

Subunit vaccines are safe and well characterised, and can be manufactured without handling the live pathogen. Their main drawback is lower immunogenicity: they generally require adjuvants and booster doses to maintain protection.
