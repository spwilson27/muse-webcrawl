The Baroque is a style of architecture, music, dance, painting, sculpture, and other arts that flourished in Europe from the early seventeenth century until the 1740s. It followed Renaissance art and Mannerism and was succeeded by the Rococo in some regions and by Neoclassicism in others. The Baroque style is characterised by grandeur, drama, movement, tension, and emotional exuberance, often in the service of the Counter-Reformation Catholic Church and absolutist monarchies.

Origins and character

The style began in Rome around 1600, encouraged by a Church seeking to communicate religious themes with direct emotional involvement. The word "baroque" probably derives from the Portuguese barroco, an irregularly shaped pearl, and was long used pejoratively by critics who saw the style as bizarre and over-ornamented before being adopted neutrally by art historians such as Heinrich Wölfflin.

Painting and sculpture

Baroque painting employed strong contrasts of light and shadow (chiaroscuro, and Caravaggio's dramatic tenebrism), dynamic diagonal compositions, and heightened realism. Leading painters included Caravaggio, Annibale Carracci, Peter Paul Rubens, Rembrandt van Rijn, Diego Velázquez, and Artemisia Gentileschi. In sculpture, Bernini's works — such as the Ecstasy of Saint Teresa and the baldacchino of St Peter's Basilica — united architecture, sculpture, and light into theatrical wholes.

Architecture and music

Baroque architecture used oval plans, undulating façades, colossal orders, and illusionistic ceiling painting, seen in works by Bernini, Borromini, and later in the Palace of Versailles and the churches of Central Europe and colonial Latin America. Baroque music (c. 1600–1750) introduced opera, the concerto, and basso continuo, and developed tonal harmony; its major composers include Monteverdi, Henry Purcell, Antonio Vivaldi, George Frideric Handel, and Johann Sebastian Bach.
