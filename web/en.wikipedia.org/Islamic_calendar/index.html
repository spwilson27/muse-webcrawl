Islamic calendar

The Islamic calendar, also called the Hijri or Muslim calendar, is a lunar calendar consisting of 12 months in a year of 354 or 355 days. Its epoch is the Hijra — the migration of the Prophet Muhammad from Mecca to Medina in 622 CE — so years are designated AH (Anno Hegirae).

Months

The twelve months are Muharram, Safar, Rabi' al-Awwal, Rabi' al-Thani, Jumada al-Awwal, Jumada al-Thani, Rajab, Sha'ban, Ramadan, Shawwal, Dhu al-Qa'dah and Dhu al-Hijjah. Ramadan is the month of fasting; Dhu al-Hijjah is the month of the Hajj pilgrimage. Four months — Muharram, Rajab, Dhu al-Qa'dah and Dhu al-Hijjah — are traditionally considered sacred.

Characteristics

Because the lunar year is about 11 days shorter than the solar year, Islamic dates drift through the seasons over a cycle of about 33 years. Traditionally the start of each month depends on sighting the new crescent moon, though many countries now use calculated calendars such as Saudi Arabia's Umm al-Qura calendar. The calendar is used to determine religious observances; most Muslim countries use the Gregorian calendar for civil purposes, with some, such as Saudi Arabia and Iran (which uses a solar Hijri calendar), retaining Hijri dating officially.
