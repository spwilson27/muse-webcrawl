Polish School of Mathematics

Periodc. 1920 – 1939
CentresLwów, Warsaw, Kraków
Key figuresStefan Banach, Wacław Sierpiński, Hugo Steinhaus, Stanisław Ulam

The Polish School of Mathematics is the informal name for the exceptionally productive community of mathematicians who flourished in Poland between the two World Wars, particularly in Lwów and Warsaw. It was renowned for work in functional analysis, set theory, topology, logic, and probability.

The Lwów school

In Lwów, Stefan Banach and Hugo Steinhaus founded the journal Studia Mathematica (1929), devoted to functional analysis. Banach's 1932 monograph established the theory of Banach spaces. Mathematicians famously worked in the Scottish Café, recording problems and prizes in the "Scottish Book"; Stanisław Ulam, Stanisław Mazur, and Mark Kac were among the younger members.

The Warsaw school

Warsaw, centred on the journal Fundamenta Mathematicae (founded 1920 by Zygmunt Janiszewski, Stefan Mazurkiewicz, and Wacław Sierpiński), specialized in set theory, topology, and mathematical logic; Kazimierz Kuratowski, Alfred Tarski, and Karol Borsuk were leading figures. Kraków hosted a smaller group strong in differential equations.

Destruction and legacy

The Second World War destroyed the school: many members were murdered in the Holocaust or in the 1941 massacre of Lwów professors, and others emigrated, notably Ulam and Tarski to the United States. Despite this, the school's concepts — Banach spaces, the Banach–Tarski paradox, Polish notation in logic — remain foundational.
