Boeotia

Boeotia (Greek: Βοιωτία) is a region of central Greece, northwest of Athens, and today one of the regional units of Greece, with its capital at Livadeia. In antiquity it was a fertile district dominated by the city of Thebes, and its inhabitants, the Boeotians, were stereotyped by other Greeks — unfairly, scholars now think — as dull and slow-witted.

Geography

Boeotia is bounded by the Gulf of Corinth to the west, the ranges of Mount Parnassus to the northwest, and the Euboean Gulf to the east. Its broad, well-watered plains, including the basin of the former Lake Copais (drained in the 19th century), made it some of the best agricultural land in Greece. Chief ancient cities included Thebes, Orchomenus, Plataea, Thespiae and Tanagra.

History

Boeotia was settled early and in the Bronze Age was home to the Mycenaean palace at Thebes and the great fortress of Gla. In the classical period the Boeotian League, a federation of cities led by Thebes, was a major military power. Boeotian troops fought at Plataea in 479 BC against Persia. Under the generals Epaminondas and Pelopidas, Thebes briefly became the leading power in Greece, defeating Sparta at Leuctra in 371 BC and Mantinea in 362 BC.

The region was also celebrated in Greek myth and poetry: Mount Helicon was sacred to the Muses, and the poets Hesiod (from Ascra) and Pindar (from Thebes) were Boeotians. The oracle of Trophonius at Lebadea was famous throughout the Greek world.

Modern Boeotia

Today's regional unit covers much the same territory. Livadeia, Thebes (Thiva) and the port of Antikyra are its main towns. The economy is based on agriculture, including cotton and tomatoes from the Copais plain, and on industry; the region is crossed by the main Athens–Thessaloniki motorway and railway.
