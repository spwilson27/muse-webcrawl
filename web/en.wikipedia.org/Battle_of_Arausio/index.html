The Battle of Arausio took place on 6 October 105 BC between the migrating Germanic tribes of the Cimbri and Teutones and two Roman armies near the town of Arausio (modern Orange) in southern Gaul, in what is now the Vaucluse area of Provence. It was one of the worst defeats in Roman history — comparable to Cannae — and it shocked Rome into far-reaching military reforms.

Background

The Cimbri and Teutones, migrating from Jutland and northern Germany, had already defeated Roman armies at Noreia (113 BC) and in Gaul. In 105 BC two Roman armies confronted them: that of the consul Gnaeus Mallius Maximus, a "new man" without noble ancestry, and that of the proconsul Quintus Servilius Caepio, a proud aristocrat who refused to cooperate with or subordinate himself to Mallius. The divided command left the two armies encamped separately on opposite banks of the Rhône.

The battle

Caepio, provoked into attacking first, led his army against the Cimbri and was annihilated; the victorious tribes then fell upon Mallius's army across the river and destroyed it as well. Ancient sources give enormous casualty figures — up to 80,000 Roman soldiers and as many camp followers killed — and although the numbers are debated, the scale of the disaster (sometimes called the clades Arausionensis) is undisputed. Caepio was prosecuted, convicted and exiled, and was blamed in popular tradition for having plundered the sacred gold of Tolosa (Toulouse).

Aftermath

The catastrophe, combined with the strain of the Jugurthine War, led to the unprecedented repeated election of Gaius Marius as consul. Marius rebuilt the Roman army as a professional force ("Marius's mules"), and in 102–101 BC destroyed the Teutones at Aquae Sextiae and the Cimbri at Vercellae, ending the Germanic threat.
