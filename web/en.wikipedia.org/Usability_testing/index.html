Usability testing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: A usability lab session with a participant and observer — placeholder]

Usability testing is a technique used in user-centred interaction design to evaluate a product by testing it on users. This can be seen as an irreplaceable usability practice, since it gives direct input on how real users use the system. It is more concerned with the design intuitiveness of the product than with its functional correctness, and it is less formal than software testing methods such as unit or integration testing.

Methods

Common approaches include moderated testing, where a facilitator guides a participant through tasks and probes their thinking; unmoderated or remote testing, where participants complete tasks independently; and guerrilla testing, conducted informally in public places. Testers typically prepare a test plan with tasks, observe participants, record completion rates and errors, and debrief findings into prioritized recommendations.

Use in industry

Usability testing is standard in software, web design, and product development. Jakob Nielsen's research showed that testing with as few as five users uncovers most usability problems — though later critiques caution that this number depends on problem heterogeneity. Eye tracking, heat maps, and analytics are often combined with observed testing sessions. Testing may be formative (during design) or summative (measuring against benchmarks).

History

Modern usability testing has roots in human factors engineering and ergonomics of the mid-20th century, and was formalized for computing in the 1980s through work at IBM, Digital Equipment Corporation, and by pioneers such as Jakob Nielsen and Carol Barnum.
