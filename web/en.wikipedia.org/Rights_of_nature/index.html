Rights of nature

Rights of nature is a legal and jurisprudential theory that describes inherent rights as associated with ecosystems and species, similar to the concept of fundamental human rights. The rights of nature concept challenges twentieth-century laws as generally grounded in a flawed frame of nature as "property" that can be owned, or as a resource to be exploited.

History

The modern movement traces in part to Christopher D. Stone's 1972 article "Should Trees Have Standing?", which argued that natural objects should have legal standing. In 2008, Ecuador became the first country to recognize rights of nature in its constitution, granting nature (Pacha Mama) the right to exist, persist, and regenerate its vital cycles.

Examples

In 2017, New Zealand granted legal personhood to the Whanganui River under the Te Awa Tupua Act. India's Uttarakhand High Court declared the Ganges and Yamuna rivers legal persons in 2017, though the ruling was later stayed. Local ordinances in the United States, such as Toledo's Lake Erie Bill of Rights (2019), have attempted similar recognitions, with mixed success in the courts.

Criticism

Critics argue that rights of nature can be vague in application, raise questions about who may speak for nature, and can conflict with property rights and economic development. Supporters respond that the framework remedies the failure of conventional environmental regulation.
