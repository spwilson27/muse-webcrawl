The Heinkel He 178 was an experimental German aircraft designed and produced by Heinkel. It was the world's first aircraft to fly under the power of a turbojet engine, making its maiden flight on 27 August 1939, piloted by Erich Warsitz. The aircraft had been developed as a private venture to test the jet propulsion concepts of Hans von Ohain.

Development

In 1936, the young engineer Hans von Ohain had patented a design for an exhaust propulsion system and joined Ernst Heinkel's company, which funded the development of a working turbojet. After bench-testing the hydrogen-fuelled HeS 1 in 1937, the team produced the flight engine HeS 3, burning gasoline, with a centrifugal compressor and a thrust of about 4.4 kN. Heinkel built a small, simple airframe around it: a shoulder-winged monoplane of metal construction with a retractable tailwheel undercarriage and the air intake in the nose, the engine exhausting beneath the tail.

First flight and testing

After taxi trials and a brief hop on 24 August 1939, Warsitz took the He 178 V1 fully airborne at Rostock-Marienehe on 27 August 1939 — just days before the outbreak of the Second World War. The aircraft reached speeds of around 598 km/h in testing, but endurance was limited to roughly ten minutes by its small fuel capacity. A second prototype, the V2, with a larger wing, was completed but never flew owing to the press of other work.

Official indifference and legacy

Heinkel demonstrated the aircraft to officials of the Reich Air Ministry, including Ernst Udet and Erhard Milch, on 1 November 1939, but the ministry showed little enthusiasm, preferring to concentrate on proven piston-engined designs. Development funding went instead to the more advanced axial-flow engines that powered the Messerschmitt Me 262. The surviving He 178 airframe was displayed in Berlin and was destroyed in an air raid in 1943. Despite the official indifference, the flight of the He 178 opened the jet age in aviation.
