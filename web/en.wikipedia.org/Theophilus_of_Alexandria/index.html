Theophilus (Greek: Θεόφιλος) was the 23rd Patriarch of Alexandria, reigning from 385 until his death in 412. One of the most powerful and controversial churchmen of his age, he consolidated the power of the Alexandrian see, oversaw the destruction of the Serapeum, and — after first defending them — turned against the Origenist monks of the Egyptian desert, a reversal that led to his campaign against John Chrysostom, Patriarch of Constantinople. He is venerated as a saint in the Oriental Orthodox tradition, particularly the Coptic Orthodox Church.

Patriarchate

Theophilus succeeded Timothy I in 385. In 391, after violent clashes between Christians and pagans, the emperor Theodosius I permitted Theophilus to convert a pagan temple into a church; the ensuing confrontation ended with the destruction of the great Serapeum, the temple of Serapis and a landmark of Alexandria, an event celebrated by Christian writers and lamented by pagans as a symbol of the old order's fall. He built extensively, enforced Nicene orthodoxy against Arians and pagans, and extended Alexandrian influence over the Egyptian church.

The Origenist controversy and Chrysostom

Having initially protected the Origenist "Tall Brothers" of the Nitrian desert, Theophilus turned against them around 399, condemning Origen's teachings and expelling some 300 monks, who fled to Constantinople. There John Chrysostom's sheltering of the monks drew Theophilus's hostility; at the Synod of the Oak in 403, presided over by Theophilus, Chrysostom was deposed and exiled. The episode remains the darkest stain on Theophilus's reputation in much later historiography, though Coptic tradition remembers him as a defender of the faith against Origenist error.

Legacy

Theophilus was succeeded by his nephew Cyril, whose patriarchate continued Alexandria's assertiveness. Works by Theophilus include festal letters, homilies, and a lost treatise against Origen; his Paschal table for computing Easter influenced later computus.
