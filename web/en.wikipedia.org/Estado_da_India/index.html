Estado da Índia

At its height the Portuguese State of India spanned a chain of ports and fortresses from East Africa to Japan.

The Estado da Índia ("State of India"), also known as the Portuguese State of India (Estado Português da Índia), was the complex of Portuguese colonial possessions and trading posts in the Indian Ocean and Asia, founded six years after the discovery of the sea route to India by Vasco da Gama in 1498. Formally established in 1505 with Francisco de Almeida as its first viceroy, its capital was at Cochin before moving to Goa in 1530.

Scope

The Estado was less a territorial empire than a network of fortified ports, factories and sea lanes, governed from Goa by a viceroy or governor answerable to Lisbon. At its 16th-century height it included Mozambique, Mombasa, Hormuz, Goa, Malabar and Coromandel ports, Sri Lanka, Malacca, the Moluccas, Macau and a trading post at Nagasaki. The Portuguese crown sought to tax and control the spice trade through the cartaz pass system and fortified chokepoints.

Decline and end

From the late 16th century the Estado lost ground to Dutch, English and regional powers; Malacca fell to the Dutch in 1641, Ceylon and the Malabar ports followed, and by the 18th century the Asian possessions were reduced chiefly to Goa, Daman, Diu, Macau and Portuguese Timor. In India the remaining enclaves of Goa, Daman and Diu were annexed by the Republic of India in December 1961 (Operation Vijay), which Portugal recognised only after the fall of the Estado Novo in 1974. Macau remained under Portuguese administration until the handover to China in 1999, closing the four-and-a-half-century history of the first European seaborne empire in Asia.
