Protestant Ascendancy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Protestant Ascendancy (also simply known as the Ascendancy) was the sociopolitical and economic domination of Ireland between the 17th and early 20th centuries by a small Anglican ruling class, composed of landowners, clergy, and military and political officials — members of the established Church of Ireland. While mainly in opposition to the majority Catholic population, which they governed in subjugation, members of the Ascendancy also discriminated against Ireland's largely Presbyterian Ulster Scots population, notably through the Penal Laws.

The phrase is first recorded around 1787–1792 and is sometimes attributed to the era of the Irish Parliament at College Green in Dublin, which was restricted to Anglican members. The Ascendancy's power rested on land ownership consolidated after the Cromwellian conquest of Ireland and the Williamite War, on the Penal Laws that barred Catholics from Parliament, the professions, and land purchase, and on the political union of Ireland with Great Britain after 1801.

Origins

The confiscations following the Irish Confederate Wars and the victory of William III at the Battle of the Boyne (1690) and Aughrim (1691) transferred the great majority of Irish land into Protestant hands. By the mid-18th century, Catholics — about three-quarters of the population — owned only about 5 percent of the land. The Penal Laws, enacted from 1695 onward, sought to entrench this order by restricting Catholic education, worship, inheritance, and political participation.

Height and character

During the 18th century the Ascendancy built the Georgian architecture that still characterizes Dublin and country houses across Ireland. Its culture was Anglo-Irish: figures such as Jonathan Swift, Edmund Burke, and later W. B. Yeats came from this class, and many resented London's control even while depending on it. The Irish Parliament's legislative independence in 1782 ("Grattan's Parliament") marked a high point of Ascendancy self-confidence, cut short by the rebellion of 1798 and the Acts of Union 1800.

Decline

The Ascendancy declined through the 19th century: Catholic emancipation in 1829, the disestablishment of the Church of Ireland in 1869 (effective 1871), and the Land Acts — culminating in the Wyndham Land Act of 1903 — transferred land ownership to tenant farmers. Political power passed to Catholic nationalists, and the Irish War of Independence and the creation of the Irish Free State in 1922 ended the Ascendancy as a governing class; many of its great houses were burned during the revolutionary period or fell into decay.
