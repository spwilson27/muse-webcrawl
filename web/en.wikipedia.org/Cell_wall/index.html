Cell wall

A cell wall is a structural layer surrounding some types of cells, just outside the cell membrane. It can be tough, flexible, and sometimes rigid. It provides the cell with both structural support and protection, and also acts as a filtering mechanism. Cell walls are absent in animals but present in most other eukaryotes including algae, fungi, and plants, as well as in most prokaryotes.

Composition

Plant primary cell walls consist of cellulose microfibrils embedded in a matrix of hemicelluloses and pectin; many plant cells later deposit a lignified secondary wall, as in wood. Fungal walls are built mainly from chitin and glucans. Bacterial walls are made of peptidoglycan — thick in Gram-positive bacteria, thin and surrounded by an outer membrane in Gram-negative bacteria — a distinction central to the Gram stain and to the action of antibiotics such as penicillin, which inhibit wall synthesis.

Functions

The wall gives the cell its shape, prevents rupture from turgor pressure as water enters by osmosis, and mediates interactions with the environment and other cells. Plasmodesmata in plants pierce the walls to connect neighboring cells. In pathogens, wall components are major targets of the immune system and of antifungal and antibacterial drugs.

Evolution

Cell walls evolved independently in several lineages with different chemistries, suggesting convergent solutions to the mechanical problems of walled cells, including the loss of phagocytosis in those lineages.
