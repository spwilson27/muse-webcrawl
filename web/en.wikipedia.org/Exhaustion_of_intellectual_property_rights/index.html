Exhaustion of intellectual property rights

The exhaustion of intellectual property rights is the legal doctrine that, once an intellectual property holder has sold a particular item embodying the protected right, the holder's control over that item is "exhausted" — the buyer may resell, lend, or otherwise dispose of it without the right holder's permission. In copyright law in the United States this is closely related to the first-sale doctrine; in patent law it is called patent exhaustion.

Scope of exhaustion

Jurisdictions differ on the territorial reach of exhaustion:

  • National exhaustion — rights are exhausted only by sales within the country, so right holders can block parallel imports of genuine goods sold abroad.
  • Regional exhaustion — as in the European Economic Area, a sale anywhere in the region exhausts rights throughout it.
  • International exhaustion — a first authorized sale anywhere in the world exhausts the right.

Key cases

The US Supreme Court addressed copyright exhaustion for foreign-made copies in Kirtsaeng v. John Wiley & Sons (2013), holding that the first-sale doctrine applies to copies lawfully made abroad. In patent law, Impression Products, Inc. v. Lexmark International, Inc. (2017) held that a patentee's authorized sale exhausts all patent rights in the item, whether the sale occurs in the United States or abroad, and that post-sale restrictions cannot be enforced through patent law. The TRIPS Agreement deliberately leaves exhaustion to member states (Article 6), making it one of the flexibilities used in disputes over access to medicines and compulsory licensing.
