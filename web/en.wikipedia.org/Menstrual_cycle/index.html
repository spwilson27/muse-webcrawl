Menstrual cycle

The menstrual cycle is a series of natural changes in hormone production and the structures of the uterus and ovaries of the female reproductive system that makes pregnancy possible. The ovarian cycle controls the production and release of eggs and the cyclic release of estrogen and progesterone; the uterine cycle governs the preparation and maintenance of the lining of the uterus (endometrium) to receive an embryo. Cycles typically last 21–35 days in adults, with a median around 28 days, and occur from menarche at puberty until menopause.

Phases

  • Menstrual phase (days 1–5 approximately) — shedding of the endometrial lining; day 1 is the first day of bleeding.
  • Follicular / proliferative phase — rising follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) matures an ovarian follicle; estrogen rebuilds the endometrium.
  • Ovulation (around day 14) — a surge of luteinising hormone (LH) triggers release of the egg.
  • Luteal / secretory phase — the corpus luteum secretes progesterone, preparing the endometrium for implantation; if no pregnancy occurs, hormone levels fall and menstruation begins again.

Regulation

The cycle is controlled by the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis: gonadotropin-releasing hormone from the hypothalamus drives pituitary release of FSH and LH, whose feedback interactions with estrogen and progesterone orchestrate the phases. Hormonal contraceptives work by suppressing this axis, chiefly preventing ovulation.

Disorders

Common disorders include dysmenorrhea (painful periods), amenorrhea (absent periods), heavy menstrual bleeding, premenstrual syndrome and premenstrual dysphoric disorder. Irregular cycles can signal conditions such as polycystic ovary syndrome or thyroid disease.
