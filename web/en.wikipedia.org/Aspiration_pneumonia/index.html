Aspiration pneumonia

Aspiration pneumonia is a type of lung infection caused by the inhalation (aspiration) of food, liquid, saliva, vomit or other material from the mouth or stomach into the airways and lungs. It is distinct from aspiration pneumonitis (Mendelson's syndrome), a chemical injury caused by inhaling sterile but acidic gastric contents. Aspiration pneumonia occurs especially in people with impaired swallowing or consciousness and is a leading cause of death in elderly patients and those with neurological disease.

Risk factors

Anything that impairs the gag and cough reflexes or swallowing increases risk: stroke and other causes of dysphagia, dementia, Parkinson's disease, reduced consciousness from sedation, anaesthesia or alcohol, gastro-oesophageal reflux, and poor oral hygiene, which raises the bacterial load of aspirated secretions. The right lower lobe is most often affected because the right main bronchus is wider and more vertical.

Presentation and diagnosis

Symptoms include fever, cough, shortness of breath, pleuritic chest pain and sometimes foul-smelling sputum when anaerobic bacteria are involved; in frail elderly patients the presentation may be subtle, with confusion or functional decline. Diagnosis rests on the clinical history of aspiration risk, chest imaging showing infiltrates in dependent lung segments, and exclusion of other causes.

Treatment and prevention

Treatment is with antibiotics covering oral anaerobes and typical respiratory pathogens, plus supportive care and oxygen. Prevention includes swallowing assessment and modified diets for patients with dysphagia, good oral hygiene, head-of-bed elevation, and careful sedation. Routine prophylactic antibiotics after a witnessed aspiration are generally not recommended when the injury is purely chemical pneumonitis.
