Tuba
ClassificationBrass instrument; aerophone
Patented1835, Prussia (Wilhelm Wieprecht and Johann Moritz)

RelatedEuphonium, sousaphone, helicon

The tuba (Latin for "trumpet") is the largest and lowest-pitched musical instrument in the brass family. As with all brass instruments, the sound is produced by lip vibration—a buzz—into a mouthpiece. It first appeared in the mid-nineteenth century, making it one of the newer instruments in the modern orchestra and concert band.

History

The modern tuba was patented in Prussia in 1835 by Wilhelm Friedrich Wieprecht and Johann Gottfried Moritz, who fitted valves to a bass instrument of the ophicleide type. Earlier low brass parts had been played by the serpent and the ophicleide. The tuba quickly found a place in military bands and, later in the nineteenth century, in orchestras, where composers such as Wagner, Bruckner, Mahler and Prokofiev wrote significant parts for it.

Types

Tubas are built in several sizes and pitches, most commonly pitched in F, E♭, C and B♭. They typically have three to six valves. Related instruments include the euphonium (smaller and conical throughout), the sousaphone, designed to be worn while marching with the bell facing forward, and the Wagner tuba, a hybrid instrument used in Wagner's Ring cycle.

Role

A single tuba is standard in the modern symphony orchestra, where it anchors the brass section. In concert and brass bands, several tubas form the foundation of the ensemble. The instrument also has a small but notable solo repertoire and is used in jazz, where it served as the original bass instrument of early New Orleans bands.
