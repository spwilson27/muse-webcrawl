Green Man

The Green Man, also known as a foliate head, is a motif in architecture, sculpture and art consisting of a face made of, or completely surrounded by, leaves. The motif is found in many cultures around the world and is especially common in the carved stonework and woodwork of medieval churches and cathedrals across Britain and continental Europe, where examples date chiefly from the 11th to the 16th centuries.

A foliate head carved on a medieval church

Interpretations

The term "Green Man" was coined by Lady Raglan in a 1939 article in the Folklore journal. Interpretations of the motif vary: it has been read as a symbol of rebirth and the cycle of growth in spring, as a remnant of pre-Christian nature worship and deities such as Cernunnos, and as a figure connected with Robin Hood, Jack in the Green, and the wild men of medieval folklore. Scholars caution that the carvings may often have been decorative or apotropaic rather than doctrinal. In the modern era the Green Man has been adopted as a symbol by environmental and neo-pagan movements, and it is a common name and sign for public houses in England.
