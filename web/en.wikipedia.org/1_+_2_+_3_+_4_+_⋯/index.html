Zeta-regularised value−1/12

The infinite series whose terms are the natural numbers, 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + ⋯, is a divergent series: the sequence of its partial sums (1, 3, 6, 10, 15, … — the triangular numbers) grows without bound, so the series has no sum in the ordinary sense. Nevertheless, the value −1/12 is widely associated with it through summation methods such as Ramanujan summation and analytic continuation of the Riemann zeta function, which assigns ζ(−1) = −1/12.

ζ(−1) = 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + ⋯ = −1/12  (in the sense of analytic continuation)

Divergence

The series clearly fails the term test: its terms do not tend to zero, so it cannot converge. Its partial sums are exactly the triangular numbers, Tn = n(n + 1)/2, which approach infinity as n grows.

Heuristic assignments of −1/12

Manipulations popularised by Srinivasa Ramanujan, and later by physics and mathematics popularisers, combine this series with the (also divergent) alternating series 1 − 2 + 3 − 4 + ⋯ (whose Abel sum is 1/4) to "derive" −1/12. Such manipulations are not valid for ordinary summation but reflect consistent values given by analytic continuation: the Riemann zeta function ζ(s) = Σ n−s, defined for Re(s) > 1, extends meromorphically to the whole complex plane, and ζ(−1) = −1/12 exactly.

Applications in physics

The value −1/12 appears in real physics through regularised calculations: the Casimir effect between conducting plates depends on the sum of zero-point mode energies, whose regularisation involves ζ(−1); similar zeta-function regularisation is used in string theory, where the sum 1 + 2 + 3 + ⋯ enters the computation that fixes the critical dimension of the bosonic string at 26.
