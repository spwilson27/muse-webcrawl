Pastry

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Assorted pastries in a bakery display

Pastry is a dough of flour, water and shortening (solid fats such as butter, lard or vegetable shortening) that may be savoury or sweetened. Baked products made from pastry include pies, tarts, quiches, croissants, pasties and innumerable regional specialities. Pastry differs from bread in its higher fat content, which contributes to a flaky or crumbly texture, and from cake batters in being worked as a dough that can be rolled and shaped.

Types

The principal types of pastry include:

  • Shortcrust pastry — the basic crumbly pastry for pies and tarts, made by rubbing fat into flour with minimal water.
  • Puff pastry — a laminated dough in which butter is folded and rolled into many alternating layers that puff apart with steam in a hot oven.
  • Choux pastry — a cooked paste of flour, water, butter and eggs, piped and baked into éclairs, profiteroles and gougères.
  • Phyllo (filo) pastry — paper-thin sheets of unleavened dough, brushed with fat and layered, used in baklava and strudels.
  • Flaky pastry and hot water crust pastry — the former a quicker alternative to puff; the latter, made with hot water and lard, forms the sturdy case of raised pork pies.

History

Grain pastes enclosing fillings are ancient — the Greeks and Romans baked meat and honey in pastry cases — and medieval European cooks built elaborate raised pies, partly as containers for preserving and presenting food. The refined laminated and choux pastries of French pâtisserie were codified by cooks such as Antonin Carême in the early nineteenth century, and the professional pastry chef (pâtissier) became a distinct figure in French kitchens and hotels.

Technique

Good pastry depends on controlling gluten development and temperature: cold fat and minimal handling keep shortcrust tender, while laminated doughs require careful rolling and resting so that the layers remain distinct. Fillings range from fruit and custards to meat and spiced vegetables, and pastry cases are used across the world's cuisines, from Cornish pasties to empanadas and samosas.
