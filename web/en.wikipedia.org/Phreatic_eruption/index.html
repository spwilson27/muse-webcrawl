Phreatic eruption

Phreatic eruption
TypeVolcanic eruption (steam-driven)
CauseMagmatic heat flashing groundwater or surface water to steam
EjectaOld rock fragments, ash, steam — no fresh magma
ExamplesMount St. Helens 1980 precursor blasts; Mount Ontake 2014

A phreatic eruption, also called a phreatic explosion, ultravulcanian eruption, or steam-blast eruption, is a volcanic explosion driven by steam produced when water — groundwater, hydrothermal fluid, or surface water — is heated by magma, lava, or hot volcanic rock. The intense heat causes the water to flash almost instantly to steam, expanding violently and shattering the surrounding rock.

Characteristics

Because the erupted material consists of pulverised pre-existing rock rather than new magma, phreatic eruptions produce ash, lapilli, and ballistic blocks of old material, often accompanied by base surges and muddy crater lakes being ejected. They typically feature little or no incandescent material and no lava. Temperatures of the fragments are usually cool to only mildly warm, distinguishing them from magmatic eruptions. When an eruption involves both steam and fresh magma it is termed phreatomagmatic.

Hazards

Phreatic eruptions are especially dangerous because they can occur with little or no detectable warning: no magma needs to rise, so the usual precursory signals (earthquake swarms, deformation) may be absent. The 2014 eruption of Mount Ontake in Japan, a phreatic event, killed 63 hikers near the summit. Phreatic blasts also preceded and accompanied the paroxysmal 1980 eruption of Mount St. Helens, and explosions at Kīlauea in 1924 hurled multi-ton blocks over a kilometre.

Occurrence

Phreatic activity is common at volcanoes with hydrothermal systems, crater lakes, or glaciers — including Ruapehu (New Zealand), Poás (Costa Rica), Taal (Philippines), and many Japanese and Indonesian volcanoes. Geysers are a benign, small-scale expression of the same steam-heated groundwater processes.
