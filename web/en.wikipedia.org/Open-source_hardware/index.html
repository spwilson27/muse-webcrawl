Open-source hardware
Movement: Open design / maker movement
Key body: Open Source Hardware Association (OSHWA)
Examples: Arduino, RepRap, RISC-V

Open-source hardware (OSH) consists of physical artefacts whose design is made publicly available so that anyone can study, modify, distribute, make and sell the hardware, in the spirit of open-source software. Designs are shared under licences — such as the CERN Open Hardware Licence or TAPR Open Hardware License — granting the four freedoms to use, study, modify and distribute.

History

The movement grew from DIY electronics and the free-software tradition in the late 1990s and 2000s. The RepRap project (founded 2005 by Adrian Bowyer) created a self-replicating 3D printer released under the GNU GPL, seeding the desktop 3D-printing industry. Arduino, launched in 2005 in Ivrea, Italy, made microcontroller development cheap and accessible, with its board designs published under Creative Commons licences. The Open Source Hardware Association (OSHWA), founded in 2012, maintains the community definition and a certification programme.

Scope and challenges

Open hardware spans electronics, mechanical designs, textiles, scientific instruments and open silicon — notably the RISC-V instruction set architecture, which underpins freely usable processor designs. Unlike software, hardware carries irreducible manufacturing costs, and patent law rather than copyright governs much of its protection, making "openness" harder to enforce. Businesses in the field typically profit through sales of manufactured units, kits, support and services rather than licensing.
