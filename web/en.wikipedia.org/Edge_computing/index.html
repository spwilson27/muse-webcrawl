Edge computing

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Edge computing is a distributed computing paradigm that brings computation and data storage closer to the sources of data — the "edge" of the network — rather than relying entirely on centralised cloud data centres. Its goals are to reduce latency, save bandwidth, improve reliability and keep sensitive data local.

Origins

The concept grew out of content delivery networks of the late 1990s, which cached web content near users. With the growth of the Internet of Things, mobile computing and 5G networks in the 2010s, edge computing expanded from content caching to running applications and analytics at or near devices such as sensors, gateways, base stations and on-premises servers.

Use cases

Typical applications include autonomous vehicles and industrial automation, where millisecond response times matter; smart cameras performing local video analytics; augmented reality; remote monitoring of oil rigs, mines and farms with poor connectivity; and content and gaming delivery. Multi-access edge computing (MEC) is a standardised form integrated with mobile networks.

Advantages and challenges

Processing data locally reduces backhaul traffic and response time and can improve privacy and resilience to network outages. Challenges include managing and securing large fleets of heterogeneous devices, limited compute and power at the edge, and coordinating workloads between edge and cloud. Edge computing is generally seen as complementary to, rather than a replacement for, cloud computing.
