Palliative care


Palliative care focuses on relief of suffering and quality of life.

Palliative care (from Latin palliare, "to cloak") is an interdisciplinary medical caregiving approach aimed at optimising quality of life and mitigating suffering among people with serious, complex, and often terminal illnesses. The World Health Organization defines it as an approach that improves the quality of life of patients and their families facing life-threatening illness, through the prevention and relief of suffering by means of early identification and impeccable assessment and treatment of pain and other problems — physical, psychosocial, and spiritual.

Scope and principles

Unlike hospice care, palliative care is not limited to the end of life and may be provided alongside curative treatment at any stage of a serious illness such as cancer, heart failure, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, kidney failure, or dementia. Its core practices include expert pain and symptom management (including dyspnoea, nausea, delirium, and fatigue), clear communication about prognosis and goals of care, advance care planning, support for families and carers, and coordination across care settings.

History

The modern movement grew out of the hospice movement: Cicely Saunders founded St Christopher's Hospice in London in 1967, and Balfour Mount coined the term "palliative care" in Montreal in the 1970s, in part because "hospice" had other connotations in French. The field became a recognised medical subspecialty in many countries — in the United States, hospice and palliative medicine was approved as a subspecialty in 2006. The WHO and the 2014 World Health Assembly resolution WHA67.19 urged member states to integrate palliative care into health systems.

Evidence and access

Randomised trials, notably a 2010 study of early palliative care in metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer published in the New England Journal of Medicine, found improved quality of life, less depression, and in that trial longer survival, among patients receiving early specialist palliative care. Despite this evidence, access remains highly unequal: the WHO estimates that each year about 40 million people need palliative care, and that only around 14% of them receive it, with access to opioid analgesics particularly limited in low- and middle-income countries.
