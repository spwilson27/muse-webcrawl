The Battle of Poltava (8 July 1709) was the decisive and largest battle of the Great Northern War. A Russian army under the command of Tsar Peter I defeated a Swedish army commanded, in place of the wounded Charles XII of Sweden, by Field Marshal Carl Gustaf Rehnskiöld. The battle ended Sweden's status as a great power and marked the beginning of Russian ascendancy in eastern Europe.

Background

Charles XII had invaded Russia in 1708 after defeating Denmark and Poland. His army, weakened by the exceptionally harsh winter of 1708–09 and by supply difficulties, besieged the fortress of Poltava on the Vorskla River in the spring of 1709. Peter the Great marched to relieve the city with a much larger force, entrenched on the opposite bank of the river.

The battle

Before dawn on 8 July, the Swedes advanced against the Russian redoubts. Part of the Swedish force became separated under General Lewenhaupt and was delayed, while the main assault broke on the Russian lines. Russian infantry and artillery, deployed in strength, drove the Swedes back with heavy losses. Charles, wounded in the foot days before, was carried on a litter and could not effectively direct the battle.

Aftermath

The Swedish army collapsed. Several thousand survivors under Lewenhaupt surrendered days later at Perevolochna, while Charles escaped south into Ottoman exile at Bender. Sweden never recovered its great-power position; Russia emerged as the dominant power of the Baltic region.
