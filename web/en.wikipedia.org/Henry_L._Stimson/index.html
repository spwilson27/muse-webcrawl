Henry Lewis Stimson (21 September 1867 – 20 October 1950) was an American statesman, lawyer, and Republican politician who served as Secretary of War under Presidents William Howard Taft and Franklin D. Roosevelt, Secretary of State under Herbert Hoover, and Governor-General of the Philippines. He is best remembered for directing the American war effort during the Second World War and for his role in the decision to use atomic weapons against Japan.

Early life and legal career

Stimson was born in New York City, educated at Phillips Academy and Yale, and read law at Harvard. He became a prominent Wall Street lawyer and served as U.S. Attorney for the Southern District of New York under Theodore Roosevelt, prosecuting antitrust cases.

Cabinet service

As Taft's Secretary of War (1911–1913) Stimson reorganised the Army's staff system. He served as an artillery officer in the First World War and reached the rank of colonel. As Governor-General of the Philippines (1927–1929) he moderated earlier policies and encouraged Filipino participation in government. As Hoover's Secretary of State (1929–1933) he formulated the Stimson Doctrine of 1932, declaring that the United States would not recognise territorial changes brought about by force, in response to Japan's seizure of Manchuria. Paradoxically, he also closed the State Department's code-breaking bureau in 1929, remarking that "gentlemen do not read each other's mail."

Second World War

Recalled by Franklin D. Roosevelt in 1940 as Secretary of War, Stimson — a Republican serving in a Democratic administration — oversaw the vast expansion of the U.S. Army, the introduction of the first peacetime draft, and the Manhattan Project. He chaired the Interim Committee that considered how the atomic bomb should be used, and recommended its use against Japanese cities; he also personally removed Kyoto from the target list because of its cultural significance. He retired in September 1945, shortly after the war's end, and died in 1950.
