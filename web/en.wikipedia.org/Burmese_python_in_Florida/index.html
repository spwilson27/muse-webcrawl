Burmese python in Florida

The Burmese python (Python bivittatus) is established as an invasive species in Florida, where a breeding population in the Everglades and surrounding wetlands has caused severe declines in native mammals. Native to Southeast Asia, the snakes entered the wild through the exotic pet trade — from escapes and deliberate releases, and possibly a breeding facility destroyed by Hurricane Andrew in 1992.

Ecological impact

Studies in Everglades National Park have documented dramatic declines — in some surveys over 90 per cent — in observations of raccoons, opossums, bobcats, and rabbits since the pythons became numerous, and the snakes prey on wading birds and even American alligators. Pythons compete with native predators and have few natural enemies in Florida.

Control efforts

Florida prohibits ownership of Burmese pythons as pets and runs removal programmes paying contractors to capture snakes, as well as the public "Python Challenge" hunting competitions. Tens of thousands of pythons have been removed, yet eradication is considered unlikely because the cryptic snakes are extremely difficult to detect in the vast wetlands. Researchers are trialling detection dogs, radio-tracked "scout snakes", and other methods.
