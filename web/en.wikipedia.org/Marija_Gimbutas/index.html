Marija Gimbutas (Lithuanian: Marija Gimbutienė, née Birutė Alseikaitė; 23 January 1921 – 2 February 1994) was a Lithuanian-American archaeologist and anthropologist known for her research into the Neolithic and Bronze Age cultures of "Old Europe" and for her Kurgan hypothesis, which located the Proto-Indo-European homeland in the Pontic Steppe.

Life and career

Gimbutas studied linguistics, ethnology, and archaeology at Vilnius University and earned her doctorate at the University of Tübingen in 1946. Fleeing the Soviet reoccupation of Lithuania, she emigrated to the United States in 1949. She worked at Harvard University, translating Eastern European archaeological texts and lecturing, before becoming professor of European archaeology at the University of California, Los Angeles, in 1964, where she remained until retirement in 1989. She directed major excavations of Neolithic sites in south-eastern Europe, including Sitagroi and Achilleion.

Kurgan hypothesis

Gimbutas proposed that the Proto-Indo-Europeans were the bearers of the Kurgan (burial mound) culture of the Eurasian steppe, who expanded westward in several waves during the 5th to 3rd millennia BCE. Her formulation of the steppe hypothesis became the mainstream model in Indo-European studies, broadly supported by later ancient-DNA research.

"Old Europe" and the goddess thesis

In works such as The Goddesses and Gods of Old Europe (1974), The Language of the Goddess (1989), and The Civilization of the Goddess (1991), Gimbutas argued that Neolithic Old Europe was a peaceful, egalitarian, matristic civilisation centred on goddess worship, later overthrown by patriarchal, warlike steppe pastoralists. These interpretations became influential in the feminist spirituality and Goddess movements, though many archaeologists regard her readings of figurines and symbolism as speculative and her matriarchal thesis remains controversial among scholars.
