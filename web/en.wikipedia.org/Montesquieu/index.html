Charles Louis de Secondat, Baron de La Brède et de Montesquieu (18 January 1689 – 10 February 1755), generally referred to simply as Montesquieu, was a French judge, man of letters, historian, and political philosopher. He is the principal source of the theory of separation of powers, which is implemented in many constitutions throughout the world.

Life

Born at the Château de la Brède near Bordeaux into a wealthy family of the nobility of the robe, Montesquieu studied law and became a counsellor in the Bordeaux parlement, later its president. He resigned his offices to devote himself to study and writing, travelled widely in Europe, and spent two years in England, where he admired the constitutional system.

Works

His first major work, the Persian Letters (1721), was a satirical epistolary novel purporting to be the correspondence of two Persian travellers in Europe, mocking French society and institutions. His masterpiece, The Spirit of Law (De l'esprit des lois, 1748), published anonymously, argued that laws should be adapted to the "spirit" of a people — their climate, religion, commerce, and manners — and defended political liberty through the separation of legislative, executive, and judicial powers. The book was placed on the Catholic Church's Index of prohibited books but was enormously influential, notably on the framers of the United States Constitution.
