Warsaw Uprising
Date1 August – 2 October 1944 (63 days)
LocationWarsaw, German-occupied Poland

Casualtiesc. 16,000 resistance fighters and 150,000–200,000 civilians killed

The Warsaw Uprising (Polish: Powstanie Warszawskie) was a major Second World War operation by the Polish underground resistance — the Home Army (Armia Krajowa), loyal to the Polish government-in-exile in London — to liberate Warsaw from German occupation before the arrival of the Soviet Red Army. It began on 1 August 1944 and lasted 63 days, the largest single military effort by any European resistance movement of the war.

Course of the uprising

Launched at "W-hour" (5 p.m., 1 August) as Soviet forces approached the Vistula, the rising initially seized large parts of the city but failed to capture key strongpoints, bridges, and airfields. The Red Army halted on the far bank of the Vistula and, for reasons still debated, did not relieve the city, while Stalin initially refused landing rights to Western Allied supply aircraft. German reinforcements under Erich von dem Bach-Zelewski counter-attacked with notorious brutality: in the Wola massacre of early August, SS and police units murdered tens of thousands of civilians.

Supplied only by costly Allied airdrops and, later, limited Soviet drops and a costly Polish landing across the Vistula, the Home Army gradually lost ground — the Old Town fell in early September, its defenders escaping through the sewers. General Tadeusz Bór-Komorowski capitulated on 2 October 1944, securing combatant status for his soldiers; the surviving civilian population was expelled and the city systematically dynamited, until some 85 percent of Warsaw lay in ruins.

Legacy

Suppressed in communist Poland's official memory except as a cautionary tale, the Uprising is now commemorated as a symbol of Polish courage and sacrifice. Every year at 5 p.m. on 1 August, sirens sound and traffic stops across Warsaw. The Warsaw Uprising Museum, opened in 2004, is among the city's most visited institutions.
