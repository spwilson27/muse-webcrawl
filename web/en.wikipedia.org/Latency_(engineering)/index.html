Latency (engineering)

Latency, from a general point of view, is a time delay between the cause and effect of some physical change in the system being observed. In engineering, latency is the delay between a stimulus and its response, or between an instruction and its execution. It is a fundamental performance metric in telecommunications, computing, and mechanical systems, distinct from throughput, which measures the rate of successful delivery.

In computing and networks

In computer networking, latency is often measured as the round-trip time for a packet to travel from source to destination and back; the ping utility reports it. One-way latency across the internet is bounded below by the speed of light in fiber (about 200 km per millisecond). In computer hardware, memory latency—the delay between requesting data and receiving it—contrasts with bandwidth: modern DRAM latency has improved slowly while bandwidth has grown enormously. High-frequency trading, online gaming, virtual reality (where motion-to-photon latency under about 20 ms avoids simulator sickness), and cloud applications are all latency-sensitive.

Other domains

In audio engineering, latency is the delay between input and audible output in digital processing; human factors engineering speaks of latency in teleoperation, where delays degrade control of remote robots and vehicles. In mechanical systems, latency can arise from backlash, elasticity, and signal processing in control loops.
