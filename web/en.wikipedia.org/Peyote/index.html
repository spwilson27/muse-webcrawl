Peyote

Scientific nameLophophora williamsii
FamilyCactaceae
Native rangeChihuahuan Desert: northern Mexico and southern Texas
Active compoundsMescaline and other phenethylamine alkaloids
Conservation statusVulnerable (IUCN)

Peyote (Lophophora williamsii) is a small, spineless cactus native to the Chihuahuan Desert of northern Mexico and southern Texas. It contains the psychoactive alkaloid mescaline and has been used for thousands of years by Indigenous peoples of the region in religious ceremonies. The name derives from the Nahuatl word peyōtl.

Description

The plant is a low, dome-shaped, blue-green cactus, usually 2–7 cm tall, divided into ribs bearing tufts of soft hairs (areoles) instead of spines. It produces small pink or white flowers and pink berries. Most of the plant is a large taproot below ground. The above-ground crown is cut into "buttons" that are dried and chewed or brewed as tea. Peyote grows extremely slowly, taking a decade or more to mature.

Traditional and religious use

Archaeological evidence suggests peyote use in ritual contexts for more than 5,000 years. It was used traditionally by peoples including the Huichol (Wixárika), for whom pilgrimages to the sacred desert of Wirikuta remain central. In the late nineteenth century, the all-night peyote ceremony spread among Plains peoples in the United States and was institutionalized in the Native American Church (founded 1918), which combines Indigenous and Christian elements.

Legal status

Mescaline and peyote are controlled substances in many countries. In the United States peyote is a Schedule I substance, but the American Indian Religious Freedom Act amendments of 1994 protect its bona fide ceremonial use by members of the Native American Church. Over-collection and habitat loss have made wild peyote scarce, and it is listed as Vulnerable; conservation gardens and cultivation programmes have been established in Texas.

Effects

Peyote produces psychedelic effects attributable mainly to mescaline, a phenethylamine that acts at serotonin 5-HT2A receptors, typically including vivid visual phenomena, altered time perception, and nausea during onset. Aldous Huxley's 1954 essay The Doors of Perception, about his mescaline experience, helped popularize awareness of the plant.
