Fundamental justice

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Supreme Court of Canada building, Ottawa]

Fundamental justice is a legal concept in Canadian constitutional law. It is the second half of section 7 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, which provides: "Everyone has the right to life, liberty and security of the person and the right not to be deprived thereof except in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice." A law that deprives a person of life, liberty or security of the person is unconstitutional unless it complies with the principles of fundamental justice.

The principles

In the Reference re BC Motor Vehicle Act (1985), the Supreme Court of Canada held that the principles of fundamental justice are not limited to procedural fairness but include substantive legal principles found "in the basic tenets of our legal system". To qualify as a principle of fundamental justice, as later articulated in cases such as R v Malmo-Levine (2003), a principle must (1) be a legal principle, (2) command significant social consensus that it is fundamental to the fair operation of the legal system, and (3) be capable of being identified with precision as a manageable standard.

Recognised principles

Principles recognised by the Court include: laws must not be arbitrary, overbroad, or grossly disproportionate to their purpose; penal laws must not be vague; the fault (mens rea) requirement must be proportionate to the offence, so that the morally innocent are not punished; there is a presumption against retroactive offences; and specific procedural guarantees such as the right to silence in some contexts, the right to make full answer and defence, and trial within a reasonable framework. In Canada (Attorney General) v Bedford (2013) and Carter v Canada (2015), the principles of arbitrariness, overbreadth and gross disproportionality were central to striking down laws on prostitution and assisted dying.

Contrast with due process

Unlike American substantive due process, which is rooted in the Due Process Clauses of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments, Canadian fundamental justice analysis is structured around the three doctrinal principles and the section 1 justification test, rather than tiers of scrutiny of enumerated and unenumerated rights.
