Helios (Greek: Ἥλιος, "Sun") is the god and personification of the Sun in ancient Greek religion and mythology. A Titan, the son of Hyperion and Theia and brother of the goddesses Selene (the Moon) and Eos (the Dawn), Helios was imagined driving a golden chariot drawn by four horses across the sky each day, rising from a palace in the east and descending into the ocean in the west.

Mythology

Because he saw everything from his chariot, Helios was regarded as an all-seeing witness and was invoked in oaths; it was Helios who informed Demeter that Hades had abducted Persephone, and who told Hephaestus of Aphrodite's affair with Ares. The best-known myth concerning him is that of his son Phaethon, who begged to drive the sun chariot for one day, lost control of the horses, and was struck down by Zeus to save the earth from burning.

In Homer's Odyssey, Odysseus's companions slaughter the sacred cattle of Helios on the island of Thrinacia despite warnings, and are destroyed by Zeus at the sun god's demand. The Colossus of Rhodes, one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, was a statue of Helios, the patron god of that island.

Cult and identification with Apollo

Helios received cult especially on Rhodes and at Corinth. In later antiquity he was increasingly syncretised with Apollo, who took over many solar attributes, though in early Greek tradition the two were distinct. His Roman counterpart was Sol, later elevated as Sol Invictus.
