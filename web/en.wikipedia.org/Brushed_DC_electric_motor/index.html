Brushed DC electric motor

A brushed DC electric motor is an internally commutated electric motor designed to be run from a direct current (DC) power source. It was the first form of electric motor widely commercialised, and remains common in low-cost applications such as toys, appliances and automotive accessories.

Construction

The motor consists of a rotating armature (rotor) carrying windings, a stationary stator providing a magnetic field — from permanent magnets or field windings — and a commutator, a segmented rotary switch fixed to the rotor shaft. Spring-loaded carbon brushes press against the commutator segments, delivering current to the windings and reversing its direction as the rotor turns, so that torque always acts in the same rotational sense.

Operation

Current through the rotor windings produces a magnetic field that interacts with the stator field, generating torque. Speed is roughly proportional to the applied voltage, and torque to the armature current, which makes brushed DC motors simple to control. When the supply is removed, a spinning motor acts as a generator, producing a back EMF proportional to speed.

Types

  • Permanent-magnet motors, common in small appliances.
  • Series-wound motors, giving high starting torque, historically used in traction.
  • Shunt-wound and compound-wound motors, offering better speed regulation.

Advantages and drawbacks

Brushed motors are cheap, simple and easily controlled, but the sliding brush contact wears out, produces electrical noise and sparking, and limits maximum speed. Brushless DC motors, which use electronic commutation, have displaced them in many higher-performance applications.
