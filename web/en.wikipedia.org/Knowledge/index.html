Knowledge is an awareness of facts, a familiarity with individuals and situations, or a practical skill. In philosophy, the study of knowledge is epistemology. Knowledge of facts is propositional knowledge; knowledge of how to do something is practical knowledge or know-how; knowledge by direct experience is knowledge by acquaintance.

The classical analysis

Since Plato, knowledge was often defined as justified true belief: a person knows a proposition if it is true, they believe it, and they are justified in believing it. In 1963 Edmund Gettier published counterexamples — cases of justified true belief that intuitively are not knowledge — sparking decades of attempts to repair the definition.

Sources of knowledge

Philosophers debate the roles of perception, memory, testimony, reason and introspection. Empiricists stress experience; rationalists stress reason. Much human knowledge is acquired through testimony — trusting what others say — including education and science.

Types and domains

A distinction is drawn between a priori knowledge (independent of experience, such as mathematics) and a posteriori knowledge (based on experience). Tacit knowledge, in Michael Polanyi's phrase, is what "we know more than we can tell", such as riding a bicycle.

Knowledge in society

Institutions such as schools, universities, libraries and peer-reviewed journals organise the production and validation of knowledge. The information age has raised new questions about misinformation, expertise and the social distribution of knowledge.
