Carbon bubble

Proven fossil fuel reserves exceed the 2 °C carbon budget several times over (placeholder)

The carbon bubble is a hypothesised bubble in the valuation of fossil fuel companies and related assets, arising from an assumption that all fossil fuel reserves will be consumed. Proven global reserves of coal, oil, and gas contain far more carbon than can be burned while limiting global warming to agreed targets such as 1.5 °C or 2 °C. If climate policy, technological change, or litigation forces much of those reserves to remain in the ground, the assets based on them would become "stranded", and their current market valuation — which assumes full exploitation — would collapse, with consequences for investors, pension funds, and financial stability.

Origins

The concept was popularised by the Carbon Tracker Initiative's 2011 report Unburnable Carbon, which compared the carbon embedded in listed companies' reserves with the remaining "carbon budget". Former Bank of England governor Mark Carney brought the issue to mainstream finance in his 2015 "Tragedy of the Horizon" speech, warning of climate-related financial risks. The idea draws on analyses, including a widely cited 2015 Nature study by McGlade and Ekins, estimating that large fractions of coal (over 80%), gas (about half), and oil (about a third) reserves should remain unused by 2050 for a reasonable chance of staying below 2 °C.

Responses and debate

The carbon bubble thesis underpins the fossil fuel divestment movement, climate stress tests by central banks (such as the Network for Greening the Financial System), and disclosure frameworks like the Task Force on Climate-related Financial Disclosures (TCFD). Critics, including major oil companies, argue that reserves are already valued conservatively, have short payback periods, and that demand will decline only gradually, so there is no bubble; others dispute the speed of the energy transition in both directions.
