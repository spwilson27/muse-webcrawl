The Great Sejm, also known as the Four-Year Sejm (Polish: Sejm Wielki / Sejm Czteroletni), was a legislature of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth that sat in Warsaw from 1788 to 1792. Convened to carry out sweeping reforms of the Commonwealth's political system, it is best remembered for adopting the Constitution of 3 May 1791, the first modern written constitution in Europe.

Background

The Commonwealth in the late eighteenth century was in decline: its elective monarchy, the liberum veto, and magnate oligarchy had paralysed government, and the First Partition of 1772 had cost it about a third of its territory to Russia, Prussia and Austria. Reformers gathered around King Stanisław August Poniatowski and figures such as Ignacy Potocki, Stanisław Małachowski and Hugo Kołłątaj saw the Russo-Turkish war, which distracted Russia, as an opportunity for change.

Proceedings

The Sejm assembled on 6 October 1788. To prevent disruption by the liberum veto it organised itself as a confederation, allowing decisions by majority. Stanisław Małachowski was elected Marshal of the Sejm for the Crown and Kazimierz Nestor Sapieha for Lithuania. Its early acts enlarged the army towards a target of 100,000 men, abolished the Permanent Council (a body seen as an instrument of Russian influence) in 1789, and reformed taxation, including a tax on noble and clerical incomes.

Reform legislation culminated in the Free Royal Cities Act of April 1791 and, on 3 May 1791, the Government Act — the Constitution of 3 May — which established a hereditary constitutional monarchy, abolished the liberum veto, separated the powers of government, and extended political rights to the townspeople.

Dissolution and aftermath

Conservative magnates opposing reform formed the Targowica Confederation and invited Russian intervention. Russian armies invaded in the Polish–Russian War of 1792; the King acceded to Targowica, the Sejm's reforms were annulled, and the Second Partition followed in 1793. The Great Sejm's last sessions took place in 1792.

Legacy

Despite its reversal, the Great Sejm became a foundational symbol of Polish political reform and national revival, and the Constitution of 3 May is commemorated as a national holiday in Poland.
