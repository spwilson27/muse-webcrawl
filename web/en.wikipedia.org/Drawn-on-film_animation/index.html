Drawn-on-film animation

Drawn-on-film animation, also known as direct animation or animation without camera, is an animation technique in which footage is produced by creating images directly on film stock, as opposed to other forms of animation in which images are photographed frame by frame. Artists draw, paint, or scratch on the film, or apply objects to its surface, and the resulting marks become the moving image when projected.

Techniques

Common methods include painting or drawing on clear leader, scratching images into black (emulsion-coated) film, applying bleach or other chemicals, gluing or taping materials onto the film, and using masking and stencils. Because standard 35 mm film has roughly 24 frames per second, even a short film requires thousands of individual marks; some artists work with a magnifying glass and fine brushes.

Practitioners

New Zealand artist Len Lye pioneered the technique with films such as A Colour Box (1935), painted directly onto film. Norman McLaren of the National Film Board of Canada became its most famous exponent, with works including Begone Dull Care (1949) and Blinkity Blank (1955), the latter scratched into black leader. Other practitioners include Stan Brakhage, who painted on film and pressed moths and other objects between film strips in Mothlight (1963), and contemporary makers such as Steven Woloshen.

Characteristics

Direct animation tends toward abstraction because the small frame size makes recognizable imagery difficult; it is often used for visual music, rhythm, and texture, and the soundtrack is sometimes also drawn or scratched optically onto the film.
