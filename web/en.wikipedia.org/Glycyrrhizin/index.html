Glycyrrhizin

Glycyrrhizin (or glycyrrhizic acid) is the chief sweet-tasting constituent of liquorice root, obtained from the plants Glycyrrhiza glabra and related species. A triterpenoid saponin, it is 30–50 times sweeter than sucrose and is used as a flavouring and sweetener in confectionery, beverages, tobacco and traditional medicines.

Pharmacology and safety

After ingestion, gut bacteria hydrolyse glycyrrhizin to glycyrrhetinic acid, which inhibits the enzyme 11β-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2. This allows cortisol to activate mineralocorticoid receptors in the kidney, so excessive consumption of liquorice can cause apparent mineralocorticoid excess: sodium retention, potassium loss, hypertension, oedema and, in severe cases, dangerous hypokalaemia. Regulators advise limiting regular intake; the EU suggests an upper level of about 100 mg glycyrrhizin per day for most adults.

Uses

Glycyrrhizin and its ammonium salt are used to flavour liquorice confectionery, soften the taste of medicines, and in Japan (as monoammonium glycyrrhizinate preparations such as Stronger Neo-Minophagen C) in the treatment of chronic hepatitis. Deglycyrrhizinated liquorice (DGL) is marketed for digestive complaints without the mineralocorticoid side effects.
