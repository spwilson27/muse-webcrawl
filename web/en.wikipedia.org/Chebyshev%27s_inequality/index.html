Chebyshev's inequality

In probability theory, Chebyshev's inequality (also called the Bienaymé–Chebyshev inequality) is a theorem that guarantees, for a wide class of probability distributions, that only a certain fraction of values can lie more than a specified distance from the mean. Specifically, no more than 1/k2 of a distribution's values can be more than k standard deviations away from the mean (for any k > 1).

Statement

Let X be a random variable with expected value μ and finite non-zero variance σ2. Then for any real number k > 1:

P(|X − μ| ≥ kσ) ≤ 1/k2

Equivalently, at least 1 − 1/k2 of the values lie within k standard deviations of the mean: at least 75% within two standard deviations, at least 89% within three, regardless of the shape of the distribution.

History and proof

The inequality was first stated without proof by Irénée-Jules Bienaymé in 1853 and proved by his friend and colleague Pafnuty Chebyshev in 1867; Andrey Markov later gave a proof in his doctoral dissertation. Chebyshev's inequality follows directly from Markov's inequality applied to the non-negative random variable (X − μ)2. It is a key step in proving the weak law of large numbers and, while less sharp than bounds available for specific distributions (such as the 68–95–99.7 rule for the normal distribution), it requires no assumptions beyond finite mean and variance.
