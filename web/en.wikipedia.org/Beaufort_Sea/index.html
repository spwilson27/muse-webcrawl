The Beaufort Sea (French: Mer de Beaufort) is a marginal sea of the Arctic Ocean, located north of the Northwest Territories, Yukon, and Alaska, and west of Canada's Arctic Archipelago. It extends roughly from Point Barrow, Alaska, eastward to Banks Island, and northward into the high Arctic. The sea is named after Sir Francis Beaufort, the Royal Navy hydrographer who devised the Beaufort wind force scale.

Geography and climate

The Beaufort Sea covers about 476,000 km². The Mackenzie River, Canada's longest river, discharges into its southeastern corner, delivering enormous quantities of freshwater and sediment; other inflows include the Firth, Kongakut, and Colville rivers. The climate is severe polar: the sea is frozen most of the year, with only a narrow lead of open water — up to about 100 km wide — opening along the coast in August and September. Surface water temperatures range from about −1.8 °C in winter to 4–8 °C in summer.

The Beaufort Gyre, a great clockwise circulation of the western Arctic Ocean, accumulates freshwater in the sea and strongly influences ice drift throughout the Arctic.

Ecology

Despite the harsh climate, the Beaufort Sea supports significant wildlife. The Mackenzie Delta and coastal lagoons are key habitat for beluga whales, which gather in the thousands in summer estuaries; bowhead whales migrate through in spring and autumn, and ringed and bearded seals, polar bears, and migratory waterfowl are abundant. Fish include Arctic cod, Arctic char, and broad whitefish.

Resources and boundary dispute

The continental shelf is believed to hold substantial oil and natural gas reserves; exploration wells were drilled from artificial islands and drill ships in the 1970s and 1980s, though development has been limited by cost, ice, and environmental concern. A wedge-shaped area of about 21,000 km² is disputed between Canada and the United States: Canada claims the maritime boundary follows the 141st meridian west, while the U.S. argues for an equidistance line — a disagreement with implications for resource rights that both countries manage cooperatively in practice.

Sea ice decline

The Beaufort Sea has experienced some of the most dramatic summer sea-ice losses in the Arctic, and portions of its multiyear ice have converted to thinner seasonal ice, with consequences for the Beaufort Gyre's freshwater storage and for global climate patterns.
