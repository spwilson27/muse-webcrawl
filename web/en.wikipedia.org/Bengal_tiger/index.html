Bengal tiger

The Bengal tiger is a population of the Panthera tigris tigris subspecies and the most numerous tiger population in Asia. It is listed as Endangered on the IUCN Red List since 2008, and was estimated to number about 3,300–3,900 adults by 2022. It is threatened by poaching, loss and fragmentation of habitat, and killing in retaliation for attacks on people and livestock. The Bengal tiger's coat is yellow to light orange, with stripes from dark brown to black; the belly and inner parts of the limbs are white, and the tail is orange with black rings. Males reach a total length of about 270 to 310 cm and weigh 180 to 260 kg; females are smaller.

Distribution and habitat

Bengal tigers live mainly in India, with smaller populations in Bangladesh, Nepal, and Bhutan. They inhabit tropical rainforests, subtropical moist forests, mangrove forests such as the Sundarbans, and temperate highland forests. India holds the majority of the world population, protected in reserves under Project Tiger, launched in 1973. The Sundarbans population, adapted to the mangrove delta of the Ganges and Brahmaputra, is famous for swimming between islands and for historical man-eating incidents.

Behaviour and ecology

Bengal tigers are solitary predators, hunting mainly at night for large ungulates such as chital, sambar, gaur, and wild boar. They are powerful swimmers and excellent climbers when young. Territories are marked by scent and scratch marks, and males' ranges overlap those of several females. Females give birth to litters of two to four cubs after a gestation of about 3.5 months, raising the cubs alone for around two years.

Conservation

Conservation efforts in India and Nepal have produced notable recoveries, with camera-trap censuses showing increases in the 2010s and 2020s. Persistent threats include poaching for skins and body parts used in traditional medicine, habitat fragmentation by roads and development, and conflicts with farmers. The Bengal tiger is the national animal of both India and Bangladesh.
