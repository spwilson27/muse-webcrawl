Four-dimensional space

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A four-dimensional space (4D) is a mathematical extension of the concept of three-dimensional space. Three-dimensional space is the simplest possible abstraction of the observation that one needs only three numbers, called dimensions, to describe the position of an object. In a four-dimensional space, a fourth coordinate is required. The best-known four-dimensional geometric object is the tesseract (four-dimensional hypercube), the 4D analogue of the cube, just as the cube is the 3D analogue of the square.

History

Mathematicians such as Lagrange, Möbius, Cayley, Grassmann, and Riemann developed four- and higher-dimensional geometry in the 18th and 19th centuries. The idea entered popular culture through works such as Edwin Abbott's satire Flatland (1884) and Charles Howard Hinton's writings, which coined the word "tesseract" and popularised methods for visualising four dimensions by analogy with lower-dimensional beings perceiving higher-dimensional objects.

Physics: spacetime

In physics, the fourth dimension is usually taken to be time. Hermann Minkowski showed in 1908 that Einstein's special relativity could be formulated geometrically as a four-dimensional spacetime, in which three spatial dimensions and one time dimension are unified; in general relativity this spacetime is curved by mass and energy. Other theories propose additional spatial dimensions: Kaluza–Klein theory adds a compactified fifth dimension, and string theories require ten or eleven dimensions.

Geometry

Four-dimensional Euclidean space has six planes of rotation rather than a single axis of rotation as in three dimensions, and supports regular polytopes with no three-dimensional analogue, such as the 24-cell. Objects in 4D can be studied through projections and cross-sections, analogously to shadows and slices of 3D objects.
