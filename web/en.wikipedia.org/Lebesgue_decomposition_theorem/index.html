Lebesgue decomposition theorem

In measure theory, the Lebesgue decomposition theorem states that for any two σ-finite signed measures μ and ν on a measurable space (Ω, Σ), there exists a unique pair of σ-finite signed measures ν0 and ν1 such that:

  • ν = ν0 + ν1;
  • ν0 is absolutely continuous with respect to μ (written ν0 ≪ μ);
  • ν1 is singular with respect to μ (written ν1 ⊥ μ): there is a set supported on a μ-null set and its complement is ν1-null.

Refinement

The singular part can be decomposed further into an atomic (discrete) part and a singular continuous part, giving the refined Lebesgue decomposition used in probability theory to classify distributions into discrete, absolutely continuous, and singular continuous components. For a probability measure on the real line, this corresponds to splitting its cumulative distribution function into a jump part, an absolutely continuous part with a density, and a continuous but singular part such as the Cantor function.

Relation to Radon–Nikodym

Combined with the Radon–Nikodym theorem, the decomposition implies that ν can be written as a density with respect to μ plus a singular measure. The theorem is named after Henri Lebesgue and underlies much of modern integration and probability theory.
