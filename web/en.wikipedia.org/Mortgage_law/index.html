Mortgage law

Mortgage law is the branch of property law governing mortgages: legal instruments by which a borrower (the mortgagor) grants a lender (the mortgagee) a security interest in real property as collateral for a loan, while usually retaining possession and ownership. Mortgage law regulates the creation, registration, transfer, enforcement and discharge of mortgages, and the rights of both parties when the borrower defaults.

History

The mortgage developed in English common law from medieval land pledges; its name derives from the Old French mort gage, "death pledge", because the pledge ended when the debt was paid or the property forfeited. Courts of equity softened the harsh common-law rules by recognising the borrower's "equity of redemption" — the right to recover the property by paying the debt even after the contractual date had passed — a principle that remains fundamental in common-law systems.

Key concepts

  • Foreclosure — the process by which the lender enforces the security, typically by selling the property, either through the courts (judicial foreclosure) or under a statutory power of sale.
  • Priority — where several charges burden the same land, registration systems generally determine which lender ranks first.
  • Equity of redemption — protected against "clogs" imposed by the lender.
  • Negative equity — when the outstanding debt exceeds the property's value.

Civil-law systems use related devices such as the hypothec, which confers a security interest without transferring title. Modern mortgage law is heavily supplemented by consumer-protection and banking regulation, especially after the 2008 financial crisis exposed abusive lending and foreclosure practices.
