Crisis of the Third Century

The Crisis of the Third Century, also known as the Military Anarchy or the Imperial Crisis, was a period in Roman history during which the Roman Empire nearly collapsed under the combined pressure of repeated foreign invasion, civil war, plague, and economic depression. The crisis began with the assassination of the emperor Severus Alexander by his own troops in 235 and lasted until the accession of Diocletian and the introduction of the Tetrarchy in 284. During this time the empire split into three competing political entities: the Gallic Empire, the Palmyrene Empire, and the Roman Empire proper.

Causes

The crisis arose from the lack of a settled system of imperial succession, which made the army kingmaker and produced a succession of short-lived "barracks emperors" — at least 26 men claimed the title in about 50 years, and most died violently. At the same time, the empire faced intensified pressure on its frontiers: Goths and other peoples pressed on the Danube and raided by sea, Alamanni and Franks crossed the Rhine, and the aggressive new Sasanian Empire defeated Roman armies in the east, capturing the emperor Valerian in 260.

Course of the crisis

The Plague of Cyprian swept the empire in the 250s and 260s, devastating cities and armies. In the west, the general Postumus founded the Gallic Empire in 260; in the east, Odaenathus of Palmyra and then his widow Zenobia built an empire that took Egypt and much of Asia Minor from Rome. Hyperinflation destroyed the silver coinage, trade networks contracted, and many cities built hasty defensive walls. Recovery began under the "Illyrian emperors": Claudius II defeated the Goths, and Aurelian (r. 270–275) reunified the empire by defeating both Palmyra and the Gallic Empire, earning the title Restitutor Orbis ("Restorer of the World").

Resolution and consequences

Diocletian's reforms after 284 stabilised the empire: he divided imperial authority (the Tetrarchy), reorganised the provinces and army, reformed taxation, and attempted to control prices. The crisis transformed the empire; the militarised, more autocratic state that emerged — the Dominate — differed fundamentally from the Principate of Augustus. Some regions, particularly in the west, suffered lasting economic and demographic damage, while the east recovered more quickly.
