Chroma key

A presenter filmed against a green screen; the coloured background is replaced during compositing.

Chroma key compositing, or chroma keying, is a visual-effects and post-production technique for compositing two or more images or video streams together based on colour hues (chroma range). The technique is commonly used in video production and filmmaking to remove a background from the subject of a photo or video — most prominently in news and weather broadcasting, film special effects, and video game streaming.

A subject is filmed against a uniformly lit, brightly coloured backdrop — usually green ("green screen") or blue ("blue screen"). In post-production, or in real time with dedicated hardware, all pixels of the key colour are made transparent and replaced with a different background image. Green became the standard for digital work because digital cameras are most sensitive to green light and because green clothing is less common than blue; blue was preferred in the film era for its finer grain.

Process

Even lighting of the screen is essential to avoid shadows and spill — light of the key colour reflected onto the subject's edges, which can cause fringing. Keying software computes a matte from the colour difference of each pixel, and modern tools can handle fine detail such as hair and translucent objects. Uneven keys can be cleaned up with garbage mattes and spill suppression.

History

Colour-difference keying grew out of travelling matte techniques used in films such as The Thief of Bagdad (1940). Colour-separation overlay was developed at the BBC and in Hollywood in the 1950s–1970s, notably by Petro Vlahos, whose refined blue-screen process earned industry awards and underpinned effects in films from Ben-Hur (1959) onward. Digital keying in software became routine in the 1990s.
