Three Sisters (agriculture)

The Three Sisters (Spanish: tres hermanas) are the three main agricultural crops of various Indigenous peoples of North America: squash, maize ("corn"), and climbing beans (typically tepary beans or common beans). In a technique called companion planting, the crops are planted together in the same mound, where they benefit one another in a mutually supportive system refined over centuries in Mesoamerica and spread across North America.

How it works

The system exploits the complementary traits of the three plants:

  • Corn is planted first, in mounds. Its tall stalk provides a natural trellis for the beans.
  • Beans, planted once the corn is established, climb the stalks. As legumes, they host nitrogen-fixing bacteria in root nodules, enriching the soil with nitrogen that the corn — a heavy nitrogen feeder — requires.
  • Squash is planted around the mound. Its broad, prickly leaves spread along the ground, forming a living mulch that shades the soil, retains moisture, suppresses weeds, and deters pests such as raccoons.

Nutritionally the three crops also complement one another: corn provides carbohydrates but lacks the essential amino acids lysine and tryptophan, which beans supply, and beans in turn lack the amino acids that corn provides; squash contributes vitamins and edible oil-rich seeds. Together they form a nearly complete diet.

History

Squash was domesticated in Mesoamerica roughly 8,000–10,000 years ago, with maize and beans following. The integrated planting system spread northward, reaching the Eastern Woodlands and the Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) peoples, for whom the Three Sisters held spiritual significance as gifts sustaining life, and who called them De-o-ha-ko, "our sustenance". European settlers adopted the technique in the colonial era. Today the Three Sisters are widely promoted in permaculture and home gardening as a model of ecological, low-input farming.
