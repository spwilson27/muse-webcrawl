Franco Modigliani

Franco Modigliani (18 June 1918 – 25 September 2003) was an Italian-American economist who received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1985 for his pioneering analyses of saving and of financial markets. He spent most of his career at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology.

Life

Modigliani was born in Rome, Italy, and studied law and economics at the University of Rome. Fleeing fascist racial laws and the outbreak of war, he emigrated to the United States in 1939 and completed his doctorate at the New School for Social Research in 1944. He taught at Carnegie Mellon University, where he did much of his famous work, before moving to MIT in 1962.

Contributions

With Richard Brumberg, Modigliani developed the life-cycle hypothesis of saving, which holds that individuals plan consumption and saving over their lifetimes, accumulating assets during working years and drawing them down in retirement. With Merton Miller he proved the Modigliani–Miller theorem (1958), which states that under idealized conditions the market value of a firm is independent of its capital structure — a foundational result of modern corporate finance. He also contributed to the IS–LM framework, monetary policy analysis, and the design of the MPS macroeconomic model used by the Federal Reserve.
