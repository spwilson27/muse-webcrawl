Buganda

Buganda is a kingdom within Uganda in East Africa, the traditional homeland of the Baganda people, the country's largest ethnic group. Its kings are known by the title Kabaka, and its capital region encompasses Kampala, Uganda's national capital.

History

Buganda grew from a small state on the northern shore of Lake Victoria into the dominant power of the Great Lakes region between the 14th and 19th centuries. Tradition counts Kintu as its first Kabaka. Under Kabakas such as Mutesa I, Buganda traded ivory and slaves for firearms and cloth, and hosted explorers John Hanning Speke and Henry Morton Stanley; Mutesa also invited both Christian missionaries and Muslim teachers to his court, sparking religious rivalries that erupted into civil wars in the 1880s and 1890s.

The Uganda Agreement of 1900 made Buganda a privileged province of the British protectorate, with its own parliament, the Lukiiko, and freehold mailo land tenure. After Ugandan independence in 1962, Kabaka Mutesa II became the country's first President, but in 1966 Prime Minister Milton Obote stormed the Kabaka's palace and abolished the kingdoms.

Restoration

The monarchy was restored as a cultural institution in 1993, when Ronald Muwenda Mutebi II was crowned Kabaka. Buganda's government at Mengo pursues development and advocates a federal status ("federo") within Uganda, and campaigns such as the restoration of the Kasubi Tombs — a UNESCO World Heritage Site damaged by fire in 2010 — symbolise its enduring identity. The Baganda speak Luganda and are organised in patrilineal totemic clans (ebika).
