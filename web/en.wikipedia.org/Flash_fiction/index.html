Flash fiction

Flash fiction is a genre of extremely brief prose fiction, typically defined as a complete story told in under 1,000 words — often far fewer. It is known by many names, including short-short stories, microfiction, sudden fiction, nanotales, and postcard fiction. Despite their length, flash pieces aim to present a complete narrative arc with characters, setting, and some form of resolution or resonant final image, relying on compression, implication, and subtext to carry what longer fiction states outright.

Categories by length

Writers and editors subdivide the genre by word count, with conventions varying: a "drabble" is exactly 100 words; a "dribble" or minisaga is 50 words; stories of 280 characters or fewer are sometimes called Twitterature, after the social-media format; and the "hint fiction" form proposed by editor Robert Swartwood limits stories to 25 words. The famous six-word story "For sale: baby shoes, never worn" is often attributed (apocryphally) to Ernest Hemingway and cited as an extreme example of the form.

History

Brevity in storytelling is ancient — Aesop's fables, parables, and Zen kōans are flash-length — and nineteenth- and early twentieth-century writers such as Anton Chekhov, Ambrose Bierce, Franz Kafka, and O. Henry produced very short pieces. The modern genre coalesced in the 1980s and 1990s with anthologies such as Sudden Fiction (1986) and Flash Fiction (1992), edited by James Thomas, Denise Thomas, and Tom Hazuka, which popularised the "flash" name. Writers associated with the form include Lydia Davis, whose often paragraph-length works won the Man Booker International Prize, as well as Yasunari Kawabata ("palm-of-the-hand stories"), Jorge Luis Borges, Fredric Brown (a master of the one-page science-fiction twist), and Etgar Keret. The internet era brought dedicated online journals, daily flash publications, and writing contests, and flash has become a standard offering in creative-writing programmes.

Technique

Flash fiction typically begins in medias res, uses a single scene and point of view, favours strong titles that do part of the narrative work, and ends on an image or turn that reframes what came before. Because every word counts, the form is often compared to poetry, and many practitioners also work as poets.
