Florilegium

A florilegium (plural florilegia) is a compilation of excerpts from other writings, especially from patristic and classical authors. The word derives from the Latin flos (flower) and legere (to gather), and corresponds to the Greek anthology (from anthos, flower). Florilegia were among the most common forms of literature in the Middle Ages.

History

Collections of extracts circulated in late antiquity, but the florilegium became a dominant literary form in the Byzantine world and the Latin Middle Ages. Compilers gathered sayings of the Church Fathers, passages of Scripture, and excerpts from Greek and Roman authors, arranging them thematically — under headings such as faith, virtue, or the nature of God — for use in preaching, teaching, and theological controversy.

Types

Dogmatic florilegia assembled authorities to settle doctrinal disputes, and were appended to the acts of church councils. Ascetic and spiritual florilegia, such as the Greek Philokalia tradition, gathered monastic wisdom. Sacro-profane florilegia mixed Christian texts with classical moral philosophy, preserving fragments of ancient works that might otherwise have been lost.

Significance

Because many ancient texts survive only in quotations, florilegia are a major source for editors reconstructing lost works. Critics, however, note that excerpting could distort meaning by removing passages from their original context — a concern raised by medieval readers themselves.
