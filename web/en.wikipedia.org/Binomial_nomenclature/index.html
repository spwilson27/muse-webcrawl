Binomial nomenclature, also called binary nomenclature, is a formal system of naming species of living things by giving each a name composed of two parts, both of which use Latin grammatical forms, although they can be based on words from other languages. Such a name is called a binomial name (or, in zoology, binomen) or a scientific name; more informally it is also historically called a Latin name.

Structure

The first part of the name — the generic name — identifies the genus to which the species belongs, and is capitalized; the second part — the specific name or specific epithet — identifies the species within the genus, and is written in lower case. The whole name is printed in italics. Thus humans are Homo sapiens, the domestic dog (a subspecies, in trinomial form) Canis lupus familiaris. Names may be shortened after first use (H. sapiens), and the naming authority is often cited after the name, in parentheses if the species has been moved to a different genus since its description.

History

The system was introduced and popularized by Carl Linnaeus, whose Species Plantarum (1753) and the tenth edition of Systema Naturae (1758) are the starting points of botanical and zoological nomenclature respectively. Earlier authors, including the Bauhin brothers, had used two-part names inconsistently; Linnaeus applied them systematically to thousands of species.

Rules and value

Modern codes — the International Code of Zoological Nomenclature and the International Code of Nomenclature for algae, fungi, and plants — govern how names are published, prioritized (principle of priority), and typified. Binomial nomenclature gives every species a single, stable, universally understood name, avoiding the ambiguity of common names, which vary by language and region and may apply to several species at once.
