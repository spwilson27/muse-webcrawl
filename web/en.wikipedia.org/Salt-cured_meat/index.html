Salt-cured meat

Hams curing with salt in a cellar.

Salt-cured meat or salted meat is meat or fish preserved or cured with salt. Salting, brining or dry-curing were among the earliest methods of food preservation, allowing meat to be stored for months without refrigeration by reducing water activity and inhibiting bacterial growth.

Methods

In dry curing, meat is rubbed with salt — often mixed with nitrates or nitrites ("pink curing salt"), sugar and spices — and left to draw out moisture. In wet curing or brining, meat is immersed in a salt solution. Famous products include prosciutto, jamón, bacon, salt pork, corned beef and gravlax (sugar-and-salt cured salmon).

History

Salted meat was a staple of sailors' diets for centuries; navies issued salt pork and salt beef that could survive long voyages, and salted fish such as bacalao (salt cod) underpinned Atlantic trade. The phrase "worth his salt" reflects the value once placed on preserved provisions.

Health considerations

Cured meats are high in sodium, and nitrite curing can lead to the formation of nitrosamines; processed meats have been classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1) on the basis of evidence linking them to colorectal cancer.
