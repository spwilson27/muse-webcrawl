Electricity market

An electricity market is a system for purchasing and selling electric power using supply and demand to set prices. Electricity markets emerged with the deregulation and restructuring of the power industry beginning in the 1980s and 1990s — in Chile, England and Wales, Scandinavia (Nord Pool), parts of the United States, and elsewhere — separating competitive generation and retail from regulated transmission and distribution networks.

Structure

Wholesale markets typically include a day-ahead market, where generators and buyers schedule energy for the next day; real-time or balancing markets that correct deviations; and ancillary services markets for frequency regulation and reserves. In nodal markets (such as PJM in the US), prices are computed at thousands of locations as locational marginal prices reflecting generation cost, transmission congestion, and losses. Zonal markets use larger price areas, as in most of Europe.

Participants

Participants include generators, retailers (load-serving entities), traders, large industrial consumers, and the independent system operator (ISO) or transmission system operator (TSO) that runs the grid and the market. Because electricity historically could not be stored economically, supply must equal demand instantaneously, making prices highly volatile — capable of spiking a hundredfold in scarcity, or turning negative when inflexible generation exceeds demand.

Issues

Debates concern market power, capacity mechanisms to ensure resource adequacy, the integration of variable wind and solar generation with near-zero marginal cost, negative prices, battery storage participation, and extreme events such as the 2021 Texas power crisis, which exposed design vulnerabilities in energy-only markets.
