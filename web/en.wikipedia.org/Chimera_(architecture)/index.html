Chimera (architecture)

In architecture, a chimera is a fantastic, mythical, or grotesque figure used for decorative purposes. Unlike a gargoyle, a chimera does not function as a waterspout; it is purely ornamental. The term derives from the Chimera of Greek mythology, a monster composed of parts of several animals.

Use in Gothic architecture

Chimeras populated the rooflines and towers of Gothic cathedrals alongside gargoyles, in part as decorative sculpture and, in medieval interpretation, as symbolic guardians or representations of evil kept outside the sacred building. The most famous examples are those of Notre-Dame de Paris, including the brooding "Stryge" gazing over the city — though many of Notre-Dame's chimeras are actually nineteenth-century creations of the restoration architect Eugène Viollet-le-Duc, whose Galerie des Chimères reimagined medieval precedent.

Later usage

Chimera figures appear in Gothic Revival buildings of the 19th and early 20th centuries, on university campuses, cathedrals, and skyscrapers, where stone grotesques echo medieval originals.
