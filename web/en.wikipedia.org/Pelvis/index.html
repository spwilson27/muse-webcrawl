Pelvis

The pelvis (Latin for "basin") is the lower part of the trunk of the human body, between the abdomen and the thighs. The bony pelvis is a basin-shaped structure formed by the two hip bones (each composed of the ilium, ischium, and pubis), the sacrum, and the coccyx. It supports the spinal column, protects the pelvic organs, and connects the trunk to the lower limbs.

Structure

Anatomists divide the pelvis into the greater (false) pelvis above the pelvic brim, which supports the lower intestines, and the lesser (true) pelvis below it, which encloses the pelvic cavity. The pelvic inlet, cavity, and outlet form the passage through which a baby passes during childbirth.

Sexual dimorphism

The female pelvis is generally broader and shallower than the male pelvis, with a wider subpubic angle and a larger pelvic outlet — adaptations for childbearing. These differences make the pelvis the most reliable skeletal indicator of sex in forensic anthropology.

Function and clinical notes

The pelvis transmits weight from the spine to the femora in standing and to the ischial tuberosities in sitting, and anchors powerful muscles of locomotion including the gluteals and hip flexors. Pelvic fractures, often from high-energy trauma, can be life-threatening because of associated bleeding. See also sacrum and hip.
