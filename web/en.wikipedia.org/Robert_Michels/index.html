Robert Michels (9 January 1876 – 3 May 1936) was a German-born Italian sociologist who contributed to elite theory by describing the political behaviour of intellectual elites. He is best known for his book Political Parties (1911), which contains a description of the "iron law of oligarchy".

The iron law of oligarchy

Drawing on his experience inside the German Social Democratic Party—then the most democratically organized mass party in Europe—Michels argued that all complex organizations, whatever their democratic intentions, inevitably develop into oligarchies ruled by a leadership class. Organization requires full-time officials who acquire specialized knowledge, control of communications, and a vested interest in self-preservation; the rank and file, busy with daily work, are grateful and deferential. As he put it, "Who says organization, says oligarchy." The thesis became a classic of political sociology and influenced the elite theories of Gaetano Mosca and Vilfredo Pareto, with whom Michels was associated.

Life

Michels, a student of Max Weber, was blocked from a German academic career by his socialist activism and emigrated to Italy, teaching at Turin, Basel, and Perugia. Disillusioned with socialism, he moved toward syndicalism and later supported Italian fascism, joining the National Fascist Party in 1924 and accepting a chair at Perugia under Mussolini's regime. He died in Rome in 1936.
