Food sovereignty

Food sovereignty is a concept and political movement asserting the right of peoples to define their own food and agriculture systems, and the right to healthy and culturally appropriate food produced through ecologically sound and sustainable methods. Coined and promoted by the international peasant movement La Via Campesina, which introduced the term at the 1996 World Food Summit in Rome, it contrasts with the narrower concept of food security, which concerns access to sufficient food regardless of how or by whom it is produced.

Local seed systems are central to food sovereignty advocacy.

Principles

The 2007 Nyéléni Forum in Mali articulated key pillars: food for people rather than profit; valuing food providers; localising food systems; local control over land, water, and seeds; building knowledge and skills; and working with nature through agroecology. The movement opposes corporate concentration in agribusiness, free-trade rules it sees as harming smallholders, and the patenting of seeds, while supporting land reform and peasants' rights — goals partly recognised in the UN Declaration on the Rights of Peasants (2018).

Adoption and criticism

Several countries have written food sovereignty into law or constitutions, notably Ecuador (2008 constitution), Bolivia, Venezuela, Nicaragua, and Mali; Nepal's constitution also guarantees it. Critics question whether localised production can feed large urban populations, note tensions with trade commitments, and warn that the term is used inconsistently across governments and movements.
