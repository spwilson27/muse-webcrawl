Carbonate rock

Carbonate rocks are a class of sedimentary rocks composed primarily of carbonate minerals. The two major types are limestone, which is composed almost entirely of calcite or aragonite, and dolomite rock, which is composed mostly of the mineral dolomite. Carbonate rocks make up roughly 20–25% of all sedimentary rock in the geological record.

Formation

Most carbonate rocks originate in shallow marine environments, where carbonate-secreting organisms such as corals, molluscs, foraminifera and calcareous algae accumulate skeletal debris. Carbonate sediments are also precipitated directly from seawater under suitable chemical conditions. Unlike siliciclastic sediments, which are transported into basins, carbonate sediment is mostly produced in place, giving rise to the concept of the "carbonate factory". Famous modern analogues include the Great Bahama Bank and the Great Barrier Reef.

Importance

Carbonate rocks are economically significant: they host roughly half of the world's conventional petroleum reservoirs, store groundwater in karst aquifers, and serve as the raw material for cement and lime. Thick carbonate platforms record long spans of Earth history and are central to the study of ancient sea level and climate. Their dissolution by slightly acidic water creates distinctive karst landscapes of caves, sinkholes and towers.
