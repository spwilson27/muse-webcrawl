The cranial nerves are the twelve paired nerves that arise directly from the brain and brainstem, rather than from the spinal cord, and innervate structures chiefly in the head and neck. They carry sensory, motor, and autonomic (parasympathetic) fibres and are conventionally numbered I–XII with Roman numerals from front to back. The vagus nerve (X) also reaches into the thorax and abdomen, and the accessory nerve (XI) supplies muscles of the neck and back.

Anatomy and clinical relevance

Except for the olfactory (I) and optic (II) nerves — actually tracts of the forebrain — the cranial nerves emerge from nuclei in the midbrain, pons, and medulla, exiting the skull through foramina such as the optic canal, superior orbital fissure, and jugular foramen. Neurological examination systematically tests each nerve: loss of smell in head injury (I), visual field defects (II), a blown pupil and "down and out" eye in third-nerve palsy (III), facial weakness in Bell's palsy (VII), hoarseness and dysphagia in lower cranial nerve lesions. A popular mnemonic runs "On Old Olympus' Towering Top, A Finn And German Viewed Some Hops."
