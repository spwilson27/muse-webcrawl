Embryonic stem cell

Embryonic stem cells (ESCs) are pluripotent stem cells derived from the inner cell mass of a blastocyst, an early-stage pre-implantation embryo. They can proliferate indefinitely in culture while retaining the ability to differentiate into cells of all three germ layers, making them valuable for developmental biology, drug testing and regenerative medicine research.

History

Mouse embryonic stem cells were first isolated in 1981 by Martin Evans, Matthew Kaufman and Gail Martin. In 1998 James Thomson's group at the University of Wisconsin derived the first human embryonic stem cell lines from surplus IVF embryos. The 2006 creation of induced pluripotent stem cells by Shinya Yamanaka provided an alternative source of pluripotent cells that avoids the use of embryos.

Properties

ESCs are characterised by indefinite self-renewal, a normal karyotype under good culture conditions, and expression of pluripotency markers such as OCT4, NANOG and SOX2. When removed from self-renewal signals they form embryoid bodies and can generate neurons, cardiomyocytes, pancreatic cells and many other cell types in vitro.

Ethics and regulation

Deriving human ESC lines destroys the embryo, which has made the research controversial; funding rules have varied over time and between countries, notably in the United States and Germany. Clinical applications face additional challenges, including tumour risk from residual undifferentiated cells and immune rejection. Early clinical trials have tested ESC-derived retinal pigment epithelial cells for macular degeneration and other products.
