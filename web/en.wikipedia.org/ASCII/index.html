ASCII

ASCII
Full name: American Standard Code for Information Interchange
First published: 1963
Standard: ASA X3.4-1963; ANSI X3.4-1986
Encoding: 7-bit, 128 characters

ASCII (American Standard Code for Information Interchange) is a character encoding standard for electronic communication. ASCII codes represent text in computers and communications equipment; most modern character-encoding schemes, including UTF-8, are based on ASCII.

History

ASCII was developed under the American Standards Association (now ANSI) by a committee led by Robert W. Bemer. It was first published in 1963 and revised in 1967. It became the dominant character encoding of early computing and the Internet's early text protocols, and formed the basis of the ISO/IEC 646 family of national variants.

Structure

ASCII specifies 128 characters in seven bits: 33 non-printing control characters (codes 0–31 and 127), which govern devices and mark data structure — such as NUL, BEL, backspace, line feed, and carriage return — and 95 printable characters (codes 32–126): space, digits 0–9, uppercase and lowercase Latin letters, punctuation, and symbols such as $ and %. Uppercase letters occupy codes 65–90 and lowercase 97–122, differing by a single bit, which simplified case-insensitive sorting and conversion.

Legacy

Because seven bits could not represent the world's scripts, many 8-bit "extended ASCII" code pages appeared, later superseded by Unicode. UTF-8 encodes the ASCII characters identically in one byte, preserving backward compatibility, so ASCII remains embedded in virtually all modern text data.
