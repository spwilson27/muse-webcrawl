Language, Thought, and Reality

Language, Thought, and Reality: Selected Writings of Benjamin Lee Whorf is a 1956 collection of papers by the American linguist Benjamin Lee Whorf (1897–1941), edited by John B. Carroll and published posthumously by the MIT Press and John Wiley & Sons. The volume brought together Whorf's writings on language, linguistics, and the relationship between language and thought, and was instrumental in popularising the idea now known as the Sapir–Whorf hypothesis.

Contents

The collection includes Whorf's major essays, among them "Science and Linguistics" (1940), "Linguistics as an Exact Science" (1940), "An American Indian Model of the Universe", and his studies of the Hopi language, in which he argued that Hopi grammar encodes a conception of time quite different from that of European languages. It also contains papers on cryptographic analysis, the Shawnee language, and Aztec hieroglyphs.

The principle of linguistic relativity

In these writings Whorf developed what he called the "linguistic relativity principle": the idea that the structure of the language a person habitually uses influences the way that person perceives and thinks about the world. He contrasted what he called "Standard Average European" languages with languages such as Hopi, arguing that grammatical categories — tense, plurality, gender, and the treatment of space and time — shape habitual thought.

Influence

The book became one of the most widely read works in linguistics and anthropology in the decades after its publication. Whorf's ideas were influential in psycholinguistics and cognitive science, though stronger versions of linguistic determinism were later criticised and substantially modified by experimental research. A second edition, with a foreword by Stuart Chase and new material, appeared in 2012.
