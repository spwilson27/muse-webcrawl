Peninsular bighorn sheep

The Peninsular bighorn sheep (Ovis canadensis nelsoni) is a population of desert bighorn sheep native to the Peninsular Ranges of Southern California and Baja California, Mexico. It is distinguished as a distinct population segment under the U.S. Endangered Species Act and has been listed as endangered since 1998.

Scientific name: Ovis canadensis nelsoni
Family: Bovidae
Range: Peninsular Ranges, California and Baja California
Status: Endangered (U.S. ESA)

Habitat and behaviour

These sheep inhabit steep, rocky desert mountain slopes and canyons, typically at elevations from near sea level to about 1,200 m. They are well adapted to hot, arid conditions, obtaining much of their water from forage such as cacti and desert shrubs, though they depend on permanent water sources in summer. Rams have large curled horns; ewes have smaller, thinner horns. Social groups are segregated by sex for much of the year, coming together in the autumn rut, when rams clash head-on for dominance.

Conservation

Numbers fell from an estimated 1,100 animals in the United States in the 1970s to fewer than 300 by the mid-1990s, owing to disease transmitted from domestic livestock, habitat loss and fragmentation, drought, and predation by mountain lions. Recovery measures under the Endangered Species Act have included habitat protection, water developments, disease monitoring and restrictions on development near lambing habitat. The population occurs in areas including Anza-Borrego Desert State Park and the Santa Rosa and San Jacinto Mountains National Monument, and has since partially recovered, though it remains the subject of active management and monitoring.
