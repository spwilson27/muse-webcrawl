35 mm format

The 35 mm format, also known as 135 film, is a cartridge film format for still photography. The film itself is 35 millimetres wide, and the standard image format is 36 × 24 mm (often called full frame). Introduced by Kodak in 1934 for use in still cameras, the format was adapted from 35 mm motion picture film stock and became the dominant photographic film format of the twentieth century.

History

Thirty-five millimetre film was standardised for cinema by William Kennedy Dickson and Thomas Edison in the 1890s. Oskar Barnack at Leitz later designed a compact still camera, the Leica, that used cine film loaded in reusable cartridges, and Kodak's 1934 introduction of the pre-loaded 135 cartridge made the format accessible to mass-market cameras such as the Argus C3. After the Second World War, Japanese manufacturers including Canon and Nikon popularised 35 mm rangefinder and single-lens reflex cameras, and the format displaced medium format for most professional and amateur work.

Characteristics

Film is supplied in cartridges, typically with 12, 24, or 36 exposures. The frame's 3:2 aspect ratio has carried over into digital imaging: "full-frame" digital sensors measure 36 × 24 mm, the APS-C format is a smaller crop of the same image circle, and the 35 mm equivalent focal length is used to compare lens fields of view across sensor sizes.

Decline and revival

Sales of 35 mm film collapsed in the 2000s as digital cameras and smartphones took over, and Kodak discontinued many emulsions. A niche revival among hobbyists and fine-art photographers in the 2010s and 2020s led to renewed production runs and new film cameras, though the format remains a small fraction of its former market.
