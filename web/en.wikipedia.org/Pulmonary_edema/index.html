Pulmonary edema

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pulmonary edema
Specialty: Cardiology, pulmonology, emergency medicine
Symptoms: breathlessness, orthopnoea, pink frothy sputum, wheeze
Causes: heart failure, ARDS, fluid overload, high altitude
Treatment: oxygen, diuretics, ventilation, treating the cause

Pulmonary edema (American English), or pulmonary oedema (British English), is fluid accumulation in the tissue and air spaces of the lungs. It leads to impaired gas exchange and may cause hypoxemia and respiratory failure. It is due to either failure of the left ventricle of the heart to remove oxygenated blood adequately from the pulmonary circulation (cardiogenic pulmonary edema), or injury to the lung parenchyma or vasculature (non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema).

Signs and symptoms

The chief symptom is breathlessness, which may develop gradually or suddenly. Characteristic features include orthopnoea (breathlessness when lying flat), paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea, cough producing pink frothy sputum, rapid breathing, sweating, anxiety, and crackles heard on examination of the chest. Severe acute pulmonary edema is a medical emergency and can be fatal without rapid treatment.

Causes

Cardiogenic causes include decompensated heart failure, myocardial infarction, severe hypertension and valvular disease. Non-cardiogenic causes include acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), sepsis, major trauma, inhaled toxins, kidney failure with fluid overload, and high-altitude pulmonary edema (HAPE).

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis draws on the history and examination, chest radiograph (which may show Kerley B lines, bat-wing opacities or pleural effusions), blood gases, ECG and natriuretic peptide levels, and echocardiography. Treatment aims to improve oxygenation — with supplemental oxygen, continuous positive airway pressure or ventilation — reduce preload with diuretics such as furosemide and vasodilators, and treat the underlying cause.
