Government-sponsored enterprise

A government-sponsored enterprise (GSE) in the United States is a publicly traded, privately owned financial institution created by Congress to enhance the flow of credit to specific sectors of the economy and to make those segments of the capital market more efficient and transparent. GSEs are distinguished from government agencies: they are privately capitalized, but benefit from an implicit federal backing that lowers their borrowing costs.

Housing GSEs

The best-known GSEs operate in housing finance. Fannie Mae (Federal National Mortgage Association, founded 1938) and Freddie Mac (Federal Home Loan Mortgage Corporation, founded 1970) buy mortgages from lenders, package them into mortgage-backed securities, and guarantee them for investors, supporting the market for long-term fixed-rate mortgages. The Federal Home Loan Banks, a system of regional cooperatives created in 1932, provide liquidity to member lenders. Ginnie Mae, by contrast, is a wholly government-owned corporation rather than a GSE.

The 2008 conservatorship

Losses during the subprime mortgage crisis led the Federal Housing Finance Agency to place Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac into federal conservatorship in September 2008, with the Treasury providing financial support. The two enterprises have remained in conservatorship ever since, and their long-term status is a recurring subject of policy debate.

Other GSEs

Other GSEs include the Farm Credit System and Farmer Mac in agricultural finance, and Sallie Mae (the Student Loan Marketing Association), which was fully privatized in 2004.
