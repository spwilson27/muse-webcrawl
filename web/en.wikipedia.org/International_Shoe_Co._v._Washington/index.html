International Shoe Co. v. Washington

International Shoe Co. v. Washington, 326 U.S. 310 (1945), is a landmark United States Supreme Court decision that established the modern standard for personal jurisdiction over out-of-state corporations. The Court held that a corporation may be subject to suit in a state, consistent with due process, if it has "minimum contacts" with the state such that the suit "does not offend traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice".

Facts

International Shoe was a Delaware corporation with its principal place of business in St. Louis, Missouri. It employed about a dozen salespeople in the state of Washington, who rented showrooms and solicited orders there, but the company had no offices or inventory in the state. Washington sought contributions from the company to its unemployment compensation fund based on its in-state salesmen's commissions. The company argued that it was not "present" in Washington and that the state's exercise of jurisdiction violated the Due Process Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment.

Decision

Chief Justice Harlan Fiske Stone wrote the opinion of the Court. Breaking from the rigid territorial framework of Pennoyer v. Neff (1878), the Court reasoned that due process requires only that a defendant, if not present in the territory of the forum, "have certain minimum contacts with it such that the maintenance of the suit does not offend 'traditional notions of fair play and substantial justice'". Because International Shoe's activities in Washington were continuous and systematic and its Washington sales generated the very obligation sued upon, jurisdiction was proper.

Significance

The decision generated the modern framework of personal jurisdiction in American civil procedure, giving rise to the distinction between general (all-purpose) jurisdiction and specific jurisdiction, and to the minimum contacts analysis elaborated in later cases such as Burger King Corp. v. Rudzewicz (1985), Asahi Metal Industry Co. v. Superior Court (1987), and Daimler AG v. Bauman (2014). The case is a staple of first-year civil procedure courses in American law schools.
