Chord progression

A chord progression or harmonic progression is a succession of chords in a piece of music. Chord progressions supply the harmonic foundation over which melody and rhythm are built, and many genres rely on a small stock of conventional patterns — such as the twelve-bar blues or the ii–V–I of jazz — that listeners recognize immediately. Progressions are commonly described with Roman numerals indicating each chord's scale degree within a key.

The I–V–vi–IV progression, one of the most common patterns in popular music.

Common progressions

  • I–V–vi–IV ("pop progression" or "Axis progression") — heard in countless pop and rock songs.
  • ii–V–I — the fundamental cadential pattern of jazz harmony.
  • Twelve-bar blues — I, IV, and V chords arranged over twelve bars (I–I–I–I–IV–IV–I–I–V–IV–I–V).
  • vi–IV–I–V — a rotation of the pop progression beginning on the minor chord.
  • Pachelbel progression — I–V–vi–iii–IV–I–IV–V, from the Canon in D.
  • Andalusian cadence — i–VII–VI–V in minor keys, characteristic of flamenco.

Theory

In functional harmony, chords are grouped as tonic (stable, home), subdominant or predominant (moving away), and dominant (creating tension that resolves to the tonic). Voice leading — the smooth movement of individual notes between chords — shapes whether a progression sounds natural. The circle of fifths describes the strong pull of root motion by descending fifth, which underlies patterns such as ii–V–I.

In popular music

Much popular music uses loops of two to four chords for entire songs, placing variety in melody, timbre, and rhythm instead. The perceived emotional character — bright, melancholic, unresolved — depends heavily on whether the loop starts on the tonic or the relative minor and on how it is voiced.
