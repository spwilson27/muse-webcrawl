Modality (music)

Modality in music refers to the use of musical modes — arrangements of pitches distinct from the major and minor scales that dominate Western tonal music. Modal systems underpin much folk music, medieval and Renaissance sacred music, and many non-Western traditions.

The church modes

Medieval theorists organized chant into eight modes (four authentic and four plagal), built on finals D, E, F, and G. Renaissance theorists such as Glarean (1547) expanded the system to twelve modes, systematizing the diatonic modes familiar today: Ionian, Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian, and Locrian — rotations of the major scale, each with a distinctive pattern of whole and half steps and characteristic melodic color.

Modal practice

Unlike tonal music, which is driven by functional harmony and cadential resolution to a tonic chord, modal music emphasizes melodic patterns and a tonal center (final) without obligatory dominant–tonic progressions. Modality survived in folk repertoires (for example in English, Irish, and Balkan music) and was revived deliberately by composers from Debussy and Vaughan Williams onward. In jazz, modal playing — exemplified by Miles Davis's Kind of Blue (1959), especially "So What" — freed soloists from rapid chord changes, letting them improvise over a single mode for extended stretches. Modes are also fundamental to Indian raga, Arabic maqam, and Persian dastgah, though those systems involve far more than scale patterns alone.
