Trinity

Trinity
Central doctrineOne God in three persons: Father, Son, Holy Spirit
Key councilsNicaea (325), Constantinople (381)
Key termsConsubstantial (homoousios), hypostasis, perichoresis
TraditionsCatholic, Eastern Orthodox, most Protestant churches

The Trinity is the central doctrine of most Christian theology concerning the nature of God, which defines one God existing in three coequal, coeternal, consubstantial persons: God the Father, God the Son (Jesus Christ), and God the Holy Spirit, three distinct persons (hypostases) sharing one essence (ousia). According to this doctrine, God is thus "one God in three Persons" — the persons are distinct, yet are one "substance, essence or nature".

Development

Although the word "Trinity" does not appear in the Bible, early Christians reflected on passages such as the Great Commission ("in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit", Matthew 28:19). The term trias was used by Theophilus of Antioch (c. 180), and Tertullian coined the Latin trinitas. The doctrine was articulated amid controversies with Arianism, which taught that the Son was a created being. The First Council of Nicaea (325) declared the Son homoousios ("of one substance") with the Father, and the First Council of Constantinople (381) completed the Nicene Creed with the divinity of the Holy Spirit.

Formulations and differences

The Athanasian Creed summarizes the Latin tradition. Eastern and Western churches later divided partly over the filioque clause — whether the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father "and the Son" — added in the West. Diagrams such as the Shield of the Trinity express the doctrine's logic: the Father is God, the Son is God, the Holy Spirit is God, yet each person is not the other.

Nontrinitarian traditions

Some groups reject the doctrine as unscriptural, including Unitarians, Jehovah's Witnesses, Oneness Pentecostals, and Latter-day Saints (who hold a distinct "Godhead" theology). Islam and Judaism affirm strict unitarian monotheism and deny the Trinity.
