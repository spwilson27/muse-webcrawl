Xenopus (from Greek, "strange foot") is a genus of highly aquatic frogs native to sub-Saharan Africa. The best-known species, the African clawed frog (Xenopus laevis), is a widely used model organism in developmental biology and was historically used in human pregnancy tests.

Xenopus laevis, the African clawed frog.

Description

Members of the genus have flattened, streamlined bodies, fully webbed toes with keratinous claws on three digits, and no tongue that can be protruded; they feed by sweeping food into the mouth with their forelimbs. They possess a lateral-line system as adults, an adaptation to murky water.

Model organism

X. laevis eggs and embryos are large, hardy, and easy to manipulate, making them central to studies of embryology, cell biology, and cloning — John Gurdon's Nobel Prize-winning nuclear transfer experiments used Xenopus. The related X. tropicalis is favored for genetics because of its diploid genome and shorter generation time.

Invasive species

Escaped and released laboratory animals have established feral populations in parts of North and South America, Europe, and Asia, where they prey on native fauna and may spread the chytrid fungus.
