Diane de Poitiers

Diane de Poitiers, royal mistress and patron of the arts.

Diane de Poitiers (1499/1500–1566) was a French noblewoman and courtier, best known as the mistress, confidante, and effective co-ruler of King Henri II of France. Renowned for her beauty, intelligence, and political influence, she was, for over two decades, arguably the most powerful woman in France.

Early life and marriage

Diane was born into the old nobility of the Dauphiné, the daughter of Jean de Poitiers, Count of Saint-Vallier. At fifteen she married Louis de Brézé, Grand Seneschal of Normandy, a man more than forty years her senior. Widowed in 1531, she wore black and white mourning for the rest of her life — colours Henri II later adopted as his own.

Mistress of Henri II

Diane had known Henri since his childhood; their relationship deepened in the 1530s and endured until his death. Twenty years older than the king, she acted as his adviser, wrote and signed official documents jointly with him (the signature "HenriDiane" survives), and received great estates, including the Château de Chenonceau and the Château d'Anet, which she rebuilt magnificently. The intertwined initials H and D appeared on royal buildings and coinage throughout the reign, and Diane exercised patronage over poets and artists of the French Renaissance. Her great rival was Henri's queen, Catherine de' Medici, who tolerated the arrangement while Henri lived.

Fall and death

When Henri was mortally wounded in a jousting accident in 1559, Catherine barred Diane from his deathbed. Diane was ordered to return the crown jewels and Chenonceau (receiving Chaumont in exchange) and retired to Anet, where she died in 1566. Forensic analysis of her exhumed remains in 2009 found extremely high concentrations of gold in her hair, supporting the tradition that she drank potable gold as a beauty treatment — which may have contributed to her death.
