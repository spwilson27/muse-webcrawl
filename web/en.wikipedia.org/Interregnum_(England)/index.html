Interregnum (England)

The Interregnum was the period between the execution of Charles I on 30 January 1649 and the arrival of his son Charles II in London on 29 May 1660, which marked the start of the Restoration. During this period the monarchies of England, Ireland and Scotland were variously governed as a republic called the Commonwealth of England, and later by Oliver Cromwell and his son Richard as Lord Protectors under the Protectorate.

Commonwealth, 1649–1653

After the trial and execution of Charles I, the Rump Parliament abolished the monarchy and the House of Lords and declared England a Commonwealth. The Council of State governed executive affairs. The Commonwealth suppressed royalist risings in Ireland and Scotland in campaigns led by Cromwell, including the sieges of Drogheda and Wexford, and defeated Charles II's invasion at the Battle of Worcester in 1651. The First Anglo-Dutch War followed in 1652–1654.

Frustrated with the Rump's failure to settle a constitution, Cromwell dissolved it in April 1653. The nominated Barebone's Parliament sat briefly before power passed to Cromwell.

The Protectorate, 1653–1659

Under the Instrument of Government, England's first written constitution, Cromwell became Lord Protector for life. The Protectorate combined parliamentary government with military authority; in 1655–1657 the country was administered regionally by major-generals. Religious toleration was extended to many Protestant groups, and Jews were informally readmitted to England.

After Cromwell's death in September 1658, his son Richard succeeded him but lacked authority over the army and resigned in May 1659. Political chaos followed until General George Monck marched on London and arranged elections that invited Charles II to return under the Declaration of Breda.

Legacy

The Interregnum remains the only period of republican government in English history. Its constitutional experiments — written constitutions, an elected head of state, and religious pluralism — influenced later political thought.
