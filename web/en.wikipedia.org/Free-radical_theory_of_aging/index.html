Free-radical theory of aging

Free-radical theory of aging
Proposed byDenham Harman (1954)
FieldBiogerontology
Key conceptsReactive oxygen species, oxidative stress, mitochondrial DNA damage
Free radicals can damage DNA, proteins, and lipids

The free-radical theory of aging states that organisms age because cells accumulate free-radical damage over time. A free radical is any atom or molecule with a single unpaired electron in an outer shell; such radicals are highly reactive and can damage all components of the cell, including proteins, lipids, and DNA. The theory was first proposed by Denham Harman in 1954 and extended by him in 1972 into the mitochondrial theory of aging, which identifies the mitochondria — the main source of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in most cells — as both the principal producer and the principal victim of free-radical damage.

Mechanisms

Normal metabolism continuously generates ROS such as superoxide and hydrogen peroxide, chiefly as byproducts of the mitochondrial electron transport chain. Cells defend themselves with antioxidant enzymes (superoxide dismutase, catalase, glutathione peroxidase) and dietary antioxidants. According to the theory, when production outstrips defence and repair — a state called oxidative stress — damage accumulates in mitochondrial DNA, membranes, and enzymes, progressively degrading cellular function and producing the aging phenotype.

Evidence and criticism

Supporting evidence includes the correlation between metabolic rate and lifespan across many species, the lifespan extension produced by caloric restriction in some models, and the fact that long-lived species often show lower rates of mitochondrial ROS production. However, the theory in its simple form has been substantially challenged: genetic manipulations that increase or decrease antioxidant defences in model organisms have often failed to change lifespan as predicted, antioxidant supplementation trials in humans have generally not extended life (and some, such as beta-carotene in smokers, caused harm), and some experiments — for example in naked mole-rats, which tolerate high oxidative damage yet live exceptionally long — suggest that ROS can also act as beneficial signalling molecules (mitohormesis).

Modern biogerontology therefore treats oxidative damage as one contributor among several "hallmarks of aging", rather than the single master cause originally envisioned.
