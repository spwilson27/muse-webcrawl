High-throughput screening

Acronym: HTS
Field: Drug discovery, pharmacology
Scale: 10,000–1,000,000+ compounds per screen

High-throughput screening (HTS) is a method for scientific experimentation widely used in drug discovery. Using robotics, data-processing software, liquid-handling devices, and sensitive detectors, it allows a researcher to quickly conduct millions of chemical, genetic, or pharmacological tests. Through this process one can rapidly identify active compounds, antibodies, or genes that modulate a particular biological pathway; the results provide starting points ("hits") for drug design.

Method

Assays are miniaturised into microtiter plates of 96, 384, 1536, or more wells, each containing a different compound from a chemical library, together with the target — an enzyme, receptor, cell line, or organism. Robotic arms dispense reagents and move plates between incubators and readers that measure fluorescence, luminescence, absorbance, or imaging readouts. Assay quality is often assessed by the Z-factor statistic; screens with Z′ above 0.5 are considered excellent. Ultra-high-throughput screening (uHTS) exceeds 100,000 compounds per day.

Hit follow-up

Primary hits are confirmed in repeat and dose–response assays, counter-screened against artefacts (such as autofluorescent or promiscuous compounds, including PAINS), and validated in orthogonal assays before medicinal chemistry optimisation in the hit-to-lead phase.

Applications

Beyond small-molecule drugs, HTS methods underpin functional genomics (genome-wide RNAi and CRISPR screens), antibody discovery, toxicology screening programmes such as Tox21, and materials science. Costs per well have fallen to a few cents in large industrial screens, making libraries of a million or more compounds routine.
