Buddhism in Mongolia

Buddhism in Mongolia derives overwhelmingly from the Gelug school of Tibetan Buddhism, though it has distinctive Mongolian features. Buddhism is the largest religion in Mongolia, historically shaping its culture, art and politics from the era of the Mongol Empire to the present day.

Early contacts

Buddhism reached the steppe through Central Asian and Chinese contacts during the Xiongnu, Xianbei and Rouran periods. Under the Mongol Empire, Kublai Khan patronised the Tibetan lama Phags-pa, who devised a script for the empire, and Buddhist institutions received privileges even as the court also honoured shamanism, Daoism, Islam and Christianity.

The second conversion

In 1578 the Tümed ruler Altan Khan met the Gelug hierarch Sonam Gyatso and bestowed on him the title "Dalai Lama". Mass conversion followed in the late 16th and 17th centuries; monasteries such as Erdene Zuu, built from 1585 on the site of Karakorum, anchored settled religious life. The reincarnate line of the Jebtsundamba Khutuktu, beginning with Zanabazar, became Mongolia's spiritual leadership.

Suppression and revival

In the Mongolian People's Republic, anti-religious purges in the 1930s destroyed more than 700 monasteries and killed tens of thousands of lamas. Only Gandantegchinlen Monastery in Ulaanbaatar was permitted to function, under surveillance, after 1944. Following the democratic revolution of 1990, Buddhism revived openly: monasteries were rebuilt, monks trained again, and the faith regained a central place in national identity.

Today

Censuses show roughly half of Mongolians identifying as Buddhist, with large minorities reporting no religion. The Dalai Lama's visits remain politically sensitive because of pressure from China.
