Ptosis, also known as blepharoptosis, is a drooping or falling of the upper eyelid. This condition is sometimes called "lazy eye", but that term normally refers to the condition amblyopia. If severe enough and left untreated, the drooping eyelid can cause other conditions, such as amblyopia or astigmatism, so it is especially important for the disorder to be treated in children before it can interfere with vision development. Ptosis may affect one or both eyes and is measured against the normal lid position, in which the upper lid covers the top 1–2 mm of the cornea.

Causes

Ptosis results from dysfunction of the muscles that raise the eyelid—the levator palpebrae superioris and Müller's muscle—or of their nerve supply. Classification by cause includes:

  • Myogenic – weakness of the levator muscle itself, as in congenital ptosis, myasthenia gravis, or muscular dystrophies.
  • Aponeurotic (involutional) – stretching or dehiscence of the levator aponeurosis, the most common type in older adults; also associated with long-term contact lens wear.
  • Neurogenic – oculomotor nerve (CN III) palsy or Horner's syndrome (loss of sympathetic supply to Müller's muscle).
  • Mechanical – the lid is weighed down by a tumour, swelling, or scarring.
  • Traumatic – injury to the muscle or nerve.

Signs and diagnosis

Patients may report a sleepy appearance, reduced superior visual field, brow ache, or compensatory head tilt and frontalis overaction. Assessment includes measuring the marginal reflex distance (MRD1), levator function, and lid crease height, and examination for associated conditions: ptosis with a dilated pupil suggests third-nerve palsy, while ptosis with a small pupil suggests Horner's syndrome. Fatigable ptosis that worsens through the day is characteristic of myasthenia gravis.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the cause: therapy of myasthenia or other underlying disease, and otherwise surgery—commonly levator resection or advancement, Müller's muscle resection, or frontalis sling suspension for poor levator function. In congenital ptosis, surgery is timed to prevent deprivation amblyopia. Non-surgical options include eyelid crutches attached to spectacles and, for mild cases, topical agents such as oxymetazoline, approved for acquired ptosis.
