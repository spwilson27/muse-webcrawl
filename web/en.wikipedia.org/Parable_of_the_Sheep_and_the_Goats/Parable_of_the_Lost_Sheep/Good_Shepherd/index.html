Good Shepherd

The Good Shepherd is a pericope and an image in the New Testament in which Jesus Christ portrays himself as the shepherd who lays down his life for his sheep. The saying is found in the Gospel of John, chapter 10, and is related to the Parable of the Lost Sheep found in the Gospels of Matthew and Luke, in which a shepherd leaves his flock of ninety-nine sheep to find the one that is lost.

The discourse in John

In John 10, Jesus says: "I am the good shepherd. The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep." The hired hand, by contrast, runs away when the wolf comes because the sheep do not belong to him. Jesus presents himself as the one who knows his own sheep and is known by them, just as he knows the Father. The discourse was delivered in the context of controversy with the Pharisees following the healing of the man born blind in John 9, and it develops imagery of the sheepfold, the gate, and the voice of the shepherd.

Old Testament background

The image draws on a long biblical tradition. Psalm 23 begins "The LORD is my shepherd; I shall not want." The prophet Ezekiel, in chapter 34, criticises the shepherds of Israel who fed themselves rather than the flock, and promises that God himself will seek out the lost sheep and bind up the injured. Isaiah 40 speaks of God feeding his flock like a shepherd and carrying the lambs in his arms. These texts provided the framework within which Jesus' hearers understood his claim.

In early Christian art

The Good Shepherd is among the most common representations of Jesus in early Christian art, appearing in the Roman catacombs from the 3rd century onward. He is usually depicted as a youthful, beardless figure carrying a sheep across his shoulders — the kriophoros type borrowed from Greco-Roman art, where it had symbolised philanthropy and care. The motif appears in catacomb paintings, sarcophagi, and in the famous marble statuette now in the Vatican Museums.

Interpretation

Theologians have read the Good Shepherd discourse as a statement about salvation, pastoral care, and the relationship between Christ and believers. The image gave rise to the pastoral model of ministry in the church: bishops and priests are described as shepherds of their flocks, a metaphor echoed in the pastoral staff (crosier). The Fourth Sunday of Easter in the Roman Catholic liturgical calendar is traditionally known as Good Shepherd Sunday.
