Parables of Jesus

The parables of Jesus are brief allegorical stories, found in the canonical gospels and a few non-canonical sources, that Jesus of Nazareth used in his teaching. Roughly a third of Jesus' recorded sayings in the Synoptic Gospels take the form of parables, making them a defining feature of his ministry. The word "parable" translates the Greek parabolē, meaning a comparison or illustration placed alongside something to explain it.

Sources

Parables appear in the Gospels of Matthew, Mark, and Luke; the Gospel of John contains longer allegorical discourses, such as the Good Shepherd and the True Vine, but few narratives usually classified as parables. Luke's Gospel contains the largest number unique to itself, including the Good Samaritan and the Prodigal Son. A number of parables also survive in the non-canonical Gospel of Thomas, which some scholars regard as containing early independent traditions.

Themes

The dominant theme of the parables is the Kingdom of God (Kingdom of Heaven in Matthew). The parables of growth — the Sower, the Mustard Seed, the Leaven — describe the kingdom's humble beginnings and surprising expansion. Parables of mercy and forgiveness include the Prodigal Son, the Lost Sheep, the Lost Coin, and the Unforgiving Servant. Parables of judgment and watchfulness include the Ten Virgins, the Talents, the Wheat and the Tares, and the Sheep and the Goats, in which the Son of Man separates the nations like a shepherd separating sheep from goats at the final judgment.

Interpretation

For much of church history, parables were read allegorically, with every detail assigned a symbolic meaning. Adolf Jülicher's influential work in the late 19th century argued that each parable makes essentially one point. Later scholars such as C. H. Dodd and Joachim Jeremias situated the parables in the historical ministry of Jesus, reading them as urgent proclamations of the kingdom's arrival, while acknowledging layers of early church reinterpretation. Modern literary approaches treat the parables as open-ended stories that invite the hearer into a new way of seeing the world.
