Chaparral


Chaparral, a drought-adapted shrubland of the American Southwest and Mexico.

Chaparral (from Spanish chaparro, a scrub oak) is a shrubland plant community found primarily in California and the northern part of the Baja California Peninsula in Mexico. It forms one of the world's five Mediterranean-climate shrubland ecosystems, alongside the maquis of the Mediterranean Basin, the matorral of Chile, the fynbos of South Africa and the mallee of Australia. Chaparral is characterised by hot, dry summers, mild wet winters and vegetation adapted to periodic wildfire.

Vegetation

Typical chaparral consists of dense, evergreen, sclerophyllous shrubs one to four metres tall, including chamise, manzanita, ceanothus, scrub oak and mountain mahogany. Many species have thick, waxy or hairy leaves that reduce water loss, and deep root systems that tap moisture far below the surface.

Fire ecology

Chaparral is a fire-adapted ecosystem. Many of its shrubs resprout vigorously from root crowns after burning, while others produce seeds that germinate only after exposure to heat or smoke. Natural fire intervals of 30 to 150 years maintain the community; overly frequent fires, often human-caused, can convert chaparral to non-native grassland.

Distribution and wildlife

Chaparral covers roughly 8.5 percent of California, from the state's northern Coast Ranges to the Sierra de San Pedro Mártir in Baja California. It shelters animals such as the coyote, California quail, wrentit, spotted towhee and numerous endemic reptiles and insects.
