Dictator

A dictator is a political leader who possesses absolute power. A dictatorship is a state ruled by one dictator or by a small clique. The term originated with the title of a magistrate in the Roman Republic who was appointed by the Senate to rule with absolute power during times of emergency, with the expectation that power would be returned when the crisis ended.

History

In the Roman Republic, a dictator was a constitutional office limited in principle to six months; famous holders include Cincinnatus, celebrated for returning to his farm after victory. The later regimes of Sulla and Julius Caesar, who was named dictator in perpetuity, broke with this tradition and helped transform the term's meaning. In the modern era, dictators typically seize or hold power unconstitutionally, ruling without free elections, a free press, or an independent judiciary, and often maintaining control through security forces, propaganda, and personality cults.

Characteristics

Dictatorships are distinguished from absolute monarchies by the dictator's lack of hereditary or traditional legitimacy. Scholars classify dictatorships by their support base — military juntas, one-party states, and personalist regimes — and study how dictators manage elites, distribute rents, and survive in office.
