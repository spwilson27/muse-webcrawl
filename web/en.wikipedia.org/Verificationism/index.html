Verificationism

Verificationism, also known as the verification principle or verifiability criterion of meaning, is the doctrine that a statement is cognitively meaningful only if it can be verified through empirical observation or is true by definition (analytic). It was the central tenet of logical positivism, the movement associated with the Vienna Circle in the 1920s and 1930s.

Doctrine

According to the principle, statements that are neither empirically verifiable nor tautological — including much traditional metaphysics, theology, and ethics understood as factual claims — are literally meaningless, though they may express attitudes or emotions. The slogan of the movement was that "the meaning of a proposition is the method of its verification." Adherents included Moritz Schlick, Rudolf Carnap, and, in English-speaking philosophy, A. J. Ayer, whose Language, Truth and Logic (1936) popularised the view.

Formulations and problems

Philosophers distinguished strong verification (conclusive establishment by observation) from weak verification (observation can render a statement probable). Universal scientific laws resist strong verification since no finite set of observations exhausts them — one motive for Karl Popper's rival criterion of falsifiability. A recurrent objection is that the verification principle is itself neither analytic nor empirically verifiable, and so meaningless by its own standard; defenders replied that it is a methodological proposal rather than a factual claim. By the 1950s the criterion had been weakened by technical difficulties — for example around disposition terms and theoretical entities — and logical positivism dissolved as a movement, though verificationist ideas persist in empiricist philosophy of science and in debates over scientific realism.
