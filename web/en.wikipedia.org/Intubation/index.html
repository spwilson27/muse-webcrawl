Intubation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Intubation is the placement of a flexible tube into a body orifice or hollow organ, most commonly tracheal (endotracheal) intubation: the insertion of a tube through the mouth or nose into the trachea to maintain an open airway, protect the lungs from aspiration, and allow mechanical ventilation or the delivery of anaesthetic gases. It is a core skill in anaesthesia, emergency medicine, intensive care and pre-hospital care.

Technique

Tracheal intubation is usually performed after induction of general anaesthesia and, in emergency settings, as rapid sequence induction with a sedative and a neuromuscular blocking agent to minimise aspiration risk. The clinician visualises the vocal cords with a laryngoscope — direct or video — passes the endotracheal tube between them, inflates a cuff to seal the airway, and confirms placement by capnography (detection of exhaled carbon dioxide), chest movement and auscultation. Nasotracheal intubation passes the tube through the nose; fibreoptic techniques are used for anticipated difficult airways, sometimes with the patient awake.

Indications

  • Airway protection in coma, overdose or reduced consciousness
  • Respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation
  • General anaesthesia for surgery, especially with muscle relaxation
  • Upper airway obstruction, severe facial or airway trauma, and burns
  • Cardiopulmonary resuscitation and shock states

Complications

Complications include failed intubation and hypoxia, misplacement into the oesophagus or a main bronchus, damage to teeth, lips and airway structures, aspiration, laryngospasm, and, with prolonged intubation, tracheal stenosis or ventilator-associated pneumonia. Difficult intubation is predicted by examination findings and grading systems such as the Mallampati score and Cormack–Lehane view; rescue devices include supraglottic airways and surgical cricothyrotomy.

Other meanings

The term also covers nasogastric intubation (a tube into the stomach for feeding or decompression) and catheterisation of other structures, but medical usage without qualification generally means tracheal intubation.
