Hauteville family

The Hauteville family (Italian: Altavilla) was a Norman family that rose to great prominence in southern Italy and Sicily in the 11th and 12th centuries. Originally minor lords from the Cotentin Peninsula in Normandy — named for their estate at Hauteville-la-Guichard — the sons of Tancred of Hauteville traveled south as mercenaries and adventurers and carved out one of the most remarkable dynastic success stories of the Middle Ages.

Origins

Tancred of Hauteville (c. 980–1041) was a petty Norman lord with twelve sons by two wives. Finding his lands too small to divide, many of his sons — including William Iron Arm, Drogo, Humphrey, Robert Guiscard and Roger — left Normandy between the 1030s and 1050s to seek their fortunes in the Mezzogiorno, where Byzantines, Lombards, the Papacy and Muslim Sicily competed for power.

Conquest of southern Italy and Sicily

The Hauteville brothers first served Lombard and Byzantine masters, then turned their arms to conquest in their own right. William Iron Arm became Count of Apulia in 1042; he was succeeded by Drogo and then Humphrey, who defeated Pope Leo IX at the Battle of Civitate in 1053. Robert Guiscard ("the Astute") extended Norman rule over Calabria and Apulia and, with his youngest brother Roger, began the conquest of Muslim Sicily in 1061 — a campaign Roger completed in 1091 as Count of Sicily.

Kingdom of Sicily

Roger's son, Roger II, united the family's Sicilian and mainland possessions and had himself crowned King of Sicily in 1130, creating one of the most prosperous and administratively sophisticated kingdoms in Europe, famous for its Arab-Norman-Byzantine culture and architecture. The male line ended with Roger II's grandson William II (died 1189); the kingdom passed through Constance, daughter of Roger II, to the Hohenstaufen emperor Henry VI.

Other branches

Another Hauteville, Bohemond, son of Robert Guiscard, became a leader of the First Crusade and founded the Principality of Antioch in 1098, which his descendants ruled for much of the 12th century. Family members also held the Principality of Taranto, Capua and Salerno at various times.

Legacy

The Hautevilles transformed a band of landless knights into kings within three generations. Their kingdom of Sicily, with its multicultural court at Palermo, left monuments such as the Palatine Chapel and the cathedrals of Cefalù and Monreale, and a tradition of efficient centralized government that influenced later rulers, including Frederick II.
