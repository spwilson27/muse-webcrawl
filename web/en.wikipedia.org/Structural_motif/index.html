Structural motif

In a chain-like biological molecule, such as a protein or nucleic acid, a structural motif is a common three-dimensional structure that appears in a variety of different, evolutionarily unrelated molecules. A structural motif does not have to be associated with a sequence motif; it can be represented by different and unrelated sequences in different proteins or RNA.

Overview

Because of the enormous complexity of protein structures, motifs provide a useful intermediate level of description between secondary structure elements (alpha helices and beta strands) and whole domains. Motifs are built from combinations of secondary structures arranged in specific geometric patterns, typically connected by loop regions.

Examples in proteins

  • Helix-turn-helix — two alpha helices joined by a short turn; the classic DNA-binding motif of many transcription factors
  • Zinc finger — a small motif stabilised by a zinc ion, found in many DNA- and RNA-binding proteins
  • Greek key — a pattern of beta strands common in immunoglobulin-like folds
  • Beta hairpin — two antiparallel beta strands connected by a short loop
  • EF hand — a helix-loop-helix motif that binds calcium ions
  • Coiled coil — two or more helices wound around each other

Examples in RNA

RNA structures also display recurring motifs, such as the kink-turn, the sarcin–ricin loop, and various tetraloops (for example GNRA loops) that cap helices and mediate tertiary interactions in ribosomes and ribozymes.

Significance

Motifs are frequently associated with particular functions — binding specific ligands, catalysing reactions, or mediating protein–protein interactions — and are used in bioinformatics to annotate newly determined structures and predict function from structure.
