Radical reformers sought a church separated from state authority.

The Radical Reformation was a response among some 16th-century European Christians to what they saw as the corruption of both the Roman Catholic Church and the more moderate "magisterial" Protestant reformations of Martin Luther and Huldrych Zwingli. Radical reformers rejected infant baptism, state churches and often oaths, military service and the doctrine of the Trinity, advocating instead a voluntary church of committed believers separated from secular government.

Strands

Historian George Huntston Williams classified the movement into three broad strands. Anabaptists — including the Swiss Brethren, Hutterites, Mennonites and the short-lived kingdom of Münster — practised believers' baptism and discipleship; the Schleitheim Confession (1527) set out their principles. Spiritualists such as Sebastian Franck emphasised the inner word over outward forms. Rationalist and anti-Trinitarian currents, represented by Michael Servetus and the Polish Brethren, questioned traditional Christology.

Persecution and legacy

Radicals were persecuted by Catholic and mainstream Protestant authorities alike; thousands were executed by drowning, burning or the sword. Survivors, particularly the followers of Menno Simons, formed pacifist communities whose descendants include today's Mennonites, Amish and Hutterites. The Radical Reformation is credited with pioneering ideas of religious liberty, separation of church and state and freedom of conscience.
