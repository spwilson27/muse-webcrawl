Grand tack hypothesis

In planetary science, the grand tack hypothesis proposes that Jupiter formed at a distance of about 3.5 AU from the Sun, then migrated inward to about 1.5 AU, before reversing direction ("tacking", like a sailboat) and migrating outward to its present orbit at 5.2 AU. The reversal is attributed to Saturn migrating inward after Jupiter, catching it in an orbital resonance and altering the torque balance in the surrounding protoplanetary disc. The scenario was proposed in 2011 by Kevin Walsh and collaborators.

What it explains

Simulations of terrestrial planet formation had long produced a Mars far more massive than the real one. In the grand tack model, Jupiter's inward sweep truncated the inner disc of planet-building material at about 1.0 AU, starving the Mars region of material and yielding a small Mars naturally. Jupiter's two passages through the asteroid belt also scattered and mixed asteroids — implanting dry inner-belt asteroids and, on its way out, water-rich outer-belt bodies — matching the belt's observed compositional diversity. The model also allows Earth to accrete water-bearing material, helping explain the origin of Earth's water.

Mechanism

Jupiter, having opened a gap in the gas disc, undergoes Type II migration inward. Saturn, migrating faster, catches up and the two planets lock into a 3:2 mean-motion resonance. The combined gravitational interaction with the disc then reverses the net torque, driving both planets outward until the gas disc dissipates.

Relation to other models

The grand tack is an alternative or complement to the Nice model, which invokes a later instability of the outer planets to explain the Late Heavy Bombardment, and to "pebble accretion" and low-mass-disc scenarios proposed to explain Mars's size without giant-planet migration. It remains an active hypothesis, tested against exoplanet demographics, asteroid composition, and meteorite chronology.
