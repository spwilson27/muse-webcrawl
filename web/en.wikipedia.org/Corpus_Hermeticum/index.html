Corpus Hermeticum

The Corpus Hermeticum is a collection of seventeen Greek philosophical-religious treatises attributed to Hermes Trismegistus, a syncretic figure combining the Greek god Hermes with the Egyptian god Thoth. Composed in Roman Egypt between roughly the 1st and 3rd centuries CE, the texts present dialogues — often between Hermes and his disciples Tat, Asclepius and Ammon — on the nature of God, the cosmos, the soul and the path to spiritual rebirth. Together with related Hermetica such as the Latin Asclepius, they form the core of Hermeticism.

Contents

The most famous treatise is the first, Poimandres, in which Hermes receives a revelation from the divine Mind (Nous) describing the creation of the cosmos out of light and darkness and the fall and ascent of humanity. Other treatises treat the incorporeality of God, the doctrine that "that which is above is like that which is below" (also found in the Emerald Tablet), the regeneration of the soul, and a "sermon on the mount" of Hermetic piety. The texts blend Middle Platonism, Stoicism and Egyptian and Jewish religious influences.

Transmission and Renaissance influence

The treatises were known to Church Fathers such as Lactantius and Augustine, who quoted them with cautious respect. The Greek corpus survived in Byzantium and was brought to Florence around 1460, where Cosimo de' Medici commissioned Marsilio Ficino to translate it into Latin (published 1471) — a task Ficino prioritised above Plato. Renaissance readers believed Hermes an ancient Egyptian sage contemporary with Moses, and the Corpus profoundly influenced Renaissance magic, alchemy and natural philosophy, feeding into the tradition Frances Yates called the "Hermetic tradition".

Dating

In 1614 the classical scholar Isaac Casaubon demonstrated, from linguistic and historical evidence, that the texts were products of the early Christian centuries rather than primordial Egyptian wisdom. Modern scholarship dates them to the intellectual world of Roman Egypt, though debate continues over how far they reflect actual ritual or mystery-cult practice, as suggested by the related Nag Hammadi Hermetic texts and the Greek magical papyri.
