Cryptography

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The Enigma machine, used in World War II

Cryptography, or cryptology (from Ancient Greek kryptós "hidden, secret" and graphein "to write"), is the practice and study of techniques for secure communication in the presence of adversarial behaviour. Modern cryptography draws on mathematics, computer science and physics, and underpins information security: confidentiality, data integrity, authentication and non-repudiation.

History

Classical ciphers included substitution systems such as the Caesar cipher and the polyalphabetic Vigenère cipher; the Arab scholar Al-Kindi described frequency analysis in the 9th century. Rotor machines like the German Enigma dominated the Second World War, and their cryptanalysis at Bletchley Park was a landmark in computing. Claude Shannon's 1949 paper founded the mathematical theory of secrecy systems.

Modern cryptography

  • Symmetric-key algorithms (e.g. AES, ChaCha20) use one shared key for encryption and decryption, usually in block or stream cipher constructions.
  • Public-key cryptography, introduced publicly by Diffie and Hellman (1976) and in the RSA system (1977), uses key pairs and hard mathematical problems such as integer factorisation and discrete logarithms.
  • Cryptographic hash functions (e.g. SHA-2, SHA-3) and message authentication codes protect integrity; digital signatures provide authenticity and non-repudiation.
  • Protocols such as TLS combine these primitives to secure the web, messaging and payments.

Current research addresses the threat of quantum computing through post-quantum cryptography — algorithms based on lattices, codes and hashes believed resistant to quantum attacks.
