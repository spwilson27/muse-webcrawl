Safety film

Archives store safety film separately from dangerously flammable nitrate stock.

Safety film is a film base with low flammability, developed to replace the dangerously combustible cellulose nitrate used in early photographic and motion picture film. The principal safety bases are cellulose acetate (in diacetate, triacetate, and acetate-butyrate forms) and, for modern stock, polyester (polyethylene terephthalate).

History

Early film used a cellulose nitrate base, which burns fiercely, can ignite spontaneously as it decomposes, and caused catastrophic fires in studios, cinemas, and archives. Cellulose diacetate was marketed as a safety base for amateur gauges such as 16 mm from the 1920s, while 35 mm theatrical film remained nitrate until around 1951, when manufacturers completed the switch to acetate "safety film" — stock so marked along its edge printing. Eastman Kodak ceased nitrate production in 1951.

Preservation issues

Safety film introduced its own archival problem: vinegar syndrome, the autocatalytic deacetylation of acetate base, which releases acetic acid, shrinks and embrittles the film, and can spread between reels. Cold, dry storage slows the decay. Polyester base, adopted for many purposes from the 1990s, is far more stable and tear-resistant, though harder to splice and prone to static. Archives therefore segregate three stocks — nitrate, acetate, and polyester — each with distinct storage and handling requirements.
