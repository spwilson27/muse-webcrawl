Plan of Saint Gall

Createdc. 820–830 AD
MediumInk on five sheets of parchment
LocationAbbey library, St. Gallen, Switzerland

The Plan of Saint Gall is a medieval architectural drawing of a monastic compound, dating to the early ninth century (c. 820–830), preserved in the abbey library of St. Gall in Switzerland (Codex Sangallensis 1092). Drawn in red and brown ink on five sewn sheets of calfskin parchment at a scale of roughly 1:192, it is the only major architectural plan to survive from the early Middle Ages and the earliest known drawn plan for a building complex in the West.

Contents

The plan depicts an ideal Benedictine monastery for some 110 monks and 150–200 lay workers and servants: a large cruciform church with two apses, a cloister with chapter house, refectory, dormitory and latrines, an infirmary, a school for externs, guest houses for nobles and for the poor, a scriptorium beside the church, workshops, mills, kilns, stables, gardens (herb, vegetable, and orchard-cemetery), and housing for craftsmen and farm labourers. More than 330 inscriptions name the rooms and their furnishings, down to altars, beds, and latrine seats.

Origin and significance

The drawing was probably made at Reichenau Abbey and sent to Abbot Gozbert of St. Gall, who was rebuilding his abbey church. Scholars debate whether it records a real project or an ideal reform model shaped by the Aachen synods of 816–817; it was never built as drawn. Walter Horn and Ernest Born's monumental three-volume study (1979) established the plan as a central document for Carolingian architecture and monastic history; a full-scale campus based on it, Campus Galli, has been under construction near Meßkirch in Germany since 2013.
