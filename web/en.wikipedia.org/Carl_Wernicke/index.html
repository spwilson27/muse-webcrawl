Carl Wernicke (1848–1905)

Carl Wernicke (15 May 1848 – 15 June 1905) was a German physician, anatomist, psychiatrist and neuropathologist. He is known for his influential research into the pathological effects of specific forms of encephalopathy and the study of receptive aphasia, both of which are commonly associated with Wernicke's name and sometimes termed the Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome.

Biography

Wernicke was born in Tarnowitz, a small town in Upper Silesia, then part of the Kingdom of Prussia. He earned his medical degree at the University of Breslau in 1870 and worked in Breslau's Allerheiligen Hospital as an assistant to the ophthalmologist Richard Förster. After serving as an army surgeon during the Franco-Prussian War, he returned to Breslau and studied under the neuropathologist Heinrich Neumann, who sent him to Vienna for six months to study with the anatomist Theodor Meynert.

In 1875, Wernicke was appointed professor of psychiatry and nervous diseases in Berlin, and in 1890 he obtained a similar position in Breslau before moving to Halle in 1904. He died in 1905 in Gräfenroda, Thuringia, due to complications from injuries suffered in a bicycle accident.

Work

Wernicke is best remembered for his description in 1874 of the region in the posterior superior temporal gyrus of the dominant hemisphere now known as Wernicke's area. Damage to this region produces a fluent but nonsensical aphasia with impaired comprehension, now called Wernicke's aphasia or receptive aphasia. His monograph Der aphasische Symptomenkomplex (1874), published when he was only 26, presented the first comprehensive model of the neural organization of language, predicting a disconnection syndrome — conduction aphasia — that was later confirmed clinically.

He also described the encephalopathy caused by thiamine deficiency that bears his name, characterized by confusion, ataxia and ophthalmoplegia (Wernicke encephalopathy), and his student Sergei Korsakoff described the associated amnestic syndrome. Wernicke's three-volume Lehrbuch der Gehirnkrankheiten (1881–1883) and his Grundriss der Psychiatrie (1894) were landmark textbooks.

Legacy

Along with Paul Broca, Wernicke founded the localizationist approach to the study of language in the brain, and the Wernicke–Geschwind model of language processing grew directly out of his work. The term "Wernicke's aphasia" and the eponymous anatomical area remain in routine clinical use today.
