Albigensian Crusade

The Albigensian Crusade or Cathar Crusade (1209–1229) was a 20-year military campaign initiated by Pope Innocent III to eliminate Catharism in Languedoc, in southern France. The crusade was prosecuted primarily by the French crown and swiftly took on a political aspect, resulting in not only a significant reduction in the number of practicing Cathars but also a realignment of the County of Toulouse in Languedoc, bringing it into the sphere of the French crown and diminishing the distinct regional culture and high level of influence of the counts of Toulouse.

Background

Catharism was a Christian dualist movement that flourished in parts of southern Europe, particularly in the Languedoc region of France, in the 12th and 13th centuries. The Cathars held that the material world was evil, created by a lesser deity, and preached apostolic poverty; they were denounced as heretics by the Catholic Church. After the murder of the papal legate Pierre de Castelnau in January 1208, Pope Innocent III called for a crusade against the heretics and their protectors, offering the same indulgences as for crusades to the Holy Land.

Course of the crusade

The crusade began in 1209 with the sack of Béziers, where, according to tradition, the papal legate Arnaud Amalric ordered "Kill them all, God will know his own" (Caedite eos. Novit enim Dominus qui sunt eius). The crusaders then took Carcassonne. Under Simon de Montfort, who became the crusade's military leader, the campaign proceeded through sieges of Minerve, Termes, and Lavaur, culminating in the decisive Battle of Muret (1213), where de Montfort defeated the forces of Peter II of Aragon and Raymond VI of Toulouse.

After de Montfort's death at the siege of Toulouse (1218), the crusade stalled until King Louis VIII of France intervened in 1226. The war ended with the Treaty of Paris (1229), by which Raymond VII of Toulouse ceded much of his territory to the French crown and accepted the establishment of the Inquisition in Languedoc.

Aftermath

The crusade devastated Languedoc and effectively ended the political independence of the counts of Toulouse. Catharism was gradually eradicated over the following decades through the work of the Inquisition, with the last Cathar stronghold, Montségur, falling in 1244.
