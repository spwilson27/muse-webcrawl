Morula

A morula (Latin: morula, "little mulberry") is an early-stage embryo consisting of a compact ball of cells, typically 16 to 32 cells called blastomeres, enclosed within the zona pellucida. It is produced by cleavage of a fertilized ovum and precedes the blastocyst stage of development.

Development

After fertilization, the zygote undergoes a series of mitotic divisions (cleavage) without overall growth, dividing into progressively smaller blastomeres. Around the 8- to 16-cell stage the blastomeres undergo compaction, maximizing contact with one another and forming a smooth, compact ball. In humans the morula forms roughly three to four days after fertilization as it travels through the fallopian tube toward the uterus.

Cells of the morula begin to differentiate into outer and inner cell populations, setting up the structures of the blastocyst: the trophoblast, which contributes to the placenta, and the inner cell mass (embryoblast), which gives rise to the embryo proper. Fluid then accumulates inside, forming the blastocoel and completing the transition to the blastocyst stage.

Clinical significance

The morula stage is relevant in assisted reproduction: embryos in in-vitro fertilization may be transferred at the cleavage or blastocyst stage, and developmental arrest at the morula stage is one cause of implantation failure.
