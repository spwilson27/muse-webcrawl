Theatre of the Absurd

Theatre of the Absurd
Years active1940s–1960s (principally)
Key figuresSamuel Beckett, Eugène Ionesco, Jean Genet, Harold Pinter, Edward Albee
Term coined byMartin Esslin (1961)

The Theatre of the Absurd is a post–World War II designation for particular plays of absurdist fiction written by a number of primarily European playwrights in the late 1950s, as well as for the style of theatre that has evolved from them. The critic Martin Esslin coined the term in his 1961 essay and book of the same name, relating it to Albert Camus's concept of the absurd — the conflict between the human tendency to seek meaning and the silence of the universe.

Characteristics

Absurdist plays typically abandon conventional plot, character development, and logical dialogue. Action is often circular or static: in Samuel Beckett's Waiting for Godot (1953), two tramps wait endlessly for a figure who never arrives. Language is shown to be unreliable — dialogue degenerates into cliché, contradiction, and nonsense, as in Eugène Ionesco's The Bald Soprano (1950) and Rhinoceros (1959). Settings are frequently bare or surreal, and tragicomedy mixes bleak subject matter with clowning derived from music hall and silent film.

Major works and writers

Core figures include Beckett, Ionesco, Jean Genet (The Balcony, The Maids), Arthur Adamov, and, in Britain, Harold Pinter, whose "comedies of menace" share the absurdist sense of threat and failed communication. In the United States, Edward Albee's The Zoo Story (1959) and Who's Afraid of Virginia Woolf? (1962) were associated with the movement.

Legacy

The Theatre of the Absurd reshaped modern drama, legitimizing non-linear, minimalist, and anti-realist forms and exerting a lasting influence on playwrights from Tom Stoppard to Caryl Churchill.
