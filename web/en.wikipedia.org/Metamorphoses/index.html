Metamorphoses

AuthorOvid
LanguageLatin
Composedc. AD 8
Form15 books, dactylic hexameter

The Metamorphoses (Latin: Metamorphōseōn librī, "Books of Transformations") is a narrative poem by the Roman poet Ovid, completed around AD 8. Comprising 11,995 lines in fifteen books, it chronicles the history of the world from its creation to the deification of Julius Caesar, weaving together more than 250 myths, nearly all involving a transformation of one form into another.

Contents

Ovid begins with the creation of the cosmos from chaos and the ages of mankind, then moves through the flood, the loves of the gods, and the great heroic cycles: Apollo and Daphne, Io, Phaethon, Narcissus and Echo, Arachne, Medea, Daedalus and Icarus, Orpheus and Eurydice, Pygmalion, and the Trojan War, down to Aeneas, Romulus and finally Caesar, whose soul becomes a star. Transformations range from Daphne turned into a laurel tree and Actaeon into a stag, to stones thrown by Deucalion and Pyrrha becoming human beings.

Themes and style

Rather than a unifying epic hero, the poem is held together by the theme of change itself — "My mind is bent to tell of bodies changed into new forms," Ovid announces. The tone shifts constantly between tragedy, comedy, elegy and parody; Ovid treats the gods with wit and irreverence and gives unusual prominence to the voices and suffering of women. Its fluent, polished hexameters made it a model of Latin verse.

Influence

The Metamorphoses is Ovid's masterpiece and one of the most influential works of Western literature. It preserved many Greek and Roman myths in the form in which later ages knew them, supplying subjects to Chaucer, Shakespeare, Bernini and countless painters and composers; without it, much of the mythology retold in the Renaissance would have been lost. Ironically, Ovid claimed he had burned the manuscript when Augustus exiled him to Tomis in AD 8, but copies survived.
