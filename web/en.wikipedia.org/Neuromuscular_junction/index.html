Neuromuscular junction

A motor end plate: the synapse between a motor neuron and a skeletal muscle fibre.

A neuromuscular junction (or myoneural junction) is a chemical synapse between a motor neuron and a muscle fibre. It allows the motor neuron to transmit a signal to the muscle fibre, causing muscle contraction. Muscles require innervation to function — even just to maintain muscle tone — and the neuromuscular junction is the archetypal synapse, historically central to the discovery of chemical neurotransmission by Otto Loewi, Henry Dale, and others.

Structure and function

The presynaptic terminal of the motor neuron contains synaptic vesicles filled with acetylcholine (ACh). When an action potential arrives, voltage-gated calcium channels open, triggering vesicle fusion and release of ACh into the synaptic cleft. ACh binds nicotinic receptors on the folded postsynaptic membrane (the motor end plate), opening cation channels and generating an end-plate potential that triggers a muscle action potential and contraction. Acetylcholinesterase in the cleft rapidly hydrolyses ACh to terminate the signal.

Disorders and toxins

Autoimmune attack on the junction causes myasthenia gravis (antibodies against the ACh receptor or MuSK) and Lambert–Eaton myasthenic syndrome (antibodies against presynaptic calcium channels). Botulinum toxin blocks ACh release by cleaving SNARE proteins, while curare and many neuromuscular-blocking drugs used in anaesthesia antagonize the postsynaptic receptor. Congenital myasthenic syndromes arise from genetic defects in junctional proteins such as rapsyn and the receptor subunits.
