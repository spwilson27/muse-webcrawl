Wolf interval

In music theory, a wolf interval is a severely dissonant interval produced when a tuning system forces one interval far from its pure value in order to close a cycle. The name evokes the howling of a wolf, from the beating and roughness of the sound.

Origin

In Pythagorean tuning, twelve perfect fifths exceed seven octaves by the Pythagorean comma; if eleven fifths are kept pure, the remaining fifth is diminished by that comma and sounds harsh. The problem persisted in meantone temperament, where most fifths are slightly narrowed to purify major thirds: the unused fifth (often G♯–E♭) becomes the "wolf fifth", around 35 cents wider than the others. Composers avoided keys that used it, or keyboards provided split keys.

Later use

The adoption of well temperaments and eventually equal temperament distributed the comma so that no wolf interval remained. "Wolf" is also used for harsh beating in other contexts, such as the wolf tone of bowed string instruments.
