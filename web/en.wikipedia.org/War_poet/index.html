War poet

A war poet is a poet who writes about war, whether as a participant, an observer, or in reflection afterward. The term is most strongly associated with the soldier-poets of the First World War, whose work recorded the experience of trench warfare and reshaped public understanding of the conflict.

First World War poets

British poets of the Great War included Rupert Brooke, whose early sonnets expressed patriotic idealism; Wilfred Owen, whose poems of gas attacks and shell shock ("Dulce et Decorum Est," "Anthem for Doomed Youth") denounced the "old Lie" of glorious death; Siegfried Sassoon, who protested the war's continuation in a public declaration of 1917; Isaac Rosenberg; Edward Thomas; Robert Graves; and Edmund Blunden. Owen was killed one week before the Armistice. Their poetry moved from early romantic enthusiasm toward bitter realism and remains central to British remembrance culture.

Earlier and later war poetry

War poetry stretches from Homer and the Anglo-Saxon Battle of Maldon through Walt Whitman's Civil War verse to the poets of the Spanish Civil War and the Second World War — including Keith Douglas, Randall Jarrell, and the Soviet and Japanese poets of their respective fronts. Conflicts in Vietnam, Iraq, and Afghanistan generated new bodies of soldier poetry.
