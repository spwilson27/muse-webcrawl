The medulla oblongata or simply medulla is a long, stem-like structure that forms the lowest part of the brainstem and connects the brain to the spinal cord. It contains the cardiac, respiratory, vomiting and vasomotor centres and therefore handles essential autonomic, involuntary functions such as breathing, heart rate and blood pressure.

Anatomy

The medulla lies between the pons above and the spinal cord below, at the level of the foramen magnum. On its front surface are the paired pyramids, carrying the corticospinal (motor) tracts, most of whose fibres cross to the opposite side at the decussation of the pyramids — which is why each side of the brain controls the opposite side of the body. Behind the pyramids sit the olives, involved in motor learning and sound localisation. Internally, the medulla contains nuclei of the last four cranial nerves and relay nuclei (gracile and cuneate) for fine touch and proprioception.

Functions

Medullary networks generate the rhythm of breathing, regulate heart rate and vessel tone in response to blood chemistry and pressure, and coordinate protective reflexes: swallowing, gagging, coughing, sneezing and vomiting.

Clinical significance

Because so many vital functions are concentrated in a small area, medullary injury — from stroke (such as lateral medullary syndrome), trauma or compression by a Chiari malformation — can be fatal or severely disabling. Opioid drugs depress the medullary respiratory centre, which is the usual cause of death in overdose.
