Jan Žižka z Trocnova a Kalicha (Czech: [jan ˈʒɪʃka]; c. 1360 – 11 October 1424) was a Czech general, a leader of the Hussite movement and a follower of Jan Hus, and is considered a Czech national hero. He was a brilliant military tactician who, despite being blind in both eyes by the end of his career, was never defeated in battle.

Early life

Žižka was born into a family of lesser nobility at Trocnov in southern Bohemia. As a youth at the court of King Wenceslaus IV he lost one eye, earning him the nickname "Žižka" (the one-eyed). He fought as a mercenary in Poland and is traditionally said to have fought at the Battle of Grunwald (1410) against the Teutonic Knights. Returning to Bohemia, he became captain of the royal bodyguard in Prague, and was radicalized by the reform preaching associated with Hus, who had been executed in 1415.

Hussite Wars

Žižka took a leading part in the Defenestration of Prague (1419) that opened the Hussite Wars. He organized the radical community of Tábor and its army of peasants and townsmen, pioneering new tactics: the vozová hradba (war-wagon fort), in which chained wagons formed a mobile fortress, armed with crossbows, handguns and small cannon — making innovative use of gunpowder weapons. His victories at Sudoměř (1420) and Vítkov Hill (1420), where he saved Prague from the crusading army of Emperor Sigismund, and at Kutná Hora and Německý Brod (1422) established his reputation. In 1421 he lost his second eye at the siege of Rábí castle and thereafter led his armies completely blind, relying on his officers and his knowledge of terrain.

Taborite leader and death

Žižka defended the radical Taborite programme — the Four Articles of Prague — against both Catholic crusaders and more moderate Hussites, and by the end of his life was effectively ruler of much of Bohemia. He died of plague while campaigning near Přibyslav in October 1424. According to legend, he asked that his skin be used to make a drum to lead his soldiers into battle after his death.

Legacy

Žižka is commemorated by one of the world's largest equestrian statues, unveiled in 1950 on Vítkov Hill in Prague, below which stands the National Memorial. The Prague district of Žižkov bears his name, and he remains a symbol of Czech resistance and military ingenuity.
