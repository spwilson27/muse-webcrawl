Complete information

In a game of complete information, all players know the payoff structures

In economics and game theory, complete information is an informational assumption about the structure of a game. A game has complete information if all players know the strategies and payoffs available to the other players — though they need not know the actions those players have taken.

Distinction from perfect information

Complete information is often confused with perfect information, which additionally requires that players know all moves that have already occurred. Chess is a game of both complete and perfect information: the rules and payoffs are known, and the full history of moves is visible. Poker, by contrast, has complete information in its basic structure (players know the rules and possible payoffs) but imperfect information, because opponents' cards are hidden. A game in which a player does not know another's payoff function — for example, not knowing whether a rival firm has high or low costs — is a game of incomplete information.

Incomplete information and Bayesian games

John Harsanyi developed the theory of Bayesian games to analyze incomplete information, modeling players as having "types" drawn from a known probability distribution. The Harsanyi transformation converts a game of incomplete information into one of imperfect information that can be analyzed with standard tools.

Applications

Auctions, bargaining, signaling, and screening models generally assume incomplete information, since private knowledge of valuations and costs is central to real markets. By contrast, classical oligopoly models such as Cournot competition are usually formulated under complete information.
