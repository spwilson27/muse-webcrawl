Gregor Johann Mendel (20 July 1822 – 6 January 1884) was an Austrian biologist, meteorologist, mathematician, Augustinian friar and abbot of St. Thomas' Abbey in Brno (Brünn), Margraviate of Moravia. Through careful experiments with pea plants, he discovered the basic principles of heredity, and is recognised as the founder of the modern science of genetics.

Life

Mendel was born into a German-speaking farming family in Silesia and studied philosophy and physics at the University of Olomouc. Against his father's wishes he entered the Augustinian monastery in Brno in 1843, taking the name Gregor. After ordination he taught at a secondary school and studied at the University of Vienna (1851–53) under teachers including Christian Doppler and Franz Unger. In 1868 he became abbot of the monastery, and his scientific work largely ceased under administrative burdens.

Pea plant experiments

Between 1856 and 1863 Mendel cultivated and tested some 28,000 pea plants (Pisum sativum), tracking seven contrasting traits such as flower colour, seed shape and plant height. He found that traits were passed on in discrete units — later called genes — according to consistent statistical patterns: dominant and recessive characters, segregation of factors in offspring, and their independent assortment. He presented the results in his paper "Experiments on Plant Hybridization" (1866).

Neglect and rediscovery

Mendel's work was largely ignored during his lifetime, in part because heredity was then usually framed in terms of blending inheritance and because his mathematical treatment was unfamiliar to biologists. In 1900 the botanists Hugo de Vries, Carl Correns and Erich von Tschermak independently arrived at similar results and recognised Mendel's priority; William Bateson championed the work in English and coined the term "genetics". The resulting principles are known as Mendelian inheritance.
