The left ventricle is one of the four chambers of the heart, located in the lower left portion of the organ. It receives oxygenated blood from the left atrium through the mitral (bicuspid) valve and pumps it out through the aortic valve into the aorta, and thereby to the whole body except the lungs. Because it must drive the high-pressure systemic circulation, it is the strongest and most muscular chamber of the heart.

Structure

The left ventricle is longer and more conical than the right ventricle, and its wall of myocardium is roughly three times thicker — typically 8 to 15 mm — reflecting the pressure load it bears. Inside, chordae tendineae anchor the mitral valve leaflets to two papillary muscles, preventing the valve from prolapsing during contraction. In cross-section the cavity is roughly circular, whereas the right ventricle wraps around it in a crescent shape.

Function

During ventricular systole the left ventricle contracts, raising pressure until the aortic valve opens and blood is ejected; the volume expelled per beat is the stroke volume. The proportion of the end-diastolic volume ejected — the ejection fraction, normally about 55–70% — is a key clinical measure of pump function, assessed by echocardiography and other imaging.

Clinical significance

Chronic pressure overload from hypertension or aortic stenosis causes left ventricular hypertrophy; volume overload or damage from myocardial infarction can lead to dilation and systolic dysfunction, central features of heart failure. Diseases of the chamber and its outflow, including hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, carry risks of arrhythmia and sudden death.
