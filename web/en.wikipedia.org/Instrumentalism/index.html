Instrumentalism

In the philosophy of science, instrumentalism is the view that scientific theories are useful instruments for predicting observable phenomena rather than literal descriptions of an unobservable reality. On this view, the entities postulated by a theory — such as atoms or electrons — need not be taken as truly existing; a theory is judged by its empirical success, not by its truth.

History

Instrumentalist themes appear in the work of Pierre Duhem and Ernst Mach, and in the pragmatism of John Dewey, who used "instrumentalism" for his own philosophy of inquiry. In the twentieth century, instrumentalism was contrasted with scientific realism, especially in debates over the interpretation of quantum mechanics and the status of theoretical entities. Karl Popper criticized instrumentalism, arguing that science aims at true explanatory theories.

Related views

Instrumentalism is related to constructive empiricism (Bas van Fraassen), which holds that science aims at empirical adequacy and that belief should extend only to a theory's observable consequences, and to logical empiricist attempts to reduce theoretical terms to observational language.
