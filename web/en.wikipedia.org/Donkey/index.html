The donkey (Equus africanus asinus) is a domesticated member of the horse family, Equidae, descended from the African wild ass. It has been used as a working animal for at least 5,000 years, and there are more than 40 million donkeys in the world, mostly in developing countries, where they are used principally as draught or pack animals. A male donkey is called a jack, a female a jenny, and a young donkey a foal. A mule is the offspring of a male donkey and a female horse.

Characteristics

Donkeys vary considerably in size, from about 90 cm to over 160 cm at the withers, and are characterised by their long ears, short upright mane, and a tufted tail resembling that of a cow. Most are grey, brown, or black, often with a pale muzzle and belly and a dark dorsal stripe. They are well adapted to marginal, arid land, can tolerate water loss of up to 30% of body mass, and are famed for their sure-footedness, intelligence, and a reputation for stubbornness that more likely reflects a strong sense of self-preservation.

History and uses

Donkeys were domesticated in Africa, probably in Egypt or Mesopotamia, around 4000 BC, and spread throughout Europe and Asia. They carried goods along trade routes such as the Silk Road, powered mills and irrigation wheels, and were indispensable in mining, agriculture, and warfare. In developed countries their role has shifted largely to companionship, leisure riding, and guarding livestock.

The donkey appears throughout culture and religion — in the Bible, in Aesop's fables, in the tale of the ass's ears of King Midas, and as the symbol of the United States Democratic Party. Working donkeys remain of great economic importance in Africa, Asia, and Latin America, although demand for donkey hides for the gelatin ejiao has raised serious welfare and population concerns.
