Mallard

The mallard (Anas platyrhynchos) is a dabbling duck that breeds throughout the temperate and subtropical Americas, Eurasia, and North Africa. It has been introduced to New Zealand, Australia, Peru, Brazil, Uruguay, Argentina, Chile, Colombia, the Falkland Islands, and South Africa. This duck belongs to the subfamily Anatinae of the waterfowl family Anatidae. Males (drakes) have green heads, while the females (hens or ducks) have mainly brown-speckled plumage. Both sexes have an area of white-bordered black or iridescent purple-blue feathers called a speculum on their wings; males especially tend to have blue speculum feathers.

Description

The mallard is a medium-sized waterfowl species that is somewhat heavier than most other dabbling ducks. It is 50–65 cm (20–26 in) long, of which the body makes up around two-thirds the length. The wingspan is 81–98 cm (32–39 in), and the bill is 4.4 to 6.1 cm (1.7 to 2.4 in) long. The breeding drake has a glossy green head separated from a dark brown chest by a white collar, a grey body, and a black rear end with upcurled tail feathers. The female is mottled brown overall. After breeding, males enter an "eclipse" plumage resembling that of females.

Behaviour and ecology

Mallards live in wetlands, eat water plants and small animals, and are social animals preferring to congregate in groups or flocks of varying sizes. They feed by dabbling — tipping up in shallow water to reach submerged vegetation — and by grazing on land. Northern populations migrate south in winter. The species is largely monogamous within a breeding season; females lay 8 to 13 creamy white eggs, incubate them for 27 to 28 days, and lead the precocial ducklings to water within a day of hatching.

Relationship with humans

The mallard is the ancestor of most breeds of domestic duck. It is among the most abundant of all waterfowl, with a global population estimated in the tens of millions, and is hunted widely as game. Releases of farm-bred mallards and hybridisation with related species such as the American black duck and the Hawaiian duck are conservation concerns.
