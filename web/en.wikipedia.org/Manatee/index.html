Manatee

Kingdom: Animalia
Order: Sirenia
Family: Trichechidae
Genus: Trichechus

Manatees are large, fully aquatic, mostly herbivorous marine mammals of the genus Trichechus, sometimes known as sea cows. They are the only living members of the family Trichechidae and, with the dugong, make up the order Sirenia. Three species are recognised: the West Indian manatee (T. manatus), the Amazonian manatee (T. inunguis) and the West African manatee (T. senegalensis).

Description and habitat

Manatees measure up to about 4 m and can weigh 500 kg or more. They have paddle-like flippers, a rounded, spatulate tail (distinguishing them from dugongs), and sparse hair and stiff whiskers (vibrissae) on the body and face. They inhabit shallow, warm coastal waters, rivers and estuaries of the Caribbean, the Gulf of Mexico, the Amazon Basin and West Africa, grazing on seagrasses and freshwater vegetation.

Conservation

All three species are classified as vulnerable. Threats include collisions with boats — a leading cause of death for Florida manatees — habitat loss, entanglement, and cold stress. Manatees have few natural predators and a low reproductive rate, making populations slow to recover; they are protected by law in many countries, and Florida warm-water refuges such as Crystal River are important wintering sites.
