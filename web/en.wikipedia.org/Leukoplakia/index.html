Leukoplakia

Leukoplakia is a firmly attached white patch on a mucous membrane which is associated with an increased risk of cancer. The term is derived from the Greek words leukos ("white") and plax ("plate").

Definition and causes

The World Health Organization defines oral leukoplakia as a predominantly white lesion of the oral mucosa that cannot be characterised as any other definable lesion. It is most often linked to chronic irritation, above all tobacco smoking or chewing, betel quid use, and alcohol. Most lesions are harmless, but a minority show epithelial dysplasia or transform into squamous cell carcinoma, so persistent patches are usually biopsied.

Types

Homogeneous leukoplakia is uniformly white and flat; non-homogeneous forms are mixed red-and-white (erythroleukoplakia), nodular or verrucous and carry a higher risk of malignancy. Proliferative verrucous leukoplakia is a rare, aggressive variant. Oral hairy leukoplakia, seen on the sides of the tongue in immunocompromised people, is caused by Epstein–Barr virus and is not precancerous.

Management

Management begins with removing irritants and stopping tobacco use, which may cause lesions to regress. Lesions may be excised surgically or treated with laser ablation or cryotherapy, and long-term follow-up is recommended because of recurrence and malignant transformation risk.
