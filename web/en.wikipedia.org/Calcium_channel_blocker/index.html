Calcium channel blocker

Calcium channel blockers (CCBs), also called calcium antagonists or calcium blockers, are a class of medications that disrupt the movement of calcium (Ca2+) through L-type calcium channels. They are widely used to treat hypertension (high blood pressure), angina, and some arrhythmias.

Classes

The class is divided into two main groups. Dihydropyridines, such as amlodipine, nifedipine, and felodipine, act mainly on vascular smooth muscle, causing vasodilation and lowering blood pressure. Non-dihydropyridines, such as verapamil (a phenylalkylamine) and diltiazem (a benzothiazepine), act more on the heart, slowing heart rate and conduction through the atrioventricular node, and are used for rate control in atrial fibrillation and for angina.

Mechanism of action

Blocking calcium entry into cardiac and smooth muscle cells reduces contractility and vascular tone. In blood vessels this lowers peripheral resistance; in the heart the non-dihydropyridines reduce the force of contraction and the speed of electrical conduction.

Side effects

Common side effects include ankle swelling (especially with dihydropyridines), headache, flushing, dizziness, and constipation (particularly with verapamil). CCBs should be used cautiously in heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (non-dihydropyridines) and not combined with beta blockers intravenously. Overdose can cause severe hypotension and bradycardia.
