Diatreme

A diatreme is a volcanic pipe or vent formed by a gas explosion when rising magma encounters groundwater and flashes it to steam, blasting a funnel- or carrot-shaped conduit through the host rock. Diatremes typically extend from a surface crater — often a broad, low-rimmed depression called a maar, surrounded by a tuff ring — down to depths of one or two kilometres, narrowing with depth into a root zone of dykes. The conduit is filled with breccia composed of shattered country-rock fragments mixed with juvenile volcanic material.

Formation

Diatremes form in phreatomagmatic eruptions: as magma ascends and interacts violently with water-saturated rock or an aquifer, the resulting explosions fragment both magma and wall rock. Each explosion deepens and widens the vent, and the ejected debris partly falls back into the pipe, producing the characteristic coarse breccia fill. Because the explosions occur below the surface, the pipe cuts across pre-existing strata, and blocks of rock from deep levels may be carried up and preserved in the fill — including, in the case of kimberlite diatremes, fragments of mantle peridotite and the diamonds they contain.

Kimberlite diatremes

The most economically important diatremes are the kimberlite pipes of southern Africa, Russia, Canada and elsewhere, which are the world's primary source of gem diamonds. Classically modelled as a "carrot": a crater facies at the top, a steep-sided diatreme (pipe) facies in the middle, and a hypabyssal root zone of coherent kimberlite dykes and sills at depth. Famous examples include the Big Hole at Kimberley, South Africa, and the pipes of the Lac de Gras field in Canada's Northwest Territories. Lamproite diatremes (such as Australia's Argyle pipe) follow a somewhat different, champagne-glass-shaped geometry.

Occurrence

Diatreme–maar complexes occur in many volcanic fields, including the Eifel district of Germany (the type region for maars), the Hopi Buttes of Arizona, and fields in New Mexico, France's Auvergne, and the West African and Siberian cratons. Erosion can strip away the surface crater and expose the pipe at deeper levels, making ancient diatremes an important record of subsurface volcanic processes.
