Ellesmere Island

Ellesmere Island (Inuktitut: Umingmak Nuna, "land of muskoxen") is Canada's northernmost island and the tenth largest island in the world, with an area of about 196,235 km². It lies in the Qikiqtaaluk Region of Nunavut, separated from Greenland by the Nares Strait. Its northern cape, Cape Columbia, is the northernmost point of land in Canada.

Geography

More than one fifth of the island is protected as Quttinirpaaq National Park. The landscape is dominated by the Arctic Cordillera: the United States Range in the northeast contains Mount Barbeau (2,616 m), the highest point in Nunavut. Large ice caps and ice shelves fringe the northern coast, and long fjords cut into the mountainous interior. Lake Hazen, one of the largest lakes by volume in the world north of the Arctic Circle, lies in the northeast.

History

Paleo-Eskimo peoples inhabited the island from about 2000 BCE, followed by Dorset and Thule cultures; the Norse also visited. The island was sighted by William Baffin in 1616 and named in 1852 for Francis Egerton, 1st Earl of Ellesmere. It served as a base for North Pole expeditions, including Robert Peary's 1909 journey, and figured in the sovereignty concerns behind the 1953 High Arctic relocation of Inuit families to Grise Fiord and Resolute.

Population and environment

The island has only a few hundred residents, in Grise Fiord, plus the military station Alert — the northernmost permanently inhabited place on Earth — and the research station at Eureka. Wildlife includes muskoxen, Peary caribou, Arctic wolves and polar bears. Fossil forests on the island record warm Eocene climates, and the early tetrapod Tiktaalik was discovered in its Devonian rocks in 2004.
