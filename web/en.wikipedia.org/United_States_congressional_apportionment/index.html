United States congressional apportionment

United States congressional apportionment is the process by which seats in the United States House of Representatives are distributed among the 50 states according to population, following each decennial census. Apportionment also determines each state's number of electors in the Electoral College.

Apportionment
BasisDecennial census (Article I, U.S. Constitution)
Total seats435 (fixed since 1929, except 1959–1963)
MethodMethod of equal proportions (Huntington–Hill)
MinimumOne seat per state

Process

Within a year of the census, the Census Bureau delivers state population counts, and the seats are assigned by the method of equal proportions, used since 1941. States then redraw congressional district boundaries (redistricting) for the new allocation.

History

The Constitution originally set the House size proportionally, and Congress expanded it after each census until the Permanent Apportionment Act of 1929 froze membership at 435. Apportionment controversies — including failed reapportionments in the 1920s and the "one person, one vote" cases of the 1960s — have repeatedly reshaped American politics.

Effects

Population shifts between regions mean states in the South and West have generally gained seats in recent cycles at the expense of the Northeast and Midwest; after the 2020 census, for example, Texas gained two seats while seven states each lost one.
