The Quran (Arabic: ٱلْقُرْآن, al-Qurʾān, "the Recitation") is the central religious text of Islam, believed by Muslims to be the literal word of God (Allah) revealed to the prophet Muhammad over a period of about 23 years, beginning in 610 CE. It is regarded as the finest work of classical Arabic literature and the primary source of Islamic theology, law and ethics.

Revelation and compilation

According to Islamic tradition, Muhammad received the first revelation in a cave on Mount Hira near Mecca, when the angel Gabriel commanded him to "Recite". Revelations continued in Mecca and, after the Hijrah in 622 CE, in Medina. The verses were memorised by companions and written on available materials. After Muhammad's death in 632, the first caliph Abu Bakr commissioned a collection; under the third caliph, Uthman, a standard consonantal text was produced around 650 CE and variant codices were ordered destroyed.

Structure

The Quran contains 114 chapters (sūrahs), ordered roughly from longest to shortest rather than chronologically, comprising about 6,236 verses (āyāt). The opening chapter, Al-Fatiha ("The Opening"), is recited in daily prayers. Chapters are conventionally divided into Meccan and Medinan, depending on where they were revealed. The text is also divided into 30 roughly equal parts (ajzāʾ) for recitation over a month, especially during Ramadan.

Contents and themes

Central themes include the absolute oneness of God (tawhid), prophecy, moral conduct, charity, the Day of Judgment, and accounts of earlier prophets such as Adam, Noah, Abraham, Moses and Jesus. The Quran presents itself as confirming earlier scriptures — the Torah, the Psalms and the Gospel — while correcting what it describes as their corruption. It contains legal material on worship, family, inheritance and commerce, though detailed law is largely elaborated in the Hadith and jurisprudence (fiqh).

Recitation and memorisation

Recitation governed by the rules of tajwīd is a devotional art; one who memorises the entire Quran is honoured with the title ḥāfiẓ. Canonical readings (qirāʾāt), transmitted through recognised chains, differ slightly in vocalisation and occasionally in wording. Early manuscripts, such as the Birmingham folios radiocarbon-dated to the mid-7th century, show the consonantal text stabilised remarkably early.
