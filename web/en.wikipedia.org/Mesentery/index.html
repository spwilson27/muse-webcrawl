Mesentery

The mesentery is an organ that attaches the intestines to the posterior abdominal wall in humans. It is formed by the double fold of peritoneum that encloses the intestines and their associated blood vessels, nerves, and lymphatics, allowing the intestines to be held in place while permitting movement.

Structure

The mesentery is a continuous structure arising from the posterior abdominal wall and fanning out to enclose the jejunum and ileum. It includes the mesocolon, which attaches the colon, and the mesorectum. Its root runs diagonally from the duodenojejunal flexure to the right sacroiliac joint.

Recognition as an organ

Historically, the mesentery was described by Leonardo da Vinci and later treated as a fragmented set of separate structures. In 2016, researchers led by J. Calvin Coffey at the University of Limerick argued that the mesentery is a single continuous organ, a view subsequently reflected in editions of Gray's Anatomy.

Clinical significance

Diseases of the mesentery include mesenteric ischemia, volvulus, and mesenteric lymphadenitis. Understanding the mesentery as a continuous organ has implications for colorectal and abdominal surgery.
