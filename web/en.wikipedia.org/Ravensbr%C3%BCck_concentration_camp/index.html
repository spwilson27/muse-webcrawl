Ravensbrück (pronounced [ʁaːvənsˈbʁʏk]) was a Nazi concentration camp built exclusively for women, located about 90 km north of Berlin near the village of Ravensbrück in Brandenburg. Opened in May 1939, it was the largest women's concentration camp in the German Reich; more than 120,000 women from over twenty countries were registered there during the war, and tens of thousands were murdered or died from starvation, disease, overwork, and medical experiments.

History

The camp was built by prisoners from Sachsenhausen. The first inmates were German women — political prisoners, Jehovah's Witnesses, and women classified as "asocial." As the war expanded, women arrived from across occupied Europe, including Polish, Soviet, French, Dutch, and Jewish prisoners. From 1942 the camp also trained female SS guards (Aufseherinnen), more than 3,500 of whom served at Ravensbrück and its subcamps.

Conditions and experiments

Prisoners worked in camp workshops, for Siemens electrical factories on site, and in dozens of subcamps. SS doctors conducted experiments, most notoriously the sulfonamide experiments on Polish political prisoners, in which wounds were deliberately infected. In early 1945 the SS murdered thousands of prisoners in a gas chamber installed near the crematorium.

Liberation and memory

As the Red Army approached, the SS forced some 20,000 women on death marches; Soviet troops liberated the remaining roughly 3,000 sick prisoners on 30 April 1945. The Ravensbrück Memorial (Mahngedenkstätte Ravensbrück) opened in 1959 and remains a major site of remembrance.
