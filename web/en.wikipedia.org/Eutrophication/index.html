Eutrophication

Eutrophication
Key nutrientsNitrogen and phosphorus
Main sourcesAgricultural fertiliser runoff, sewage, detergents
ConsequencesAlgal blooms, hypoxia ("dead zones"), fish kills, loss of biodiversity
Famous examplesGulf of Mexico dead zone; Lake Erie; Baltic Sea

Eutrophication is a general term describing a process in which nutrients accumulate in a body of water, resulting in an increased growth of microorganisms that may deplete the water of oxygen. It occurs naturally over long timescales as lakes age, but human activities — especially fertiliser runoff from farms and discharge of sewage — have greatly accelerated it ("cultural eutrophication").

Mechanism

Excess nitrogen and phosphorus trigger blooms of algae and cyanobacteria. When the bloom dies, its decomposition by bacteria consumes dissolved oxygen, producing hypoxic or anoxic conditions that suffocate fish and other animals. Some blooms also produce toxins harmful to wildlife and humans. In lakes, phosphorus is typically the limiting nutrient; in coastal waters, nitrogen usually is.

Effects and control

Eutrophication degrades drinking-water sources, fisheries and recreation, and creates large hypoxic "dead zones" such as the one at the mouth of the Mississippi River. Control measures include reducing fertiliser use, buffer strips along waterways, improved sewage treatment (notably phosphate removal, as pioneered after the decline and recovery of Lake Erie), and wetland restoration.
