Heterochrony

Changes in developmental timing can produce strikingly different adult forms

Heterochrony is a developmental change in the timing or rate of events, leading to changes in size and shape of an organism compared with its ancestors. It is a central concept in evolutionary developmental biology (evo-devo) and was coined by Ernst Haeckel in the 1870s; its modern formulation owes much to Gavin de Beer and Stephen Jay Gould's Ontogeny and Phylogeny (1977).

Categories

Heterochronic changes fall into two broad families. Paedomorphosis results in adults retaining juvenile features of ancestors, through neoteny (slowed somatic development), progenesis (early end to development) or postdisplacement (delayed onset). Peramorphosis produces adults that "overshoot" the ancestral adult form, through acceleration, hypermorphosis (delayed offset) or predisplacement (earlier onset).

Examples

The classic example is the axolotl, a salamander that retains larval gills and an aquatic lifestyle while becoming sexually mature — a case of neoteny. Paedomorphic features have also been proposed for human evolution, including our relatively flat faces and large brains compared with other apes. In plants, heterochrony affects flowering time and leaf shape; in domestic dogs, paedomorphosis accounts for many breed traits that resemble wolf pups.

Mechanisms

Heterochrony can arise from mutations in regulatory genes controlling development, including the heterochronic genes first identified in the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans (such as lin-4 and let-7, the latter the first microRNA discovered). Changes in endocrine signalling, such as thyroid hormone control of amphibian metamorphosis, provide another route.
