Louis Henry Sullivan (September 3, 1856 – April 14, 1924) was an American architect, called the "father of the skyscraper" and a founder of modernist architecture. He was a mentor to Frank Lloyd Wright, who called him his Lieber Meister ("beloved master"). Sullivan is credited with coining the maxim "form follows function," later adopted as a central principle of modernist design.

Early life and education

Sullivan was born in Boston to an Irish father and a Swiss-born mother. He studied architecture briefly at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology — among its first students in that subject — and then worked in Philadelphia for Frank Furness and in Chicago for William Le Baron Jenney. In 1874 he went to Paris to study at the École des Beaux-Arts, returning to Chicago the following year.

Adler and Sullivan

In 1879 Sullivan joined the firm of Dankmar Adler, becoming a full partner in 1883. Adler & Sullivan became one of the most successful practices in the rapidly rebuilding Chicago, where the demand for tall office buildings after the Great Fire of 1871 encouraged steel-frame construction. Their Auditorium Building (1889) combined a hotel, offices and a superbly engineered theatre. The Wainwright Building in St. Louis (1891) is regarded as a masterly expression of the tall office building, and the Guaranty Building in Buffalo (1896) shows Sullivan's distinctive terra-cotta ornament at its richest.

Design philosophy

In his 1896 essay "The Tall Office Building Artistically Considered," Sullivan argued that a building's form should express its function: "form ever follows function. This is the law." His architecture combined clear vertical expression of structure with dense, organic ornament inspired by natural forms, often executed by master craftsmen such as George Elmslie. The Carson, Pirie, Scott Building in Chicago (1899–1904) is a celebrated example.

Later years and legacy

After the partnership dissolved in 1895, Sullivan's practice declined; a series of small banks in Midwestern towns — the "jewel boxes," including the National Farmers' Bank in Owatonna, Minnesota (1908) — are late masterpieces. He published Kindergarten Chats (1901–02) and The Autobiography of an Idea (1924). He died in Chicago in 1924 in straitened circumstances, but his writings and buildings profoundly shaped the Prairie School and modern architecture generally.
