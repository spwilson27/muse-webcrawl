National Workshops

The National Workshops (French: Ateliers nationaux) were a programme of public works relief established in France by the Provisional Government of the Second Republic in February 1848, following the French Revolution of 1848. They were intended to provide paid employment to unemployed Parisian workers and to give practical effect to the proclaimed "right to work".

Operation

Administered by the minister of public works, Marie, under the engineer Émile Thomas, the workshops enrolled workers — initially tens of thousands, rising to over 100,000 by June 1848 — on road-mending, earthworks and planting projects, paying a daily wage of two francs for workdays and one franc for idle days. The scheme differed from Louis Blanc's proposed "social workshops" (producer co-operatives); the National Workshops were essentially a relief works scheme, and critics treated them as an expensive dole.

Closure and the June Days

The conservative majority elected to the Constituent Assembly in April 1848 regarded the workshops as a costly incubator of radicalism. On 21 June 1848 the government decreed their dissolution: younger workers were to enlist in the army and others were sent to provincial earthworks. The closure provoked the June Days uprising (23–26 June), a bloody insurrection of Parisian workers suppressed by General Cavaignac, in which thousands were killed and many more deported — an episode that shattered the alliance between the republican bourgeoisie and the working class and paved the way for the election of Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte.
