Hellenism (religion)

Hellenism (Greek: Ἑλληνισμός), also known as Hellenic ethnic religion or Hellenic polytheism, is the contemporary reconstruction and revival of the traditional religion of ancient Greece — the worship of the Olympian gods and other deities of the Greek pantheon, together with associated practices such as sacrifice, festivals, household worship and mystery traditions. Adherents, often called Hellenes or Hellenic polytheists, regard the revival as the indigenous ethnic religion of the Greek people. It is part of the wider modern Pagan (neopagan) movement.

Beliefs and practices

Hellenic polytheists venerate the gods of ancient Greece — Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Athena, Apollo, Artemis, Aphrodite, Ares, Hephaestus, Demeter, Hermes and Dionysus or Hestia among the Twelve — along with heroes, daimones and household gods. Practice centres on reciprocity (charis) expressed through offerings, libations, hymns and prayers at home altars and, where possible, temples. Many follow a reconstructed festival calendar drawn from the Attic calendar, celebrating occasions such as the Noumenia (new moon), Deipnon, and city festivals like the Panathenaea and Dionysia. Ethical frameworks draw on maxims from Delphi — "know thyself" and "nothing in excess" — and virtues such as xenia (hospitality) and eusebeia (piety).

History of the revival

Worship of the Greek gods survived in pockets into late antiquity and was suppressed under Christian Roman emperors; the last academies closed under Justinian in 529 AD. Interest revived through Renaissance humanism and Romantic Hellenism, but organised religious practice is a late-20th-century development. In Greece the Supreme Council of Ethnic Hellenes (YSEE), founded in 1997, coordinates groups and public rituals, including the Prometheus festival held near Mount Olympus since the 1990s and ceremonies at ancient temples. In 2017 the Greek state granted the Hellenic religion recognition as a "known religion", allowing legal worship spaces and marriages. Estimates of adherents in Greece range from about 2,000 committed practitioners to far larger numbers of sympathisers.

Varieties and debates

The movement spans reconstructionists, who aim to rebuild ancient practice from scholarship, and more eclectic practitioners who blend Greek devotion with other Pagan traditions. International groups include Elaion and Hellenion in North America. Debates within the community concern the balance between historical fidelity and modern adaptation, the relationship to Greek national identity, and the rejection by most groups of far-right appropriations of Greek heritage.
