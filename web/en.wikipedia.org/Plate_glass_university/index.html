Plate glass university

The term plate glass university refers to a group of universities in the United Kingdom founded in the 1960s, following the expansion of higher education recommended by the Robbins Report of 1963. The name, coined by Michael Beloff in his 1968 book The Plateglass Universities, reflects the modern architectural style of the new campuses, built extensively with flat sheets of glass, in contrast to the older red brick and ancient universities.

The universities

The institutions usually grouped under the term are the universities of East Anglia, Essex, Kent, Lancaster, Sussex, Warwick, and York. Most were founded on greenfield campus sites outside existing cities, designed as self-contained academic communities with distinctive modernist architecture — Sussex by Basil Spence and York with its collegiate system and central lake are noted examples.

Character

The plate glass universities introduced new interdisciplinary structures, innovative curricula, and modular degree schemes influenced partly by American models. Several, notably Warwick, grew rapidly into major research institutions, and the group is sometimes contrasted with the "redbrick" civic universities founded in the Victorian and Edwardian eras.
