Nib (pen)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A nib is the part of a quill, dip pen, fountain pen, or stylus that comes into contact with the writing surface in order to deposit ink. Different types of nibs vary in their purpose, shape and size, as well as the material from which they are made.

History

Broad nibs were cut from reeds and quills for millennia. Metal nibs appeared in ancient Rome, made of bronze, but became common only in the 19th century, when mass-produced steel dip pen nibs — centred in Birmingham, England — replaced quills in schools and offices. Gold nibs, tipped with hard alloys of iridium or similar metals to resist corrosion and wear, became standard on quality fountain pens.

Anatomy and types

A fountain pen nib typically consists of a metal plate slit from a round breather hole to the tip; ink flows down the slit by capillary action, and the slit opens slightly under pressure, varying the line width. The feed underneath delivers ink and air. Nibs are graded by width — extra fine, fine, medium, broad — and by shape, including round, stub, italic, and oblique points. Flexible nibs, prized for expressive line variation in handwriting and calligraphy, were common in vintage pens and have seen a modern revival. Dip pen nibs for calligraphy and drawing include pointed nibs, broad-edge nibs, and specialist forms such as the ruling pen and the crowquill.
