The Moralia (Ancient Greek: Ἠθικά, Ethika; loosely translated as "Morals" or "Works on moral subjects") is a group of manuscripts dating from the 10th–13th centuries that collect some 78 essays and transcribed speeches of the Greek writer Plutarch (c. AD 46 – after AD 119). The works that make up the Moralia represent everything of Plutarch's output apart from his more famous Parallel Lives.

The Moralia were first collected under that title by the Byzantine scholar Maximus Planudes in the 13th century. Their subjects range across ethics, religion, politics, education, literary criticism, and natural philosophy, and they provide a vivid picture of Greek and Roman intellectual life under the Roman Empire.

Contents

Among the best-known pieces in the collection are On the Malice of Herodotus, a polemical critique of the historian Herodotus; On Isis and Osiris, a major source for Egyptian religion; the Roman Questions and Greek Questions, explaining customs and institutions; On the Delays of Divine Vengeance; On Superstition; and the dialogue Gryllus, in which one of Odysseus's men, transformed into a pig, argues that animal life is preferable to human life.

Influence

The Moralia profoundly influenced Renaissance and early modern European literature. They were translated into Latin and vernacular languages, and an English version by Philemon Holland appeared in 1603. Michel de Montaigne drew heavily on Plutarch in his Essais, and the essays were a favourite of writers including Shakespeare, Emerson, and Beethoven, who kept a copy close at hand.

Editions and numbering

Works in the Moralia are conventionally cited by the page numbers of the 1572 edition of Plutarch's works prepared by Henri Estienne (Stephanus), running from page 1 to 1,142. The standard modern text appears in the Loeb Classical Library in fifteen volumes.
