Logarithmic integral function

The logarithmic integral li(x)

In mathematics, the logarithmic integral function or integral logarithm li(x) is a special function of great importance in number theory. It appears in the prime number theorem as an approximation to the prime-counting function π(x), the number of primes less than or equal to a given value.

Definition

The logarithmic integral is defined as an improper integral:

li(x) = ∫0x dt / ln t,

understood as a Cauchy principal value because of the singularity of the integrand at t = 1. The offset logarithmic integral, often denoted Li(x), instead begins the integral at 2: Li(x) = li(x) − li(2). It is the offset form that is usually used in statements of the prime number theorem.

Relation to prime numbers

The prime number theorem states that π(x) ~ Li(x), meaning the ratio of the two tends to 1 as x grows. For moderate values of x, Li(x) consistently overestimates π(x), and it was long thought it always did; in 1914 J. E. Littlewood proved the difference changes sign infinitely often, though the first crossing (related to Skewes's number) occurs at an astronomically large value. The logarithmic integral also admits an asymptotic expansion li(x) ~ x/ln x (1 + 1/ln x + 2/ln² x + …).

Series expansion

The function can be expressed through the exponential integral Ein, and has the rapidly converging Ramanujan–Soldner series involving the Ramanujan–Soldner constant μ ≈ 1.4513692348, the unique positive zero of li(x).
