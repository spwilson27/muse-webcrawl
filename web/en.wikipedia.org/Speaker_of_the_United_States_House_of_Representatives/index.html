Speaker of the United States House of Representatives


The Speaker presiding over the House

The speaker of the United States House of Representatives, commonly known as the speaker of the House, is the presiding officer of the United States House of Representatives. The office was established in 1789 by Article I, Section 5 of the US Constitution. The speaker is the political and parliamentary leader of the House and is simultaneously the chamber's presiding officer, the de facto leader of its majority party, and the institution's administrative head.

Role and powers

The speaker presides over debates, decides which members may speak, rules on questions of procedure, and appoints members to select and conference committees. Through control of the Rules Committee and the scheduling of floor business, the speaker exerts decisive influence over the legislative agenda. The speaker is second in the United States presidential line of succession, after the vice president, under the Presidential Succession Act of 1947.

Election

The speaker is elected by the full House by roll-call vote at the start of each Congress, or when the office falls vacant. The Constitution does not require the speaker to be a member of the House, though every speaker in history has been one. Each party's caucus nominates a candidate, and election requires an absolute majority of votes cast.

History

The office grew from a largely procedural role into the centre of party government under speakers such as Henry Clay, who championed the "American System" from the chair, and Joseph Gurney Cannon, whose domination provoked a revolt in 1910 that curtailed the office's powers. Later influential speakers include Sam Rayburn, the longest-serving speaker, Tip O'Neill, Newt Gingrich, and Nancy Pelosi, the first woman to hold the office. In October 2023, Kevin McCarthy became the first speaker removed by a motion to vacate; he was succeeded by Mike Johnson.
