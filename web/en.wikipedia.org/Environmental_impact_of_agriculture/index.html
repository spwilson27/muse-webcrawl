Environmental impact of agriculture

Agriculture occupies roughly half of the world's habitable land and is a major driver of land-use change, water use and emissions.

The environmental impact of agriculture is the effect that farming practices have on the ecosystems around them. Agriculture is a leading driver of deforestation, habitat loss and biodiversity decline; it accounts for around 70% of global freshwater withdrawals and roughly a quarter to a third of anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions when land-use change and supply chains are included. Impacts vary widely by system, from intensive monoculture cropping and industrial livestock production to low-input and subsistence farming.

Climate change

Agricultural emissions include methane from enteric fermentation in ruminants and rice paddies, nitrous oxide from fertilised soils and manure, and carbon dioxide from land clearing, drained peatlands and fossil-fuel use in machinery and fertiliser manufacture. Conversely, climate change itself harms agriculture through heat stress, altered rainfall, and increased pests and disease.

Land and soil

Conversion of forests and grasslands to cropland and pasture releases stored carbon and destroys habitat. On farmed land, soil erosion, nutrient depletion, salinisation from irrigation, and soil compaction degrade productivity. Globally, a large share of agricultural land is considered moderately or severely degraded.

Water

Irrigation depletes rivers, lakes and aquifers. Runoff of nitrogen and phosphorus fertilisers causes eutrophication of water bodies, producing algal blooms and hypoxic "dead zones". Pesticides can contaminate surface water and groundwater and harm non-target species, including pollinators.

Biodiversity

Habitat loss to farmland is the single largest cause of terrestrial biodiversity decline. Intensification — larger fields, fewer hedgerows, simplified rotations, heavy pesticide use — reduces farmland wildlife even where land area is stable.

Mitigation

Responses include sustainable intensification, agroforestry, conservation agriculture, precision application of fertilisers, improved manure management, dietary shifts away from emissions-intensive foods, reduction of food waste, and restoration of degraded land. Organic farming reduces synthetic inputs but typically yields less per unit area, so its net environmental benefit depends on land-use consequences.
