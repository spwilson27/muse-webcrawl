Visual system

The visual system is the physiological basis of visual perception — the ability of organisms to detect and interpret light from their surroundings to build a representation of the world. In humans and other vertebrates it comprises the eyes, which capture light and convert it into neural signals, the optic nerves and central pathways that transmit those signals, and the visual cortex and associated brain areas that interpret them. Together they produce the sense of sight.

The eye and retina

Light entering the eye is focused by the cornea and lens onto the retina, where photoreceptor cells — about 120 million rods for low-light vision and 6–7 million cones for colour and detail — transduce photons into electrical signals. Retinal ganglion cells preprocess contrast, colour opponency and edges, and their axons form the optic nerve, carrying roughly a million fibres per eye. A portion of fibres serves non-image functions such as the pupillary light reflex and circadian entrainment via the suprachiasmatic nucleus.

Central pathways

At the optic chiasm, fibres from the nasal retina cross to the opposite side, so each hemisphere receives the contralateral visual field. Most fibres relay in the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus and project through the optic radiations to the primary visual cortex in the occipital lobe. A smaller pathway runs to the superior colliculus and supports orienting movements of the eyes and head.

Higher processing

Beyond V1, visual information is analysed along ventral ("what") and dorsal ("where/how") streams for recognition and action respectively. The system extracts form, colour, motion, depth and texture, integrates the two eyes' views for stereoscopic depth perception, and combines vision with memory and attention to yield stable perception despite constant eye movements.
