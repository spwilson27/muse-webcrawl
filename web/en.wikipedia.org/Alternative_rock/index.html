Alternative rock

Alternative rock (also known as alternative music, alt-rock, or simply alternative) is a category of rock music that emerged from the independent music underground of the 1970s and became widely popular in the 1990s. "Alternative" refers to the genre's distinction from mainstream rock, originally expressed through independent record labels, college radio, and a do-it-yourself ethic inherited from punk. Its sounds range widely — from the noisy guitars of grunge and shoegaze to the gothic and jangle-pop strands of the 1980s underground and the later stadium success of bands such as Radiohead and Coldplay.

History

1980s underground

After punk's initial explosion, a network of independent labels (SST, Dischord, Rough Trade, 4AD, Creation), fanzines, and college radio stations sustained bands outside the commercial mainstream. R.E.M., The Smiths, The Cure, Sonic Youth, Pixies, and the American hardcore diaspora built devoted followings through touring. The term "alternative" was in common use by the end of the decade; in 1991 MTV launched 120 Minutes and Alternative Nation programming around the scene.

1990s breakthrough

Nirvana's Nevermind (1991) and its single "Smells Like Teen Spirit" propelled alternative rock — especially Seattle's grunge (Pearl Jam, Soundgarden, Alice in Chains) — to the centre of popular music. The Lollapalooza festival (founded 1991 by Perry Farrell of Jane's Addiction) became the genre's showcase. In Britain, Britpop (Blur, Oasis, Pulp, Suede) reacted against grunge with a revival of 1960s-influenced guitar pop, while Radiohead, The Smashing Pumpkins, Beck, and others pushed the genre's boundaries.

2000s and after

Post-grunge, nu metal, emo, and the garage-rock revival (The Strokes, The White Stripes) all drew on the alternative umbrella, while "indie rock" increasingly displaced "alternative" as the label for guitar music outside the mainstream. By the 2010s alternative survived mainly as a radio format and an aesthetic descriptor as streaming dissolved the underground/mainstream boundary the term described.
