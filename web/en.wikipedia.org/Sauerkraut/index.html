Sauerkraut (German: [ˈzaʊ.ɐˌkʁaʊt], "sour cabbage") is finely cut raw cabbage that has been fermented by various lactic acid bacteria. It has a long shelf life and a distinctive sour flavour, both resulting from the lactic acid formed when the bacteria ferment the sugars in the cabbage leaves. It is one of the best-known national dishes of Germany and is eaten across Central and Eastern Europe and in Alsace, and in the Americas wherever those cuisines travelled.

Production

Shredded cabbage is layered with about 2% salt, which draws out water to form a brine. Kept submerged and anaerobic, the cabbage ferments over days to weeks as Leuconostoc and then Lactobacillus species acidify it to a pH below 4.6, preserving it. Commercial sauerkraut is often pasteurised, which kills the live cultures; traditional raw kraut is a probiotic food rich in vitamin C and K.

History and uses

Fermented cabbage was known in China (where labourers on the Great Wall ate cabbage fermented in rice wine) and in Roman writers' accounts, but sauerkraut in its modern form developed in German-speaking Europe. Sailors carried it on long voyages as protection against scurvy. Classic dishes include sauerkraut with pork and sausages, Alsatian choucroute garnie, Polish bigos and kapusta, and as a topping for hot dogs and Reuben sandwiches.
