Boeing 737 MAX groundings

The Boeing 737 MAX passenger airliner was grounded worldwide between March 2019 and December 2020 — and in many jurisdictions longer — after 346 people died in two similar accidents within five months: Lion Air Flight 610 on 29 October 2018 and Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 on 10 March 2019.

Background: MCAS

The 737 MAX, the fourth generation of the Boeing 737, was fitted with larger, more fuel-efficient CFM LEAP engines mounted further forward and higher than on earlier models. This changed the aircraft's pitch behavior at high angles of attack. To compensate and preserve handling commonality with earlier 737s, Boeing added the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System (MCAS), software that automatically pushed the nose down when a single angle-of-attack sensor indicated a risk of stall.

In both accidents, a faulty sensor reading caused MCAS to activate repeatedly, overpowering the flight crews. Investigations found that Boeing had not adequately disclosed MCAS to airlines and pilots, and that the U.S. Federal Aviation Administration (FAA) had delegated significant certification work to Boeing itself.

The grounding

After the second crash, aviation regulators around the world grounded the type; China's civil aviation authority was the first to act, and the FAA grounded the U.S. fleet on 13 March 2019. The grounding lasted 20 months in the United States, with the FAA rescinding its order on 18 November 2020 after Boeing implemented software changes making MCAS depend on two sensors, added limits on its authority, and revised pilot training. Other regulators, including in Europe and Canada, followed; China did not clear the type until December 2021.

Consequences

The crisis cost Boeing more than $20 billion in compensation, fines and production losses. Boeing's CEO Dennis Muilenburg was dismissed in December 2019. In January 2021 Boeing entered a deferred prosecution agreement with the U.S. Department of Justice, paying over $2.5 billion, after being charged with conspiracy to defraud the FAA. A further incident in January 2024, when a door plug blew out of an Alaska Airlines 737 MAX 9 in flight, led to a temporary grounding of the MAX 9 variant and renewed regulatory scrutiny of Boeing's manufacturing quality.
