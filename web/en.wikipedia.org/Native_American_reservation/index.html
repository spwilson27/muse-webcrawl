Native American reservation

A Native American reservation (formally an Indian reservation) is an area of land in the United States managed by a federally recognized Native American tribal nation under the U.S. Bureau of Indian Affairs, rather than by the government of the state in which it is located. Tribes exercise a degree of sovereignty over reservation land, operating their own governments, courts, police and services, subject to federal law and the government-to-government relationship with the United States.

History

Reservations arose from treaties and federal policies that confined tribes to portions of their ancestral lands as settlers expanded westward. The Indian Removal Act of 1830 forced southeastern tribes to the Indian Territory (now Oklahoma), and after 1851 the reservation system expanded across the West amid warfare and coerced treaties. The Dawes Act of 1887 broke reservations into individual allotments and opened "surplus" land to non-Natives, costing tribes some 90 million acres by 1934, when the Indian Reorganization Act ended allotment and encouraged tribal self-government. A mid-twentieth-century policy of "termination" was reversed in the 1970s by the self-determination era.

Geography and law

There are about 326 federal Indian reservations, the largest being the Navajo Nation (about 27,000 square miles across Arizona, New Mexico and Utah); together with other trust lands they total about 56 million acres. Reservation jurisdiction is famously complex, divided among tribal, federal and — limited — state authority; the Supreme Court's 2020 decision in McGirt v. Oklahoma affirmed that the Muscogee (Creek) reservation in eastern Oklahoma had never been disestablished. Many reservations face poverty, infrastructure and health challenges, while tribal enterprises such as gaming (authorized by the Indian Gaming Regulatory Act of 1988), energy and tourism provide major revenue for some nations.
