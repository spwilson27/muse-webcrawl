The viola da gamba (Italian: [ˈvjɔːla da ˈɡamba]), or viol, is a family of bowed, fretted string instruments held vertically between the legs (gamba meaning "leg"). Viols were the dominant family of bowed instruments in Europe during the Renaissance and Baroque eras before being eclipsed by the violin family, and have enjoyed a major revival since the early 20th century as part of the historically informed performance movement.

Characteristics

Viols typically have six strings tuned in fourths with a major third in the middle, tied gut frets, sloping shoulders and a flat back. They are played with an underhand bow grip, producing a soft, resonant tone suited to polyphonic consort music. Sizes range from the small pardessus and treble down to the large violone.

History

The viol developed in late-15th-century Spain from instruments such as the vihuela de arco, spread through Italy and was taken up across Europe. Consorts of viols were central to English music of the 16th and 17th centuries, with composers such as William Byrd, John Dowland, Orlando Gibbons and Henry Purcell writing for them; in France, Marin Marais and Antoine Forqueray developed the solo bass viol repertoire to a virtuoso peak. Demand declined in the 18th century as the louder violin family suited larger halls and orchestras.

Revival

Pioneers such as Arnold Dolmetsch revived viol building and playing in the early 1900s; performers including Jordi Savall and ensembles such as Fretwork and Phantasm have since restored the viol to concert life and recording.
