Amistad

La Amistad ("The Friendship") was a 19th-century two-masted schooner built in Spain and owned by a Spaniard living in Cuba. It became famous in 1839 when enslaved Africans aboard the vessel seized control of it in a revolt that led to a landmark legal case in the United States, United States v. The Amistad, decided by the U.S. Supreme Court in 1841.

The revolt

In 1839, fifty-three Mende captives who had been illegally transported from Sierra Leone to Cuba were placed aboard La Amistad for transfer to another part of the island. Led by Sengbe Pieh, known as Joseph Cinqué, the captives freed themselves, killed the captain and the cook, and ordered the surviving Spanish owners to sail back to Africa. The Spaniards instead steered northward at night, and the ship was eventually intercepted off Long Island by the U.S. Navy brig Washington.

The legal case

The Africans were imprisoned in Connecticut while their status was litigated. Abolitionists raised funds and organised their defence; former president John Quincy Adams argued their case before the Supreme Court. In March 1841 the Court ruled that the captives had been illegally enslaved, that they had acted in self-defence, and that they were free. Thirty-five survivors returned to Sierra Leone in 1842.

Legacy

The Amistad affair became a powerful episode in the American abolitionist movement and is commemorated by a memorial in New Haven and by a replica vessel, the freedom schooner Amistad, launched in Mystic, Connecticut in 2000. The story was retold in Steven Spielberg's 1997 film Amistad.
