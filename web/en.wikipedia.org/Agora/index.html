Agora

The agora (Greek: ἀγορά) was a central public space in ancient Greek city-states: an open area serving as a marketplace and as the civic heart where citizens gathered for commerce, politics, law, religion, and philosophy. The word derives from the verb ageirein, "to gather together," and survives in terms such as agoraphobia.

Functions

The agora hosted market stalls and workshops, law courts, council meetings, religious festivals and processions, athletic events, and theatrical performances. Around it stood stoas (colonnaded porches), temples, altars, council houses (bouleuteria), and fountains. Socrates famously conducted his philosophical conversations in the Athenian agora, and the Stoic school took its name from the Stoa Poikile there.

The Athenian agora

The Agora of Athens, northwest of the Acropolis, is the best-known example. Developed from the 6th century BC, it included the Hephaisteion (one of the best-preserved Greek temples), the Tholos, and the reconstructed Stoa of Attalos, which now houses the Agora Museum. Boundary stones inscribed "I am the boundary of the Agora" marked its limits, and ostracism votes were held there, with citizens inscribing names on pottery sherds (ostraka).

Decline and legacy

Under Rome the forum took over many agora functions. The agora remains the archetype of the public square — the physical setting of democratic deliberation that later theorists of the public sphere have taken as a model.
