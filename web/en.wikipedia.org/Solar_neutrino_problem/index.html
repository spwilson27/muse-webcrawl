Solar neutrino problem

The solar neutrino problem was a major discrepancy between the flux of neutrinos predicted to arrive at Earth from the Sun and the flux actually measured by detectors, observed from the late 1960s until its resolution around 2001. Experiments detected only about one-third to one-half of the electron neutrinos that standard solar models predicted, posing a puzzle for both astrophysics and particle physics.

The Homestake experiment

Beginning in the 1960s, Raymond Davis Jr. built a detector in the Homestake gold mine in South Dakota: a large tank of perchloroethylene (cleaning fluid) whose chlorine atoms occasionally converted to radioactive argon when struck by solar neutrinos produced in the Sun's core. John Bahcall's calculations of the expected rate, based on the standard solar model and the Sun's measured luminosity, consistently predicted roughly three times more captures than Davis observed.

Further experiments

Gallium experiments in Europe (GALLEX) and Russia (SAGE), and the Kamiokande and Super-Kamiokande water Cherenkov detectors in Japan, confirmed the deficit across different neutrino energies. The consistency of the shortfall across independent experiments made an error in any single measurement unlikely, and solar models were verified independently by helioseismology, shifting suspicion to the physics of neutrinos themselves.

Resolution: neutrino oscillation

The problem was resolved by the discovery of neutrino oscillation: neutrinos produced as electron neutrinos in the Sun change flavor during their journey to Earth, arriving partly as muon and tau neutrinos that the early detectors could not (or could only weakly) register. Matter-enhanced oscillation in the Sun (the MSW effect) contributes to the conversion. In 2001, the Sudbury Neutrino Observatory (SNO) in Canada measured the total flux of all neutrino flavors and found it matched solar model predictions, while the electron-neutrino flux alone was reduced — exactly as oscillation predicted.

Recognition

Raymond Davis Jr. and Masatoshi Koshiba shared part of the 2002 Nobel Prize in Physics for the detection of cosmic neutrinos. The 2015 Nobel Prize went to Takaaki Kajita and Arthur B. McDonald for the discovery of neutrino oscillation, which implies that neutrinos have mass — a result beyond the original Standard Model of particle physics.
