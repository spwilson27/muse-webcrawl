In actuarial science and demography, a life table (also called a mortality table or actuarial table) is a table which shows, for each age, what the probability is that a person of that age will die before their next birthday (the "probability of death"). In other words, it represents the survivorship of people from a certain population, typically modelled as a hypothetical cohort of, say, 100,000 people born at the same time.

Structure

A standard life table tabulates, for each age x: the mortality rate qx; the number of survivors lx; the number of deaths dx; the person-years lived Lx; the total remaining person-years Tx; and the remaining life expectancy ex. Tables may be period tables, based on mortality observed in a single period, or cohort tables, following an actual generation through its lifetime.

History

John Graunt's analysis of London's bills of mortality (1662) is generally regarded as the first attempt at a life table, and Edmond Halley constructed a recognisably modern table from Breslau data in 1693. Life tables became foundational to the life insurance industry in the 18th and 19th centuries, enabling premiums to be calculated from observed mortality rather than guesswork.

Applications

Insurers and pension schemes use life tables for pricing annuities and reserving; public health researchers use them to compute life expectancy and summarise population health; ecologists construct analogous tables for plants and animals to study survival and reproduction. Abridged tables (using five-year age groups) are common in official statistics, while national statistical offices such as the UK Office for National Statistics and the US Social Security Administration publish standard tables.
