Dispersed knowledge

Dispersed knowledge is the notion that no single agent has complete information about all the factors that influence prices and production throughout the economy, and that the relevant knowledge is instead scattered among many individuals, each possessing only a partial, local, and often tacit view. The concept is central to the work of economist Friedrich Hayek.

Hayek's argument

In his 1945 essay "The Use of Knowledge in Society", Hayek argued that the economic problem is not allocating given resources with full information, but utilizing knowledge that is not given to anyone in its totality. Much of this knowledge concerns "the particular circumstances of time and place" and cannot be fully transmitted to a central authority in statistical form. Hayek presented the price system as a mechanism that communicates the essential information in condensed form, allowing individuals to coordinate their plans without knowing the whole picture.

Implications

The dispersed-knowledge argument is a foundational critique of central economic planning and informs debates about market processes, entrepreneurship, and organizational design; related ideas appear in discussions of local knowledge, tacit knowledge, and distributed systems.
