Downward entailing

In formal semantics, a downward entailing (DE) proposition is a logical expression which validates inferences "from sets to subsets". A downward entailing operator reverses the usual direction of entailment: if an expression is true of a set, it is also true of any subset of that set. The concept is central to the theory of negation and to explaining the distribution of negative polarity items such as any and ever.

Examples

Compare the upward entailing environment in Some student read a book, which entails Some student read a novel (moving from a set to a subset works in the object position of some only in the other direction). In contrast, in No student read a novel, we may infer No student read a book that was printed in 1900: making the description narrower preserves truth. Negation, no, few, at most n, the antecedent of a conditional (if), and the complement of verbs like doubt create downward entailing environments.

Negative polarity items

The linguist Gilles Fauconnier and, independently, William Ladusaw proposed that expressions like any are licensed in downward entailing contexts:

  • I didn't see anything. (negation — DE, grammatical)
  • \*I saw anything. (plain affirmative — not DE, ungrammatical)
  • Few people ate any of the cake. (DE)

An environment that is not downward entailing but has certain related logical properties is called nonveridical; modern theories often analyse polarity sensitivity in terms of the broader notion of (non)veridicality.
