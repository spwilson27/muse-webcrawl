Wingtip vortices

Wingtip vortices are circular patterns of rotating air left behind a wing as it generates lift. They trail from the tips of an aircraft's wings, and they are also sometimes called wake vortices or lift-induced vortices, because they form part of the aircraft's wake. Wingtip vortices are the primary and most powerful component of the wake turbulence produced by an aircraft in flight.

Formation

A wing generates lift because the pressure on its lower surface is higher than on its upper surface. Near the wingtip, air from below curls around the tip toward the low-pressure region above, and the trailing sheet of air rolls up into a concentrated vortex at each tip. The two vortices rotate in opposite senses, and between them the air is driven downward — the downwash associated with induced drag.

Hazards

Wingtip vortices can persist for minutes and drift with the wind. An aircraft encountering the wake of a larger aircraft ahead of it can be rolled violently, which is why air traffic control enforces separation distances between aircraft based on weight category, particularly during takeoff and landing. Vortices also set limits on how closely aircraft may fly in formation and influence the design of wingtip devices.

Mitigation

Winglets and other wingtip devices reduce the strength of the vortices and the induced drag they cause by spreading the pressure difference over a larger area. Ground effect also disrupts vortex formation when an aircraft is close to the runway. Birds in formation flight exploit the upwash at the edges of a leader's vortices to save energy.
