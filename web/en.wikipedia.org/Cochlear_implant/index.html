Cochlear implant

A cochlear implant (CI) is a surgically implanted neuroprosthesis that provides a person who has moderate-to-profound sensorineural hearing loss with a modified sense of sound. A cochlear implant bypasses the normal acoustic hearing process to replace it with electric signals that directly stimulate the auditory nerve. A person with a cochlear implant receiving intensive auditory training may learn to interpret those signals as sound and speech. However, one-third of deaf children do not develop age-appropriate language skills when only receiving a cochlear implant by age three without additional interventions such as sign language.

How it works

The system has an external part—a microphone and speech processor worn behind the ear—and an internal receiver-stimulator implanted under the skin, connected to an electrode array threaded into the cochlea. The processor converts sound into digital signals that are transmitted across the skin and decoded into electrical pulses delivered to electrodes along the cochlea's tonotopic axis, stimulating the auditory nerve directly where the hair cells that normally transduce sound are damaged or absent. Modern devices have 12 to 22 electrodes.

History

Experiments with electrical stimulation of hearing date to Alessandro Volta around 1800. The first direct stimulations of the auditory nerve were performed in France in 1957 by André Djourno and Charles Eyriès. William House developed single-channel implants in the 1970s, and multichannel devices—notably Graeme Clark's in Australia, leading to the company Cochlear—were approved in the 1980s. Hundreds of thousands of people worldwide have since received implants, increasingly in both ears and at ever younger ages.

Outcomes and debate

Outcomes vary widely: many recipients understand speech well, especially those implanted early in childhood or after losing hearing post-lingually, while music perception remains limited. Cochlear implants have been the subject of controversy within parts of the Deaf community, where deafness is viewed as a cultural identity rather than a disability, and the implantation of deaf children has raised ethical questions about parental choice and sign language access. Medical consensus holds that implants are safe and effective for suitable candidates, with surgical risks including infection and device failure being uncommon.
