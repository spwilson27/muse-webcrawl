Digital subscriber line

Digital subscriber line (DSL; originally digital subscriber loop) is a family of technologies that are used to transmit digital data over telephone lines. In telecommunications marketing, the term DSL is widely understood to mean asymmetric digital subscriber line (ADSL), the most commonly installed type for internet access, though symmetric and very-high-bit-rate variants exist.

Principles

Telephone lines were designed to carry voice within roughly 300–3400 Hz, but the copper twisted pair can support much higher frequencies over short distances. DSL uses frequency bands above the voice channel for data, with a splitter or microfilter separating voice and data at the customer's premises. Discrete multitone modulation divides the data band into many narrow subcarriers, each adapted to the line conditions; attainable speed falls sharply with loop length and line quality.

Variants

ADSL provides higher downstream than upstream rates for typical consumer use; ADSL2+ extended downstream rates to about 24 Mbit/s. SDSL offers symmetric rates for business use. VDSL and VDSL2 deliver much higher speeds — up to hundreds of megabits per second — over short loops, typically from a street cabinet fed by fibre ("fibre to the cabinet"). G.fast pushes rates higher still over very short drops.

Deployment and legacy

Commercial DSL services began in the late 1990s and, together with cable modems, provided the first mass-market broadband. Because it reused existing copper plant, DSL rolled out quickly but has been progressively displaced by fibre-to-the-premises networks offering far greater speed and reliability; many operators are retiring copper services entirely.
