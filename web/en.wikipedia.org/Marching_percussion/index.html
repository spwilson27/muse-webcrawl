Marching percussion

A drumline: snare drums, multi-tenors and bass drums carried on harnesses.

Marching percussion encompasses the percussion instruments and performance practices used in marching ensembles such as marching bands, drum and bugle corps, drumlines and pipe bands. Unlike stationary concert percussion, marching percussion instruments are carried by the performer using harnesses or slings, allowing the ensemble to play while moving in drill formations. The marching battery typically consists of snare drums, tenor drums (quads/quints), tonal bass drums and cymbals, while the front ensemble ("pit") plays stationary instruments such as marimbas, vibraphones, timpani and accessories.

Instruments

Snare drums for marching are deeper and built with high-tension heads (often Kevlar-based) that produce a short, articulate, high-pitched sound capable of cutting through a full band outdoors. Tenor drums are sets of four to six tuned drums mounted together ("quads" or "quints") played with mallets or sticks, providing melodic tom-tom figures. Bass drums come in graduated sizes (commonly 14–32 inches) carried vertically, each player sounding one pitch of a melodic bass line that unfolds across the section ("split parts"). Cymbals are played in pairs with crashes, chokes and visual effects. Pipe bands use their own tradition of side drums, tenor drums and a single bass drum.

Technique and notation

Marching snare technique emphasises matched or traditional grip, precise rudimental figures descended from military drumming, and unison articulation across the line; the standard repertoire of rudiments is codified by the Percussive Arts Society (the 40 PAS rudiments). Composers and arrangers write demanding, highly articulated parts that are central to the competitive drum corps style popularised by Drum Corps International (DCI). Indoor percussion ensembles, competing in circuits such as Winter Guard International (WGI), extend the art form into theatrical arena productions during the winter season.

History

Marching percussion descends from the field music of early modern armies — fifes and drums used to signal commands and keep step. In the United States, veteran organisations sustained drum and bugle corps after the Civil Wars and World Wars, which evolved into the modern competitive drum corps and influenced school marching bands. Free-floating harnesses, high-tension heads and multi-drum configurations were key twentieth-century innovations.
