Levallois technique

The Levallois technique is a distinctive method of stone knapping developed by prehistoric humans, in which a stone core is carefully shaped in advance so that a flake of predetermined size and shape can be struck from it. Named after the Paris suburb of Levallois-Perret, where examples were found in 19th-century gravel pits, the technique appears from about 300,000 years ago in Africa and Eurasia and is a hallmark of the Middle Palaeolithic Mousterian industries associated with Neanderthals and early modern humans.

Method

The knapper first shapes the core — often compared to a tortoise shell — by removing flakes around its perimeter to create a convex upper surface and a faceted striking platform. A final blow then detaches a single large flake whose shape was determined by the preparation. Variants produce points (including the Mousterian point) or blades. Because the result is planned in advance, archaeologists regard Levallois as evidence of advanced cognition and abstract planning in its makers.

Distribution and significance

Levallois technology appears in the later Acheulean of Africa (for example in the Kapthurin Formation of Kenya) and becomes widespread across Africa, Europe and Asia during the Middle Stone Age and Middle Palaeolithic. Its presence is used to track the movements of hominin populations, and in the Levant both Neanderthals and modern humans used it. Refitting studies at sites such as Maastricht-Belvédère have reconstructed complete reduction sequences, confirming the complexity of the method.
