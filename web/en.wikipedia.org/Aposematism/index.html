Aposematism

The strawberry poison dart frog: its conspicuous colours honestly advertise chemical defences.

Aposematism is the advertising by an animal, whether terrestrial or marine, to potential predators that it is not worth attacking or eating. This unprofitability may consist of any defences which make the prey difficult to eat, such as toxicity, venom, foul taste or smell, sharp spines, or aggressive nature. These advertising signals may take the form of conspicuous colouration, sounds, odours, or other perceivable characteristics. Aposematic signals are beneficial to both predator and prey, since both avoid potential harm.

The term was coined in 1877 by Edward Bagnall Poulton for Alfred Russel Wallace's concept of warning coloration. Aposematism is exploited in Müllerian mimicry, where species with genuine defences resemble each other, and also in Batesian mimicry, where harmless species copy the signals of defended ones.

Mechanism

Aposematism works through predator learning and innate avoidance: a predator that samples a toxic, brightly coloured prey learns to associate the signal with an unpleasant experience and thereafter avoids similar prey. Conspicuousness aids memorability, so selection favours signals that are easy to detect and remember. Defences advertised include alkaloid poisons (poison dart frogs), cardiac glycosides (monarch butterflies), venomous stings (wasps), and chemical sprays (bombardier beetles).

Examples

  • Poison dart frogs of Central and South America — vivid blues, reds and yellows.
  • The black-and-yellow banding of wasps and bees.
  • The skunk's bold black-and-white pattern, often preceded by warning behaviour.
  • Monarch butterflies, whose milkweed-derived toxins also protect their mimic, the viceroy.
  • Marine nudibranchs and the blue-ringed octopus.

Aposematic displays contribute to local biodiversity patterns, as mimicry rings of many species share the cost of educating predators.
