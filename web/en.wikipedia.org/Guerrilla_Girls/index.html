The Guerrilla Girls are an anonymous group of feminist, female artists devoted to fighting sexism and racism within the art world. The group was formed in New York City in 1985, following the Museum of Modern Art's exhibition An International Survey of Recent Painting and Sculpture, which included 169 artists, of whom only 13 were women. Members are known for wearing gorilla masks in public and taking the names of deceased women artists as pseudonyms — founding members adopted names such as Frida Kahlo and Käthe Kollwitz — a practice they explain as keeping the focus on the issues, not on their personalities.

Work and methods

The Guerrilla Girls use "culture jamming" tactics: provocative posters, billboards, stickers, books and public appearances that present statistics about discrimination in a humorous, fact-based style. Their first actions in 1985 plastered SoHo with posters naming galleries and museums that showed few or no women artists. Their best-known poster, commissioned by the Public Art Fund in 1989 and initially rejected as a billboard, paired Ingres's Grande Odalisque — with a gorilla mask on the figure — with the question "Do women have to be naked to get into the Met. Museum?" and the statistic that less than 5% of the artists in the Met's Modern Art sections were women, but 85% of the nudes were female. The group has updated the count several times (in 2012 the figures were under 4% artists and 76% of nudes).

Expansion of focus

From the 1990s the group broadened its campaigns to Hollywood (the underrepresentation of women and people of colour behind the camera), politics, reproductive rights, and the art market, and published books including Confessions of the Guerrilla Girls (1995) and The Guerrilla Girls' Bedside Companion to the History of Western Art (1998). In 2001 the group split into three entities (Guerrilla Girls, Inc.; GuerrillaGirlsBroadBand; and the theatre troupe Guerrilla Girls On Tour), and later internal copyright litigation underscored tensions within the collective.

Reception and legacy

Their works are in the collections of major museums, and the group received retrospectives and commissions from institutions it once picketed, including Tate Modern and the Whitechapel Gallery. They remain a canonical example of institutional critique and feminist art activism, though statistics they helped publicise show that representation of women artists in major museums and markets has improved only slowly.
