Eye chart

An eye chart is a chart used by eye care professionals to measure visual acuity. Eye charts are used during an eye examination to assess a patient's vision, to detect refractive errors such as myopia, and to determine the need for corrective lenses.

A Snellen chart, the most common type of eye chart

Types

The best-known eye chart is the Snellen chart, developed in 1862 by Dutch ophthalmologist Herman Snellen. It consists of rows of letters called optotypes, which decrease in size from top to bottom. The patient reads the chart from a standard distance, typically 6 metres (20 feet), and the smallest line that can be read accurately indicates the visual acuity, expressed as a fraction such as 20/20 (or 6/6).

Other charts include the Landolt C, used internationally and with patients who cannot read letters; the Tumbling E chart, widely used with children and illiterate patients; the Lea test, which uses pictures of common objects for young children; and the logMAR chart (such as the ETDRS chart), which offers a more precise and scientifically rigorous measurement of acuity and is standard in research.

Procedure

During the test, each eye is examined separately, usually with the other eye covered. The patient reads aloud the letters on the chart, beginning with the largest row. The line at which the patient can correctly identify more than half of the letters is recorded as that eye's visual acuity.
