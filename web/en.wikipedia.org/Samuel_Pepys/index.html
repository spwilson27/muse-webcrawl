Samuel Pepys (23 February 1633 – 26 May 1703) was an English diarist and naval administrator. He rose to be Chief Secretary to the Admiralty under Kings Charles II and James II, and served as a Member of Parliament and President of the Royal Society. He is celebrated today for the diary he kept from 1660 to 1669, one of the most important primary sources for the Restoration period.

Early life and education

Pepys was born in Salisbury Court, Fleet Street, London, the son of a tailor. He was educated at St Paul's School and at Magdalene College, Cambridge, where he took his degree in 1654. Through his father's cousin Edward Montagu, later Earl of Sandwich, he entered the service of the navy administration, which shaped the rest of his career.

The diary

Pepys began his diary on 1 January 1660 and wrote almost daily until 31 May 1669, when failing eyesight forced him to stop. Written in Thomas Shelton's shorthand system, it gives first-hand accounts of the Restoration of Charles II, the Second Anglo-Dutch War, the Great Plague of London (1665) and the Great Fire of London (1666). Its frankness about Pepys's ambitions, marriage, infidelities and everyday pleasures made it a literary as well as historical landmark.

The diary was not deciphered in full until the nineteenth century; the first published edition appeared in 1825, and the definitive Latham and Matthews edition was published in 1970–1983. The manuscript forms part of the Pepys Library at Magdalene College, Cambridge, which holds his collection of some 3,000 books bequeathed with the diary.

Naval administration

As Clerk of the Acts and later Secretary of the Admiralty, Pepys professionalised the administration of the Royal Navy, reforming examinations for lieutenants, record-keeping and supply. Briefly imprisoned in the Tower of London in 1679 amid the Popish Plot hysteria, and again in 1690 under suspicion of Jacobitism, he nonetheless remained a central figure in naval affairs for three decades.

Death and legacy

Pepys died at Clapham in 1703. His name is attached to the Pepys Library, to a well-known idiom of intimate daily record-keeping, and to the image of Restoration London that his diary preserves. His former home in Seething Lane and the church of St Olave's, Hart Street, where he is buried, are associated with his memory.
