Geoffrey of Monmouth

Geoffrey of Monmouth (c. 1095 – c. 1155) was a Welsh cleric and author who played an important role in the development of British historiography and in popularising tales of King Arthur. He is best known for his chronicle Historia Regum Britanniae (History of the Kings of Britain), which purported to record the history of Britain from its mythical Trojan founding through a long line of kings to the Anglo-Saxon period.

Life

Little is certain about Geoffrey's life. He appears in Oxford charters between 1129 and 1151, styling himself magister (teacher), and was probably of Breton or Welsh descent. In 1152 he was consecrated Bishop of St Asaph in Wales, though there is no evidence he ever visited his diocese, then a war zone; he died within a few years.

Works

The Historia Regum Britanniae, completed around 1136, traces British kings from Brutus of Troy through Lear, Cymbeline, and Arthur to Cadwaladr. Geoffrey claimed to have translated "a certain very ancient book in the British tongue" supplied by Walter, Archdeacon of Oxford, a claim few modern scholars credit; most of the narrative is now regarded as imaginative construction woven from Gildas, Bede, Welsh tradition, and invention. His earlier Prophetiae Merlini popularised Merlin as a prophet, and the Vita Merlini (c. 1150) presents Merlin as a wild man of the woods.

Influence

Geoffrey's history was enormously influential, surviving in more than 200 manuscripts. It fixed the framework of the Arthurian legend — Arthur's conception, reign, and departure to Avalon — that later writers from Wace and Layamon to Chrétien de Troyes and Malory developed. Though contemporaries such as William of Newburgh already denounced its fabrications, it shaped British national myth for centuries.
