Black-body radiation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Black-body radiation is the thermal electromagnetic radiation emitted by a body in thermodynamic equilibrium, where a black body is an idealised object that absorbs all incident radiation and emits a spectrum determined solely by its temperature.

Planck's law

The spectrum is described by Planck's law (1900), which gives the intensity at each frequency as a function of temperature. Deriving it led Max Planck to propose that energy is exchanged in discrete quanta — the origin of quantum theory. At higher temperatures the peak of the spectrum shifts to shorter wavelengths (Wien's displacement law) and total emitted power grows with the fourth power of temperature (Stefan–Boltzmann law).

The ultraviolet catastrophe

Classical physics, via the Rayleigh–Jeans law, predicted infinite emission at short wavelengths — the "ultraviolet catastrophe". Planck's quantisation resolved the discrepancy and matched measurements by Lummer, Pringsheim, Rubens, and Kurlbaum.

Applications

Approximate black bodies include a cavity with a small hole, glowing furnaces, and stars. Black-body theory underlies pyrometry, thermal imaging, incandescent lighting, and cosmology: the cosmic microwave background is the most precise black-body spectrum known in nature, at 2.725 K.
