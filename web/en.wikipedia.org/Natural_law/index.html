Natural law

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Natural law (Latin: ius naturale, lex naturalis) is a system of law based on a close observation of human nature, from which a system of moral values—thought to be intrinsic to humans and knowable by human reason—can be deduced. According to natural law theory, all people have inherent rights, conferred not by act of legislation but by "God, nature, or reason". Natural law theory can also refer to theories of ethics, politics, and civil law.

History

Ideas of natural law appear in Greek philosophy, notably in Aristotle and the Stoics, who distinguished universal law from local convention. Cicero articulated a classic formulation: true law is right reason in agreement with nature. In the medieval period, Thomas Aquinas placed natural law within a hierarchy of eternal, divine, natural and human law in his Summa Theologiae. Early modern theorists such as Hugo Grotius, Samuel von Pufendorf, John Locke and Jean-Jacques Burlamaqui developed natural law into foundations for international law, natural rights, and social contract theory.

Natural law and positive law

Natural law is contrasted with positive law, the man-made law enacted by states. In strong versions of the theory, an unjust law is said not to be truly law (lex iniusta non est lex). Natural law reasoning influenced declarations of rights, including the United States Declaration of Independence and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen, as well as the modern concept of human rights.

Criticism

Legal positivists such as Jeremy Bentham and H. L. A. Hart criticized natural law theory as conflating law as it is with law as it ought to be. Critics also question whether moral conclusions can be derived from facts about nature, a problem associated with David Hume's is–ought distinction and G. E. Moore's naturalistic fallacy.
