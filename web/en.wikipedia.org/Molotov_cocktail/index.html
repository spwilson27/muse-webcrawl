A Molotov cocktail is a hand-thrown incendiary weapon consisting of a frangible container filled with flammable substances and equipped with a fuse. In use, the fuse is lit and the weapon is thrown, igniting the fuel when the container shatters on impact. The weapon's name, though not its use, derives from Finland's conflict with the Soviet Union in the Winter War.

Etymology

The name "Molotov cocktail" was coined by the Finns during the Winter War (1939–1940) in mocking reference to Vyacheslav Molotov, the Soviet foreign minister. Molotov had claimed in radio broadcasts that Soviet bombers were not dropping bombs on Finland but delivering food supplies to starving Finns. The Finns sarcastically dubbed the Soviet cluster bombs "Molotov bread baskets" and named the petrol bombs they threw in reply "a drink to go with the food" — Molotov cocktails.

History

Improvised incendiary bottles were used before the name existed. In the Spanish Civil War (1936–1939), Nationalist troops used petrol bombs against Soviet T-26 tanks supporting the Republicans, and Finnish and other forces observed their effectiveness. During the Winter War, the Finnish company Alko mass-produced standardized petrol bombs at its Rajamäki distillery, producing some 450,000 during the war, complete with matches and instructions.

In the Second World War, Molotov cocktails were used by regular and irregular forces alike. The British Home Guard prepared vast stocks during the invasion scare of 1940, and the weapons were employed by resistance movements across occupied Europe. They have remained a symbol of improvised resistance and have appeared in conflicts and civil unrest worldwide ever since, from the Hungarian Revolution of 1956 to the Warsaw Pact invasion of Czechoslovakia in 1968 and beyond.

Design

The basic design is a breakable glass bottle containing a flammable liquid such as gasoline, alcohol, or napalm-like mixtures, with a cloth wick that serves as the fuse. Some variants add substances to make the fuel stick to surfaces or produce smoke. Military versions produced by armies have used self-igniting chemical fuses to avoid the need to light the weapon before throwing.

Legality

Molotov cocktails are illegal to manufacture or possess in many jurisdictions, where they are typically classified as destructive devices or incendiary weapons. Their use against personnel is restricted under international humanitarian law, and Protocol III of the Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons limits the use of incendiary weapons, particularly against civilians.
