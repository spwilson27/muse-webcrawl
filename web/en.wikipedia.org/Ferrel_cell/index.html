Ferrel cell

The Ferrel cell is a component of Earth's global atmospheric circulation, located in the mid-latitudes between roughly 30° and 60° latitude in both hemispheres. It lies between the Hadley cell to the equatorward side and the polar cell to the poleward side. The cell is named after William Ferrel (1817–1891), the American meteorologist who first described this circulation pattern in 1856.

Description

Unlike the thermally direct Hadley and polar cells, the Ferrel cell is thermally indirect: air rises near 60° latitude, flows poleward aloft, sinks near 30°, and returns equatorward... in fact, at the surface the Ferrel cell's flow is poleward, and, deflected by the Coriolis effect, gives rise to the prevailing westerlies of the mid-latitudes. Near the tropopause, the descending branch of the Ferrel cell meets the Hadley cell, and the strong temperature contrast there supports the subtropical jet stream.

Nature of the circulation

Modern meteorology understands the Ferrel cell less as a coherent ring of circulating air than as a statistical average produced by the continual passage of mid-latitude eddies — the cyclones and anticyclones of temperate weather. These eddies transport heat poleward and drive the surface westerlies through their momentum fluxes; the Ferrel cell is the Eulerian-mean circulation that results. Its strength and position shift seasonally, following the migration of the jet streams and storm tracks.
