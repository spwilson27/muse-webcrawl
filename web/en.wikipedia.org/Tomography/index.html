Tomography

Tomography
DefinitionImaging by sections through an object
Key methodsCT, PET, SPECT, MRI-related, ultrasound, seismic
Mathematical basisRadon transform (1917); inverse Radon transform
Key developersJohann Radon, Allan Cormack, Godfrey Hounsfield

Tomography is imaging by sections or sectioning: it reconstructs a two- or three-dimensional representation of the interior of an object from a series of measurements taken through it from different directions. The word derives from the Greek tomos (slice) and graphein (to write). Tomographic methods are fundamental to medical imaging and are also used in geophysics, materials science, archaeology, oceanography and industrial inspection.

Principles

Most tomographic techniques measure how some penetrating wave or particle — X-rays, gamma rays, ultrasound, seismic waves, or radio waves — is attenuated or delayed along many lines through the object. Mathematically, each measurement approximates a line integral of a property of the object. The collection of line integrals is described by the Radon transform, introduced by Johann Radon in 1917; reconstructing the image requires inverting it, classically by filtered back-projection and increasingly by iterative and model-based algorithms.

Types

In medicine, X-ray computed tomography (CT), developed commercially by Godfrey Hounsfield at EMI in the early 1970s using algorithms of Allan MacLeod Cormack — work that won both men the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine — produces cross-sections from X-ray attenuation. Emission tomography (PET and SPECT) maps radiotracer distributions. Electrical impedance tomography, optical coherence tomography, seismic tomography (which images Earth's interior from earthquake waves) and atom probe tomography apply the same principle at vastly different scales.

History

Before computers, "planigraphy" and conventional tomography moved an X-ray tube and film in opposite directions to blur out structures above and below a focal plane. Digital reconstruction from projections — first demonstrated by Cormack in 1963–64 and realised in Hounsfield's EMI scanner of 1971 — transformed radiology by yielding quantitative, truly cross-sectional images.
