Dmitry Grigorevich Pavlov (23 October 1897 – 22 July 1941) was a Soviet general who commanded the Western Front of the Red Army during the first days of the German invasion of the Soviet Union, Operation Barbarossa. His front's collapse in the opening weeks of the war led to his arrest and execution, one of the most notorious episodes of Stalin's punishment of failed commanders in 1941.

Career

Pavlov joined the Red Army in 1919 and served in the Russian Civil War. In the 1930s he became one of the Red Army's leading advocates of mechanised warfare, commanding tank units and serving as a military adviser in the Spanish Civil War, for which he received the title Hero of the Soviet Union in 1937. He became head of the Directorate of Armoured Forces in 1937 and commander of the Belorussian Military District in 1940. In February 1941 he was promoted to General of the Army, the second-highest rank, and given command of the Western Front, responsible for the defence of the western border, including the salient around Białystok.

Operation Barbarossa

When Germany attacked on 22 June 1941, the Western Front was struck by Army Group Centre. Within days, German panzer groups encircled the bulk of Pavlov's forces in the Białystok–Minsk pocket; Minsk fell on 28 June, and some 300,000 Soviet soldiers were captured. Pavlov's command and control had broken down, communications were destroyed, and his armies were in disarray. He was relieved of command on 30 June and arrested in early July. After a summary trial, he was convicted of cowardice and criminal inactivity and shot on 22 July 1941, together with several of his staff officers.

Rehabilitation

Historians have debated the fairness of Pavlov's fate: his front was indeed positioned disadvantageously, his forces were not fully mobilised, and Soviet strategy and equipment were ill-suited to the German onslaught. In 1957, during the Khrushchev Thaw, Pavlov was posthumously rehabilitated, his rank and honours restored.
