Amelogenesis imperfecta (AI) is a congenital disorder which presents with a rare abnormal formation of the enamel or external layer of the crown of teeth, unrelated to any systemic or generalized conditions. Enamel is composed mostly of mineral, that is formed and regulated by the proteins in it. Amelogenesis imperfecta is due to the malfunction of the proteins in the enamel — ameloblastin, enamelin, tuftelin, and amelogenin.

People afflicted with amelogenesis imperfecta may have teeth with abnormal colour (yellow, brown, or grey), a higher risk for dental cavities, teeth that are hypersensitive to temperature changes, and enamel that chips and wears rapidly. The condition affects both the primary (baby) teeth and the permanent dentition.

Classification

Amelogenesis imperfecta is broadly divided into three main types:

  • Hypoplastic type — enamel is reduced in quantity: thin, pitted, or grooved, but normally mineralised and hard.
  • Hypocalcified type — enamel is of normal thickness but poorly mineralised, soft, and easily lost, with a dull, opaque appearance.
  • Hypomaturation type — enamel is of near-normal thickness but incompletely matured, appearing mottled, opaque, and pigmented, and tending to chip.

Genetics

Mutations in genes including AMELX, ENAM, MMP20, KLK4, FAM83H, WDR72, and others have been identified as causes. Inheritance may be autosomal dominant, autosomal recessive, or X-linked, depending on the gene involved. AI can also occur as part of syndromes such as amelogenesis imperfecta with nephrocalcinosis.

Treatment

There is no cure; treatment focuses on preserving the teeth and restoring function and appearance. Preventative and restorative dental care includes fluoride treatments, crowns, veneers, and, in severe cases, full-coverage restorations. Early intervention helps manage sensitivity and reduce enamel loss.
