Walter Adolph Georg Gropius (18 May 1883 – 5 July 1969) was a German-American architect and founder of the Bauhaus School, who, along with Ludwig Mies van der Rohe, Le Corbusier, and Frank Lloyd Wright, is widely regarded as one of the pioneering masters of modernist architecture. He was a founder of the International Style.

Early career

Gropius studied architecture in Munich and Berlin and worked in the office of Peter Behrens, alongside Mies van der Rohe and Le Corbusier, before opening his own practice with Adolf Meyer in 1910. Their Fagus Factory in Alfeld (1911), with its glass curtain walls and lack of applied ornament, is considered a landmark of early modernism. His design for the Werkbund exhibition building in Cologne (1914) further established his reputation.

The Bauhaus

In 1919 Gropius became director of the merged art academy and arts-and-crafts school in Weimar, which he renamed the Bauhaus. His founding manifesto proclaimed the unity of art, craft, and technology. Under his leadership the school moved from an expressionist, craft-based orientation toward industrial design—summarized in his 1923 slogan "Art and Technology: a new unity"—and included teachers such as Paul Klee, Wassily Kandinsky, László Moholy-Nagy, and Josef Albers. In 1925 the school moved to Dessau, into the Bauhaus Building designed by Gropius, an icon of functionalist architecture with its asymmetric composition, glass workshop wing, and studio balcony block. Gropius resigned as director in 1928 and was succeeded by Hannes Meyer.

Exile and America

Gropius left Germany in 1934, as the Nazis came to power, living first in London, where he worked with Maxwell Fry, and emigrating to the United States in 1937. He became chair of architecture at Harvard's Graduate School of Design, reshaping American architectural education, and founded The Architects Collaborative (TAC) in 1945. His own Gropius House in Lincoln, Massachusetts (1938), adapted International Style principles to New England materials and climate. Later works included the Harvard Graduate Center (1949–50) and, as consultant, the Pan Am Building in New York (1963).

Legacy

Gropius's influence operated as much through teaching and institutions as through buildings: the Bauhaus model of design education spread worldwide, and his students and collaborators—including Marcel Breuer—carried modernism across the Atlantic. Critics have debated the social legacy of the movement he helped found, but his Dessau buildings, inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List, remain definitive monuments of twentieth-century architecture.
