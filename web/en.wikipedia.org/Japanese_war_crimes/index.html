Japanese war crimes

Japanese war crimes are the crimes committed by the armed forces and authorities of the Empire of Japan during its period of expansion, particularly in the Second Sino-Japanese War (1937–1945) and the Pacific War (1941–1945). They include mass killings of civilians and prisoners of war, biological and chemical warfare, forced labour, sexual slavery (the "comfort women" system), and inhumane human experimentation. Estimates of the death toll vary widely: historian R. J. Rummel estimated that Japanese militarism caused between roughly 3 and 10 million deaths, while total Chinese wartime deaths from all causes in 1937–45 are commonly estimated at 15–20 million.

Background

Japan's military conduct in its earlier wars (1894–95, 1904–05) was generally regarded as comparatively disciplined, and Japan had ratified the Hague Conventions. From 1931, however, a combination of ultranationalist ideology, the doctrine of the imperial way, contempt for surrender instilled in Japanese troops, and the brutalisation of a long war in China produced systematic atrocities. Japan had signed but not ratified the 1929 Geneva Convention on prisoners of war, and its military code treated surrender as dishonour — an attitude extended, with lethal effect, to enemy prisoners and occupied populations.

Major crimes

China

During the capture of Nanjing in December 1937–January 1938, Japanese troops committed mass murder of Chinese civilians and disarmed soldiers, widespread rape, looting, and arson — the Nanjing Massacre. Death toll estimates range from tens of thousands in some Japanese accounts to the 300,000 figure maintained by China; the International Military Tribunal for the Far East found that more than 200,000 civilians and prisoners were killed in the first weeks. The "Three Alls Policy" (sankō sakusen — "kill all, burn all, loot all") applied in counter-insurgency campaigns in North China from 1941 caused enormous civilian losses.

Biological and chemical warfare

Unit 731, based at Pingfang near Harbin under Shirō Ishii, conducted lethal experiments on thousands of prisoners and deployed plague, cholera, and anthrax weapons against Chinese cities and armies. Other units operated in Changchun, Nanking, and elsewhere. Japan also used chemical weapons extensively in China despite having signed the Geneva Protocol.

Prisoners of war and forced labour

Allied prisoners died at rates far exceeding those in German captivity — roughly a quarter to a third of Western POWs died, against about 4% in German hands. Notorious episodes include the Bataan Death March (1942), the Sandakan Death Marches (1945, of some 2,400 Australian and British prisoners only six survived), and the construction of the Burma–Thailand Railway, on which some 12,000–13,000 Allied POWs and perhaps 90,000 Asian rōmusha labourers died. Millions of Chinese, Korean, and Southeast Asian labourers were conscripted; Korean and Chinese workers in Japanese mines and factories, and POWs transported in unmarked "hell ships," suffered heavy mortality.

Comfort women

The Imperial Japanese military established a system of brothels staffed by "comfort women" — women and girls, mostly Korean but also Chinese, Filipina, Dutch, and others — many deceived, coerced, or abducted into sexual slavery. Estimates of their number range from tens of thousands to several hundred thousand. The issue remains a major point of contention in Japan's relations with South Korea and China.

Southeast Asia and the Pacific

Atrocities accompanied occupation across Southeast Asia: the Sook Ching massacres in Singapore and Malaya (1942), the massacre of patients and staff at Alexandra Hospital in Singapore, the Laha and other killings of Australian prisoners, cannibalism cases in New Guinea, and the Manila massacre of February 1945, in which about 100,000 Filipino civilians were killed during the battle for the city.

Trials and legal aftermath

The International Military Tribunal for the Far East (1946–48) tried 28 "Class A" defendants for crimes against peace, war crimes, and crimes against humanity; seven, including Prime Minister Hideki Tōjō, were executed. Allied national tribunals across Asia and the Pacific tried several thousand "Class B and C" suspects. Emperor Hirohito was not indicted, a decision shaped by American occupation policy under Douglas MacArthur. Personnel of Unit 731 were controversially granted immunity in exchange for their experimental data, while the Soviet Union separately tried some in the 1949 Khabarovsk trial.

Memory and controversy

Japan has issued numerous official apologies, including the Kono Statement (1993) on comfort women and the Murayama Statement (1995), and paid reparations under the San Francisco Treaty and bilateral agreements. Nevertheless, disputes persist over Japanese history textbooks, visits by politicians to Yasukuni Shrine (where convicted Class A war criminals are enshrined), and the adequacy of compensation — issues that continue to strain relations with China and South Korea. Within Japan, debate between acknowledgment, revisionism, and denial remains politically charged.
