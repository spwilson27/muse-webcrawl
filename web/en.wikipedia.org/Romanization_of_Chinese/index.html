The romanization of Chinese is the use of the Latin alphabet to write Chinese languages. Because Chinese characters are logographic and do not systematically encode pronunciation, romanization schemes transcribe the sounds of spoken Chinese — most often Standard Mandarin — for learners, dictionaries, maps, signage, and computing. The dominant system today is Hanyu Pinyin, adopted by the People's Republic of China in 1958 and by the United Nations and ISO as the standard for Chinese names; earlier systems such as Wade–Giles long shaped how Chinese words entered English (Peking, Tao, kung fu, versus pinyin Beijing, Dao, gongfu).

Early systems

The first romanizations were devised by Catholic missionaries in the late Ming dynasty: Michele Ruggieri and Matteo Ricci drafted a Portuguese-based transcription around 1605, and Nicolas Trigault systematised one in 1626. Protestant missionaries and diplomats produced competing English-based systems in the 19th century, including the romanization used by the Chinese Imperial Post Office for place names ("postal romanization": Peking, Canton, Amoy). In 1867 Thomas Wade published the system refined by Herbert Giles in his 1892 dictionary into the Wade–Giles system, which dominated English-language sinology for most of the 20th century, with spellings such as Mao Tse-tung and Chou En-lai.

Chinese-designed systems

Chinese reformers, seeking literacy and language unification, created their own schemes: the phonetic symbols of Zhuyin (Bopomofo, 1918), still used in Taiwan for teaching and input; Gwoyeu Romatzyh (1928), devised by Y. R. Chao and colleagues, which ingeniously spells tone through letter patterns (e.g., guo/gwo/gwoo/guoh) but proved hard to learn; and Latinxua Sin Wenz (Latinised New Writing) of the 1930s. Zhou Youguang and a committee then developed Hanyu Pinyin, promulgated in 1958 — a clean alphabetic transcription with tone marks (mā má mǎ mà) that became the PRC's official standard in 1979 for foreign use, an ISO standard (ISO 7098) in 1982, and the system of the UN and most world media. Taiwan officially adopted Hanyu Pinyin in 2009 after years of promoting the rival Tongyong Pinyin (2002).

Other Chinese varieties

Cantonese is romanised in the Linguistic Society of Hong Kong's Jyutping (1993) and in Yale Cantonese, while Hong Kong place and personal names use an unsystematic colonial romanization (Cheung Chau, Tsim Sha Tsui). Taiwanese Hokkien is written in Pe̍h-ōe-jī, a missionary system from the 19th century, and Hakka in Pha̍k-fa-sṳ. Such systems double as tools for writing the spoken languages, not merely transcribing Mandarin.

Uses and characteristics

Romanization serves language teaching (annotating characters for learners), dictionaries and indexes (alphabetical ordering), cartography and passports, library cataloguing (ALA-LC), and — decisively in the digital era — input methods: pinyin-based IMEs are the principal way Chinese speakers type characters on phones and computers. Phonemic accuracy varies by system: pinyin uses some letters idiosyncratically (q, x, c, zh) and omits tone marks in most everyday contexts, leaving ambiguity (e.g., Shaanxi, written with double a to distinguish it from Shanxi). Debates over romanization have always carried cultural weight — touching national identity, literacy reform, and even proposals, now abandoned, to replace characters with the alphabet entirely.
