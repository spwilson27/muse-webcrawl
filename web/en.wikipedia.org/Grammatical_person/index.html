Grammatical person

In linguistics, grammatical person is the distinction between deictic references to participants in a speech event: the speaker, the addressee, and others who are neither. Typically, languages distinguish first person (the speaker: I, we), second person (the person spoken to: you), and third person (anyone else: he, she, it, they). Person is marked on pronouns and often by verb agreement in conjugation.

The three persons

The first person refers to the speaker or a group including the speaker; the second person to the addressee or a group including the addressee but not the speaker; the third person to anyone else — people, animals, objects, or abstractions. In English, person distinctions appear in pronouns (I/you/he, me/you/him), possessives (my/your/his), and in the present-tense third-person singular verb ending -s ("she walks").

Additional distinctions

Many languages split first-person plural into inclusive "we" (including the addressee) and exclusive "we" (excluding them) — a distinction found in many Austronesian, Australian Aboriginal, and Indigenous American languages. Algonquian languages distinguish two third persons, a proximate and an obviative (sometimes called a "fourth person"). Many European languages historically distinguished familiar and formal second persons (French tu/vous, German du/Sie), and some languages have separate person systems for different politeness registers, as in Japanese and Korean honorifics.

Person and agreement

Person combines with number and sometimes gender in agreement paradigms. In pro-drop languages such as Spanish and Italian, subject pronouns can be omitted because the verb ending itself encodes person and number (hablo "I speak", hablan "they speak").
