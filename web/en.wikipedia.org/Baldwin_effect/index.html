Baldwin effect

The Baldwin effect, also known as Baldwinian evolution or ontogenic evolution, is a theory of evolution proposed in 1896 by the psychologist James Mark Baldwin which suggests that an organism's ability to learn new behaviours can affect its reproductive success and therefore the genetic makeup of its species. Put simply, learned or otherwise plastically acquired traits can create selective conditions under which similar traits produced directly by genetic variation are favoured, so that over generations a trait that first appeared through learning or phenotypic plasticity may become genetically assimilated.

History

Baldwin proposed the effect in his 1896 paper "A New Factor in Evolution," and similar ideas were advanced independently in the same period by Conwy Lloyd Morgan and Henry Fairfield Osborn. The idea was offered partly as a reconciliation between Darwinian natural selection and the apparent inheritance of acquired characteristics associated with Lamarckism: organisms that learn adaptive behaviours survive, and their descendants may then be selected for innate versions of those behaviours, without any direct inheritance of acquired traits.

Mechanism

The proposed mechanism has two broad steps. First, phenotypic plasticity — including learning — allows individuals to produce adaptive responses to a novel environment within their lifetimes, keeping the population alive in conditions it could not otherwise tolerate. Second, this survival changes the selective landscape: mutations and recombinations that produce the adaptive trait more reliably, more cheaply, or without the need for learning are then favoured by natural selection, a process related to what Conrad Hal Waddington later called genetic assimilation.

Reception and significance

The Baldwin effect was largely sidelined during the consolidation of the modern evolutionary synthesis, but interest revived in the later twentieth century, notably through computer simulations such as the 1987 model by Geoffrey Hinton and Steven Nowlan, which showed that learning could smooth a rugged fitness landscape and accelerate genetic evolution. The concept now features in discussions of niche construction, developmental plasticity, and evolutionary developmental biology, though its importance in nature remains debated.
