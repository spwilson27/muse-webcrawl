Mithridates VI Eupator

King of Pontus
Reign: 120 – 63 BC
Born: c. 135 BC, Sinope
Died: 63 BC, Panticapaeum
Also known as: Mithridates the Great

Mithridates VI Eupator (Greek: Μιθριδάτης Εὐπάτωρ; c. 135 – 63 BC) was the king of Pontus in northern Anatolia and one of the Roman Republic's most formidable and enduring enemies. His decades of resistance in the Mithridatic Wars made him the greatest threat to Rome in the East since Hannibal.

Early life

Mithridates claimed descent from both Persian royalty — the Achaemenid kings Cyrus and Darius — and from the successors of Alexander the Great. After his father's assassination, legend holds that the young king withdrew into the wilderness for seven years to avoid the plots of his mother and regents, hardening himself and studying the languages of his many subject peoples; he was said to speak more than twenty languages.

Wars with Rome

In 88 BC Mithridates invaded the Roman province of Asia and coordinated the massacre of tens of thousands of Roman and Italian residents — the Asiatic Vespers. The ensuing First Mithridatic War pitted him against Sulla. In the Second and Third Mithridatic Wars he faced Lucullus and finally Pompey, who defeated him decisively in 66 BC. Mithridates fled to the Bosporan Kingdom, where, betrayed by his son Pharnaces and facing capture, he died in 63 BC.

Mithridatism

Mithridates was famed for his knowledge of poisons and antidotes. Fearing assassination, he reportedly immunised himself by ingesting gradually increasing doses of toxins — a practice now called mithridatism. According to legend, when he tried to poison himself at the end, the antidotes in his system neutralised the poison, and he ordered a bodyguard to kill him with a sword. The universal antidote associated with his name, mithridatium, remained in pharmacopoeias for nearly two thousand years.
