Front-engine, front-wheel-drive layout

Automobile drivetrain layout, abbreviated FF

In automotive design, a front-engine, front-wheel-drive layout (FF layout) places both the internal combustion engine and the driven wheels at the front of the vehicle. It is the dominant layout for passenger cars worldwide, used in most hatchbacks, sedans and many crossover SUVs.

Design

The engine is usually mounted transversely (sideways) in front of or over the front axle line, in a combined unit with the gearbox and final drive known as a transaxle. Because the entire drivetrain is concentrated in one compartment, no propeller shaft or transmission tunnel is needed, leaving a flat floor and more passenger and luggage space for a given exterior size. The concentration of weight over the driven wheels also gives good traction in slippery conditions.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include lower manufacturing cost, lighter weight, better interior packaging and safe, predictable handling — front-drive cars tend to understeer at the limit, which is considered more manageable for ordinary drivers. Disadvantages include torque steer under hard acceleration, unequal-length driveshaft effects, a practical limit on engine power and size, and less favourable weight distribution for high-performance driving, which is why sports cars generally retain rear-wheel drive.

History

Early examples include the 1931 DKW F1 and the 1934 Citroën Traction Avant. The layout was popularised in its modern transverse form by the 1959 Mini designed by Alec Issigonis, and became the industry standard for small and mid-size cars from the 1970s onward, adopted by makers such as Volkswagen, Honda and Toyota.
