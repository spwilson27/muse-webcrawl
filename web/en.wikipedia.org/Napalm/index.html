Napalm

Napalm is an incendiary mixture of a gelling agent and a volatile petrochemical, usually gasoline, used in warfare. Developed in the United States in 1942 by a team led by chemist Louis Fieser at Harvard University, its name is a portmanteau of naphthenic acid and palmitic acid, the original thickening agents. The gel sticks to surfaces and burns at temperatures of 800–1,200 °C (1,470–2,190 °F) for far longer than plain fuel.

Use

Napalm was used extensively by American forces in the Second World War (including in flamethrowers and firebombing), the Korean War and the Vietnam War, where it became a potent symbol of the conflict — notably in the 1972 photograph of Phan Thi Kim Phuc fleeing a napalm strike. The later formulation "Napalm B" used polystyrene, benzene and gasoline.

Legality

The 1980 United Nations Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons, Protocol III, prohibits the use of incendiary weapons against civilians and against military targets located within concentrations of civilians, though it does not ban napalm in all uses and some states have not ratified it. Many countries have destroyed their stockpiles; the United States signed Protocol III in 2009 with a reservation.
