Incretins are a group of metabolic hormones released from the gut after eating that augment the secretion of insulin from the pancreatic beta cells at a given level of blood glucose. The two principal incretins are glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), secreted by L cells of the lower intestine, and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP), secreted by K cells of the upper intestine.

The incretin effect

The existence of incretins was inferred from the "incretin effect": glucose taken by mouth provokes a much larger insulin response than the same glucose given intravenously, even when blood glucose levels are matched. The difference — estimated to account for roughly half or more of the insulin secreted after a meal — was attributed to signals from the gut, and the responsible hormones were isolated in the later 20th century. In people with type 2 diabetes the incretin effect is blunted, partly because the insulin-stimulating action of GIP is largely lost, whereas GLP-1 retains much of its effect.

Actions

Incretins act only when glucose is elevated, so they carry little risk of provoking hypoglycaemia on their own. GLP-1 additionally suppresses glucagon secretion, slows gastric emptying and promotes satiety in the brain — the basis of its weight-reducing effect. Both hormones are broken down within minutes by the enzyme dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4).

Therapeutic use

Two drug classes exploit the incretin system. GLP-1 receptor agonists (such as exenatide, liraglutide and semaglutide) are DPP-4-resistant analogues that powerfully lower blood glucose and body weight and have shown cardiovascular benefits; they have also been approved for obesity treatment. DPP-4 inhibitors (such as sitagliptin) are oral drugs that raise the body's own incretin levels more modestly. Dual GIP/GLP-1 agonists such as tirzepatide extend the approach still further.
