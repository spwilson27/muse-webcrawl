A spiral antenna is a type of radio frequency antenna shaped as a two-arm spiral, or sometimes a one- or four-arm spiral. It belongs to the class of frequency-independent antennas: because its shape is defined entirely by angles (self-similarity), its impedance and radiation pattern remain nearly constant over a very wide bandwidth, often 10:1 or more. Spiral antennas radiate circularly polarised waves, the sense of polarisation being determined by the direction in which the spiral winds.

Design

The most common forms are the planar Archimedean spiral and the equiangular (logarithmic) spiral, printed on a dielectric substrate and often backed by a shallow cavity (usually lined with absorber) to make the radiation unidirectional. Radiation occurs mainly from the "active region" of the spiral where the circumference is about one wavelength. The operating band is set by the geometry: the outer diameter fixes the lowest frequency and the inner feed region the highest.

Feeding the balanced spiral arms from an unbalanced coaxial line requires a wideband balun, often a tapered microstrip ("Marchand" or infinite balun). Conical spiral antennas, wound on a cone, are a three-dimensional variant with similar wideband behaviour.

Applications

Because of their bandwidth and circular polarisation, spiral antennas are widely used in electronic warfare and signals intelligence receivers, radar warning receivers on aircraft, spectrum monitoring and direction finding, telemetry, and satellite communications. In consumer devices, small spiral-derived designs appear in GPS and RFID applications.

History

The spiral antenna was introduced in the mid-1950s, with Edwin M. Turner credited for the planar concept at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base, and John D. Dyson publishing key analyses at the University of Illinois around 1959. It became one of the canonical frequency-independent antennas alongside the log-periodic array, following Victor Rumsey's principle.
