Sejm of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Sejm in session at the Royal Castle in Warsaw.

The Sejm of the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth (Polish: Sejm walny) was the bicameral parliament of the Commonwealth, one of the three pillars of its political system alongside the Senate and the elected king. It comprised the Chamber of Deputies (Izba Poselska), elected by the local dietines (sejmiki) of the nobility, and the Senate, consisting of bishops, voivodes, castellans, and ministers. Together with the king, these formed the three "estates of the Sejm".

History and powers

The general Sejm evolved from ad hoc assemblies of the Polish nobility and was institutionalized in the 15th and 16th centuries; the statute Nihil novi of 1505 required the king to obtain the Sejm's consent for new laws. After the Union of Lublin in 1569, a single Sejm served the unified Commonwealth. Its powers included legislation, taxation, foreign policy, declarations of war, and the election of kings (free election held at Wola outside Warsaw, in which all nobles could participate). Ordinary sessions were to be held every two years for six weeks.

The liberum veto

Decisions required unanimity, and from the mid-17th century this hardened into the liberum veto, under which a single deputy could dissolve the session and annul all its acts. First used to break up a Sejm in 1652, the veto — increasingly manipulated by magnates and foreign powers — paralyzed governance in the 18th century, contributing to the Commonwealth's decline and partitions.

Reform and end

The Great Sejm (1788–1792) abolished the liberum veto and enacted the Constitution of 3 May 1791, Europe's second modern constitution. The reforms were overturned by Russian intervention and the Targowica Confederation; the last Sejm, held at Grodno in 1793 under Russian coercion, ratified the Second Partition. The Commonwealth disappeared with the Third Partition in 1795.
