Brigid of Kildare

Saint Brigid holding a crozier and a lamp

Brigid of Kildare (c. 451 – c. 525), also known as Saint Brigid and "Mary of the Gael," is one of Ireland's three patron saints, alongside Saint Patrick and Saint Columba. An early Christian abbess, she is credited with founding the double monastery of Kildare (Cill Dara, "church of the oak") and is venerated in Ireland and the Scottish Hebrides. Her feast day is 1 February.

Life

According to tradition, Brigid was born at Faughart, County Louth, to a pagan chieftain father and a Christian slave mother. Refusing marriage, she took the veil — legend says from Saint Patrick or Saint Mel — and gathered a community of women. At Kildare she founded a monastery for both men and women, unusual for the time, and a school of art famed for illuminated manuscripts such as the lost Book of Kildare. Tales emphasise her generosity: giving away her father's sword and butter to the poor, and miraculously multiplying food and ale for guests.

Legends and traditions

At Kildare a perpetual fire was said to be kept burning in her honour, tended by nineteen nuns — a feature scholars link to pre-Christian worship of the goddess Brigid, with whom the saint shares a name, feast day (Imbolc, 1 February), and associations with fire, healing, and poetry. Brigid's crosses, woven from rushes, remain a familiar Irish emblem, and holy wells dedicated to her dot the country.

Veneration

Brigid is patroness of Ireland, dairymaids, midwives, poets, blacksmiths, and healers. In 2023 her feast day became a public holiday in Ireland. Her relics were traditionally kept at Downpatrick with those of Patrick and Columba.
