Zoning

Zoning is a system of land-use regulation used by local governments in many countries to divide a municipality into districts, or zones, each with rules governing what may be built and how land may be used. Typical zones separate residential, commercial, industrial and agricultural uses, and regulations may also control building height, density, lot size, setbacks and parking.

History

Modern zoning is often traced to New York City's 1916 Zoning Resolution, adopted in response to tall buildings such as the Equitable Building that cast large shadows. The constitutionality of comprehensive zoning in the United States was upheld by the Supreme Court in Village of Euclid v. Ambler Realty Co. (1926), giving rise to the term Euclidean zoning. Other traditions, notably in much of Europe and Japan, regulate land use differently, often with more mixed uses.

Types

Single-use (Euclidean) zoning separates uses strictly. Form-based codes instead regulate the physical form of buildings. Inclusionary zoning requires or incentivises affordable housing in new developments, while exclusionary zoning has been criticised for reinforcing segregation and limiting housing supply. Variances, special-use permits and rezoning provide flexibility within the system.

Criticism and reform

Critics argue that low-density single-family zoning raises housing costs and encourages sprawl and car dependence. Reform movements advocate upzoning, transit-oriented development and the elimination of parking minimums.
