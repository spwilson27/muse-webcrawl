The United States dollar (symbol: $; currency code: USD) is the official currency of the United States and several other countries. The Coinage Act of 1792 introduced the U.S. dollar at par with the Spanish silver dollar, divided it into 100 cents, and authorized the minting of coins denominated in dollars and cents. U.S. banknotes are issued in the form of Federal Reserve Notes, popularly called greenbacks due to their predominantly green color.

History

The dollar was defined under a bimetallic standard of silver and gold. The gold standard was progressively abandoned in the 20th century: domestic convertibility ended in 1933, and under the Bretton Woods system after 1944 the dollar was pegged to gold at $35 per troy ounce while other currencies were pegged to the dollar. In 1971 President Richard Nixon suspended convertibility (the "Nixon shock"), and by 1973 the dollar had become a freely floating fiat currency.

International use

The dollar is the world's primary reserve currency, held by central banks and used to price commodities such as oil and gold. As of recent years, it has accounted for roughly 58–59 percent of disclosed global foreign exchange reserves and is involved in the large majority of global foreign-exchange transactions. Several countries officially use the U.S. dollar (dollarization), and many more peg their currencies to it.

Coins and banknotes

Coins are produced by the United States Mint and banknotes by the Bureau of Engraving and Printing. Circulating denominations include the penny, nickel, dime, and quarter; half-dollar and dollar coins are minted but circulate rarely. Federal Reserve Notes are printed in denominations of $1 through $100; larger denominations were discontinued in 1969 but remain legal tender.

Monetary policy

The value and supply of the dollar are influenced by the Federal Reserve, the central bank created in 1913, which conducts monetary policy through tools such as open market operations and the federal funds rate target.
