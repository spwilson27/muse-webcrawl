Ventricular septal defect

A ventricular septal defect (VSD) is a birth defect of the heart in which there is a hole in the interventricular septum, the wall dividing the left and right ventricles. It is the most common congenital heart defect. VSDs also occur as a complication of myocardial infarction in adults.

SpecialtyCardiology, paediatrics
SymptomsOften none; large defects: breathlessness, poor feeding, failure to thrive
ComplicationsHeart failure, pulmonary hypertension, Eisenmenger syndrome, endocarditis
DiagnosisEchocardiography; heart murmur on examination

Signs and pathophysiology

Because left ventricular pressure exceeds right ventricular pressure, blood shunts left-to-right through the defect, overloading the pulmonary circulation and the left heart. Small "restrictive" defects may produce only a loud pansystolic murmur — classically described as a heart that is "much ado about nothing." Large unrepaired shunts can cause pulmonary vascular disease; eventually the shunt may reverse (Eisenmenger syndrome), producing cyanosis.

Types

VSDs are classified by location: perimembranous (most common, ~80%), muscular (which often close spontaneously in childhood), inlet (atrioventricular septal), and outlet/supracristal defects. VSD is also a component of tetralogy of Fallot and occurs with increased frequency in Down syndrome.

Treatment

Many small defects close on their own and require only observation and endocarditis precautions. Large defects causing heart failure or pulmonary hypertension are treated by surgical patch closure, usually in infancy; selected defects can be closed with catheter-delivered devices. Prognosis after successful repair is generally excellent.
