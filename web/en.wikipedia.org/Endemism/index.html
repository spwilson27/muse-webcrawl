Endemism

Endemism is the state of a species being found only in a defined geographic location, such as an island, state, nation, country, or other delimited habitat, with no naturally occurring populations elsewhere. A species found in such a single place is said to be endemic to it. The opposite of an endemic species is one with a cosmopolitan distribution, found worldwide. The term is most used in ecology and biogeography, though a related usage exists in epidemiology (see endemic (epidemiology)).

Causes

Endemism arises through isolation, adaptation to unique local conditions, and limited dispersal. Islands are classic engines of endemism: the Galápagos finches and tortoises, the lemurs of Madagascar, and the flora and fauna of New Zealand evolved largely cut off from mainland competitors and predators. Mountain ranges, lakes such as Baikal and Tanganyika, and isolated valleys (refugia) can likewise hold high proportions of endemic species.

Palaeoendemics and neoendemics

Palaeoendemics are ancient lineages that once were widespread but are now restricted to a small area — for example the ginkgo tree in China or the coelacanth. Neoendemics are recently evolved species that have not yet spread beyond their place of origin, common among rapidly radiating island groups such as Hawaiian honeycreepers.

Conservation significance

Because endemic species exist nowhere else, their extinction is global and irreversible. Regions with many endemics — biodiversity hotspots such as Madagascar, the Cape Floristic Region, and the California Floristic Province — are conservation priorities. Endemics are often especially vulnerable to habitat loss, climate change, and introduced species, since their ranges are small and their populations may be adapted to narrow conditions.
