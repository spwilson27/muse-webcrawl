Nitinol

Nitinol (nickel titanium, also known as NiTi) is a metal alloy of nickel and titanium in roughly equal atomic percentages, distinguished by two remarkable properties: the shape-memory effect and superelasticity (pseudoelasticity). Its name combines its composition with the place of its development, the Naval Ordnance Laboratory, where William J. Buehler and Frederick Wang discovered its properties in the early 1960s.

Properties

Nitinol exists in two crystal phases. In its high-temperature austenite phase it is strong and rigid; when cooled it transforms to martensite, which can be deformed easily. Reheating restores the original "remembered" shape. In its superelastic regime, the alloy can sustain strains of up to about 8% and spring back completely, far beyond ordinary metals. It is also corrosion-resistant and biocompatible.

Applications

Nitinol is widely used in medicine — in self-expanding vascular stents, orthodontic archwires, guidewires, and surgical instruments — as well as in eyeglass frames, mobile phone antennas, actuators, couplings for aircraft hydraulics (Cryofit connectors), and temperature-sensitive devices such as fire sprinkler valves.
