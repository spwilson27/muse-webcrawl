Semi-active radar homing

Semi-active radar homing
AbbreviationSARH
TypeMissile guidance system
PrincipleMissile homes on radar energy reflected from the target and supplied by an external illuminator
ExamplesAIM-7 Sparrow, R-27, SM-2 (midcourse/terminal), Buk

Semi-active radar homing (SARH) is a common type of missile guidance system, perhaps the most common for longer-range air-to-air and surface-to-air missile systems. The missile itself contains only a radar receiver: the target must be "illuminated" by a separate radar — on the launching aircraft, ship, or ground battery — and the missile homes on the reflected signal.

Principle

The seeker in the missile's nose detects the radar energy scattered by the target and computes a course to intercept, usually using proportional navigation. Because the missile carries no transmitter, its seeker can be simpler, lighter, and cheaper than in an active radar missile, and the powerful external radar gives good range and resistance to jamming at long distances.

Disadvantages

The launching platform must keep its radar pointed at the target for the entire engagement, which limits manoeuvring, prevents "fire-and-forget" tactics, and historically restricted the number of targets that could be engaged simultaneously. This vulnerability motivated the development of active radar missiles such as the AIM-120 AMRAAM, which need support only in midcourse. Some systems, like the US Navy's SM-2, use inertial and command midcourse guidance with semi-active homing only in the terminal phase, combined with time-sharing of phased-array illuminators.

History

SARH became practical in the 1950s and armed the AIM-7 Sparrow family, used extensively in the Vietnam War with initially poor results that improved with later versions. Soviet counterparts include the R-23, R-24, and R-27. Despite the spread of active seekers, SARH remains widespread in naval air defence. See also active radar homing and missile guidance.
