Oscar Ribeiro de Almeida Niemeyer Soares Filho (15 December 1907 – 5 December 2012) was a Brazilian architect considered to be one of the key figures in the development of modern architecture. He is best known for designing the civic buildings of Brasília, Brazil's new capital inaugurated in 1960, which is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Early life and career

Niemeyer was born in Rio de Janeiro and graduated as an architectural engineer in 1934. He joined the team working under Lúcio Costa and Le Corbusier on the Ministry of Education and Health building in Rio, a landmark of Brazilian modernism. His design for the Brazilian pavilion at the 1939 New York World's Fair and the Pampulha complex in Belo Horizonte (1940–43), with its free-form concrete curves, brought him international attention.

Brasília

In 1956, President Juscelino Kubitschek invited Niemeyer to design the major buildings of the new capital, Brasília, planned by Lúcio Costa. Niemeyer designed the principal palaces — the National Congress, the Cathedral of Brasília, the Palácio da Alvorada, the Palácio Itamaraty, and the Palácio do Planalto — using reinforced concrete in bold sculptural forms. He famously said he was attracted "by the curve — free, sensual — which I find in the mountains of my country, in the sinuous course of its rivers, in the waves of the ocean."

Exile and later work

A lifelong communist, Niemeyer went into exile in Europe after Brazil's 1964 military coup, designing works such as the French Communist Party headquarters in Paris. Returning to Brazil in the 1980s, he continued working into his second century, completing the Niterói Contemporary Art Museum in 1996 and the Oscar Niemeyer Museum in Curitiba in 2002. He died in 2012, ten days short of his 105th birthday, having designed over 600 projects.
