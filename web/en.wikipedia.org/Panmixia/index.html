Panmixia

Panmixia (or panmixis) is a pattern of mating in which individuals in a population mate entirely at random, with no preference or restriction based on genotype, phenotype, location, or relatedness. A panmictic population is one in which all individuals are potential partners, so that gene flow is unrestricted throughout the population.

Role in population genetics

Random mating is a central assumption of the Hardy–Weinberg principle: under panmixia, with no selection, mutation, migration, or genetic drift, allele and genotype frequencies remain constant across generations. Departures from panmixia — such as inbreeding, assortative mating, population subdivision (the Wahlund effect), or sexual selection — cause genotype frequencies to deviate from Hardy–Weinberg expectations and can create population structure.

Examples and limitations

Few natural populations are perfectly panmictic, because distance, behaviour, and ecology limit mate choice; however, some marine species with planktonic larvae and broadcast spawning (many fish and invertebrates) approach panmixia over large areas. Genetic studies often test for panmixia as a null hypothesis when assessing stock structure in fisheries and conservation biology: significant genetic differentiation between samples indicates that a population is not panmictic and should be managed as separate units.
