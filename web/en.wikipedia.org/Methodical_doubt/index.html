Cartesian doubt

Cartesian doubt, also called methodical doubt or methodological scepticism, is a systematic process of questioning the truth of one's beliefs, developed by René Descartes in his Meditations on First Philosophy (1641) and Discourse on the Method (1637). Descartes resolved to reject as false anything that admitted even the slightest doubt, in order to rebuild knowledge on an absolutely certain foundation.

The method

Descartes argued that sense perceptions can deceive; that in dreams one may believe oneself awake, so experience cannot prove the existence of the external world; and, as a limiting hypothesis, that an evil demon might systematically deceive him even about mathematics. This radical, "hyperbolic" doubt was not an end in itself but an instrument for finding what cannot be doubted.

Cogito ergo sum

The first certainty to survive the doubt is the thinker's own existence while thinking: cogito, ergo sum — "I think, therefore I am." From this foundation Descartes attempted to prove God's existence and, through God's veracity, restore confidence in clear and distinct ideas and the external world.

Criticism and influence

Critics have questioned whether the cogito smuggles in assumptions about the self, and whether Descartes' appeal to God is circular (the "Cartesian circle"). Methodical doubt nonetheless founded modern epistemology and rationalist philosophy.
