Hypocretin

Hypocretin-1 (orexin-A), a 33-amino-acid peptide.

Hypocretin, also known as orexin, is a neuropeptide that regulates arousal, wakefulness, and appetite. It exists in two forms, hypocretin-1 and hypocretin-2 (orexin-A and orexin-B), derived from a common precursor protein, prepro-hypocretin. The peptides are produced exclusively by a small cluster of neurons—only some 10,000 to 80,000 cells—in the lateral hypothalamus and perifornical area of the human brain, which project widely throughout the central nervous system.

Discovery

Hypocretin was discovered in 1998 by two groups independently: one identified the peptides as hypothalamic regulators resembling secretin, naming them from hypothalamus and secretin; the other found them by orphan-receptor screening and named them orexins (from Greek orexis, appetite) for their appetite-stimulating effect when injected into rats.

Function and narcolepsy

Hypocretin neurons stabilise wakefulness by exciting arousal centres including the locus coeruleus, tuberomammillary nucleus, and raphe nuclei. Loss of these neurons causes narcolepsy type 1: cerebrospinal-fluid hypocretin-1 is absent or very low in most patients with narcolepsy with cataplexy, and the condition is believed to result from autoimmune destruction of hypocretin cells. Dogs with hereditary narcolepsy carry a mutation in the hypocretin receptor 2 gene.

Clinical significance

Blocking hypocretin receptors promotes sleep: dual orexin receptor antagonists such as suvorexant, lemborexant, and daridorexant are approved treatments for insomnia. Conversely, orexin agonists are under development as treatments for narcolepsy. The system also participates in feeding, reward, and energy homeostasis.
