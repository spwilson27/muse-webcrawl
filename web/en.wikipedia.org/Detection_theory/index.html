Detection theory

Detection theory or signal detection theory is a means to measure the ability to differentiate between information-bearing patterns (called stimulus in living organisms, signal in machines) and random patterns that distract from the information (called noise, consisting of background stimuli and random activity of the detection machine and of the nervous system of the operator).

History

The theory grew out of radar engineering during the Second World War, where operators had to decide whether a blip on a screen represented an enemy aircraft or noise. It was formalised for psychology in the 1950s by researchers such as Wilson Tanner and John Swets, applying statistical decision theory to human perception experiments.

Basic concepts

Every detection task involves a trade-off between two errors: a miss (failing to detect a present signal) and a false alarm (responding to noise as if it were signal), alongside hits and correct rejections. Varying the observer's decision criterion shifts the balance between hits and false alarms, tracing a receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve. The separation between the signal and noise distributions, expressed as d′ (d-prime), measures sensitivity independent of the observer's bias or criterion placement.

Applications

Signal detection theory is used wherever decisions are made under uncertainty: medical diagnosis and screening, quality control, lie detection, eyewitness identification, weather forecasting and the evaluation of diagnostic tests of all kinds. In medicine, ROC analysis quantifies how test performance depends on the threshold chosen.
