Overbreadth doctrine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In United States constitutional law, the overbreadth doctrine holds that a statute may be invalidated on its face — rather than only as applied to a particular defendant — if it prohibits a substantial amount of constitutionally protected speech or conduct, judged in relation to the statute's plainly legitimate sweep. The doctrine is primarily applied in First Amendment cases.

Rationale

The doctrine is an exception to the usual rule that a litigant may not challenge a law on the ground that it might be unconstitutionally applied to others, and to the general rule against facial challenges. Its justification is the "chilling effect": a broadly worded law may deter people from engaging in protected speech because they fear prosecution, even if the law could constitutionally be applied to the challenger. Courts therefore permit a defendant whose own conduct is unprotected to attack the statute so that protected speakers are not silenced.

Application

To succeed, a challenger must show that the law's unconstitutional applications are substantial relative to its legitimate reach. The United States Supreme Court has applied the doctrine in cases such as Broadrick v. Oklahoma (1973), which emphasised that overbreadth must be "substantial"; Virginia v. Hicks (2003); and United States v. Stevens (2010), which struck down a federal statute criminalising depictions of animal cruelty. Laws regulating only conduct, or commercial speech, are less likely to be invalidated for overbreadth.

Relation to vagueness

Overbreadth is related to but distinct from the void-for-vagueness doctrine. A vague law fails to give fair notice of what is prohibited, whereas an overbroad law may be perfectly clear yet sweep in too much protected activity. The two challenges are frequently raised together against speech regulations.
