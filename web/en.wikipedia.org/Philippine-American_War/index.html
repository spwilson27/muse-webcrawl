Philippine–American War

The Philippine–American War (February 4, 1899 – July 2, 1902) was an armed conflict between the First Philippine Republic and the United States that arose following the Treaty of Paris (1898), in which Spain ceded the Philippines to the United States. The Filipinos, who had declared independence from Spain, resisted American annexation, and the resulting war is considered a continuation of the Philippine struggle for independence that began in 1896.

Background

Following the Spanish–American War, Spain ceded the Philippines to the United States for $20 million under the Treaty of Paris. Filipino nationalists under Emilio Aguinaldo had already declared independence on June 12, 1898, and established the First Philippine Republic at Malolos. Tensions escalated until fighting broke out near Manila on February 4, 1899.

Course of the war

The war began with conventional battles around Manila, where American forces enjoyed superiority in arms and naval support. After the fall of the Malolos capital, the Philippine Army shifted to guerrilla warfare in late 1899. The conflict was marked by harsh counterinsurgency measures, including reconcentration policies, and by atrocities on both sides, notably the Balangiga massacre of 1901 and the brutal American retaliation on Samar. Aguinaldo was captured in March 1901 and subsequently swore allegiance to the United States.

End and aftermath

President Theodore Roosevelt declared the war ended on July 2, 1902, although resistance continued in some areas, including the Moro Rebellion in the south. The Philippines remained under American rule until independence in 1946. The war resulted in an estimated 200,000 or more Filipino civilian deaths, largely from disease and famine.
