Central Committee of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union

The Central Committee of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (Russian: Центральный комитет Коммунистической партии Советского Союза) was the principal executive organ of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union (CPSU) between party congresses. In theory elected by the Congress, in practice it was dominated by its smaller bodies, the Politburo and the Secretariat, and by the General Secretary.

History

The Bolsheviks' Central Committee was elected at the party's 1917 congresses and became, after the October Revolution, a centre of state as well as party power. Under Joseph Stalin, real authority concentrated in the Politburo and Stalin's own secretariat; the Central Committee itself was purged repeatedly, and most members elected in 1934 did not survive the Great Terror. After Stalin's death in 1953, the Central Committee regained some significance: it was the body that removed Nikita Khrushchev in 1964 and elected successive general secretaries, including Mikhail Gorbachev in 1985.

Structure

By the 1980s the Central Committee had over 300 full members and about 170 candidate (non-voting) members, drawn from senior party, state, military, and cultural figures. Its apparatus — departments overseeing industry, agriculture, ideology, and foreign affairs — shadowed the government ministries. Plenary sessions (plenums) formally ratified leadership decisions. The Central Committee's headquarters stood on Staraya Ploshchad (Old Square) in Moscow.

Dissolution

Following the failed August 1991 coup, Gorbachev dissolved the Central Committee and resigned as General Secretary; the CPSU was banned on Russian territory later that year, ending the committee's existence.
