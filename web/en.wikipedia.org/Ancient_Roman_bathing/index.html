Ancient Roman bathing

Bathing played a central role in ancient Roman society. Public bathhouses, known as thermae for the great imperial complexes and balneae for smaller establishments, were centres of social life, exercise, business, and leisure, open for a small fee to nearly all citizens. By the 4th century CE the city of Rome alone listed more than 800 baths.

The bathing routine

Bathers typically moved through a sequence of rooms: the apodyterium (changing room), tepidarium (warm room), caldarium (hot room, often with a hot plunge bath), and frigidarium (cold room). Floors and walls were heated by the hypocaust system, in which hot air from a furnace circulated beneath raised floors. Romans cleaned themselves with olive oil scraped off by a curved metal strigil, as soap was uncommon. Complexes also included palaestrae for exercise, gardens, libraries, and shops.

Imperial thermae

Emperors built enormous complexes as gifts to the populace, including the Baths of Caracalla and the Baths of Diocletian in Rome, which could accommodate thousands of bathers. The aqueduct system of Rome supplied the vast quantities of water they required.
