The Battle of Stalingrad (23 August 1942 – 2 February 1943) was a major battle of the Second World War fought between the Soviet Union and Nazi Germany and its Axis allies for control of the city of Stalingrad (now Volgograd) on the Volga River. It is regarded as the largest and bloodiest battle in the history of warfare and the decisive turning point of the war on the Eastern Front: the German Sixth Army was encircled and destroyed, and Germany never recovered the strategic initiative in the east.

Background

Germany's 1942 summer offensive, Case Blue, aimed at the Caucasus oilfields; the Sixth Army under Friedrich Paulus was diverted to take Stalingrad, which barred the Volga and bore Stalin's name. A massive Luftwaffe raid on 23 August killed tens of thousands and reduced much of the city to rubble—rubble that favoured the defenders. Vasily Chuikov's 62nd Army clung to a narrow strip along the west bank, fighting room by room for strongpoints such as Pavlov's House, the grain elevator and the great factories of the northern industrial district, while Soviet artillery on the east bank and nightly ferry crossings sustained them.

Operation Uranus

While German forces bled in the city, Soviet planners Zhukov and Vasilevsky massed over a million men against the weakly held flanks, guarded by Romanian, Italian and Hungarian armies. On 19 November 1942 Operation Uranus struck: the pincers met at Kalach four days later, trapping some 250,000–300,000 Axis troops in the Stalingrad pocket. Manstein's relief attempt, Operation Winter Storm, was stopped short in December; the airlift promised by Göring delivered a fraction of the needed supplies, and the pocket starved and froze.

Surrender

Operation Ring in January 1943 compressed the pocket. On 31 January Paulus, promoted to field marshal by Hitler in expectation that he would kill himself, surrendered the southern pocket; the northern pocket capitulated on 2 February. Some 91,000 survivors marched into captivity, from which only about 5,000–6,000 ever returned. Total casualties—the "Stalingrad cauldron" and the wider campaign—are estimated between 1.7 and 2 million.

Significance

Stalingrad shattered the myth of German invincibility, destroyed an entire army group sector's striking power, and shifted the war's momentum decisively. The city received the title Hero City; the colossal Motherland Calls statue on Mamayev Kurgan commemorates the battle.
