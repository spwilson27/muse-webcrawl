Ozone hole

The ozone hole is an annual thinning of the ozone layer in Earth's stratosphere over Antarctica, occurring during the Southern Hemisphere spring (September to early December), when total ozone drops dramatically — by as much as 60 per cent in the most affected region. A smaller thinning occurs over the Arctic.

Cause

The phenomenon is caused by manufactured chemicals, principally chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) and halons. In the extremely cold Antarctic winter stratosphere, polar stratospheric clouds convert stable chlorine compounds into reactive forms; returning spring sunlight then drives catalytic cycles that rapidly destroy ozone.

Discovery and response

Scientists from the British Antarctic Survey — Joe Farman, Brian Gardiner, and Jonathan Shanklin — reported the severe Antarctic ozone loss in 1985, a finding confirmed by NASA satellite data. The discovery spurred the Montreal Protocol of 1987, which phased out ozone-depleting substances worldwide.

Recovery

Atmospheric chlorine levels are declining, and the ozone layer is expected to recover to 1980 levels around the middle of the 21st century, though the size of the hole varies considerably from year to year with stratospheric weather.
