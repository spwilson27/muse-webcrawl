Carotenoid

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

ClassTetraterpenoids (C40)
SubclassesCarotenes (hydrocarbons); xanthophylls (oxygenated)
Examplesβ-carotene, lycopene, lutein, zeaxanthin, astaxanthin
RolesPhotosynthesis (accessory pigments), antioxidant action, provitamin A activity, animal coloration

Carotenoids are yellow, orange, and red organic pigments produced by plants, algae, and photosynthetic bacteria, as well as by some non-photosynthetic bacteria and fungi. They are tetraterpenoid compounds built from eight isoprene units (40 carbon atoms) and characterised by a long system of conjugated double bonds, which absorbs blue and green light and gives them their vivid colours. More than 1,100 carotenoids are known.

Classes and chemistry

Carotenoids divide into two classes: the carotenes, which are pure hydrocarbons (such as β-carotene and lycopene), and the xanthophylls, which contain oxygen (such as lutein, zeaxanthin, and astaxanthin). Animals cannot synthesise carotenoids de novo — a rare exception being some aphids and mites that acquired the genes by horizontal transfer — and must obtain them from their diet.

Biological roles

In photosynthetic organisms, carotenoids act as accessory pigments, harvesting light at wavelengths chlorophyll absorbs poorly and transferring the energy to chlorophyll; equally importantly, they protect the photosynthetic apparatus by quenching triplet chlorophyll and singlet oxygen. The carotenoids masked by chlorophyll in green leaves are revealed in autumn colours. In animals, carotenoids colour the plumage of flamingos and many other birds, the flesh of salmon, and crustacean shells. Some — above all β-carotene — are provitamins, cleaved in the body to form vitamin A (retinal), essential for vision; lutein and zeaxanthin accumulate in the human macula and are associated with eye health. Carotenoids also function as antioxidants, though high-dose β-carotene supplementation proved harmful in trials of smokers.

Uses

Carotenoids are widely used as natural food colourings (annatto, paprika oleoresin, E160–E161 series), in animal feed to colour egg yolks and farmed salmon, and in dietary supplements and cosmetics.
