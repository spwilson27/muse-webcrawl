Jejunum

The jejunum is the middle section of the small intestine in most higher vertebrates, including mammals, reptiles and birds. It lies between the duodenum and the ileum and is the principal site for the absorption of nutrients from digested food.

Structure

In adult humans the jejunum is about 2.5 metres (8 feet) long, making up roughly two-fifths of the small intestine. There is no sharp anatomical boundary with the duodenum except at the suspensory ligament of Treitz, and the transition to the ileum is likewise gradual. The inner surface is thrown into tall circular folds (plicae circulares) and lined with villi and microvilli, greatly increasing the absorptive area. Unlike the duodenum, the jejunum lacks Brunner's glands; unlike the ileum, it has few Peyer's patches.

The name derives from the Latin ieiunus, "empty" or "fasting", because early anatomists frequently found this part of the gut empty after death.

Function

The jejunum absorbs most of the products of digestion: amino acids from proteins, fatty acids and glycerol from fats, monosaccharides from carbohydrates, together with most vitamins and minerals. Its enterocytes express a dense array of transport proteins, and absorbed nutrients pass into the mesenteric blood and lymph. The contents move onward by peristalsis to the ileum, which specialises in absorbing vitamin B12 and bile salts.

Clinical significance

The jejunum may be involved in coeliac disease and other malabsorptive states, in Crohn's disease, and in obstruction or volvulus. Because of its absorptive capacity, segments of jejunum are used in surgical bypasses (such as Roux-en-Y gastric bypass) and in feeding jejunostomies for patients unable to use the stomach.
