Specific gravity (relative density)

Relative density, also called specific gravity, is a dimensionless quantity defined as the ratio of the density (mass of a unit volume) of a substance to the density of a given reference material. Specific gravity for liquids is nearly always measured with respect to water at its densest (at 4 °C or 39.2 °F); for gases, the reference is air at room temperature (20 °C or 68 °F). The term "relative density" is preferred in modern scientific usage.

Definition

Relative density RD = ρsubstance / ρreference, where ρ is density. If a substance's relative density is less than 1 it is less dense than the reference; if greater than 1 it is denser. If the relative density is exactly 1, the densities are equal. Two substances with the same relative density will occupy volumes in inverse proportion to their masses for a given sample size.

Measurement

Relative density can be measured with a hydrometer, which floats higher in less dense liquids; with a pycnometer, a precisely weighed flask; or with digital oscillating U-tube density meters. Archimedes is credited with an early understanding of the principle: a body immersed in fluid experiences buoyancy equal to the weight of the displaced fluid, so the relative density can be found from weights in air and in water. Specific gravity is widely used in industry: in brewing and winemaking (to track sugar content and alcohol), in urine analysis in medicine, in battery electrolyte testing, in gemmology, and in soil mechanics.

Temperature dependence

Because densities change with temperature, measurements specify both the sample temperature and the reference temperature, written for example as SG 20/4, meaning the sample at 20 °C and water at 4 °C. Water at 4 °C has a density very close to 1.000 g/cm³, so for liquids the specific gravity is numerically almost equal to the density in g/cm³.
