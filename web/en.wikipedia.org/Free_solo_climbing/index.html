Free solo climbing

Free solo climbing, or free soloing, is a form of rock climbing in which the climber ascends alone and without any form of protective equipment or ropes — relying only on their skill and physical strength, so that any fall is likely to be fatal. It is distinct from free climbing, in which ropes and protection are used for safety but not for upward progress, and from bouldering, which is done at low heights.

Practice and practitioners

Because of its extreme consequences for error, free soloing is practiced by a small minority of climbers, almost always on routes they have rehearsed extensively with ropes first. Early pioneers included John Bachar, who soloed hard routes in Yosemite and Joshua Tree in the 1970s and 1980s, and Peter Croft, whose 1987 solo link-ups in Yosemite set new standards. Derek Hersey, Dan Osman, and others pushed the discipline in the 1990s.

The most famous free soloist of the modern era is Alex Honnold, who soloed the Regular Northwest Face of Half Dome in 2008 and, on June 3, 2017, completed the first free solo ascent of El Capitan in Yosemite via the Freerider route (5.13a) in 3 hours 56 minutes — an achievement widely regarded as one of the great athletic feats in history. Honnold's El Capitan ascent was chronicled in the Academy Award-winning documentary Free Solo (2018).

Ethics and risk

Free soloing is controversial within the climbing community. Admirers see it as the purest expression of climbing mastery; critics regard it as unacceptably dangerous, note that many prominent soloists have died — including Bachar, Hersey, and Osman (the latter while rope jumping) — and worry about its influence on impressionable climbers. Practitioners typically emphasize preparation, rehearsal, and the freedom to back off.
