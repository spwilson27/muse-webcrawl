Scopolamine

Other nameHyoscine
ClassTropane alkaloid; anticholinergic
SourcesNightshade-family plants (Solanaceae)
UsesMotion sickness; postoperative nausea; drooling in palliative care

Scopolamine, also known as hyoscine, is a naturally occurring tropane alkaloid found in plants of the nightshade family, including henbane, jimsonweed, and Scopolia. It is an anticholinergic drug used medically to prevent nausea and vomiting caused by motion sickness or medications used during surgery, often delivered by a transdermal patch.

Medical use

Scopolamine blocks muscarinic acetylcholine receptors. It is among the most effective agents for motion sickness and is also used to reduce excessive salivation, for example in palliative care, and in ophthalmology it has been used to dilate the pupil. Side effects include dry mouth, drowsiness, blurred vision, and confusion, particularly in the elderly.

History and other uses

Isolated in the 19th century, scopolamine was combined with morphine in the early 1900s to produce "twilight sleep" in childbirth. It acquired a popular reputation as a "truth serum" after early 20th-century experiments, though its reliability for that purpose is not supported. In Colombia, criminal use of powdered borrachero plant material ("Devil's breath") containing scopolamine has been reported in robberies; the drug's amnesic and suggestive effects make such accounts a subject of ongoing concern and some exaggeration.
