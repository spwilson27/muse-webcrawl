Feelings

Feelings are subjective, conscious experiences of emotion or sensation. In psychology and philosophy, a feeling is usually understood as the conscious, subjective component of an emotion, as distinct from the emotion's physiological arousal, behavioral expression, and cognitive appraisal. Everyday language also uses "feeling" for bodily sensations such as hunger, pain, or touch, and for intuitions or hunches.

Feelings and emotions

Many theorists distinguish the two terms. William James proposed in 1884 that an emotion just is the perception of bodily changes: we feel afraid because we tremble, rather than trembling because we are afraid. Modern affective neuroscience, notably the work of Antonio Damasio, similarly treats feelings as the mental representation of physiological states, while emotions are the (possibly unconscious) bodily response programs themselves. In this view, feelings can occur only in creatures with consciousness, whereas emotion-like responses can be observed in simpler animals.

Types

Feelings include basic affective states such as joy, sadness, anger, fear, disgust, and surprise; bodily feelings such as warmth, fatigue, and hunger; and more reflective states sometimes called sentiments or moods, such as pride, nostalgia, contentment, or unease. Psychologists also discuss feelings of knowing, aesthetic feelings, and the "feeling of agency" over one's own actions.

Measurement and study

Because feelings are private experiences, researchers rely largely on self-report instruments such as the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule, supplemented by physiological measures — skin conductance, heart rate, and neuroimaging — that correlate with reported experience. The philosophical problem of how subjective feelings (qualia) arise from neural processes remains a central topic in philosophy of mind.
