Walker circulation

The Walker circulation: air rises over the warm western Pacific and sinks over the cold eastern Pacific.

The Walker circulation is an east–west overturning circulation of the atmosphere over the tropical Pacific Ocean, named after Sir Gilbert Walker, the British meteorologist who identified the related Southern Oscillation in the 1920s while searching for predictors of the Indian monsoon. The cellular circulation was described in its modern form by Jacob Bjerknes in 1969.

Mechanism

Trade winds push warm surface water westward across the Pacific, so sea-surface temperatures are several degrees warmer in the western "warm pool" near Indonesia than off South America, where cold water upwells. Air rises over the warm pool, producing the deep convection and heavy rainfall of the Maritime Continent, flows eastward at high altitude, and descends over the eastern Pacific, where subsidence maintains the dry climates of the Galápagos and the Peruvian coast. The returning surface flow closes the loop as the trade winds.

El Niño and La Niña

During an El Niño event the trade winds weaken, warm water spreads east, and the Walker circulation flattens and shifts: convection moves toward the central and eastern Pacific, bringing floods to western South America and drought to Indonesia and Australia. During La Niña the circulation strengthens beyond normal. The atmospheric component of this coupled oscillation is measured by the Southern Oscillation Index, the pressure difference between Tahiti and Darwin.

Effects

Fluctuations of the Walker circulation drive weather anomalies worldwide through atmospheric teleconnections — influencing rainfall in India, Australia, Africa, and the Americas — and are a central focus of seasonal climate prediction. Climate models project a long-term weakening of the circulation under global warming, though observations remain debated.
