Cumulus cloud

Cumulus clouds are clouds that have flat bases and are often described as "puffy", "cotton-like" or "fluffy" in appearance. Their name derives from the Latin cumulus, meaning "heap" or "pile". They are convective clouds formed by rising warm air (thermals): as moist air ascends and cools to its dew point, water vapour condenses at a fairly uniform height, giving the cloud its flat base, while continued rising builds the domed, cauliflower-like tops.

Species and significance

Small, scattered fair-weather clouds are cumulus humilis; larger, moderately towering ones are cumulus mediocris; and the tallest, cumulus congestus, can produce showers and, given sufficient instability and moisture, develop into cumulonimbus — the thundercloud responsible for heavy rain, lightning, hail and tornadoes. Because cumulus mark rising air, glider pilots seek them out as visible signs of lift.

Formation

Cumulus typically form on sunny days when the sun heats the ground unevenly, generating thermals. Cloud base height depends on surface temperature and humidity, and the tops grow as long as the rising air remains warmer than its surroundings. When the atmosphere is stable or dry aloft, the clouds flatten and dissipate by evening.

Effects on climate

Low cumulus and related stratocumulus clouds strongly influence Earth's energy balance: their bright tops reflect sunlight to space (a cooling effect), while their role in the hydrological cycle and in atmospheric circulation makes their representation in climate models an important research problem.
