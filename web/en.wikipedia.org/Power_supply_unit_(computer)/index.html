Power supply unit (computer)

A power supply unit (PSU) converts mains AC to low-voltage regulated DC power for the internal components of a desktop personal computer. Modern personal computers universally use switched-mode power supplies. Some power supplies have a manual switch for selecting input voltage, while others automatically adapt to the mains voltage.

Functions

The desktop computer power supply changes alternating current from a wall socket to several low-voltage direct-current rails. In the ATX standard that dominates desktop PCs, the main rails are +3.3 V, +5 V, and +12 V, with the 12-volt rail supplying most of the power to modern processors and graphics cards via dedicated converters on or near the devices themselves. The PSU also provides a standby 5 V rail (+5VSB) that powers wake-up circuitry, and signalling such as the "power good" line that tells the motherboard the outputs are stable. Connectors include the 24-pin ATX motherboard connector, 4- or 8-pin CPU power connectors, PCIe connectors for graphics cards, and SATA and Molex connectors for storage and peripherals.

Rated wattage—commonly 300 to 1200 watts for consumer units—indicates the total output a PSU can deliver. Efficiency certifications such as 80 Plus (Bronze through Titanium) grade how much input power is converted to output rather than lost as heat. Modular PSUs allow unused cables to be detached to improve airflow and cable management.

History

Early microcomputers used linear power supplies or simple bespoke units. The IBM PC in 1981 and the AT form factor in 1984 standardized PSU sizes and connectors; the AT supply was switched on by a mains-voltage switch on the unit itself. The ATX standard, introduced by Intel in 1995, moved switching to a low-voltage signal from the motherboard, added the 3.3 V rail, and enabled software-controlled shutdown and standby. Later revisions added SATA power, increased 12 V capacity, and (with ATX 3.0) connectors and excursion requirements for high-power graphics cards.

Testing and safety

Faulty power supplies can damage components or present fire risks, and counterfeit or underrated units are a recognized problem. Certification by safety agencies and independent reviews measuring ripple, voltage regulation, and hold-up time are used to evaluate quality.
