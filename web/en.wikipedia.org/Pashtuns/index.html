Pashtuns

Pashtuns (Pashto: پښتانه, romanized: Pəx̌tānə), also known historically as Afghans or Pathans, are an Iranian ethnic group native to Central and South Asia, primarily in Afghanistan and Pakistan. They speak Pashto, an Eastern Iranian language in the Indo-European family, and follow the traditional cultural code of Pashtunwali.

Pashtuns form the largest ethnic group in Afghanistan (estimated at around 42–48% of the population) and the second-largest in Pakistan, numbering in the tens of millions overall, with a large global diaspora.

History

The origins of the Pashtuns are debated; their ethnogenesis took place in the region between the Hindu Kush and the Indus. Ahmad Shah Durrani, founder of the Durrani Empire in 1747, united the Pashtun tribes and is regarded as the founder of modern Afghanistan. The Durand Line of 1893 divided Pashtun lands between Afghanistan and British India, a border Pakistan inherited and Afghanistan has never formally recognised.

Culture

Pashtun society is traditionally organised into tribes and clans governed by the Pashtunwali code, whose central tenets include hospitality (melmastia), asylum (nanawatai), and honour (nang), with disputes mediated by councils of elders (jirgas). The majority of Pashtuns are Sunni Muslims of the Hanafi school.
