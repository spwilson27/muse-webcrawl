Mira (Latin for "wonderful"), also designated Omicron Ceti, is a binary star in the constellation Cetus consisting of a red giant and a white dwarf companion. The red giant is the prototype of the Mira variables: pulsating stars whose brightness varies dramatically over periods of hundreds of days. Mira's apparent magnitude swings from about 2 — easily visible to the naked eye — down to about 10, beyond naked-eye visibility, over a cycle of roughly 332 days.

The star's variability was recorded by David Fabricius in 1596, making Mira the first variable star discovered (other than novae and supernovae). Its periodic disappearance and return puzzled early astronomers and helped undermine the doctrine of the unchanging heavens.

Tail and companion

In 2007, images from NASA's GALEX ultraviolet space telescope revealed that Mira, which is moving rapidly through the interstellar medium, trails a spectacular comet-like tail some 13 light-years long, shed from its outer layers, along with a bow shock ahead of it. Its companion, Mira B, is a white dwarf accreting material from the giant's wind.
