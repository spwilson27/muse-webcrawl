Yugoslavia (Serbo-Croatian: Jugoslavija, "Land of the South Slavs") was a country in Southeast and Central Europe that existed from 1918 to 1992, in several political forms. It united the South Slavic peoples — Serbs, Croats, Bosniaks, Slovenes, Macedonians, and Montenegrins — together with substantial Albanian, Hungarian, and other minorities.

Kingdom (1918–1945)

The state was proclaimed on 1 December 1918 as the Kingdom of Serbs, Croats and Slovenes under the Serbian Karađorđević dynasty, and renamed the Kingdom of Yugoslavia in 1929 by King Alexander I. In April 1941 the Axis powers invaded and partitioned the country. A large resistance movement, the Partisans under Josip Broz Tito, liberated most of the territory by 1945.

Socialist Federal Republic (1945–1992)

The Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia comprised six republics — Serbia, Croatia, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Slovenia, Macedonia, and Montenegro — with the autonomous provinces of Kosovo and Vojvodina within Serbia. Tito, president for life from 1953, broke with Stalin in 1948 and pursued an independent "Yugoslav road to socialism" based on workers' self-management. Yugoslavia was a founding member of the Non-Aligned Movement, whose first summit was held in Belgrade in 1961.

Breakup

After Tito's death in 1980, a rotating collective presidency governed amid economic crisis and rising nationalism. Slovenia and Croatia declared independence in June 1991, followed by Bosnia and Herzegovina in 1992; wars in Croatia and Bosnia (1992–1995) and later Kosovo (1998–1999) completed the dissolution. The Federal Republic of Yugoslavia (Serbia and Montenegro) continued until 2003 and dissolved entirely in 2006.
