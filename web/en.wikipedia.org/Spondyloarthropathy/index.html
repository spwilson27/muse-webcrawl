Spondyloarthropathies (or spondyloarthritides, abbreviated SpA) are a family of chronic inflammatory rheumatic diseases that share distinctive clinical and genetic features. They characteristically cause inflammation of the spine and sacroiliac joints (axial disease) and/or an asymmetric peripheral arthritis, together with enthesitis (inflammation where tendons and ligaments attach to bone), and they are "seronegative" — rheumatoid factor is typically absent.

Members of the family

  • Ankylosing spondylitis (radiographic axial spondyloarthritis) — the prototype, which can progress to spinal fusion.
  • Non-radiographic axial spondyloarthritis — similar symptoms without definite X-ray changes.
  • Psoriatic arthritis — associated with psoriasis and nail changes.
  • Reactive arthritis — triggered by gastrointestinal or genitourinary infections.
  • Enteropathic arthritis — associated with inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis).
  • Undifferentiated spondyloarthritis.

Features

Typical symptoms are inflammatory back pain (worse with rest, improved by activity, often starting before age 45), morning stiffness, dactylitis ("sausage digits"), uveitis, and skin and gut inflammation. A strong genetic link exists with the HLA-B27 allele, present in the great majority of people with ankylosing spondylitis but also in a minority of the healthy population. ASAS classification criteria distinguish axial from peripheral disease.

Treatment

Exercise and physiotherapy are central. NSAIDs are first-line drug therapy; conventional DMARDs such as sulfasalazine help peripheral but not axial disease. Biologic agents — TNF inhibitors and IL-17 inhibitors — and targeted JAK inhibitors have transformed the management of active disease.
