The Satanic Verses controversy, also known as the Rushdie affair, concerns the storm of protest, violence, and debate over freedom of expression that followed the publication in September 1988 of Salman Rushdie's novel The Satanic Verses. Many Muslims regarded the novel's dream sequences depicting a prophet-like figure, and its use of names and episodes echoing early Islamic history, as blasphemous.

Background

The novel's title refers to the "satanic verses," verses said in some Islamic traditions to have been briefly accepted into the Quran as spoken by Muhammad before being recognized as inspired by Satan and abrogated. Within weeks of publication, India — home to a large Muslim population — banned imports of the book after lobbying by Muslim parliamentarians, the first country to do so. Protests spread to Britain, where copies were burned in Bradford in January 1989, and to Pakistan, South Africa, and other countries.

The fatwa

On 14 February 1989, Iran's Supreme Leader, Ayatollah Ruhollah Khomeini, issued a fatwa calling for the deaths of Rushdie and his publishers, and an Iranian foundation placed a bounty on the author's head. Rushdie went into hiding under British police protection for nearly a decade. Diplomatic relations between Britain and Iran were severed. In 1998 the Iranian government publicly distanced itself from the fatwa, though it was never formally revoked by the religious authorities who succeeded Khomeini.

Violence

The controversy produced deadly riots in Islamabad and Kashmir and attacks on people connected with the book: its Japanese translator, Hitoshi Igarashi, was stabbed to death in 1991; its Italian translator Ettore Capriolo was stabbed and survived; its Norwegian publisher William Nygaard was shot in 1993 and survived; and 37 people died in the 1993 Sivas arson attack in Turkey targeting the novel's Turkish translator, Aziz Nesin. In August 2022, Rushdie himself was stabbed repeatedly on stage in Chautauqua, New York, losing the sight of one eye; his memoir Knife (2024) recounts the attack.

Debate

The affair became a defining episode in Western debates about free speech, multiculturalism, and blasphemy, dividing writers and politicians and prompting both defenses of Rushdie's right to publish and arguments about the responsibilities of authors toward religious sensibilities.
