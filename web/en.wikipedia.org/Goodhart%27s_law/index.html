Goodhart's law

Goodhart's law is an adage often stated as: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure." It is named after British economist Charles Goodhart, who is credited with expressing the core idea of the adage in a 1975 article on monetary policy in the United Kingdom.

Origin

Goodhart's original formulation, from his 1975 paper "Problems of Monetary Management: The U.K. Experience," was:

Any observed statistical regularity will tend to collapse once pressure is placed upon it for control purposes.

The shorter, popular phrasing is due to anthropologist Marilyn Strathern, who in 1997 generalized the principle beyond economics, writing: "When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure."

Explanation

The law describes how optimizing for a proxy measure can distort the underlying behaviour the measure was meant to track. When people know they are being assessed against a specific metric, they tend to game the metric — improving the number without improving, or even while harming, the real objective. Examples cited include teaching to the test in education, hospitals manipulating waiting-list statistics, and Soviet factories meeting nail-production quotas by producing vast quantities of useless tiny nails or a few enormous ones, depending on whether the target was by count or by weight.

Related concepts

Goodhart's law is closely related to Campbell's law in social science and to the Lucas critique in economics. It is also discussed in the context of performance indicators, key performance indicators (KPIs), and artificial intelligence alignment, where a misspecified reward function can produce unintended behaviour.
