Indian rock-cut architecture

Indian rock-cut architecture
Periodc. 3rd century BCE – 10th century CE
Notable sitesAjanta, Ellora, Elephanta, Karla, Bhaja, Kanheri, Mahabalipuram
ReligionsBuddhist, Hindu, Jain

Indian rock-cut architecture is the practice of creating structures by excavating solid natural rock. India has more than 1,500 rock-cut structures, most of them religious in nature, making the tradition one of the most extensive in the world. Unlike built masonry, rock-cut caves were carved from the top down and often imitate wooden construction in stone.

Early caves

The earliest examples are the Barabar and Nagarjuni caves in Bihar, polished granite chambers donated in the 3rd century BCE, during the Mauryan period, to ascetics of the Ājīvika sect. Buddhist monks subsequently excavated prayer halls (chaityas) and monasteries (viharas) along trade routes in the Western Ghats, at sites such as Bhaja, Karla, and Kanheri.

Ajanta and Ellora

The Ajanta Caves (2nd century BCE to 5th century CE) are celebrated for their murals depicting the Jataka tales. The Ellora Caves comprise 34 Buddhist, Hindu, and Jain excavations; their centrepiece, the Kailasa temple (Cave 16), a monolithic structure attributed to the Rashtrakuta king Krishna I in the 8th century, is considered the largest rock-cut temple complex in the world.

Later developments

Hindu and Jain rock-cut temples flourished under the Chalukyas at Badami and Aihole, the Pallavas at Mahabalipuram, and the Pandyas in Tamil Nadu. By about the 10th century the tradition declined in favour of structural stone temples, though rock-cut excavation continued in some regions.
