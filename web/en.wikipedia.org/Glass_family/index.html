Glass family

The Glass family is a fictional family created by the American writer J. D. Salinger. The seven brilliant, precocious children of vaudeville performers Les Glass and Bessie Gallagher Glass appear in a series of short stories and novellas published between 1948 and 1965, including A Perfect Day for Bananafish (1948), Franny and Zooey (1961), Raise High the Roof Beam, Carpenters and Seymour: An Introduction (1963), and Hapworth 16, 1924 (1965).

The family

The eldest child, Seymour Glass (1917–1948), is the family's spiritual centre; his suicide in "A Perfect Day for Bananafish" shadows the whole cycle. The narrator of most of the later stories is Buddy Glass, the second son, a writer widely read as Salinger's alter ego. The other siblings are Boo Boo, the twins Walt and Waker, Zooey (Zachary), and Franny (Frances), the youngest. As children, the older siblings were famous as quiz-show performers on the radio programme "It's a Wise Child", a source of both pride and damage in their adult lives.

Themes

The Glass stories explore spirituality and religious seeking (Seymour's blend of Zen Buddhism, Vedanta, and Christian mysticism), the tension between genius and ordinary social life, grief, and the corrupting effects of ego — Salinger's recurring targets of "phoniness". Franny and Zooey, centred on Franny's breakdown and Zooey's famous bathtub-side counsel, is the most widely read of the cycle and ends with Seymour's advice to shine one's shoes for the "Fat Lady".

Influence

The Glass family helped establish Salinger's reputation after The Catcher in the Rye. The stories' intimate, digressive style influenced later writers, and the family has been invoked in film and literature, notably in Wes Anderson's The Royal Tenenbaums, whose gifted, damaged siblings are an acknowledged homage.
