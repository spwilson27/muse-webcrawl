Robot-assisted surgery

Robot-assisted surgery or robotic surgery are any types of surgical procedures that are performed using robotic systems. Robotically assisted surgery was developed to try to overcome the limitations of pre-existing minimally-invasive surgical procedures and to enhance the capabilities of surgeons performing open surgery.

In the case of robotically assisted minimally-invasive surgery, instead of the surgeon directly moving the instruments, the surgeon uses one of two methods to perform the procedure. In one, a direct telemanipulator is used, in which the surgeon uses a computer console to control the instruments, and the telemanipulator performs the surgeon's movements. The other method is computer-controlled, in which a pre-programmed robot performs the surgery autonomously or semi-autonomously.

History

The first robot to assist in surgery was the Arthrobot, developed and used for the first time in Vancouver in 1985. In 1988, the PROBOT, developed at Imperial College London, was used to perform prostatic surgery. The ROBODOC system, introduced in 1992, was used to mill out precise fittings in the femur for hip replacement surgery. The da Vinci Surgical System, developed by Intuitive Surgical, was cleared by the US Food and Drug Administration in 2000 and became the most widely used surgical robot platform in the world.

Advantages

Proponents claim several advantages over conventional laparoscopy: greater precision, miniaturization of instruments, smaller incisions, decreased blood loss, less pain, and healing time that is quicker. The systems provide the surgeon with a magnified three-dimensional view of the operative field, and wristed instruments that filter out hand tremor and offer a greater range of motion than the human hand.

Disadvantages and criticism

Surgical robots are expensive to purchase and maintain, and critics note a lack of definitive evidence that robotic surgery produces better long-term outcomes than conventional laparoscopic surgery for many procedures. Procedure times can be longer, especially during a surgical team's learning curve, and there is no tactile feedback (haptics) in most systems, forcing surgeons to rely on visual cues.

Applications

Robot-assisted systems are most commonly used in urology (especially radical prostatectomy), gynecology, cardiothoracic surgery, and general surgery, including hernia repair, colorectal surgery, and bariatric surgery. Specialized platforms have also been developed for orthopedics, neurosurgery, and single-port surgery.
