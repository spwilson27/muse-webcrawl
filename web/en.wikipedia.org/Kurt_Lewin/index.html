Kurt Lewin (1890–1947) was a German-American psychologist, widely regarded as the founder of modern social psychology. Drawing on Gestalt psychology, he developed field theory — the analysis of behaviour within the total "life space" of person and environment — and pioneered the experimental study of group dynamics, leadership and social change.

Life and career

Lewin studied in Berlin, served in the German army in the First World War, and became a professor at the University of Berlin, working with the Gestalt school. Fleeing Nazi Germany in 1933, he emigrated to the United States, holding posts at Cornell, the University of Iowa — where he founded the Iowa Child Welfare Research Station group — and finally MIT, where he established the Research Center for Group Dynamics in 1945.

Contributions

His experiments with Ronald Lippitt and Ralph White on autocratic, democratic and laissez-faire leadership climates (1939) were landmark studies of group life. Lewin coined the term "group dynamics", founded the journal Human Relations and the National Training Laboratories, and developed "action research" and the unfreeze–change–refreeze model of organizational change. His equation B = f(P, E) summarized his conviction that behaviour must be explained by the person and their psychological environment together.
