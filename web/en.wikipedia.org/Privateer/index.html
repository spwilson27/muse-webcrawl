Privateer

AuthorisationLetter of marque and reprisal
EraMainly 16th–19th centuries
AbolitionDeclaration of Paris (1856), among signatories

A privateer was a private person or ship authorised by a government, through a document called a letter of marque and reprisal, to attack and capture enemy vessels in wartime and bring them before admiralty courts for condemnation and sale as prizes. Privateering was a form of state-sanctioned commerce raiding that blurred the line between naval warfare and piracy: a privateer operating without a valid commission, or exceeding it, could be treated as a pirate.

History

Privateering flourished from the sixteenth century as European states expanded their navies cheaply through private capital. English sea dogs such as Francis Drake harassed Spanish shipping; during the American War of Independence and the War of 1812, American privateers captured hundreds of British merchant ships, and Confederate raiders and privateers operated during the American Civil War. Crews were paid not wages but shares of prize money, which made the trade risky and potentially lucrative.

Abolition

The Declaration of Paris of 1856, concluded after the Crimean War, abolished privateering among its signatories, which included most European powers. The United States did not sign but observed its terms in practice, and privateering faded with the professionalisation of navies, though the United States Constitution still grants Congress the power to issue letters of marque.
