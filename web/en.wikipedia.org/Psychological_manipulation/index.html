Psychological manipulation

Psychological manipulation is a type of social influence that aims to change behaviour or perception through indirect, deceptive, or underhanded tactics, typically for the manipulator's benefit at the expense of others.

Psychological manipulation is a type of social influence that aims to change the behaviour or perception of others through indirect, deceptive, or underhanded tactics. By advancing the interests of the manipulator, often at another's expense, such methods can be considered exploitative and devious, distinguishing manipulation from healthy social influence and persuasion.

Requirements and motives

Manipulation generally involves a manipulator with a concealed agenda, a willingness to exploit the vulnerabilities of the target, and indifference to the harm caused. Motives include advancing one's own goals, gaining power and control, deriving vicarious enjoyment, avoiding boredom, or seeking attention and validation. Research has associated chronic manipulativeness with personality traits in the so-called Dark Triad — narcissism, Machiavellianism, and psychopathy.

Tactics

Common manipulative tactics described by researchers such as Harriet Braiker and George K. Simon include lying and lying by omission, denial, rationalisation, minimisation, guilt-tripping, shaming, evasion, playing the victim, vilifying the victim, feigning innocence or confusion, brandishing anger, and diverting attention. A widely discussed form is gaslighting, in which the manipulator causes the target to doubt their own memory and perception.

Vulnerabilities

Targets may be more susceptible if they are people-pleasing, highly empathic, fearful of negative emotion or conflict, lacking assertiveness, or uncertain of their own judgment. Recognising manipulation is complicated by the fact that manipulative behaviour occurs on a continuum and in many ordinary social contexts.
