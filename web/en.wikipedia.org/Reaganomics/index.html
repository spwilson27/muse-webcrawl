Reaganomics

Reaganomics refers to the economic policies of U.S. President Ronald Reagan during the 1980s.

Reaganomics (a portmanteau of "Reagan" and "economics" attributed to Paul Harvey), also known as supply-side economics or trickle-down economics by its critics, refers to the neoliberal economic policies promoted by U.S. President Ronald Reagan during the 1980s. These policies are commonly associated with supply-side economics and emphasized tax cuts, deregulation, decreased government spending, and tight money policy.

The four pillars

Reagan's stated goals were summarized by his chief economic adviser Martin Feldstein as four pillars: reduce the growth of government spending, reduce the marginal tax rate on income from labor and capital, reduce government regulation, and reduce inflation by controlling the money supply. The Economic Recovery Tax Act of 1981 cut the top marginal income tax rate from 70 percent to 50 percent and reduced the capital gains tax; the Tax Reform Act of 1986 further lowered the top rate to 28 percent while broadening the tax base.

Results

The Reagan administration coincided with the end of the stagflation of the 1970s. Inflation fell sharply after the Federal Reserve under Paul Volcker raised interest rates, though this also produced the deep recession of 1981–82, during which unemployment peaked at 10.8 percent. A long expansion followed from 1983 onward: real GDP grew strongly, unemployment fell, and the stock market rose. Federal tax revenues rose in nominal terms, but federal spending as a share of GDP did not fall, military budgets increased, and the federal budget deficit and national debt grew substantially — the debt roughly tripled over the decade.

Criticism and legacy

Supporters credit Reaganomics with revitalizing the American economy, encouraging entrepreneurship and investment, and helping win the Cold War. Critics argue the tax cuts disproportionately benefited the wealthy, increased income inequality, and swelled public debt without the promised spending restraint. The policies strongly influenced later Republican economic programs and the broader international turn toward market-oriented reform in the 1980s.
