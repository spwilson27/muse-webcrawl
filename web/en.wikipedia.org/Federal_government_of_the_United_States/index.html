The federal government of the United States (U.S. federal government or U.S. government) is the national government of the United States, a federal republic of 50 states, a federal district, and various territories. It was established in 1789 under the Constitution of the United States, which replaced the weaker Articles of Confederation. The Constitution divides the government into three branches — legislative, executive, and judicial — each checked and balanced by the others, and divides power between the federal government and the states under the principle of federalism.

Legislative branch

Congress, established by Article I, is a bicameral legislature of the Senate (100 senators, two per state, serving six-year terms) and the House of Representatives (435 members apportioned by population, serving two-year terms). Congress makes federal law, declares war, controls taxation and spending, confirms executive and judicial appointments (Senate), and can impeach and remove federal officers.

Executive branch

Article II vests executive power in the President of the United States, who is head of state and head of government, commander-in-chief of the armed forces, and responsible for enforcing federal law. The executive branch includes the Vice President, the fifteen executive departments headed by Cabinet secretaries (the largest being Defense, Veterans Affairs, and Homeland Security), and numerous independent agencies such as the Environmental Protection Agency and the Federal Reserve's Board of Governors.

Judicial branch

Article III establishes the federal judiciary, headed by the Supreme Court of the United States, with thirteen courts of appeals and ninety-four district courts created by Congress. Federal judges are nominated by the President, confirmed by the Senate, and serve for life during good behaviour. The courts exercise judicial review — the power, established in Marbury v. Madison (1803), to strike down laws and executive actions that violate the Constitution.

Federalism and scale

Powers not granted to the federal government are reserved to the states by the Tenth Amendment, though the commerce clause and the Fourteenth Amendment have greatly expanded federal authority since the 1930s. The federal government spends roughly six trillion dollars a year — around a quarter of GDP — and is one of the nation's largest employers.
