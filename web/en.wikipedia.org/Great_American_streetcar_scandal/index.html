Great American streetcar scandal

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Great American streetcar scandal refers to the replacement of streetcar (tram) systems in many United States cities during the mid-20th century by buses, a process in which companies affiliated with the automobile, oil, and tyre industries played a significant and partly illicit role. The affair centred on National City Lines (NCL) and its subsidiary Pacific City Lines, which acquired transit systems in dozens of cities and converted them from electric rail to bus operation.

Background

In the 1930s and 1940s, General Motors, Firestone Tire, Standard Oil of California, Phillips Petroleum, and Mack Trucks invested in NCL and supplied it with buses, tyres, and fuel under exclusive dealing arrangements. Between 1936 and 1950, NCL and its affiliates took over systems in cities including Los Angeles (the Pacific Electric and Los Angeles Railway), St. Louis, Baltimore, and Salt Lake City, and scrapped or sold off much of their electric fleets.

Conviction

In 1949, in United States v. National City Lines, the companies and several executives were convicted of conspiring to monopolise the supply of buses and related products to the NCL operating companies. They were acquitted of the more dramatic charge of conspiring to monopolise public transit itself. Penalties were token: General Motors was fined $5,000 and its treasurer $1.

Historiography

Historians debate the scandal's importance. Streetcars were already in steep decline because of the automobile, suburbanisation, regulated fares, and buses' genuine flexibility; many systems were converted with no NCL involvement. Critics argue the conspiracy accelerated and locked in the transition, destroyed irreplaceable infrastructure, and helped entrench car dependence in American cities. The episode was popularised by the 1988 film Who Framed Roger Rabbit, whose plot loosely references the dismantling of Los Angeles's Red Cars.
