Plica semilunaris of conjunctiva

The plica semilunaris lies at the medial canthus, beside the lacrimal caruncle.

The plica semilunaris is a small, crescent-shaped fold of conjunctiva on the surface of the human eye, located at the inner (medial) canthus, lateral to the lacrimal caruncle. It is generally regarded as the vestigial remnant of the nictitating membrane — the "third eyelid" that in many other vertebrates can be drawn across the eye for protection and to moisten it while maintaining vision.

Structure and function

The fold consists of conjunctival epithelium over loose connective tissue, containing goblet cells and some accessory lacrimal gland tissue. Though it cannot move across the eye as a functional third eyelid, the plica contributes to tear drainage and allows a greater range of rotation of the eyeball than would be possible if the conjunctiva were taut; during abduction of the eye the fold unfolds. Its associated muscles, described by some anatomists as tiny vestigial retractors, have no significant function in humans.

Comparative anatomy

In birds, reptiles, and many mammals such as cats, dogs, and polar bears, the nictitating membrane is a large, functional structure, sometimes transparent, which sweeps across the cornea. In primates the membrane is reduced to varying degrees; among humans and most other haplorhines only the plica semilunaris remains. Charles Darwin cited the plica among the rudimentary organs evidencing descent with modification in The Descent of Man (1871), noting that it is somewhat more developed in some human populations than others.

Clinical aspects

The plica can become congested and prominent in allergic or infectious conjunctivitis, and folds of its type should not be confused with pathological membranes. Benign lesions such as naevi may occur in the region, and the area is examined in assessments of the medial orbit and lacrimal drainage system.
