Vaccine hesitancy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Vaccine hesitancy ranges from mild uncertainty to outright refusal of some or all vaccines.

Vaccine hesitancy is a delay in acceptance, or refusal, of vaccines despite the availability of vaccine services. The term covers a spectrum of attitudes, from people who accept all vaccines but worry about them, to those who refuse some vaccines or delay schedules, to complete refusal. It is influenced by issues of confidence, complacency and convenience — the "3Cs" model of the World Health Organization's SAGE Working Group — and varies across time, place and vaccine. The WHO named vaccine hesitancy one of the top ten threats to global health in 2019.

History

Opposition to vaccination is nearly as old as vaccination itself: Edward Jenner's cowpox inoculation (1796) provoked satirical prints and religious objections, and compulsory smallpox vaccination in 19th-century Britain produced organized anti-vaccination leagues and the Leicester demonstration of 1885. The 1970s–80s saw controversy over alleged harms from the whole-cell pertussis vaccine, sharply reducing coverage in the UK, Japan and Sweden before studies showed the risk had been greatly overstated.

The modern era of hesitancy was galvanized by Andrew Wakefield's 1998 Lancet paper fraudulently linking the MMR vaccine to autism. The paper was fully retracted in 2010 and Wakefield was struck off the UK medical register, and large studies have found no association; nevertheless the claim persisted and measles — declared eliminated in several countries — resurged, with global cases climbing sharply in 2018–2019. During the COVID-19 pandemic, hesitancy extended to rapidly developed mRNA vaccines, driven by safety concerns, distrust of authorities, political polarization and online misinformation.

Causes

  • Confidence: trust in vaccine safety and effectiveness, in health systems and in policymakers; fears of side effects, often amplified by anecdote and media coverage.
  • Complacency: low perceived risk of disease, itself a product of vaccination's success — diseases become rarer than the (also rare) adverse events that follow immunization.
  • Convenience: cost, distance, clinic hours, language and health literacy barriers.
  • Calculation and collective responsibility (added in the later "5C" model): extensive information-seeking that can expose people to misinformation, and willingness to protect others through herd immunity.

Consequences

Hesitancy lowers coverage below herd-immunity thresholds (about 95 percent for measles), producing outbreaks concentrated in under-vaccinated communities. WHO/UNICEF estimates recorded millions of "zero-dose" children — those who received no vaccines at all, mainly for access reasons — and coverage for several childhood vaccines fell during the COVID-19 pandemic, the largest sustained backslide in decades.

Responses

Evidence supports multi-component strategies: strong provider recommendations, reminder and recall systems, presumptive ("announcement") approaches to appointments, school-entry requirements, and community engagement that listens to concerns rather than dismissing them. Mandates raise coverage but can provoke backlash; risk communication research emphasizes transparency about genuine uncertainties and adverse events, and countering misinformation through trusted local voices and pre-bunking.
