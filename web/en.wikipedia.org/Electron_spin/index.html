Electron spin

In atomic physics and quantum mechanics, electron spin is an intrinsic form of angular momentum carried by electrons. Spin is a purely quantum-mechanical property: the electron behaves as if it possesses angular momentum of magnitude ħ/2 (it is a spin-½ particle), yet it is not literally spinning like a classical rotating sphere. Spin, together with charge and mass, is one of the intrinsic properties that define the electron.

History

Spin was proposed in 1925 by George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit to explain the fine structure of atomic spectra and the results of the Stern–Gerlach experiment (1922), in which a beam of silver atoms split into two components in a magnetic field. Wolfgang Pauli incorporated spin into quantum mechanics with his spin matrices and the exclusion principle; in 1928 Paul Dirac's relativistic equation predicted electron spin naturally, along with its magnetic moment (g-factor ≈ 2).

Properties

The component of spin measured along any axis can take only two values, +ħ/2 ("spin up") or −ħ/2 ("spin down"). Spin endows the electron with a magnetic dipole moment, making it a tiny magnet; spin–orbit coupling splits spectral lines (fine structure). The Pauli exclusion principle — no two electrons in an atom may share the same set of quantum numbers, including spin — determines the shell structure of atoms and hence the whole of chemistry. Electron spin is exploited in electron spin resonance spectroscopy, MRI-related techniques, and the emerging field of spintronics.
