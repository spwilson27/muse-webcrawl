Lyndon Baines Johnson (1908–1973), often called LBJ, was the 36th president of the United States, serving from 1963 to 1969. He assumed office after the assassination of John F. Kennedy and won election in his own right in a 1964 landslide. His presidency combined the sweeping domestic reforms of the Great Society with the escalating quagmire of the Vietnam War, which ultimately drove him from seeking re-election.

Early political career

Born in the Texas Hill Country, Johnson worked his way through Southwest Texas State Teachers College and entered politics as a congressional aide. Elected to the House in 1937 and the Senate in 1948 (by a famously narrow, contested margin of 87 votes), he became Senate Majority Leader in 1955 and one of the most powerful legislators in American history, renowned for his mastery of procedure and personal persuasion — the "Johnson treatment." He joined Kennedy's ticket as vice-presidential nominee in 1960 to balance it regionally.

Presidency

As president, Johnson pushed through the Civil Rights Act of 1964, the Voting Rights Act of 1965, Medicare and Medicaid, federal aid to education, and the War on Poverty — the core of his Great Society and the most productive legislative record since the New Deal. He was re-elected in 1964 with 61% of the popular vote. Simultaneously he deepened US involvement in Vietnam after the Gulf of Tonkin Resolution; by 1968 over 500,000 American troops were deployed, protests engulfed the country, and on 31 March 1968 Johnson announced he would not seek re-election.

Later life and legacy

Johnson retired to his Texas ranch, where he died in 1973. Historians credit his transformative civil-rights and social legislation while judging Vietnam a catastrophic failure that shadowed his achievements. His presidential library in Austin preserves his papers.
