Agrarian law

Agrarian law is the body of law governing land ownership, land tenure, and agricultural production. It encompasses the rules by which agricultural land is held, transferred, inherited, leased, and used, as well as the legal status of the people who work it. Agrarian law has been central to political conflict throughout history, from the land reforms of the Roman Republic to the redistributive programs of the twentieth century.

History

In ancient Rome, the leges agrariae (agrarian laws) regulated the distribution of public land (ager publicus) among citizens. The reforms proposed by the Gracchi brothers in the second century BCE sought to break up large estates worked by slaves and redistribute parcels to poor citizens, provoking violent political crises. Similar concerns animated later agrarian legislation, including enclosure acts in England and homestead laws in the United States.

Scope

Modern agrarian law typically covers land registration and cadastral systems, tenancy and sharecropping contracts, agricultural credit and cooperatives, water rights for irrigation, and land-use planning. In many legal systems it interacts with environmental law, tax law, and food law.

Land reform

Land reform — the redistribution of agricultural land from large holders to tenants and smallholders — has been one of the most consequential applications of agrarian law. Major reforms took place in Japan, Taiwan, and South Korea after the Second World War, in Mexico under its 1917 constitution, and across Eastern Europe after 1989.
