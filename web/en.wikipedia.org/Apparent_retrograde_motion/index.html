Apparent retrograde motion

Apparent retrograde motion is the apparent movement of a planet in a direction opposite to that of most other bodies in its system, as observed from a particular vantage point. Most famously, the outer planets periodically appear to halt and move westward against the background stars as seen from Earth, before resuming their usual eastward (prograde) course.

Explanation

The effect is an optical consequence of relative orbital motion: as the faster-moving Earth overtakes an outer planet near opposition, the planet's line of sight sweeps backward. For the inner planets Mercury and Venus, retrograde episodes occur around inferior conjunction as they overtake Earth. Mars's retrograde loop, recurring about every 26 months, is the most prominent.

Historical significance

Retrograde motion posed a major challenge to ancient astronomy. The geocentric model of Ptolemy explained it with epicycles — small circles superimposed on the planets' deferents. The Copernican heliocentric model of 1543 explained retrograde motion naturally as a perspective effect, a key argument in its favour.
