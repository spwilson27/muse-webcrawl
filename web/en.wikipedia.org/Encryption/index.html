Encryption

Encryption is the process of converting information (plaintext) into an unintelligible form (ciphertext) so that only authorised parties can access it. An encryption algorithm, together with a key, transforms the data; decryption reverses the process with the appropriate key. Encryption is a fundamental tool of information security, protecting confidentiality in communications, stored files, financial transactions, and much of the infrastructure of the internet.

Encryption and decryption using a key

Types

In symmetric-key encryption, the same key encrypts and decrypts; modern examples include the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES), used worldwide for securing data at rest and in transit. In asymmetric (public-key) encryption, introduced publicly by Diffie and Hellman in 1976 and embodied in the RSA algorithm (1977), each party has a public key for encryption and a private key for decryption, solving the problem of key distribution and enabling digital signatures. Most real systems are hybrid: public-key methods establish a shared secret, which then keys a fast symmetric cipher — the design underlying TLS, which secures web browsing (HTTPS).

History

Ciphers are ancient: the Caesar cipher shifted letters by a fixed amount, and polyalphabetic ciphers such as Vigenère's resisted casual analysis for centuries. Rotor machines like Germany's Enigma mechanised encryption in the Second World War, and their cryptanalysis at Bletchley Park was a landmark in computing history. The Data Encryption Standard (DES), adopted in 1977, brought encryption into commercial computing; it was succeeded by AES in 2001 after an open international competition.

Applications

Encryption protects messaging (end-to-end encrypted apps such as Signal and WhatsApp), online banking and commerce, virtual private networks, full-disk encryption on phones and laptops, password storage (via one-way hashing), and cryptocurrencies. End-to-end encryption ensures that only the communicating endpoints can read messages, a feature that has generated ongoing public debate with law-enforcement agencies seeking exceptional access — the so-called "crypto wars".

Security considerations

The security of encryption rests on key secrecy and algorithm strength: by Kerckhoffs's principle, a system should remain secure even if everything except the key is public. Practical failures usually arise from implementation errors, weak keys, or endpoint compromise rather than from breaking the mathematics. Future quantum computers threaten current public-key algorithms via Shor's algorithm, motivating the development and standardisation of post-quantum cryptography.
