Yersinia pestis

Yersinia pestis
Domain: Bacteria
Family: Yersiniaceae
Shape: Gram-negative coccobacillus
Disease: Plague (bubonic, septicemic, pneumonic)
Discovered: 1894, Alexandre Yersin

Yersinia pestis is a Gram-negative, non-motile bacterium that causes plague — the disease behind the Plague of Justinian, the Black Death of the 14th century, and the Third Pandemic of the 19th–20th centuries.

Discovery and biology

The bacillus was isolated in Hong Kong in 1894 by Alexandre Yersin of the Pasteur Institute (independently noted by Kitasato Shibasaburo), and the flea vector was demonstrated by Paul-Louis Simond in 1898. Y. pestis evolved recently — within the last several thousand years — from the enteric pathogen Yersinia pseudotuberculosis, acquiring two plasmids (pMT1 and pPCP1) that confer flea transmission and deep tissue invasion.

Transmission and disease

The bacterium circulates in rodent reservoirs worldwide and reaches humans mainly through the bite of the oriental rat flea, Xenopsylla cheopis. Bubonic plague inflames lymph nodes into painful buboes; septicemic plague invades the bloodstream; and pneumonic plague infects the lungs and can spread person-to-person by droplets. Without treatment mortality is very high, but early antibiotics such as gentamicin and doxycycline are effective.

Modern status

Plague persists in enzootic foci in Madagascar, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Peru, and the western United States, causing typically a few hundred to a few thousand reported cases yearly. Ancient DNA from Neolithic and Bronze Age graves shows Y. pestis lineages circulating in Eurasia 5,000 years ago, reshaping understanding of prehistoric epidemics.
