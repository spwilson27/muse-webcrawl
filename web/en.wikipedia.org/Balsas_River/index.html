The Balsas River (Spanish: Río Balsas) is one of the longest rivers of Mexico. Rising in the central highlands of Puebla state as the Atoyac River, it flows west and southwest for some 771 kilometres through the deep gorges of the Sierra Madre del Sur, forming much of the boundary between Michoacán and Guerrero, before emptying into the Pacific Ocean near the port of Lázaro Cárdenas. In its upper course in Puebla it is known as the Atoyac, and in Guerrero also as the Mezcala River.

Course and basin

The Balsas basin drains about 113,000 square kilometres across six states. The river descends from over 2,000 metres on the Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt into one of Mexico's most pronounced rain-shadow depressions: the hot, dry Balsas valley, whose tropical dry forests are a distinctive ecoregion with many endemic species. Major tributaries include the Mixteco, Tlapaneco, Amacuzac and Cutzamala rivers.

Human significance

The Balsas valley is famous in archaeology and crop science: the wild teosinte Zea mays subsp. parviglumis, the ancestor of maize, is native to the middle Balsas basin, which is considered a leading candidate for the region where maize was first domesticated roughly 9,000 years ago. The river is also heavily dammed for hydroelectric power and irrigation; major works include the Infiernillo (Adolfo López Mateos) and La Villita dams on the lower river and El Caracol and El Gallo upstream.

Ecology

The basin's rivers support endemic fish such as the Balsas catfish and several goodeid and poeciliid species, while its dry forests harbour endemic birds including the Balsas screech owl. Water diversion, pollution from the Puebla industrial corridor and invasive species threaten the system's ecology.
