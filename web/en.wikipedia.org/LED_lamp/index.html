LED lamp

An LED lamp designed to replace a standard incandescent bulb.

An LED lamp or LED light is an electric light that produces light using light-emitting diodes (LEDs). LED lamps are significantly more energy-efficient than equivalent incandescent lamps and can be significantly more efficient than most fluorescent lamps. The most efficient commercially available LED lamps have efficiencies exceeding 200 lumens per watt (lm/W). Commercial LED lamps have a lifespan many times longer than incandescent lamps.

LED lamps require an electronic LED driver circuit to convert incoming alternating current into the regulated direct current needed by the LEDs. This driver is usually built into the lamp. LEDs come to full brightness immediately without a warm-up delay, and frequent switching does not reduce their lifespan. Light output decreases gradually over the lifetime of the LED (lumen depreciation).

History

The first visible-spectrum LED was developed in 1962 by Nick Holonyak, but early LEDs were dim and only red. High-brightness blue LEDs, developed in the early 1990s by Shuji Nakamura and others using gallium nitride (work recognised by the 2014 Nobel Prize in Physics), made white light practical by combining a blue emitter with a yellow phosphor. Through the 2000s and 2010s prices fell rapidly, and LED lamps displaced incandescent and compact fluorescent lamps in homes, offices, street lighting and vehicles.

Technology

A typical LED lamp consists of one or more LED packages (often surface-mounted devices or chip-on-board arrays), a heat sink, a driver, and optics or a diffuser to spread the light. Because LEDs are sensitive to heat, thermal management strongly affects lifespan; rated lifetimes of 25,000 to 50,000 hours are common for domestic lamps, against about 1,000 hours for a typical incandescent bulb. Lamp colour is described by correlated colour temperature (e.g. 2700 K "warm white" to 5000 K "daylight") and by colour rendering index (CRI).

Efficiency and policy

LED lamps typically use 75–85% less energy than equivalent incandescent lamps. Many jurisdictions — including the European Union, the United States and others — have phased out general-purpose incandescent lamps or set minimum efficiency standards that effectively made LED lamps the default technology for general lighting in the 2010s and 2020s.
