Proteus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In Greek mythology, Proteus (/ˈproʊtiəs, -tjuːs/; Ancient Greek: Πρωτεύς, Prōteús) is an early prophetic sea god or god of rivers and oceanic bodies of water, one of several deities referred to by Homer as the "Old Man of the Sea" (halios gerôn). Some who ascribe a specific domain to Proteus call him the god of "elusive sea change", which suggests the constant shifting nature of the sea or the liquid quality of water. He is one of the gods of the sea in classical antiquity, the subject of one of the most famous tales in literature.

Proteus was known for his ability to change his shape at will, transforming into animals, plants, or elements such as fire and water to evade capture. Those who wished to consult him — since he possessed the gift of prophecy and knew all things past, present, and future — had to seize him while he slept among his flock of seals and hold on through all his transformations until he resumed his true form and answered their questions.

In literature

The most famous account appears in Book 4 of Homer's Odyssey: Menelaus, stranded in Egypt on his return from Troy, ambushes Proteus on the island of Pharos, holds him through his changes into a lion, a serpent, a leopard, a boar, water, and a tree, and learns from him the fate of the Greek heroes and how to return home. In Virgil's Georgics, Aristaeus similarly captures Proteus to learn the cause of his bees' death. Herodotus also gives the name Proteus to a historical king of Egypt who figures in his version of the story of Helen of Troy.

Legacy

From Proteus comes the adjective "protean", meaning versatile, mutable, or capable of assuming many forms. His name has been given to many things, including a moon of Neptune, the bacterial genus Proteus, the olm (also called Proteus anguinus), and Proteus syndrome, a rare disorder causing overgrowth of tissues. In modern retellings, Proteus embodies the evasive nature of truth and identity, appearing in works from Shakespeare's The Two Gentlemen of Verona (whose character Proteus is named for his changeable affections) to contemporary fiction and film.
