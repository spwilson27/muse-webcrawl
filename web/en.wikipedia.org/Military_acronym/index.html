Military acronym

Military acronyms and abbreviations are used extensively by armed forces worldwide to compress the long, formal names of units, equipment, procedures and doctrines into short spoken and written forms. Military culture is famous for generating thousands of initialisms — including AWOL (absent without leave), SNAFU (situation normal: all fouled up), FUBAR (fouled up beyond all recognition), and more recent coinages such as IED (improvised explosive device), UAV (unmanned aerial vehicle) and ROE (rules of engagement).

Characteristics

Acronyms serve radio brevity, written orders and interoperability. NATO standardisation agreements (STANAGs) and joint publications such as the U.S. Department of Defense's DOD Dictionary maintain official lists running to tens of thousands of entries. Because the same letter group can mean different things in different services, contexts and nations (for example, "CO" for commanding officer or company), official communication often requires expansion on first use.

History

Mass acronym usage dates from the First World War, when bureaucratic abbreviation spread through vast conscript armies, and expanded dramatically in the Second World War alongside the NATO phonetic alphabet (Alfa, Bravo, Charlie ...), which is used to spell acronyms and call signs unambiguously over noisy channels. Soldiers have always generated irreverent unofficial examples — SNAFU and FUBAR began as enlisted slang in the U.S. Army of the 1940s — which satirise the official style.

Criticism

Excessive acronym use ("alphabet soup") is criticised for excluding new personnel, allies and the public, and style guides in several defence ministries now limit acronym density in documents.
