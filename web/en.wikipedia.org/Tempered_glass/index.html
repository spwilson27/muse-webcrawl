Tempered glass

Tempered or toughened glass is a type of safety glass processed by controlled thermal or chemical treatments to increase its strength compared with normal glass. Tempering puts the outer surfaces into compression and the interior into tension. Such stresses cause the glass, when broken, to shatter into small granular chunks instead of splintering into jagged shards as ordinary annealed glass does; the granular chunks are less likely to cause injury.

Manufacturing

In thermal tempering, glass is heated in a tempering oven to more than 600 °C and then rapidly cooled with high-pressure air in a process called quenching. The surfaces cool and contract first, placing them in compression, while the slower-cooling interior develops balancing tensile stress. Tempered glass is typically four to five times stronger than annealed glass of the same thickness. It cannot be cut or drilled after tempering, so all fabrication must be done beforehand.

Uses

Because of its strength and safe breakage pattern, tempered glass is used in shower doors, glass doors and tables, refrigerator trays, oven doors, the side and rear windows of vehicles, mobile phone screen protectors, and as a component of bulletproof glass. Building codes in many jurisdictions require its use in locations where impact is likely, such as doors and low windows. A well-known demonstration of internal stress is Prince Rupert's drop, a glass tadpole shape that resists blows to its head but explodes if its tail is snipped.
