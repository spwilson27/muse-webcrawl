In number theory, the Green–Tao theorem, proved by Ben Green and Terence Tao in 2004, states that the sequence of prime numbers contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions. In other words, for every natural number k there exist k primes in arithmetic progression—that is, primes p, p+d, p+2d, …, p+(k−1)d for some common difference d.

Background and proof

The result generalizes Szemerédi's theorem on arithmetic progressions in dense sets of integers, but the primes are not dense, so Green and Tao developed a "transference principle": they embedded the primes in a pseudorandom "majorant" set (built using the Goldston–Yıldırım sieve estimates) in which a relative version of Szemerédi's theorem still applies. The proof combines ergodic theory, Fourier analysis, and sieve theory. The theorem does not by itself give an explicit progression; the longest known prime arithmetic progressions, found computationally, run to dozens of terms.

Extensions

Green and Tao later proved related results for Gaussian primes and, with Tamar Ziegler, the existence of arithmetic progressions in more general polynomial patterns in the primes. Tao received the Fields Medal in 2006 partly for this work.
