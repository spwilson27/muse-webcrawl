Just-noticeable difference

In the branch of experimental psychology known as psychophysics, a just-noticeable difference (JND), also known as the difference threshold, is the minimum level of stimulation that a person can detect 50 percent of the time. For example, if asked to hold two objects of different weights, the just-noticeable difference would be the minimum weight difference between the two that the subject could reliably perceive.

Weber's law

The JND is a statistical, rather than an exact, quantity: trials vary, and the JND is the difference reported by a subject on a particular trial that they could perceive. Ernst Heinrich Weber observed that the JND is a constant proportion of the original stimulus value — a relationship known as Weber's law. Gustav Fechner later built on this work, and the ratio of the JND to the reference stimulus is called the Weber fraction.

Applications

Difference thresholds are measured for all senses, including brightness, loudness, pitch, taste and touch. The JND is applied in fields such as audio engineering (perceptible changes in sound level), marketing (price and product changes kept below detection), and human–computer interaction. In music, the JND for pitch for trained listeners can be as small as a few cents.
