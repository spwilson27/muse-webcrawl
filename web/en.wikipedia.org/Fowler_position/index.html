Fowler's position

In Fowler's position the head of the bed is raised, typically to 45–60 degrees.

Fowler's position, also known as semi-sitting position, is a standard patient position in medicine in which the patient lies on their back with the head and trunk raised, typically between 45 and 60 degrees. It is named after George Ryerson Fowler, an American surgeon who promoted the position in the late 19th century for patients with peritonitis, on the theory that infected fluid would pool in the pelvis, where it could drain more safely, rather than spreading through the abdomen.

Variations

PositionHead of bed elevation
Semi-Fowler's (low Fowler's)15–45°
Fowler's (standard)45–60°
High Fowler's60–90° (near sitting)

The knees may be slightly flexed or supported to prevent the patient from sliding down the bed.

Uses

Fowler's position eases breathing: it lets the diaphragm descend and the chest expand more fully, so it is used for patients in respiratory distress and for those with heart failure. It is also standard positioning during shoulder and nasal surgery, for feeding patients at risk of aspiration, for nasogastric tube insertion, and to promote drainage after abdominal surgery. Sitting patients up also reduces intracranial pressure in certain neurosurgical contexts. Care must be taken to protect the sacrum and heels from pressure injuries, since much of the patient's weight rests on them.
