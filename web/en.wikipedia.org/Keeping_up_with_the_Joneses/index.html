Keeping up with the Joneses

"Keeping up with the Joneses" is an idiom in many parts of the English-speaking world referring to the comparison of oneself to one's neighbour as a benchmark of social class or the accumulation of material goods. To fail to "keep up" is perceived as demonstrating socio-economic or cultural inferiority.

Origin

The phrase originates with the comic strip Keeping Up with the Joneses, created by Arthur R. "Pop" Momand in 1913 and published in the New York World and other papers until 1938. The strip satirised the McGinis family's desperate attempts to match the lifestyle of the never-seen Joneses next door. Momand based the idea on his own experience living in Cedarhurst, New York, among wealthy neighbours. The "Joneses" may also have alluded to the famously rich Jones family of New York, builders of grand mansions on Fifth Avenue, though Momand said the name was simply common.

Social science

The expression encapsulates concepts later formalised by economists and sociologists: Thorstein Veblen's conspicuous consumption in The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899), relative income theory, and status competition. Studies have linked neighbour-comparison spending to household debt, bankruptcy and reduced well-being; the related idea that people care about relative rather than absolute income is sometimes called the "Joneses effect" in economics.
