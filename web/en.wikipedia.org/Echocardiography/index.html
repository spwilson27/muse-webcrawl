Echocardiography

Echocardiography, also known as cardiac ultrasound, is the use of ultrasound to examine the heart. It is one of the most widely used diagnostic tests in cardiology, providing real-time images of the heart's chambers, valves, walls, and blood flow without ionising radiation. A standard echocardiogram is obtained either by placing a transducer on the chest wall (transthoracic echocardiography, TTE) or by passing a specialised probe into the oesophagus (transoesophageal echocardiography, TOE/TEE), which lies directly behind the heart and yields clearer images of posterior structures.

Techniques

Echocardiography combines several ultrasound modes. M-mode records motion along a single scan line with high temporal resolution. Two-dimensional (B-mode) imaging shows cross-sectional views of the beating heart; 3D echocardiography reconstructs volumes. Doppler techniques measure blood velocities: pulsed-wave and continuous-wave Doppler quantify flow across valves and calculate pressure gradients via the Bernoulli equation, while colour Doppler maps flow direction in real time, revealing regurgitant jets and shunts. Tissue Doppler measures myocardial motion itself. Contrast agents (microbubbles) can outline the endocardium or assess perfusion, and stress echocardiography images the heart before and after exercise or pharmacological stress to detect inducible ischaemia.

Clinical uses

Echocardiography is the principal test for assessing left ventricular size and ejection fraction, valvular stenosis and regurgitation, congenital heart defects, cardiomyopathies, pericardial effusion and tamponade, infective endocarditis (for which transoesophageal imaging is especially sensitive), intracardiac masses and clots, and diseases of the aorta such as dissection. It is used to evaluate heart failure, murmurs, chest pain, breathlessness, and syncope, and to guide interventions from pericardiocentesis to transcatheter valve replacement. Foetal echocardiography examines the heart before birth.

History

Medical ultrasound developed from sonar and industrial flaw detection. Swedish physician Inge Edler and physicist Carl Hellmuth Hertz produced the first usable echocardiograms in Lund in the early 1950s using a borrowed ultrasonic reflectoscope, initially recording mitral valve motion — Edler is often called the father of echocardiography. Harvey Feigenbaum advanced and popularised the technique in the United States in the 1960s; the subsequent decades added two-dimensional imaging, Doppler and colour flow mapping, transoesophageal probes, and digital 3D systems, making the echocardiogram the stethoscope-like cornerstone of modern cardiology.

Limitations

Image quality depends on the acoustic window: obesity, lung disease, and chest deformity can degrade transthoracic images. The test is operator-dependent, and measurements such as ejection fraction are subject to variability; cardiac MRI and CT complement it where echocardiography is inconclusive. Transoesophageal studies carry small procedural risks and require sedation.
