Pythagorean tuning

Pythagorean tuning is a system of musical tuning in which the frequency ratios of all intervals are based on the ratio 3:2, the perfect fifth. The system is attributed to the ancient Greek philosopher Pythagoras and his followers, who investigated the mathematical ratios of vibrating strings. A scale is built by stacking twelve perfect fifths and reducing them into one octave.

Construction and the Pythagorean comma

Because (3/2)12 does not equal 27, twelve fifths overshoot seven octaves by a small interval — the Pythagorean comma, about 23.5 cents. Consequently one fifth in the circle (the "wolf fifth") must be narrowed, and the twelve semitones fall into two sizes: the limma (90 cents) and the apotome (114 cents). Pythagorean major thirds, generated by four fifths, are wider than the pure 5:4 third by the syntonic comma, giving them a bright but restless character.

Use

Pythagorean tuning was the principal tuning system of medieval European music, well suited to the fifths- and fourths-based polyphony of the era. As triadic harmony emphasising thirds spread in the Renaissance, it was displaced by meantone temperament and eventually by equal temperament. It survives in the performance of medieval music and in the intonation habits of melody instruments and a cappella singers, who naturally drift toward pure fifths.
