Burrata is an Italian cow's milk (sometimes buffalo milk) cheese made from mozzarella and cream. The outer casing is solid cheese, while the inside contains stracciatella and clotted cream, giving it an unusually soft, fluid texture. It is a delicacy of the Apulia region in southern Italy; its name derives from burro, Italian for butter.

Production

Cheesemakers form a pouch of stretched mozzarella curd (pasta filata), fill it with a mixture of shredded mozzarella strands (stracciatella) and fresh cream, then seal it, often tying the top with a knot or leaf of asphodel. Burrata is best eaten very fresh—ideally within 24–48 hours—and historically was wrapped in asphodel leaves whose wilting indicated freshness.

History

Burrata was invented in Andria, in the Murgia region, around the 1920s—commonly credited to Lorenzo Bianchino at the Piana Padura farm—as a way to use mozzarella scraps and morning cream. It remained a local product until the late 20th century, when it spread to fine restaurants worldwide; the EU granted "Burrata di Andria" protected geographical indication (IGP) status in 2016.

Serving

Burrata is served at room temperature, often with prosciutto crudo, tomatoes, grilled bread, and olive oil. When the casing is sliced, the creamy centre flows out. Because of its short shelf life, it is frequently air-freighted fresh from Apulia.
