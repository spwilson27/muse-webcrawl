Doppelgänger


The doppelgänger is a look-alike double of a living person in folklore.

A doppelgänger (German for "double-goer") is a biologically unrelated look-alike, double, or apparition of a living person. In folklore and mythology, seeing one's own doppelgänger is often taken as an omen of illness, bad luck, or death, while a doppelgänger seen by friends or relatives is also considered a harbinger of misfortune.

Etymology and folklore

The word entered English from German literature of the Romantic period; Jean Paul, who coined much of the popular usage in his 1796 novel Siebenkäs, used it for a double who shares one's appearance. Analogous concepts appear in many cultures: the Egyptian ka, the Norse vardøger (a spirit that precedes a person), the Finnish etiäinen, and the "fetch" of Irish folklore.

In literature and psychology

Doubles recur throughout Gothic and psychological fiction, from Dostoevsky's The Double and Poe's "William Wilson" to Stevenson's Strange Case of Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde and Oscar Wilde's The Picture of Dorian Gray. In psychiatry, the experience of seeing one's double is termed heautoscopy and is associated with certain neurological and psychiatric conditions.
