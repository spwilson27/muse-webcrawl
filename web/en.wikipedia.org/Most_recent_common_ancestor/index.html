Most recent common ancestor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In biology and genealogy, the most recent common ancestor (MRCA), also known as the last common ancestor (LCA) or concestor, of a set of organisms is the most recent individual from which all the organisms of the set are descended. The term is also used in reference to the ancestry of groups of genes (haplotypes) rather than organisms.

Concepts

The MRCA of a set of individuals is identified by tracing pedigrees or gene genealogies back in time until the lines converge. In a sexually reproducing population the MRCA is rarely a single individual alone: mates who lived earlier or at the same time also contribute ancestry. For genes inherited from only one parent, the MRCA is traced through a single lineage: the matrilineal MRCA of all living humans is Mitochondrial Eve, and the patrilineal MRCA is Y-chromosomal Adam — two different individuals who lived at different times.

Time to most recent common ancestor

Statistical models of population genetics show that the genealogical MRCA of all living humans is surprisingly recent — plausibly only a few thousand years ago — because pedigrees branch exponentially, whereas genetic common ancestors (who actually contributed DNA) are far older. The time to the MRCA (TMRCA) of a gene is estimated using the coalescent process and molecular-clock methods.
