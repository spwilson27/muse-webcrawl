Drinking horn

A drinking horn is a drinking vessel made from the horn of an animal, typically cattle or the extinct aurochs, used from antiquity into the early modern period and especially associated with the cultures of ancient Europe and Scandinavia. Horns were emptied of their core, cleaned, polished, and often fitted with metal rims, tips and mounts.

History

Horn vessels were used by the Scythians, Thracians, Greeks and Romans; Greek sources describe the rhyton, a related ritual vessel. In the Germanic world, drinking horns were prestigious feasting equipment: the 7th-century Sutton Hoo ship burial contained large mounted horns, and the 5th-century BC Guldhøi and the Golden Horns of Gallehus (c. 400 AD) from Denmark were famously ornate. Old English heroic poetry such as Beowulf depicts horns passed ceremonially in the mead hall.

Ritual and later use

Horns featured in toasts, oaths and libations, and in Norse tradition in ritual blessings (the minni toasts). Because a horn cannot be set down full, stands were made for display and storage. Horns remained in ceremonial use in guilds and universities into modern times, and replica horns are popular in historical re-enactment, Ásatrú ritual, and as novelty drinkware today.
