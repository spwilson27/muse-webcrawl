Rickets


Bowed legs, a classic sign of rickets in children

Rickets is a disorder of growing bone in children caused by defective mineralisation of the growth plates, most commonly from vitamin D deficiency. Because the unmineralised bone is soft, the skeleton deforms under load, producing bowed legs, a distorted pelvis and chest, delayed growth and dental problems. The equivalent condition in adults, where growth plates have closed, is osteomalacia.

Causes

The most common cause worldwide is inadequate vitamin D, from insufficient sunlight exposure and dietary intake; vitamin D is needed for intestinal absorption of calcium and phosphate. Other causes include dietary calcium deficiency, malabsorption (for example in coeliac disease), chronic kidney disease, and inherited disorders of vitamin D or phosphate metabolism, such as X-linked hypophosphataemic rickets — the most common genetic form, caused by mutations in the PHEX gene that raise levels of the phosphaturic hormone FGF23.

Signs and symptoms

Features include delayed closure of the fontanelles, frontal bossing of the skull, a "rachitic rosary" of swollen rib–cartilage junctions, pigeon chest, Harrison's groove, widened wrists and ankles, delayed walking, short stature, and bowing of the legs (or knock-knees in older children). Severe vitamin D deficiency can also cause muscle weakness and hypocalcaemic seizures in infants.

History

Rickets was described in the 17th century by Daniel Whistler and Francis Glisson. It became rampant in the smoky industrial cities of the 19th century — "the English disease" — because air pollution and indoor life blocked ultraviolet light. In the early 20th century researchers including Edward Mellanby and Elmer McCollum showed that cod liver oil and ultraviolet irradiation cured the disease, leading to the identification of vitamin D and to food fortification programmes that largely eliminated nutritional rickets in developed countries. It has since re-emerged in some populations with low sun exposure or unsupplemented restrictive diets.

Treatment and prevention

Nutritional rickets is treated with vitamin D and calcium supplementation; deformities often remodel in young children, though severe cases may need orthopaedic surgery. Hereditary hypophosphataemic forms are treated with phosphate and active vitamin D analogues, or with the anti-FGF23 antibody burosumab. Prevention centres on adequate sunlight, dietary vitamin D, and supplementation for at-risk infants and children.
