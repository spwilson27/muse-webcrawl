Juvenalian satire

Juvenalian satire is a form of satire named after the Roman poet Juvenal (late 1st–early 2nd century AD), author of the Satires. In contrast to the gentler, more humorous Horatian satire, Juvenalian satire is bitter, indignant and moralising: the satirist adopts the persona of an enraged public moralist attacking vice, corruption and folly in society.

Characteristics

Juvenal's motto-like phrases — panem et circenses ("bread and circuses"), quis custodiet ipsos custodes? ("who will guard the guards themselves?") and mens sana in corpore sano ("a healthy mind in a healthy body") — come from his tenth satire and others. His style features savage irony, exaggerated catalogues of vice, and declamatory anger; his famous opening declares it is hard not to write satire (difficile est saturam non scribere).

Influence

Juvenal's model shaped later satirists including Samuel Johnson, whose poems "London" (1738) and "The Vanity of Human Wishes" (1749) directly imitate Juvenal's third and tenth satires, and Jonathan Swift, whose Gulliver's Travels and "A Modest Proposal" display Juvenalian contempt for human pride and corruption. The term is still used for harshly condemnatory satire in literature, journalism and stand-up comedy.
