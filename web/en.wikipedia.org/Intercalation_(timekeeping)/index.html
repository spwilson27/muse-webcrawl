Intercalation (timekeeping)

Intercalation
PurposeKeep calendars aligned with astronomical cycles
FormsLeap day, leap month, leap second
Used inGregorian, Julian, Hebrew, Chinese, and other calendars

Intercalation or embolism in timekeeping is the insertion of a leap day, week, or month into some calendar years to make the calendar follow the seasons or Moon phases. Because astronomical cycles do not contain a whole number of days, calendars without intercalation drift against the phenomena they are meant to track.

Lunisolar calendars

Lunisolar calendars, such as the Hebrew, Chinese, Hindu, and Buddhist calendars, add an intercalary month ( embolismic month ) in some years to reconcile the roughly 354-day lunar year with the 365-day solar year. In the Hebrew calendar, seven years in each 19-year Metonic cycle receive a thirteenth month, Adar I.

Solar calendars

Purely solar calendars intercalate days: the Julian calendar added a leap day every four years, and the Gregorian calendar refined this by omitting leap days in most century years. The leap day is 29 February. The Egyptian and Coptic calendars similarly add intercalary days.

Other forms

The leap second, added occasionally to Coordinated Universal Time (UTC), is a form of intercalation compensating for irregularities in Earth's rotation. Some week-based calendars, such as the ISO week date system, intercalate a 53rd week in certain years.
