Double Irish arrangement

The Double Irish was a base erosion and profit shifting (BEPS) corporate tax avoidance strategy used by U.S.-based multinationals to avoid paying corporation tax on non-U.S. profits. The strategy used payments between related entities in a corporate structure to shift income from a higher-tax country to a lower-tax country. It relied on the fact that Irish tax law did not include U.S. transfer pricing rules, and that Ireland considered a company tax-resident where it was managed and controlled, rather than where it was incorporated.

Mechanism

In the classic structure, a U.S. parent created two Irish subsidiaries. The first (Irish company 1) held the group's non-U.S. intellectual property rights and licensed them to the second (Irish company 2), which sold products to customers abroad. Irish company 2 paid large royalties to Irish company 1, stripping its Irish profits. Irish company 1, although incorporated in Ireland, was managed and controlled from a tax haven such as Bermuda, making it tax-resident there — and because the haven levied no corporation tax, the royalty income was effectively untaxed. Adding a Dutch intermediate company ("Double Irish with a Dutch sandwich") avoided Irish withholding taxes on the payments.

Users and scale

The arrangement was famously used by Apple, Google, Microsoft, Facebook and other technology and pharmaceutical companies. Apple's variant, examined by a U.S. Senate committee in 2013, became the subject of a European Commission state-aid decision in August 2016 ordering Ireland to recover €13 billion in back taxes from Apple — a decision annulled by the EU General Court in 2020 but reinstated by the European Court of Justice in September 2024.

Abolition

Ireland announced the closure of the arrangement in its October 2014 budget; existing users were grandfathered until the end of 2020. Companies migrated to successor structures such as the "Single Malt" and Ireland's capital allowances for intangible assets (the "Green Jersey" structure), which reproduced much of the effect within Irish law.
