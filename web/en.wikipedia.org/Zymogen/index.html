Zymogen

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In biochemistry, a zymogen (/ˈzaɪmədʒən, -moʊ-/), also called a proenzyme (/ˌproʊˈɛnzaɪm/), is an inactive precursor of an enzyme. A zymogen requires a biochemical change — usually the hydrolysis of one or more peptide bonds — for it to become an active enzyme. The change often occurs in a lysosome or at the site where the enzyme is needed. Zymogens are frequently denoted by the prefix "pro-" or the suffix "-ogen": for example, proinsulin and pepsinogen.

Function

Producing enzymes as inactive precursors protects the cell that synthesises them. The pancreas secretes the digestive zymogens pepsinogen's counterpart trypsinogen, chymotrypsinogen and procarboxypeptidase into the small intestine, where they are activated (trypsinogen is converted to trypsin by enteropeptidase, and trypsin then activates the others). Without this precaution, potent proteases would digest the pancreas itself; premature activation within the gland is a cause of acute pancreatitis.

Examples

Other important zymogen systems include the blood clotting cascade (prothrombin → thrombin, fibrinogen → fibrin), the complement system, caspases in apoptosis, and prohormones such as proinsulin, which is cleaved to insulin. Zymogen granules in pancreatic acinar cells store the precursors before secretion.
