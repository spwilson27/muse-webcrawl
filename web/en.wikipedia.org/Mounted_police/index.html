Mounted police

Mounted police are police who patrol on horseback. Mounted units combine the height and visibility of a horse with its mobility and crowd presence; they remain in service worldwide for patrol, crowd management, ceremonial duty, and search and rescue in terrain inaccessible to vehicles.

History

London's Bow Street Horse Patrol, founded in 1805 to combat highwaymen, is often considered the first modern mounted police unit; it was absorbed into the Metropolitan Police, whose mounted branch, established in 1828, still patrols the capital. Colonial expansion spread mounted policing: the North-West Mounted Police (1873), forerunner of the Royal Canadian Mounted Police — the "Mounties" — policed Canada's western frontier, and mounted constabularies served across Africa, India, and Australia.

Roles

A mounted officer at roughly 10 feet (3 m) eye level can survey crowds that would swallow a foot officer; a single police horse is often said to provide the crowd-control presence of about ten officers on foot. Horses also excel at community policing — they draw the public in — and at patrolling parks, beaches, and rural areas, as well as at ceremonial occasions and state occasions escorts.

Notable units

Famous units include the Royal Canadian Mounted Police, the Metropolitan Police Mounted Branch (London), the New York City Police Department Mounted Unit (one of the oldest in the US, dating to 1858), the Hong Kong Police Mounted Unit, and mounted regiments of the Indian police and Border Security Force.

Tactics and equipment

Police horses are desensitised (trained to remain calm amid noise, flags, smoke, and crowds), and may wear protective visors and leg guards during public-order deployments. Riders use longer batons and specific formations — wedges and lines — to move crowds.
