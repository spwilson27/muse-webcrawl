An automatic landing in fog, flown by the autopilot down to touchdown and rollout.

In aviation, autoland describes a system that fully automates the landing procedure of an aircraft's flight, with the flight crew supervising the process. Such systems enable airliners to land in weather conditions too poor for a manual landing, including dense fog, provided the airport and aircraft are equipped for Category III ILS operations.

History

Autoland was developed in the United Kingdom by the Royal Aircraft Establishment's Blind Landing Experimental Unit (BLEU) in the late 1940s and 1950s, driven by Britain's foggy climate. The first automatic landing by a scheduled passenger aircraft was made by a British European Airways Hawker Siddeley Trident in 1965 — in routine development service — and the Trident performed the first autoland in regular passenger service at London Heathrow in 1968. Early systems were required to be "fail-operational": duplicated or triplicated autopilots ensured that a single failure could not prevent a safe landing.

Operation

During an autoland approach, the autopilot flies the aircraft down the ILS glideslope and localiser, flares it at the correct height, retards the throttles, touches down on the runway centreline, and in many types controls the rollout until taxi speed. Decision heights for Category IIIb approaches can be as low as 50 feet or nonexistent, with runway visual range as low as 75 metres. Autoland is also practised regularly in good weather to maintain crew and system currency.
