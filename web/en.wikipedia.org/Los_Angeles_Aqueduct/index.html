Los Angeles Aqueduct

The Los Angeles Aqueduct system, comprising the First Los Angeles Aqueduct (Owens Valley aqueduct) and the Second Los Angeles Aqueduct, is a water conveyance system operated by the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power. The aqueduct diverts water from the Owens River in the Eastern Sierra Nevada to the city of Los Angeles, California.

History

The aqueduct was designed and built by the city's water department under William Mulholland, Los Angeles' supervising engineer. Completed in November 1913 at a cost of about $23 million, it opened with Mulholland's famous instruction at the dedication ceremony: "There it is. Take it." The project was built ahead of schedule and under budget, but the means used to acquire water rights in the Owens Valley were controversial: Los Angeles agents purchased land and water rights in ways that residents considered deceptive, sparking the California Water Wars.

Construction

The 233-mile (375 km) aqueduct carries water by gravity alone, using the drop from the Owens Valley elevation to the city to power hydroelectric plants along the route. Construction employed thousands of workers and involved tunnels, canals, pipelines, and siphons crossing mountainous terrain north of Los Angeles. The St. Francis Dam, built as part of the system, collapsed catastrophically in 1928, killing at least 431 people in one of the worst American engineering disasters of the twentieth century and effectively ending Mulholland's career.

Second Aqueduct and later issues

The Second Los Angeles Aqueduct, completed in 1970, roughly paralleled the first and increased deliveries to the city. Diversions dried up Owens Lake, creating severe dust pollution, and later reductions in pumping were ordered to protect Mono Lake and restore the Owens Valley environment; Los Angeles has spent billions of dollars on dust mitigation at the dry lakebed.
