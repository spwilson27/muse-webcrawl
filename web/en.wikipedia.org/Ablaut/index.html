Ablaut

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In linguistics, ablaut (from German ab- "off" + Laut "sound") is a system of apophony—regular vowel alternation—in which a word's internal vowel changes to indicate grammatical function such as tense, case, or derivation. The term was coined by Jacob Grimm. Ablaut was a central feature of Proto-Indo-European morphology and survives most visibly in the "strong verbs" of Germanic languages, as in English sing–sang–sung and drive–drove–driven.

Indo-European ablaut

Proto-Indo-European roots typically contained the vowel *e, which alternated in a pattern called vowel gradation. The principal grades are the e-grade (full grade), the o-grade, and the zero grade, in which the vowel disappears entirely, along with lengthened grades with *ē and *ō. For example, the root *bʰer- "carry" gives e-grade *bʰer- (Greek phérō), o-grade *bʰor- (Sanskrit bhāra- "burden", with lengthening), and zero grade *bʰr̥- (Sanskrit bhṛti- "carrying"). Originally conditioned by the position of the accent, the alternations became grammaticalised as the accent system changed.

In Germanic languages

Proto-Germanic organised its strong verbs into seven ablaut classes, each with a characteristic vowel series across the principal parts (infinitive, past singular, past plural, past participle). English preserves these patterns in verbs such as drink–drank–drunk (Class III), bear–bore–borne (Class IV) and fall–fell–fallen (Class VII). Over time many strong verbs have become weak, taking the dental suffix -ed instead, and the system is no longer productive, though new analogical forms occasionally arise.

Other examples

Ablaut-like patterns occur beyond Indo-European: the consonantal-root morphology of Semitic languages relies on vowel alternation (apophony) between related words, and similar internal vowel changes mark plurals in some Nilotic and Cushitic languages. English also shows fossilised ablaut in pairs like foot–feet (via i-mutation/umlaut, a related but distinct phenomenon) and in reduplicative compounds such as zigzag and flip-flop.
