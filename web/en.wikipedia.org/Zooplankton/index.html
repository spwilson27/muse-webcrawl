Zooplankton are the animal component of the planktonic community ("zoo" comes from the Greek zôion, meaning "animal", and planktos, meaning "wanderer" or "drifter"). They are heterotrophic organisms that drift in oceans, seas, and freshwater bodies, ranging in size from microscopic rotifers and copepods to large jellyfish. Because many are weak swimmers, their horizontal distribution is governed mainly by water currents, although many undertake daily vertical migrations of hundreds of metres.

Groups and life cycles

Holoplankton spend their entire lives in the plankton — including copepods (the most abundant multicellular animals in the sea), krill, chaetognaths (arrow worms), salps, and many protists. Meroplankton spend only part of their life cycle as plankton, typically the larval stages of crabs, molluscs, barnacles, echinoderms, and fish (ichthyoplankton). In freshwater, cladocerans such as Daphnia, rotifers, and copepods dominate.

Ecology

Zooplankton graze on phytoplankton and are in turn the principal food of many fish, seabirds, and baleen whales, making them a central conduit of energy in aquatic food webs. Their faecal pellets and moulted exoskeletons also export carbon to the deep ocean, contributing to the biological pump. Many zooplankton undertake diel vertical migration — rising to surface waters at night to feed and descending by day to avoid visual predators — the largest synchronized movement of biomass on Earth. Zooplankton communities are sensitive indicators of climate change, ocean acidification (which impairs shell-forming pteropods), and pollution.
