Kings Canyon National Park is a national park in the southern Sierra Nevada of California, east of Fresno. Established in 1940, it preserves a rugged landscape of deep glacier-carved canyons, high alpine peaks, and groves of giant sequoia trees. The park is administered jointly with the adjoining Sequoia National Park to the south, and the two are managed as a single unit.

Geography

The park's centrepiece is Kings Canyon, carved by the Middle Fork of the Kings River and glaciers into one of the deepest canyons in the United States, with a depth of more than 8,000 feet (2,400 m) from rim to river in places. The park contains two main visitor areas: Grant Grove, a detached western section protecting the General Grant Tree — the second-largest known tree on Earth by volume and designated the "Nation's Christmas Tree" in 1925 — and Cedar Grove, at the bottom of the canyon on the South Fork of the Kings River. The eastern two-thirds of the park is roadless wilderness, traversed by the John Muir Trail and Pacific Crest Trail, and includes peaks over 14,000 feet along the Sierra crest.

History

The area was home to Mono (Monache) and Yokuts peoples. John Muir championed the region, comparing Kings Canyon favourably with Yosemite Valley. General Grant Grove was protected as a small national park in 1890; after decades of debate over dams on the Kings River, the modern Kings Canyon National Park was created in 1940, incorporating Grant Grove and the canyon country, and the proposed reservoirs were never built within it. In 1984 most of the park was designated wilderness, and in 1986 it became part of the Sequoia–Kings Canyon Biosphere Reserve.

Nature

Elevation ranges from about 1,370 feet in the foothills to 14,248 feet at Mount Pinchot-adjacent high country on the park boundary, supporting habitats from oak woodland and chaparral through ponderosa pine and sequoia forests to alpine tundra. Wildlife includes black bears, mule deer, bighorn sheep in the high country, and the endangered Sierra Nevada yellow-legged frog.
