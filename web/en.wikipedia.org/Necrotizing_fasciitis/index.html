Necrotizing fasciitis (NF), commonly known in the media as the "flesh-eating disease", is a rapidly progressive, life-threatening bacterial infection of the deep soft tissues that spreads along the fascial planes, causing necrosis of the fascia and subcutaneous tissue while often initially sparing muscle. It is a surgical emergency: without prompt debridement and antibiotics, it commonly leads to sepsis, multi-organ failure, and death.

Classification and causes

Type I necrotizing fasciitis is polymicrobial, involving mixtures of aerobic and anaerobic bacteria, and typically affects people with diabetes or peripheral vascular disease; Fournier gangrene of the perineum is a form of it. Type II is usually monomicrobial, caused by group A Streptococcus (Streptococcus pyogenes), sometimes with Staphylococcus aureus, and can strike healthy people after minor trauma. Rarer forms are associated with water exposure (Vibrio vulnificus, Aeromonas) or with fungal infection in the immunocompromised.

Bacteria enter through a break in the skin — sometimes trivial — or spread from another site. Toxins and enzymes destroy tissue and impair blood flow, allowing the infection to advance along fascia faster than the overlying skin changes suggest.

Signs and symptoms

Early features mimic cellulitis: pain, swelling, and redness. Classic warning signs include pain far more severe than the examination findings ("pain out of proportion"), rapid progression despite antibiotics, tense edema, hemorrhagic blisters, skin discoloration and necrosis, crepitus (gas in tissues), fever, and signs of septic shock. The LRINEC score, based on laboratory values, can support suspicion but cannot exclude the diagnosis.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is clinical and is confirmed at surgery, where grey necrotic fascia, lack of bleeding, and "dishwater" fluid are characteristic; imaging must not delay exploration when suspicion is high. Treatment requires immediate broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics, aggressive fluid resuscitation and intensive care, and — most importantly — urgent, repeated surgical debridement of all necrotic tissue; amputation is sometimes necessary. Adjunctive therapies such as intravenous immunoglobulin and hyperbaric oxygen are controversial.

Epidemiology and history

Necrotizing fasciitis is uncommon, with incidence on the order of a few cases per million people per year in many countries, though rates vary. Descriptions date to Hippocrates and to Joseph Jones, a Confederate surgeon who described "hospital gangrene" in 1871; the term "necrotizing fasciitis" was coined by B. Wilson in 1952. Even with modern care, mortality is commonly reported around 20–30%, and survivors often face extensive reconstructive surgery.
