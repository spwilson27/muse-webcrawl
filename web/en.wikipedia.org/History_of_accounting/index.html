History of accounting

The history of accounting is as old as writing itself: accounting records in ancient Mesopotamia date back more than 7,000 years. Accounting developed through the ancient and medieval worlds, was codified as double-entry bookkeeping in Renaissance Italy, and became a modern profession during the industrial nineteenth century.

Ancient origins

The earliest accounting records are clay tokens and cuneiform tablets from Mesopotamia used to count livestock, grain and labour; some scholars argue that writing itself emerged from accounting. Ancient Egypt kept detailed records of taxes and stores, Rome developed elaborate household and state accounts, and India, China and the Islamic world each produced sophisticated bookkeeping traditions, including early forms of partnership and credit accounting.

Double-entry bookkeeping

Italian merchant cities developed the double-entry system during the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries; the oldest surviving full example is in the books of Florentine bankers and the Genoese massari accounts of 1340. In 1494 the Franciscan friar Luca Pacioli published the first printed description of the Venetian method in his Summa de arithmetica, earning him the title "father of accounting". The system spread through Europe with printed textbooks and was used by merchants and by great enterprises such as the Dutch East India Company.

The professional era

The Industrial Revolution created large-scale enterprises requiring capital accounting, cost accounting and audit. Joint-stock company legislation in Britain (1844, 1855–56) made audited accounts central to corporate life, and professional bodies were founded — in Scotland in 1854, England and Wales in 1880 (ICAEW), and the United States in 1887. The twentieth century brought income-tax accounting, standard-setting bodies, and after the 1929 crash, securities regulation and mandatory disclosure.

Modern accounting

Since the late twentieth century accounting has been transformed by computers, the move toward international standards (IFRS alongside US GAAP), management accounting techniques, and recurring debates over audit quality, fair value and the accounting failures behind scandals such as Enron (2001). Contemporary developments include sustainability reporting and the digitalisation of tax and audit.
