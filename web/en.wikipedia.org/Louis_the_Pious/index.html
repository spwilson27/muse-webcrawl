Louis the Pious

Louis the Pious (Latin: Ludovicus Pius; 778 – 20 June 840), also called Louis the Fair and Louis the Debonaire, was King of the Franks and co-emperor with his father Charlemagne from 813, becoming sole emperor in 814. He was the only surviving adult son of Charlemagne, and his reign was marked by struggles to preserve the empire's unity amid the ambitions of his sons.

Early reign

Crowned King of Aquitaine as a child, Louis campaigned in Spain and Italy before being crowned co-emperor by Charlemagne in 813. On succeeding in 814, he purged his father's court, exiled Charlemagne's counsellors and his own illegitimate half-brothers to monasteries, and pursued reforms of monastic life under Benedict of Aniane. In 817 his Ordinatio Imperii settled the succession on his eldest son Lothair, with subordinate kingdoms for Pippin and Louis the German — an attempt to preserve imperial unity against Frankish partible inheritance custom.

Civil wars

The birth of a fourth son, Charles, to his second wife Judith in 823, and Louis's wish to provide for him, provoked rebellions by his elder sons in 830 and 833. In 833 Louis was captured, publicly deposed, and subjected to a humiliating penance at Soissons, though he was restored the following year. Renewed civil strife among his sons continued until his death on an island in the Rhine near Ingelheim in 840.

Legacy

Louis's epithets reflect the piety and perceived weakness that shaped his reputation; modern historians view him as a conscientious ruler overwhelmed by the structural tensions of the Carolingian succession. Within three years of his death the empire was partitioned at Verdun (843) among his sons, a division with lasting consequences for the map of Europe.
