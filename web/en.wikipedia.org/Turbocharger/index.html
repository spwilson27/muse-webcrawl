Turbocharger

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A turbocharger, formally a turbosupercharger and colloquially a turbo, is a turbine-driven, forced induction device that increases an internal combustion engine's power output by forcing extra compressed air into the combustion chamber. This improvement over a naturally aspirated engine's power output is due to the compressor forcing more air — and proportionately more fuel — into the combustion chamber than atmospheric pressure alone.

Operation

A turbocharger consists of a turbine and a compressor connected by a common shaft. Hot exhaust gases spin the turbine, which in turn drives the compressor, drawing in and pressurising intake air. A wastegate regulates boost pressure, and an intercooler often cools the compressed air to increase its density and reduce knock.

History

Swiss engineer Alfred Büchi patented the turbocharger in 1905. It was applied extensively to aircraft engines in the Second World War — notably American bombers such as the B-17 — to maintain power at altitude, and spread to diesel trucks, ships and locomotives. Passenger-car use took off in the 1970s and 1980s, and since the 2010s turbocharging has become nearly universal in new cars as manufacturers downsize engines to meet fuel-economy and emissions standards.

Characteristics

Turbocharging recovers waste exhaust energy, improving efficiency, but introduces "turbo lag" — a delay in boost while the turbine spools up — mitigated by twin-scroll designs, variable-geometry turbines, twin turbos and electrically assisted turbos. Compared with a mechanically driven supercharger, a turbo places no direct parasitic load on the crankshaft.
