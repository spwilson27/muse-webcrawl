Federal question jurisdiction

The U.S. Supreme Court in Washington, D.C.

In United States law, federal question jurisdiction is the subject-matter jurisdiction of the federal courts over civil actions "arising under" the Constitution, laws or treaties of the United States. It derives from Article III, Section 2 of the Constitution and is conferred on the district courts by statute, principally 28 U.S.C. § 1331.

The well-pleaded complaint rule

Under the well-pleaded complaint rule, established in Louisville & Nashville Railroad Co. v. Mottley (1908), a federal question must appear on the face of the plaintiff's properly pleaded complaint; a federal defence or an anticipated federal issue is not enough. In American Well Works Co. v. Layne & Bowler Co. (1916), Justice Holmes formulated the test that a suit "arises under the law that creates the cause of action", which governs most cases in which a federal statute itself supplies the claim.

Embedded federal issues

State-law claims may also support federal question jurisdiction when they necessarily raise a substantial, actually disputed federal issue whose resolution in federal court is consistent with Congress's allocation of judicial business — the test consolidated in Grable & Sons Metal Products, Inc. v. Darue Engineering & Manufacturing (2005). Unlike diversity jurisdiction, federal question jurisdiction has no minimum amount in controversy.
