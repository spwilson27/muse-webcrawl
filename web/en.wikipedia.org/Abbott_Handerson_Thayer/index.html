Abbott Handerson Thayer

Born12 August 1849, Boston, Massachusetts
Died29 May 1921, Dublin, New Hampshire
NationalityAmerican
Known forPainting; camouflage theory

Abbott Handerson Thayer (1849–1921) was an American artist, naturalist and teacher. He is known for his ethereal paintings of women in white — often his own family members posed as winged "angels" — and for his pioneering work on protective colouration in nature, which laid foundations for the science of military camouflage.

Art

Thayer studied in Brooklyn and Paris, where he worked under Jean-Léon Gérôme. After returning to the United States he gained a reputation as a portraitist and figure painter. Works such as The Virgin (1892–93) and Winged Figure (1889) show young women in white robes against landscape backgrounds and are held by major American museums.

Camouflage research

From the 1890s Thayer devoted himself to the study of animal coloration. In 1909 he published, with his son Gerald, Concealing-Coloration in the Animal Kingdom, setting out the principle of countershading — "Thayer's Law" — by which animals are darker above and lighter below so as to cancel shadows. His claims were controversial (Theodore Roosevelt publicly disputed his ideas about zebra and giraffe colouring), but his concepts of countershading and disruptive patterning were adopted by military camouflage designers in both world wars.
