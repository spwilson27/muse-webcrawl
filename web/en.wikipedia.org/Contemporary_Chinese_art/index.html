Contemporary Chinese art

Contemporary Chinese art refers to art produced in China and by Chinese artists since the end of the Cultural Revolution, particularly the avant-garde movements that emerged from the late 1970s onward. It encompasses painting, sculpture, installation, performance, photography and video, and became a major presence in the international art market in the 2000s.

Emergence

After Mao Zedong's death in 1976, unofficial groups such as the Stars (Xingxing) in 1979 asserted artistic freedom against socialist realism. The '85 New Wave movement of the mid-1980s absorbed Western modernist and conceptual influences. The 1989 exhibition "China/Avant-Garde" at the National Art Museum in Beijing, closed early after an artist fired a pistol at her installation, marked both the movement's peak and its interruption after the Tiananmen Square protests that year.

Styles and movements

In the 1990s, Political Pop combined socialist iconography with advertising imagery, while Cynical Realism portrayed disillusioned, grinning figures amid rapid social change. Artists such as Ai Weiwei, Cai Guo-Qiang, Zhang Xiaogang, Yue Minjun, Fang Lijun and Wang Guangyi achieved international recognition; Xu Bing and others explored language, print and installation. Scar painting and native-soil realism had earlier addressed the wounds of the Cultural Revolution.

Institutions and market

Gallery districts such as Beijing's 798 Art Zone and Shanghai's M50, the Ullens Center for Contemporary Art, and strong auction demand made Chinese contemporary art one of the world's largest market segments in the late 2000s, though prices later moderated. Official tolerance has fluctuated, and artists negotiate censorship alongside commercial success.
