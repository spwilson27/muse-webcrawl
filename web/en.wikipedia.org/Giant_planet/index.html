Giant planet

A giant planet is a diverse type of planet much larger than Earth, usually composed primarily of materials with low boiling points (gases or ices) rather than rock or other solid matter. The Solar System contains four giant planets: Jupiter and Saturn (the gas giants), and Uranus and Neptune (the ice giants). Numerous giant planets have been detected orbiting other stars.

Giant planets are sometimes called Jovian planets, after Jupiter. Gas giants consist mainly of hydrogen and helium, while ice giants are richer in heavier volatile substances such as water, ammonia and methane, with smaller hydrogen-helium envelopes.

Formation

In the core accretion model, giant planets formed beyond the frost line of the young Solar System, where abundant ice allowed solid cores of roughly ten Earth masses to grow quickly and then gravitationally capture large envelopes of hydrogen and helium before the protoplanetary disc dispersed.

Exoplanets

Many of the first exoplanets discovered were giant planets, including "hot Jupiters" orbiting very close to their stars — configurations that challenged formation theories and led to the concept of planetary migration. Today giant exoplanets are known across a wide range of orbits and masses, up to the boundary with brown dwarfs at about 13 Jupiter masses.
