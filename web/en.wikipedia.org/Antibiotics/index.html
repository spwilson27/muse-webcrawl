Antibiotics are medicines that inhibit the growth of or destroy bacteria. They are used to treat and sometimes prevent bacterial infections; they have no effect on viruses. Antibiotics are among the most commonly prescribed drugs and transformed twentieth-century medicine, making previously lethal infections curable and enabling surgery, chemotherapy, and organ transplantation.

History

Paul Ehrlich's arsphenamine (Salvarsan, 1909) was the first modern antimicrobial chemotherapy. Sulfonamide drugs followed in the 1930s. Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin in 1928 from the mould Penicillium, and its development into a drug by Howard Florey, Ernst Chain, and colleagues in the early 1940s ushered in the antibiotic era. Selman Waksman, who coined the word "antibiotic", discovered streptomycin in 1943, the first effective treatment for tuberculosis.

Mechanisms of action

Antibiotics act by inhibiting bacterial cell wall synthesis (beta-lactams such as penicillins and cephalosporins; glycopeptides such as vancomycin), protein synthesis (aminoglycosides, tetracyclines, macrolides), nucleic acid synthesis (fluoroquinolones, metronidazole), or folate metabolism (sulfonamides, trimethoprim). They may be bactericidal or bacteriostatic, and narrow- or broad-spectrum.

Resistance

Antimicrobial resistance arises through mutation and horizontal gene transfer and is accelerated by overuse and misuse in medicine and agriculture. Multidrug-resistant organisms such as MRSA, resistant Mycobacterium tuberculosis, and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacterales are major public-health threats, prompting antibiotic stewardship programmes and efforts to develop new agents.
