Extradition

Extradition is governed by treaties between states.

Extradition is an act by which one jurisdiction delivers a person accused or convicted of committing a crime in another jurisdiction, at the request of that other jurisdiction, so that the person may stand trial or serve a sentence there. It is a cooperative law-enforcement process that depends on the arrangements between the states involved, usually embodied in extradition treaties.

Principles

Most treaties require dual criminality: the alleged act must be a crime in both jurisdictions. Common exceptions and bars include political offences, military offences, the risk of the death penalty in states that have abolished it, and the principle of non-refoulement, which forbids surrender where the person faces torture or persecution. Many civil-law states decline to extradite their own nationals, prosecuting them at home instead. The rule of specialty generally limits prosecution to the offences for which surrender was granted.

Process

A request is transmitted through diplomatic channels; courts in the requested state typically assess whether the treaty conditions and evidentiary thresholds are met, while the final decision often rests with the executive. Within the European Union, the European Arrest Warrant replaces traditional extradition with a faster system of judicial surrender between member states.

Related concepts

Extradition differs from deportation and expulsion, which are unilateral immigration measures, and from extraordinary rendition, the extra-legal transfer of suspects criticized as a circumvention of extradition safeguards.
