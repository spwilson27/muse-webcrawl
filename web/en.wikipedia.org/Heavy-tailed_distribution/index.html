Heavy-tailed distribution

In probability theory, a heavy-tailed distribution is a probability distribution whose tail decays more slowly than that of any exponential distribution — informally, extreme values occur far more often than a normal distribution would predict. Heavy tails are a central theme in risk theory, finance, insurance, telecommunications and extreme value statistics, because standard statistical methods calibrated to light tails can badly underestimate the probability of large outcomes.

Defining propertyTail decays slower than exponential

Definitions

Several related definitions exist. A distribution is heavy-tailed if its moment generating function is infinite for all positive arguments — equivalently, its survival function decays slower than any exponential. An important subclass is the regularly varying distributions, whose tails behave like a power law, P(X > x) ≈ x−α, characterised by a tail index α > 0: smaller α means a heavier tail. Distributions with α ≤ 1 have no finite mean, and those with α ≤ 2 have infinite variance, invalidating assumptions behind the classical central limit theorem. Another wide class is the subexponential distributions, important in insurance ruin theory.

Examples and applications

The Pareto distribution models phenomena such as incomes, city sizes and file sizes; the Cauchy distribution arises in physics and has no defined mean; log-normal and Weibull distributions with low shape parameters are also heavy-tailed. Financial returns, insurance claim sizes, flood magnitudes, and internet traffic bursts are all empirically heavy-tailed. The distinction between "fat tails" and thin-tailed models featured prominently in debates over risk management before and after the 2008 financial crisis, popularised by Nassim Nicholas Taleb's writings.

Estimation

Estimating the tail index from data is notoriously delicate: the Hill estimator and related extreme-value methods use only the largest observations, and results are sensitive to how many are included. Practitioners complement statistical fits with extreme value theory, which describes block maxima by the generalised extreme value distribution and threshold exceedances by the generalised Pareto distribution.
