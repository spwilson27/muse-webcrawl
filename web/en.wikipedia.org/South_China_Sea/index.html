South China Sea

South China Sea
Type: marginal sea of the Pacific Ocean
Area: about 3,500,000 km²
Bordering states: China, Taiwan, the Philippines, Vietnam, Malaysia, Brunei, Indonesia, Singapore
Major features: Spratly and Paracel Islands

The South China Sea is a marginal sea of the western Pacific Ocean, bounded by Southeast Asia to the west and south, China and Taiwan to the north, and the Philippines to the east. It connects to the East China Sea through the Taiwan Strait and to the Philippine Sea through the Luzon Strait. Major archipelagos within it include the Spratly Islands, the Paracel Islands, Pratas Island, Macclesfield Bank and Scarborough Shoal.

Importance

The sea is one of the world's busiest shipping lanes: an estimated one-third of global shipping, worth several trillion dollars annually, passes through it, including energy supplies bound for China, Japan and South Korea. Its fisheries supply a significant share of the region's protein, and its seabed is believed to hold substantial oil and natural gas reserves.

Territorial disputes

Overlapping sovereignty claims by China, Taiwan, Vietnam, the Philippines, Malaysia and Brunei — notably China's "nine-dash line" claim — have made the sea a major geopolitical flashpoint. In 2016 an arbitral tribunal constituted under the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea ruled in Philippines v. China that China's historic-rights claim had no legal basis; China rejected the ruling. Island-building, militarisation and coast-guard confrontations continue, alongside freedom-of-navigation operations by the United States and allies.

Geography and ecology

Rivers including the Pearl, Red and Mekong discharge into the sea. Its coral reefs and atolls support rich marine biodiversity that is threatened by overfishing, dredging and giant-clam harvesting.
