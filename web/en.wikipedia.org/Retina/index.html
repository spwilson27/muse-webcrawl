The retina is the innermost, light-sensitive layer of tissue of the eye of most vertebrates and some molluscs. It converts light that enters the eye into neural signals, performing substantial image processing before sending the results along the optic nerve to the visual cortex.

Structure

The retina is a thin sheet of neural tissue organised in layers. Light passes through the transparent inner layers to reach the photoreceptors at the back: rods, which mediate vision in dim light, and cones, concentrated in the central fovea, which mediate colour vision and fine detail. Signals pass from photoreceptors through bipolar cells to retinal ganglion cells, whose axons form the optic nerve; horizontal and amacrine cells modulate the signals laterally. Where the optic nerve exits there are no photoreceptors, creating the blind spot. Behind the photoreceptors lies the retinal pigment epithelium, which nourishes them, recycles visual pigments, and absorbs stray light.

Function

In the photoreceptors, photons are absorbed by opsin pigments — rhodopsin in rods — triggering the phototransduction cascade that converts light into changes in membrane potential. Retinal circuits extract features such as contrast, edges, colour opponency, and motion direction before transmission; roughly 100 million photoreceptors converge onto about 1.2 million ganglion-cell axons, a substantial compression of the visual signal.

Clinical significance

Retinal diseases are major causes of vision loss. They include age-related macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, retinal detachment, retinitis pigmentosa, and glaucoma (which damages ganglion cells and the optic nerve head). The retina can be examined directly with an ophthalmoscope or by optical coherence tomography, and it is unique as a site where blood vessels and central nervous tissue can be viewed non-invasively — a window used in assessing vascular and neurological disease.
