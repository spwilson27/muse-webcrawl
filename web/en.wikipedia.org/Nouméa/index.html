Nouméa (French pronunciation: [numea]) is the capital and largest city of New Caledonia, a French special collectivity in the South Pacific. Situated on a large peninsula at the southern end of the island of Grande Terre, it is sheltered by one of the largest lagoon systems in the world and serves as the territory's principal port, commercial centre, and seat of government.

History

The site was founded in 1854 by Tardy de Montravel as Port-de-France, shortly after France annexed New Caledonia. Renamed Nouméa in 1866, it grew as the administrative centre of a penal colony and as the port for the nickel mining that still dominates the economy. During the Second World War it served as a major United States naval and air base in the Pacific campaign. The city was the focus of the political tensions of the 1980s between pro-independence Kanak movements and loyalist settlers, eased by the Matignon (1988) and Nouméa (1998) Accords — the latter, which set out a path toward referendums on independence, takes its name from the city.

Geography and sights

Nouméa enjoys a tropical climate tempered by trade winds. Its waterfront bays — Baie des Citrons and Anse Vata — are lined with beaches, hotels, and restaurants. Landmarks include the Tjibaou Cultural Centre, designed by Renzo Piano and dedicated to Kanak culture, the territorial museum, and the Amédée Lighthouse offshore. The wider metropolitan area, including Dumbéa, Mont-Dore, and Païta, holds roughly two-thirds of New Caledonia's population.
