Odawa

The Odawa (also spelled Ottawa) are an Indigenous American ethnic group who primarily inhabit land in the Eastern Woodlands region, commonly known as the upper Midwest of the United States and southern Ontario, Canada. They are an Anishinaabe people, closely related to the Ojibwe and Potawatomi, with whom they form the Council of Three Fires.

History

The Odawa were renowned traders — their name is often said to derive from an Algonquian word meaning "to trade" — and they controlled commerce across the upper Great Lakes from their base on Manitoulin Island and the Bruce Peninsula. During the colonial era they allied with New France, and the Odawa war leader Pontiac led a major resistance against British occupation in 1763.

Modern nations

Federally recognized Odawa tribes in the United States include the Little Traverse Bay Bands of Odawa Indians and the Little River Band of Ottawa Indians in Michigan, and the Ottawa Tribe of Oklahoma. In Canada, Odawa communities include those at Wikwemikong and Sheshegwaning on Manitoulin Island.

Language

The Odawa language is a dialect of Ojibwe, part of the Algonquian language family, and is the subject of ongoing revitalization efforts.
