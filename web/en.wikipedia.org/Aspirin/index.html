Aspirin

Aspirin, also known as acetylsalicylic acid (ASA), is a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) used to reduce pain, fever, and inflammation, and as an antithrombotic. Specific inflammatory conditions it is used to treat include Kawasaki disease, pericarditis, and rheumatic fever. Long-term low-dose aspirin is used to help prevent heart attacks, ischaemic strokes, and colorectal cancer in some high-risk people.

History

Salicylate medicines from willow bark were used since antiquity. In 1853 Charles Frédéric Gerhardt first prepared acetylsalicylic acid, and in 1897 Felix Hoffmann at Bayer synthesised it in a chemically pure and stable form. Bayer marketed it under the name Aspirin from 1899. Its mechanism of action — inhibition of prostaglandin synthesis — was elucidated by John Vane in 1971.

Medical uses

Aspirin is used for pain, fever, and inflammatory disorders, and immediately after a heart attack to reduce the risk of death. Its irreversible inhibition of cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1) in platelets underlies its antiplatelet effect.

Adverse effects

Common side effects include gastrointestinal irritation and bleeding; aspirin increases the risk of Reye syndrome in children with viral infections and is generally not recommended for them. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines and is one of the most widely used medications globally, with tens of billions of tablets consumed annually.
