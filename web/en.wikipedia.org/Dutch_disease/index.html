Dutch disease

In economics, Dutch disease is the apparent causal relationship between the increase in the economic development of a specific sector (for example natural resources) and a decline in other sectors (like the manufacturing sector or agriculture). The term was coined in 1977 by The Economist to describe the decline of the manufacturing sector in the Netherlands after the discovery of the large Groningen natural gas field in 1959.

A booming resource sector can squeeze other traded industries.

Mechanism

The standard model, formalised by W. Max Corden and J. Peter Neary in 1982, divides the economy into a booming tradable sector (typically oil, gas, or minerals), a lagging tradable sector (manufacturing and agriculture), and a non-tradable sector (services and construction). A resource boom produces two effects. The resource movement effect: the booming sector bids labour and capital away from the other sectors. The spending effect: higher income from resource exports raises demand for non-tradable goods, pushing up their prices and appreciating the real exchange rate. The stronger currency makes manufactured exports and import-competing goods less competitive, so the lagging tradable sector contracts — a form of deindustrialisation. When the resource is exhausted or prices fall, the damage to the traded sector may be hard to reverse, because skills, supplier networks, and market share have been lost.

Examples

Beyond the Netherlands, the pattern has been discussed in relation to North Sea oil in the United Kingdom and Norway, oil in Nigeria and Venezuela, minerals in Australia and Canada, and even non-resource windfalls such as large foreign aid inflows, remittances, or tourism booms. The broader concept overlaps with the "resource curse" literature, which finds that many resource-rich countries grow more slowly than expected, though economists debate how much of that correlation is explained by the Dutch disease mechanism specifically, and how much by corruption, volatility, and institutional weakness.

Policy responses

Governments attempt to counter Dutch disease by saving windfall revenues abroad in sovereign wealth funds — Norway's Government Pension Fund Global is the leading example — thereby limiting currency appreciation and preserving income after resources run out. Other responses include sterilising inflows, fiscal rules that smooth spending, investment in infrastructure and education to support the traded sector, and deliberate economic diversification. The reversibility problem means prevention is generally considered easier than cure.
