Very-long-baseline interferometry

Very-long-baseline interferometry (VLBI) is a type of astronomical interferometry in which the baseline between the telescopes is so long that the elements of the interferometer have no physical connections other than through the usual convention of an atomic time standard.

Operation

Each antenna in a VLBI array receives signals from the same astronomical radio source — typically a distant quasar — and records them together with a precise time signal from a local atomic clock. The recordings are then transported to a correlator, which combines them to simulate a telescope as large as the maximum distance between the antennas, achieving angular resolution far beyond that of any single dish.

History

VLBI was pioneered in 1967 when Canadian and American astronomers independently demonstrated interference fringes between widely separated antennas. It is now used for astrometry, geodesy (measuring tectonic plate motion), and the first direct images of black holes by the Event Horizon Telescope.
