Cassini Division

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Cassini Division is a wide, dark gap in the rings of Saturn, separating the bright outer B ring from the A ring. It is approximately 4,800 kilometres (3,000 miles) wide and lies about 118,000–122,000 km from Saturn's centre. It is named after the Italian-French astronomer Giovanni Domenico Cassini, who discovered it in 1675 using a telescope at the Paris Observatory — the first major feature ever recognised in Saturn's rings.

Structure

The division is not empty: it contains tenuous ringlets and dusty material, and its inner boundary is defined by the sharply edged Huygens Gap, within which lies the narrow Huygens Ringlet. The division's outer edge is maintained at the 2:1 mean-motion resonance with the moon Mimas: ring particles there orbit Saturn exactly twice for each orbit of Mimas, and the moon's periodic gravitational tugs expel material, keeping the gap clear. The ring-moon Pan orbits within the separate, narrower Encke Gap in the A ring.

Observations

Visible from Earth in amateur telescopes as a dark line when the rings are well presented, the Cassini Division was studied in detail by the Voyager spacecraft in 1980–81 and by the Cassini orbiter between 2004 and 2017, which revealed fine structure, density waves and vertical corrugations within the gap. Cassini's radio occultations showed the division's optical depth is much lower than the B ring's but higher than the C ring's.
