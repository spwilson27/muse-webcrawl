Glittering generality

Glittering generalities use emotionally appealing virtue words to persuade without evidence.

A glittering generality is an emotionally appealing phrase so closely associated with highly valued concepts and beliefs that it carries conviction without supporting information or reason. Such concepts are often described in highly general, vague terms: "freedom", "hope", "change", "progress", "honor", and "family values" are common examples. Glittering generalities are a recognized device of propaganda and a form of loaded language.

Origin of the term

The phrase is usually traced to William H. Seward and was popularized in the mid-19th century; American politician Rufus Choate used it in 1856, complaining of "glittering and sounding generalities of natural right." The concept was systematically analyzed in 1937 by the Institute for Propaganda Analysis, which listed the glittering generality among its seven propaganda devices, alongside name-calling, transfer, testimonial, plain folks, card stacking, and bandwagon.

Use

Because virtue words evoke strong positive feelings, audiences tend to accept the statements containing them without examining the evidence. Advertisers and political campaigns rely on the technique heavily: a candidate may promise "a brighter future for our children" without specifying any policy, and listeners supply their own favorable meanings. The same device can serve negative ends when the vague terms are redefined by the speaker, as when "reform" or "security" are used to justify measures that might otherwise face scrutiny.

Analysis

Critics of the technique note that glittering generalities trade precision for emotion: they assert conclusions rather than argue for them, and they exploit ambiguity, since words like "freedom" mean different things to different listeners. Media-literacy education commonly trains readers to ask what a virtue word concretely means in context and what evidence accompanies it.
