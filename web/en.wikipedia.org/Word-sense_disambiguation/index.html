Word-sense disambiguation

A polysemous word and its possible senses in context

Word-sense disambiguation is the process of identifying which sense of a word is meant in a sentence or other segment of text. For example, consider two examples of the word "bass": a bass is a type of fish, and a bass is a type of musical instrument. Given the sentence "I went fishing for some sea bass", the system can tell which sense of "bass" is intended using cues from the surrounding text. WSD is a classic problem of artificial intelligence and computational linguistics.

Approaches

Knowledge-based methods draw on lexical resources such as dictionaries and wordnets. The influential Lesk algorithm (1986) selects the sense whose dictionary definition overlaps most with the word's context. Supervised machine-learning methods train classifiers on sense-tagged corpora such as SemCor, using features from the surrounding words; unsupervised methods cluster occurrences of a word into sense groups without predefined labels. Modern systems based on large language models and contextual embeddings achieve high accuracy on standard benchmarks.

Evaluation and difficulty

WSD is evaluated on shared-task benchmarks such as those of Senseval and SemEval. Performance depends heavily on the granularity of the sense inventory: systems perform well at coarse-grained distinctions (for example, fish vs. instrument) but struggle with the fine-grained senses of resources such as WordNet, where human inter-annotator agreement is itself limited. WSD is an enabling technology for machine translation, information retrieval, and question answering.

History

The problem was articulated in the 1940s–50s in the context of early machine translation (notably by Warren Weaver), making it one of the oldest problems in computational linguistics.
