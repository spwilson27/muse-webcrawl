Jus cogens

Jus cogens (also spelled ius cogens; from Latin for "compelling law") is a fundamental principle of international law referring to peremptory norms from which no derogation is permitted. Treaties that conflict with a peremptory norm are void, and such norms bind all states regardless of consent.

Legal basis

Article 53 of the Vienna Convention on the Law of Treaties (1969) defines a peremptory norm as one "accepted and recognized by the international community of States as a whole as a norm from which no derogation is permitted and which can be modified only by a subsequent norm of general international law having the same character." Article 64 provides that a treaty becomes void if it conflicts with a newly emerged peremptory norm.

Recognised norms

Widely accepted peremptory norms include the prohibitions of genocide, slavery and the slave trade, torture, crimes against humanity, piracy, and wars of aggression, as well as the principles of self-determination and racial non-discrimination. The International Law Commission adopted conclusions on peremptory norms in 2022, providing guidance on their identification and legal consequences.

Effects

Obligations arising from jus cogens norms are owed erga omnes — to the international community as a whole — so any state may invoke responsibility for their breach. Peremptory norms sit at the top of the hierarchy of international law, prevailing over ordinary treaty and customary rules.
