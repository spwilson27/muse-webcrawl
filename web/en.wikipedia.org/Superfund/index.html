Superfund

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Superfund is a United States federal programme authorised by the Comprehensive Environmental Response, Compensation, and Liability Act of 1980 (CERCLA) to clean up sites contaminated with hazardous substances and pollutants. It is administered by the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). The law was enacted following public alarm over disasters such as Love Canal in New York.

How it works

The EPA identifies potentially contaminated sites, assesses them using a Hazard Ranking System, and lists the most serious on the National Priorities List (NPL). Under the "polluter pays" principle, parties responsible for contamination — owners, operators, transporters, and generators — are liable for cleanup costs; where no solvent responsible party exists, the Superfund trust fund pays. The fund was originally financed mainly by taxes on the chemical and petroleum industries, which expired in 1995; some taxes were reinstated by legislation in 2021–2022.

Scope and criticism

More than a thousand sites have been placed on the NPL, ranging from abandoned mines and smelters to chemical plants, landfills, and military facilities. Many cleanups take decades and cost tens of millions of dollars. The programme has been criticised for slow progress, litigation costs, and uneven protection of affected — often low-income — communities, and praised for remediating some of the country's most dangerous sites and establishing strict, retroactive liability for hazardous waste.
