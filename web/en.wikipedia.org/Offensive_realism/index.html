Offensive realism

Offensive realism is a structural theory of international relations that belongs to the realist school of thought. It was first articulated by John Mearsheimer in his 2001 book The Tragedy of Great Power Politics. The theory holds that the anarchic structure of the international system encourages states to seek maximum relative power, ideally regional hegemony, because no state can ever be certain of another's intentions.

John Mearsheimer, the principal theorist of offensive realism.

Assumptions

Mearsheimer rests the theory on five assumptions: the international system is anarchic; great powers possess offensive military capability; states can never be certain of each other's intentions; survival is the primary goal; and states are rational actors. From these it follows that states fear one another, rely on self-help, and think strategically about maximizing power.

Contrast with defensive realism

Defensive realists, following Kenneth Waltz, argue that states seek an appropriate amount of power and that excessive expansion is counterproductive. Offensive realists reply that the security dilemma and uncertainty make power maximization the safest strategy, though geography — above all the "stopping power of water" — limits global hegemony and leaves regional hegemony as the practical goal. The theory's account of state behaviour treats governments as advised by a professional officer corps whose capabilities feed assessments of relative power.
