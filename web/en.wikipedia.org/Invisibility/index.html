Invisibility

Invisibility is the state of an object that cannot be seen. An object in this state is said to be invisible (literally, "not visible"). The term is used in fantasy and science fiction, where objects cannot be seen by conventional means, and in scientific contexts describing transparency-like properties across the electromagnetic spectrum.

In science

An object can be classified as "invisible" if it does not reflect, absorb, or otherwise interact with visible light in a way an observer can detect. Materials that transmit light without significant scattering, such as clean glass in water, approach invisibility in particular media. Research into invisibility cloaks uses metamaterials — engineered structures with negative refractive index — to bend light around an object. Working demonstrations since 2006 have cloaked small objects at microwave wavelengths, and later at specific visible wavelengths, though broadband, macroscopic cloaking remains beyond reach.

Animals exploit partial invisibility through transparency (many jellyfish and glass frogs), countershading, and camouflage; active camouflage in cephalopods changes skin colour and texture to match surroundings. Stealth technology similarly reduces visibility to radar and infrared rather than the human eye.

In fiction and mythology

Invisibility is a recurring motif in mythology and fiction: the cap of Hades in Greek myth, the tarnhelm of Germanic legend, and the One Ring in Tolkien's works all confer it. H. G. Wells's The Invisible Man (1897) explored its scientific framing and moral consequences, while the invisibility cloak of the Harry Potter series made the device a popular cultural icon. Plato's story of the Ring of Gyges used invisibility to ask whether people are just only because they fear being caught.

Perceptual and social senses

The word is also used metaphorically: "invisible" people may be socially overlooked, and in physics particles or forces may be invisible to a given detector while remaining detectable by other means.
