Compounding

This article is about the financial concept. For pharmaceutical compounding, see Pharmacy compounding.

Compounding is the process in which an asset's earnings, from either capital gains or interest, are reinvested to generate additional earnings over time. This exponential growth occurs because the investment will generate earnings from both its initial principal and the accumulated earnings from preceding periods. Compounding differs from simple growth, in which only the principal earns a return each period.

Compound interest

In finance and economics, compound interest is the addition of interest to the principal sum of a loan or deposit, so that from that moment on the interest that has been added also accrues interest. If a principal P earns interest at rate r compounded n times per year for t years, the accumulated amount is:

A = P(1 + r/n)nt

With continuous compounding, the formula becomes A = Pert, where e is Euler's number.

Frequency of compounding

Interest may be compounded annually, semi-annually, quarterly, monthly, daily, or continuously. More frequent compounding produces greater accumulation for the same nominal rate. The effective annual rate expresses the true annual return after compounding is taken into account, enabling comparison between accounts with different compounding schedules.

Rule of 72

A popular approximation estimates the time needed for an investment to double: divide 72 by the annual percentage rate. At 8% interest, for example, money doubles in roughly nine years.

Historical note

Compound interest was known in ancient Mesopotamia and was discussed by medieval mathematicians; Richard Witt's Arithmetical Questions (1613) gave early tables of its growth. Albert Einstein is popularly (though apocryphally) said to have called compound interest the most powerful force in the universe.
