Brown v. Board of Education of Topeka, 347 U.S. 483 (1954), is a landmark decision of the U.S. Supreme Court that ruled that U.S. state laws establishing racial segregation in public schools are unconstitutional, even if the segregated schools are otherwise equal in quality.

Background

The case consolidated challenges from Kansas, South Carolina, Virginia, Delaware, and the District of Columbia (the latter decided as Bolling v. Sharpe under the Fifth Amendment). The named plaintiff, Oliver Brown, was a parent whose daughter Linda was denied entry to an all-white elementary school in Topeka, Kansas. NAACP attorney Thurgood Marshall—later a Supreme Court justice himself—argued the cases, attacking the "separate but equal" doctrine of Plessy v. Ferguson (1896).

Decision

In a unanimous opinion by Chief Justice Earl Warren, the Court held: "We conclude that, in the field of public education, the doctrine of 'separate but equal' has no place. Separate educational facilities are inherently unequal." The opinion drew on social-science evidence, including Kenneth and Mamie Clark's doll studies, about segregation's harm to Black children. A 1955 implementation decree (Brown II) ordered desegregation "with all deliberate speed."

Legacy

Brown met massive resistance in the South and catalyzed the modern civil rights movement, paving the way for the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and further desegregation rulings. It remains a cornerstone of equal-protection jurisprudence and American constitutional identity.
