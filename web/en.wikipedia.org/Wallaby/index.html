Wallaby

Wallabies
Family: Macropodidae
Genera include: Notamacropus, Wallabia, Petrogale, Lagostrophus, Onychogalea, Lagorchestes
Range: Australia and New Guinea
Diet: Herbivorous

A wallaby is any of roughly thirty species of small to medium-sized macropod marsupials native to Australia and New Guinea. Wallabies are not a distinct taxonomic group but the informal name for macropods smaller than kangaroos and wallaroos; they share the kangaroo body plan — powerful hind legs for hopping, long muscular tails for balance, small forelimbs, and grazing dentition — scaled down for a wider variety of habitats.

Diversity and habitat

Wallabies occupy habitats from dense rainforest to rocky outcrops and arid scrub. Well-known groups include the agile and red-necked (Bennett's) wallabies of the genus Notamacropus; rock-wallabies (Petrogale), which climb and shelter among cliffs; the swamp wallaby (Wallabia bicolor), an unusually solitary browser; hare-wallabies (Lagorchestes); nail-tail wallabies (Onychogalea), named for a horny spur at the tail tip; and the banded hare-wallaby (Lagostrophus fasciatus), sole member of its subfamily lineage. Forest wallabies of New Guinea belong to the genus Dorcopsis.

Biology

Like other marsupials, wallabies give birth to tiny, altricial young that develop in the mother's pouch, and females can practise embryonic diapause, pausing a second embryo while one nurses. Most species are nocturnal or crepuscular grazers and browsers. They move by pentapedal walking at slow speeds and bipedal hopping when travelling fast, an energy-efficient gait that recycles elastic energy in the Achilles tendons.

Conservation and introductions

Several species have declined or become extinct since European settlement — the toolache wallaby is extinct, and the bridled nail-tail wallaby was thought extinct for decades before a remnant population was found in 1973 — due to predation by foxes and cats, habitat change, and competition with livestock. Conversely, wallabies introduced abroad have established wild populations, notably red-necked wallabies in Britain (including a long-lived colony on the Isle of Man and escapees in the Peak District) and in France and New Zealand.
