Hair cell

Hair cell

LocationCochlea, vestibular system, lateral line
FunctionMechanoreception (hearing and balance)
ShapeFlask- or cylinder-shaped epithelial cell with apical stereocilia bundle
SystemAuditory and vestibular systems

Hair cells are the sensory receptors of both the auditory system and the vestibular system in the ears of all vertebrates, and in the lateral line organ of fishes and some amphibians. Through mechanotransduction, hair cells detect movement in their environment: in mammals, they convert the mechanical energy of sound waves and head movements into electrical signals that the brain interprets as hearing and balance.

Structure

Hair cells are named for the bundle of stiff projections — stereocilia — protruding from their apical surface. The stereocilia are arranged in rows of increasing height, like a staircase, and are connected by fine filaments called tip links. In the mammalian cochlea there are two types: a single row of inner hair cells, which are the true sensory receptors sending signals to the brain, and three rows of outer hair cells, which act as mechanical amplifiers.

Mechanotransduction

When the stereocilia bundle is deflected toward its tallest edge, tension in the tip links opens mechanically gated ion channels. Potassium ions flow into the cell from the surrounding endolymph, depolarising it and triggering the release of neurotransmitter (glutamate) onto afferent nerve fibres of the auditory or vestibular nerve. Deflection in the opposite direction closes the channels and hyperpolarises the cell.

Cochlear amplification

Outer hair cells change length in response to voltage changes — a phenomenon called electromotility, driven by the motor protein prestin. This active process amplifies the motion of the basilar membrane, sharpening frequency tuning and increasing sensitivity to quiet sounds by as much as 100-fold.

Damage and hearing loss

In mammals, hair cells do not regenerate once destroyed. Damage from excessive noise, ageing, certain antibiotics and chemotherapy drugs, and infections is a leading cause of sensorineural hearing loss. Birds and fishes, by contrast, can regenerate hair cells, a difference that is a major focus of research into treatments for deafness.
