Trophy hunting

Animal heads and antlers kept as hunting trophies

Trophy hunting is the hunting of wild game animals in which the hunter keeps parts of the animal — such as the head, horns, antlers, or skin — as a trophy or memento of the hunt. It is practised worldwide, notably in sub-Saharan Africa, North America, and parts of Europe and Asia, and is distinct from subsistence hunting, though meat from trophy hunts is often consumed locally.

Practice and regulation

Trophy hunts are typically conducted under government licences and quotas, often with professional hunters or guides, and fees for charismatic big game — elephants, lions, leopards, rhinoceroses, buffalo, and big-horned sheep — can reach tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars. International trade in trophies of endangered species is regulated by CITES permits. Record-keeping organisations such as Safari Club International and the Boone and Crockett Club rank trophies by measurements of horns and antlers.

Conservation debate

Supporters, including some conservation biologists and the IUCN, argue that well-regulated trophy hunting can fund anti-poaching patrols, habitat protection, and local communities, giving landowners an economic incentive to tolerate dangerous wildlife; hunting areas in Africa cover very large tracts of habitat. Programmes such as Zimbabwe's CAMPFIRE and Namibia's communal conservancies are cited as successes.

Critics contend that trophy hunting removes prime breeding males, can corrupt quota-setting, delivers little money to local people, and is ethically indefensible; they favour photographic tourism as an alternative. The 2015 killing of Cecil the lion in Zimbabwe by an American dentist triggered global outrage and led several airlines to ban carriage of hunting trophies, and periodic legislative proposals in Western countries seek to ban trophy imports.
