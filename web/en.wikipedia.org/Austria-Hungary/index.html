Austria-Hungary, formally the Austro-Hungarian Monarchy, was a multinational constitutional monarchy in Central and Eastern Europe between 1867 and 1918. Created by the Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867, it united the Austrian Empire and the Kingdom of Hungary under a single Habsburg monarch, who reigned as Emperor of Austria and King of Hungary. It was one of the great powers of Europe and, at the outbreak of the First World War, the continent's second-largest country by area and third-largest by population.

Structure

The monarchy consisted of two states: Cisleithania (the Austrian lands) and Transleithania (the Lands of the Crown of Saint Stephen, chiefly Hungary and Croatia-Slavonia). They shared a monarch, a common army and navy, and joint ministries for foreign affairs and war finance, linked by a customs union renegotiated every ten years; otherwise each half governed itself through its own parliament in Vienna or Budapest. Bosnia and Herzegovina, occupied in 1878 and annexed in 1908, was administered jointly.

Peoples and culture

Austria-Hungary was famously multinational: Germans, Hungarians, Czechs, Poles, Ukrainians, Romanians, Croats, Serbs, Slovaks, Slovenes, Italians and others lived within its borders, and national tensions were the central political problem of its later decades. The empire's capitals were also brilliant cultural centres: Vienna of Freud, Mahler, Klimt and Wittgenstein; Budapest; and Prague.

First World War and dissolution

The assassination of the heir to the throne, Archduke Franz Ferdinand, by a Bosnian Serb nationalist in Sarajevo on 28 June 1914 triggered the July Crisis and the First World War. Austria-Hungary fought as a Central Power alongside Germany and the Ottoman Empire. Defeat in 1918 brought collapse: national councils proclaimed independent states, Emperor Charles renounced participation in government in November 1918, and the monarchy was replaced by Austria, Hungary, Czechoslovakia and other successor states, a settlement codified in the treaties of Saint-Germain (1919) and Trianon (1920).
