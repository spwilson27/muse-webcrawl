Environmental economics

Environmental economics is a sub-field of economics concerned with environmental issues and the relationship between the economy and the environment. It studies how economic activity affects environmental quality and how environmental policy can be designed, emphasizing the role of market failures such as externalities, public goods, and common-pool resources.

Key concepts

The central problem is the externality: costs such as pollution that fall on third parties and are not reflected in market prices, leading to overproduction of harmful goods. Environmental economists analyze corrective instruments including Pigouvian taxes (such as carbon taxes), tradable permit systems (cap and trade), subsidies, and regulation, comparing their efficiency and distributional effects. Other core topics include the valuation of non-market goods and ecosystem services (using methods such as hedonic pricing, travel cost, and contingent valuation), the optimal management of renewable and exhaustible resources, and cost-benefit analysis of environmental rules.

Relation to ecological economics

Environmental economics works largely within the neoclassical framework, treating nature as a sector of the economy. Ecological economics, a related but distinct field, instead treats the economy as a subsystem of the biosphere and gives greater weight to physical limits, strong sustainability, and critiques of perpetual growth. Debates between the approaches concern topics such as discounting future damages from climate change, where the choice of discount rate — famously low in the Stern Review and higher in William Nordhaus's models — drives very different policy recommendations.

Applications

The field underpins major policies including the U.S. sulfur dioxide cap-and-trade program under the Clean Air Act Amendments of 1990, the EU Emissions Trading System, carbon pricing schemes in many countries, fisheries catch-share programs, and payments for ecosystem services.
