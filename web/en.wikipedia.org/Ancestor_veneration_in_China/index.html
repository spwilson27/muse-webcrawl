Ancestor veneration in China

Ancestor veneration in China (Chinese: 敬祖, jìngzǔ) comprises the beliefs and practices of honouring deceased family members, founded on the conviction that ancestors continue to exist after death, watch over their descendants, and can influence their fortunes. It is a central element of Chinese folk religion and is interwoven with Confucian ideals of filial piety (xiào). The practice dates back at least to the Shang dynasty (c. 1600–1046 BC), whose oracle bones record royal divination addressed to ancestors.

Practices

Families maintain ancestral shrines — at home, in clan halls, or in temples — holding spirit tablets inscribed with ancestors' names. Offerings of incense, food, tea, and wine are made on festivals and death anniversaries; paper money and paper replicas of goods are burned for the ancestors' use in the afterlife. Graves are swept and repaired, especially at the Qingming Festival (Tomb-Sweeping Day), and offerings are also made during the Ghost Festival and Chinese New Year.

Beliefs

Traditional thought holds that a person has multiple souls; after death one soul resides in the grave, one in the ancestral tablet, and one journeys to the afterlife. Properly cared-for ancestors become benevolent protective spirits, while neglected dead may become hungry ghosts. Ancestor veneration survived the upheavals of events such as the An Lushan Rebellion and remained embedded in family life for over three millennia.

Social role

The cult reinforced lineage organisation: ancestral halls anchored clans that kept genealogies, managed communal land, ran schools, and settled disputes. Neo-Confucian thinkers such as Zhu Xi systematised domestic rites in works like the Family Rituals.

Modern practice

Suppressed during the Cultural Revolution, ancestor veneration revived after the 1980s and remains widely practised in China and across the Chinese diaspora, often alongside Buddhism, Taoism, or Christianity.
