Filo

Filo (also spelled phyllo) is a very thin unleavened dough used for making pastries such as baklava and börek in Middle Eastern, Balkan, and Mediterranean cuisines. Filo-based pastries are made by layering many sheets of filo brushed with oil or butter and then baking them; the layers become crisp and flaky.

Description

The name filo comes from the Greek word φύλλο (phyllo), meaning "leaf", reflecting the paper-thin character of the sheets. The dough is made from flour, water, and a small amount of oil or vinegar, then rolled and stretched until extremely thin — traditionally over a large round table, using long rolling pins, until it is nearly translucent.

Uses

Filo is used in both sweet and savoury dishes. Sweet preparations include baklava, layered with chopped nuts and sweetened with syrup or honey, and galaktoboureko, filled with custard. Savoury uses include spanakopita, filled with spinach and feta cheese, tiropita with cheese, and the various böreks of Turkish cuisine filled with meat, cheese, or vegetables. In modern home cooking, commercially produced frozen filo sheets are common, and are kept covered with a damp cloth while working because the dough dries out quickly.

History

Thin layered pastries are documented in Central Asian Turkic cuisines and in the kitchens of the Ottoman Empire, from which filo techniques spread through the Balkans, the Levant, and the eastern Mediterranean. Similar thin doughs include the yufka of Turkey and the strudel dough of Central Europe.
