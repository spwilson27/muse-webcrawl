Dachau (German pronunciation: [ˈdaxaʊ]) was one of the first concentration camps built by Nazi Germany and the longest-running, opening on 22 March 1933 near the town of Dachau, about 16 km northwest of Munich. Initially intended to hold political prisoners, it served as a model and training centre for the SS camp system.

History

Under commandant Theodor Eicke, Dachau's organisation, regulations, and guard formations became the template for all later Nazi concentration camps. Over twelve years, more than 200,000 prisoners from across Europe were held there — political opponents, Jews, Roma, Jehovah's Witnesses, homosexuals, clergy, and others — along with a large system of subcamps supplying forced labour to the armaments industry. Tens of thousands died from disease, starvation, medical experiments, executions, and the conditions of imprisonment, and thousands more were murdered after transfer or on death marches.

Liberation and memorial

American forces liberated the main camp on 29 April 1945. After the war the site was used for displaced persons and then, from 1965, as a memorial site with a museum, preserved barracks, and religious memorials. It remains a central place of remembrance and education about Nazi persecution.
