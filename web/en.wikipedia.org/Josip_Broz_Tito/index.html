Josip Broz (Serbo-Croatian Cyrillic: Јосип Броз; 7 May 1892 – 4 May 1980), commonly known as Tito, was a Yugoslav communist revolutionary and statesman who led Yugoslavia from 1944 until his death in 1980. A founder of the socialist federation of Yugoslavia, he governed as prime minister (1945–1953) and president (1953–1980), and was the chief architect of Yugoslavia's "third way" between East and West during the Cold War.

Early life and rise

Born in Kumrovec to a Croat father and Slovene mother, Broz worked as a locksmith and metalworker, was drafted into the Austro-Hungarian army in the First World War, and was captured by the Russians in 1915. Radicalised by the Russian Revolution, he returned home in 1920 and joined the Communist Party of Yugoslavia. After the party was banned he operated underground — adopting the conspiratorial name "Tito" — was imprisoned (1928–1934), worked for the Comintern in Moscow, and in 1937 became general secretary of the party.

Second World War

After the Axis invasion of Yugoslavia in April 1941, Tito organised the Partisan resistance, which grew into the largest resistance movement in occupied Europe. At the second session of AVNOJ at Jajce in November 1943 the Partisans proclaimed a provisional government, and by 1945, with Soviet and Western Allied support, they liberated the country largely by their own efforts, allowing Tito to resist Stalin's control afterwards.

Socialist Yugoslavia

Tito established the Federal People's Republic of Yugoslavia (later the Socialist Federal Republic) comprising six republics. The 1948 Tito–Stalin split expelled Yugoslavia from the Cominform; Tito responded with a distinctive model of socialist self-management and non-alignment. He co-founded the Non-Aligned Movement with Nehru, Nasser, Sukarno and Nkrumah, formalised at the 1961 Belgrade Conference. His rule combined political authoritarianism — dissent was suppressed, notably at the Goli otok prison camp — with relative openness, market-socialist experiments and extensive Western contacts. The 1974 constitution made him president for life and decentralised power to the republics.

Death and legacy

Tito died in Ljubljana on 4 May 1980 and was buried in the House of Flowers in Belgrade; his funeral drew representatives of 128 countries. Yugoslavia survived him by barely a decade before collapsing into war in 1991–92. Assessments of Tito remain sharply divided: credited with unifying a fractured country, defeating fascism and keeping Yugoslavia independent of Moscow, he is also criticised for one-party rule, political repression and post-war mass killings by his forces.
