Boring (manufacturing)

Process: Machining

Tool: Single-point boring bar

Machines: Lathe, boring mill, jig borer

Purpose: Enlarging and finishing holes

In machining, boring is the process of enlarging a hole that has already been drilled or cast, using a single-point cutting tool mounted on a boring bar. Unlike drilling, which creates a hole, boring refines an existing one: it can achieve tight diameter tolerances, correct the hole's position and roundness, and produce a fine surface finish. It is one of the fundamental operations of the machine shop.

Process

On a lathe, the workpiece rotates and the boring bar is fed into the hole; on a boring mill or machining centre, the bar itself rotates and is fed into a stationary workpiece. Because the bar must reach into the hole, it is prone to deflection and vibration (chatter), which limits the depth-to-diameter ratio achievable without special damped bars. Typical boring achieves tolerances around IT9 to IT7; fine boring and jig boring reach higher precision, after which honing or reaming may finish the surface.

Machines and applications

Horizontal boring mills handle large workpieces such as engine blocks and gearbox housings, machining several faces in one setup. Jig borers were developed for the precise location of holes in jigs and dies. Classic applications include boring the cylinders of engines, the barrels of cannon (for which John Wilkinson's 1774 boring machine, built for steam-engine cylinders, was a landmark of the Industrial Revolution), bearing seats, and hydraulic cylinders.

Related processes

Boring is closely related to internal turning, and contrasts with reaming (a multi-edge finishing operation that cannot correct hole position) and honing (an abrasive finishing process). For solid material, the hole must first be created by drilling or core drilling. In tunnelling, "boring" refers instead to excavation by a tunnel boring machine.
