The Hephthalites (Bactrian: Ηβοδαλο, Ebodalo), sometimes called the White Huns, were a people and tribal confederation who formed an empire in Central Asia in the 5th and 6th centuries CE. At its height their empire extended from modern Turkmenistan and Bactria through Sogdiana and the Hindu Kush into northwestern India and Kashmir.

History

Rising in Bactria in the mid-5th century, the Hephthalites repeatedly defeated the Sasanian Empire of Persia; Shah Peroz I was killed in battle against them in 484, and Persia paid them tribute for decades. Under kings such as Toramana and his son Mihirakula, Hephthalite forces penetrated deep into the Gupta Empire of India, contributing to its decline; Mihirakula became notorious in Buddhist sources for persecuting the religion, though the Hephthalites in Bactria were more often patrons of Buddhism and other faiths.

Decline

In about 557–561 CE the empire was destroyed by an alliance between the Sasanian shah Khosrow I and the Western Turkic Khaganate, and its lands were divided along the Oxus. Hephthalite principalities lingered in the Hindu Kush region into the 7th century before being absorbed by Turkic and later Arab powers. Their language and ethnic origins remain debated; they used Bactrian, an Eastern Iranian language written in Greek script, for administration and coinage.
