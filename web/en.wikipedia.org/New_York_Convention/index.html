New York Convention

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

United Nations headquarters, New York

The Convention on the Recognition and Enforcement of Foreign Arbitral Awards, commonly known as the New York Convention, was adopted by a United Nations diplomatic conference on 10 June 1958 and entered into force on 7 June 1959. The Convention requires courts of contracting states to give effect to private agreements to arbitrate and to recognise and enforce arbitration awards made in other contracting states. Widely considered the foundational instrument for international arbitration, it applies with remarkable breadth: more than 170 states are parties to it.

Background

The Convention succeeded the Geneva Protocol on Arbitration Clauses of 1923 and the Geneva Convention on the Execution of Foreign Arbitral Awards of 1927, which imposed cumbersome requirements — notably "double exequatur", under which an award had to be confirmed in its country of origin before it could be enforced abroad. The New York Convention abolished that requirement and simplified the procedure, shifting the burden of proof to the party resisting enforcement.

Key provisions

Article II requires courts to recognise written arbitration agreements and to refer parties to arbitration unless the agreement is null and void, inoperative, or incapable of being performed. Article III obliges contracting states to recognise arbitral awards as binding and to enforce them in accordance with local procedural rules, without imposing substantially more onerous conditions than apply to domestic awards. Article V sets out an exhaustive list of grounds on which recognition and enforcement may be refused, including invalidity of the arbitration agreement, lack of proper notice, excess of authority, and conflict with the public policy of the enforcing state.

Significance

By making arbitral awards readily enforceable across borders — far more so than foreign court judgments — the Convention is credited with underpinning the growth of international commercial arbitration in the second half of the twentieth century.
