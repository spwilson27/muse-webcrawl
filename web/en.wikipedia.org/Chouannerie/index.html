Chouannerie

Chouannerie
Dates: 1792–1800 (with later flare-ups to 1815)
Location: Brittany, Maine, Normandy, Anjou
Result: Republican victory; pacification under Napoleon

The Chouannerie was a royalist and Catholic counter-revolutionary insurrection in western France — chiefly Brittany, Maine, Anjou, and parts of Normandy — during the French Revolution. Its peasant fighters were called Chouans. A parallel but distinct revolt, the War in the Vendée, raged to the south.

Origins

The name "Chouan" is usually traced to Jean Chouan (Jean Cottereau), a smuggler and early leader, or to the tawny owl (chat-huant) whose call the insurgents used as a signal. Grievances driving the revolt included the Civil Constitution of the Clergy (1790), which persecuted refractory priests, and the mass conscription decree of 1793, which ignited risings across the west. Leaders such as Georges Cadoudal organised guerrilla bands that ambushed republican columns, tax collectors, and buyers of nationalised church lands.

Course

After the failure of the émigré landing at Quiberon in 1795, savagely repressed by General Hoche, the movement declined into brigandage and episodic ambushes. Hoche's combination of military pressure and amnesty pacified much of the region by 1796, but Cadoudal kept the cause alive until Napoleon's settlement — the Concordat of 1801 and amnesties — finally ended the revolt; Cadoudal was executed in 1804 for plotting against Napoleon. Brief Chouan risings recurred in 1815 during the Hundred Days. The revolt inspired Balzac's novel Les Chouans (1829).
