Mars in fiction

Mars in fiction is a recurring theme in literature and popular culture. It has been imagined as a setting since the 19th century, when telescopic observation — including Giovanni Schiaparelli's reported canali ("channels", popularised in English as "canals") and Percival Lowell's books arguing for an inhabited, drying Mars — inspired a wave of Martian romances and invasion stories. By the later 20th century, space probes revealed a cold, apparently lifeless desert, and fiction shifted toward exploration, colonisation and terraforming.

Early depictions

Early works include Kurd Lasswitz's Two Planets (1897) and H. G. Wells's The War of the Worlds (1898), whose Martians invade Earth. Edgar Rice Burroughs's Barsoom series, beginning with A Princess of Mars (1912), depicted a swashbuckling dying Mars; C. S. Lewis's Out of the Silent Planet (1938) portrayed a harmonious Malacandra.

Golden Age and after

Ray Bradbury's The Martian Chronicles (1950) made Mars a poetic frontier of colonisation, while Robert A. Heinlein, Arthur C. Clarke (The Sands of Mars) and Philip K. Dick (Martian Time-Slip) offered varied Martian societies. Post-Mariner fiction emphasised realism: Kim Stanley Robinson's Mars trilogy (1992–1996) chronicled terraforming and Martian politics in detail, and Andy Weir's The Martian (2011) dramatised survival science. Recurring motifs include native Martians (often extinct or hidden), Mars as a new frontier or penal colony, and Mars as a symbol of war, after the Roman god.
