The Eleatics were a school of pre-Socratic philosophers founded by Parmenides in the early fifth century BCE in Elea, a Greek colony in southern Italy. They taught that reality is a single, eternal, unchanging and indivisible whole, and that the senses — which report plurality, motion and change — are deceptive.

Parmenides

In his poem On Nature, Parmenides argued from pure logic: what is, is, and what is not cannot be thought or spoken of; therefore being cannot come into existence, perish, change or move, since any of these would require reference to non-being. The goddess of the poem dismisses the everyday world of appearance as a "way of opinion", contrasted with the "way of truth". Later pluralists and atomists developed their systems largely as answers to this challenge, saving change and plurality while accepting Parmenides' ban on generation from nothing.

Zeno and Melissus

Zeno of Elea, Parmenides' younger associate, defended the doctrine indirectly with his famous paradoxes — Achilles and the tortoise, the Dichotomy, the Arrow and the Stadium — designed to show that assuming motion or plurality leads to contradictions. Melissus of Samos extended the argument, holding that being is also infinite in extent and incorporeal, departing from Parmenides' finite sphere.

Influence

Eleatic logic shaped Plato, who staged Parmenides as a revered elder interlocutor, and Aristotle, whose physics and metaphysics wrestle with Eleatic problems. The school's insistence on deductive rigour is often seen as the beginning of Western metaphysics and logic.
