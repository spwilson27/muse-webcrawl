War of the currents

The war of the currents was a series of events surrounding the introduction of competing electric power transmission systems in the late 1880s and early 1890s. It grew out of the rivalry between Thomas Edison’s direct current (DC) system and the alternating current (AC) system promoted by George Westinghouse and based in part on the patents of Nikola Tesla.

Background

Edison’s early power stations, beginning with Pearl Street in New York in 1882, distributed low-voltage DC over short distances. DC could not then be efficiently stepped up or down in voltage, limiting its range. AC, by contrast, could be raised to high voltage by transformers for efficient long-distance transmission and reduced for safe use. Westinghouse bought Tesla’s polyphase AC patents in 1888 and built a competing system.

The dispute

As AC installations spread, Edison’s camp waged a public-relations campaign portraying AC as deadly. Engineer Harold P. Brown, secretly supported by Edison interests, staged public demonstrations electrocuting animals with AC and lobbied for laws limiting AC voltages. Brown also helped develop the first electric chair for New York State — powered by AC — in an attempt to brand alternating current as the executioner’s current; the chair was first used on William Kemmler in 1890. Edison himself opposed capital punishment but sought to make the public associate AC with death.

Resolution

AC’s technical and economic advantages proved decisive. Westinghouse lit the World’s Columbian Exposition in Chicago in 1893 and won the contract to harness Niagara Falls, delivering power to Buffalo, New York, in 1896. Edison’s own company, merged into General Electric in 1892, itself moved into the AC business. By the early twentieth century AC had become the universal standard for power distribution, a status it retains today.
