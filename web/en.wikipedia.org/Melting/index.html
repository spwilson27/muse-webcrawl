Melting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Melting, or fusion, is a physical process that results in the phase transition of a substance from a solid to a liquid. This occurs when the internal energy of the solid increases, typically by the application of heat or pressure, increasing the substance's temperature to the melting point. At the melting point, the ordering of ions or molecules in the solid breaks down to a less ordered state, and the solid melts to become a liquid.

Thermodynamics

Melting is a first-order phase transition. During melting, the temperature of a pure substance remains constant while heat is absorbed as the latent heat of fusion, which is used to break intermolecular bonds rather than raise temperature. The reverse process, freezing, releases the same amount of energy.

Melting point

The temperature at which a solid melts is its melting point; for water at standard atmospheric pressure it is 0 °C (32 °F). Pressure generally affects the melting point only slightly, though water is anomalous: its melting point decreases with increasing pressure because ice is less dense than liquid water.

Related phenomena

Premelting, or surface melting, is a phenomenon in which a thin quasi-liquid layer forms on the surface of a solid below its bulk melting point; it helps explain why ice is slippery. Some materials, such as glass, lack a sharp melting point and instead soften gradually over a temperature range.
