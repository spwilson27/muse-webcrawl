Generic filter

In set theory, a generic filter is a filter on a partially ordered set (usually a notion of forcing) that meets every dense set belonging to a specified countable collection — typically the dense sets in a countable transitive model of set theory. Generic filters are the central tool of the forcing method introduced by Paul Cohen in 1963 to prove the independence of the continuum hypothesis and the axiom of choice from the axioms of Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory.

Definition and existence

Given a poset (P, ≤) and a countable family D of dense subsets of P, a filter G ⊆ P is called D-generic if G intersects every member of D. The Rasiowa–Sikorski lemma guarantees that for any condition p ∈ P there exists a generic filter containing p, provided the family of dense sets is countable. In practice, one takes a countable transitive model M of a finite fragment of ZFC and a poset P in M; a filter generic over M meets every dense subset of P that lies in M.

Role in forcing

Because a generic filter cannot, in general, be found inside the ground model M itself, one adjoins it to form the generic extension M[G], a larger model of ZFC in which G is an element. Statements about M[G] are controlled through the forcing relation between conditions in P and sentences in the forcing language. Cohen used a generic filter over a poset of finite partial functions to add many new subsets of the natural numbers, constructing a model in which the continuum hypothesis fails. The generic filter conceptually parallels the notion of a "generic point" in algebraic geometry, an analogy made explicit in later treatments of forcing.
