Critical period hypothesis

The critical period hypothesis is a long-standing hypothesis in linguistics and language acquisition, first proposed by neurologist Wilder Penfield and Lamar Roberts in 1959 and popularised by Eric Lenneberg in 1967. It states that there is a limited developmental window during which a first language can be acquired to native-like proficiency; after this window closes — often placed around puberty — acquisition becomes markedly more difficult and typically less complete.

Evidence

Supporters point to cases of extreme isolation, such as the feral child Genie, who was discovered at age 13 having had almost no linguistic exposure and never fully acquired syntax, and to studies of deaf children whose exposure to sign language is delayed. Research on second-language acquisition shows that age of arrival in a new language environment correlates with ultimate attainment, particularly in pronunciation and grammar, though the sharpness and even existence of a strict cutoff remain debated.

Criticism and alternatives

Many researchers prefer the weaker notion of a "sensitive period," in which plasticity declines gradually rather than ending abruptly. Some argue that observed age effects reflect social, educational, and input factors rather than a biologically timed window, and a few late learners have achieved near-native mastery, complicating strong versions of the hypothesis.
