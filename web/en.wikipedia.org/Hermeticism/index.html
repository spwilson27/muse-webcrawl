Hermeticism, or Hermetism, is a religious, philosophical and esoteric tradition based on the writings attributed to Hermes Trismegistus, the syncretic Greco-Egyptian sage. It flourished in late antiquity and was revived in the Renaissance, leaving a lasting mark on Western esotericism, alchemy and astrology.

History

The Hermetic literature was produced in Roman Egypt between roughly the 1st and 3rd centuries AD, blending Greek philosophy (Platonism, Stoicism), Egyptian religion, Jewish and early Christian ideas. The philosophical treatises describe a path of salvific knowledge (gnosis) by which the soul escapes the material world and the domination of the planets and returns to God. The "technical" Hermetica — on alchemy, astrology, and magical medicine — circulated widely in late antiquity and the Islamic world.

Renaissance revival

In 1460 the Corpus Hermeticum reached Florence and was translated into Latin by Marsilio Ficino (printed 1471). Believed to record a pristine theology older than Moses and Plato, it inspired the Renaissance Hermeticism of Ficino, Pico della Mirandola, Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa and Giordano Bruno. Isaac Casaubon's philological redating of the texts in 1614 weakened their prestige, but Hermetic ideas continued through Rosicrucianism, Freemasonry, Theosophy and the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn in the 19th century.

Beliefs

Central Hermetic themes include a transcendent God (the One, Nous) who creates through a demiurgic intellect; the divinity and order of the cosmos; humanity's unique position uniting matter and spirit; the correspondence between macrocosm and microcosm ("as above, so below"); and salvation through knowledge of God, sometimes framed as a literal rebirth and ascent through the planetary spheres. Hermetic practice ranged from contemplative prayer to alchemy, astrological talisman-making and theurgy — the animation of statues described in the Asclepius.

Legacy

Historians debate the degree to which Renaissance Hermeticism contributed to the Scientific Revolution, but its influence on alchemy, natural magic and esoteric movements is undisputed, and the term "hermetic" (sealed, secretive) derives from the tradition's reputed secrecy and from Hermes' association with alchemical vessels sealed "with the seal of Hermes".
