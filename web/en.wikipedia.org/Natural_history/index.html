Natural history

Natural history is a domain of inquiry involving organisms, including animals, fungi, and plants, in their natural environment, leaning more towards observational than experimental methods of study. A person who studies natural history is called a naturalist or natural historian.

History

The term descends from Pliny the Elder's Naturalis Historia (1st century AD), an encyclopedia of the natural world. Natural history flourished in the 18th and 19th centuries with the work of Carl Linnaeus, Georges-Louis Leclerc de Buffon, Alexander von Humboldt, and Charles Darwin, whose observations as a naturalist on the voyage of HMS Beagle led to the theory of evolution by natural selection. Gilbert White's The Natural History of Selborne (1789) exemplified the close local observation characteristic of the tradition.

Modern practice

With the professionalization of science, natural history's descriptive and classificatory work was absorbed into disciplines such as zoology, botany, geology, and ecology. Natural history museums — such as those in London, New York, and Washington, D.C. — preserve vast collections and remain centres of research and public education. Citizen science and nature documentary media carry the observational tradition to wide audiences.
