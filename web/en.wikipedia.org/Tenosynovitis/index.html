Tenosynovitis is inflammation of the fluid-filled sheath (the synovium) that surrounds a tendon, causing pain, swelling, and difficulty moving the affected joint. It most often affects the hands, wrists, and feet, where long tendons run through tight synovial sheaths.

Causes and forms

The commonest cause is repetitive strain or overuse. Well-known forms include de Quervain's tenosynovitis, affecting the thumb-side tendons of the wrist, and stenosing tenosynovitis (trigger finger), in which a thickened flexor tendon catches in its pulley so the finger clicks or locks. Infections — from bites, puncture wounds, or spread of bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus — can cause infectious flexor tenosynovitis of the hand, a surgical emergency signalled by Kanavel's signs (a fusiform swollen finger held flexed, with pain on passive extension and tenderness along the sheath). Tenosynovitis also occurs as part of systemic inflammatory diseases, especially rheumatoid arthritis, and occasionally with disseminated gonococcal infection.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is clinical, sometimes aided by ultrasound showing fluid and thickening of the sheath. Non-infectious cases are treated with rest or splinting, ice, NSAIDs, and corticosteroid injection into the sheath, which is highly effective in de Quervain's disease and trigger finger; persistent cases may need surgical release. Infectious tenosynovitis requires urgent surgical drainage and intravenous antibiotics to prevent tendon necrosis and permanent stiffness.
