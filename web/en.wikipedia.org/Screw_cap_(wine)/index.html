Screw cap (wine)

A screw cap is a metal, normally aluminium, cap that screws onto threads on the finish of a wine bottle, generally with a metal skirt down the neck to resemble the traditional wine capsule. It is one of the principal alternative wine closures to natural cork.

History

Screw caps were adapted for wine in the 1960s and 1970s, commercialised as the "Stelvin" closure. Early adoption was limited by an association with cheap wine, but from the late 1990s producers in Australia and especially New Zealand adopted screw caps for most of their production, including premium wines, to eliminate cork taint.

Advantages and disadvantages

Screw caps virtually eliminate contamination with 2,4,6-trichloroanisole (TCA), the compound responsible for "corked" wine, and provide a consistent, nearly airtight seal that preserves fresh, fruity characters. Critics have pointed to the possibility of reductive off-aromas from too little oxygen ingress and questioned the long-term ageing of fine wines under screw cap, though liners with controlled oxygen transmission are now available. Consumer resistance persists in some markets where the ritual of pulling a cork is valued.

Usage

Screw caps now seal the large majority of wines produced in New Zealand and Australia and a growing share worldwide, particularly for white and early-drinking red wines.
