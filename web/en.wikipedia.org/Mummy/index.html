Mummy
DefinitionPreserved dead body with skin and organs intact
Famous culturesAncient Egypt, Chinchorro, Inca
Oldest knownChinchorro mummies, c. 5050 BC

A mummy is a dead human or animal whose soft tissues and organs have been preserved by either intentional or accidental exposure to chemicals, extreme cold, very low humidity, or lack of air, so that the recovered body does not decay further if kept in suitable conditions. Mummies are found on every continent as a result of both natural preservation and deliberate cultural practice.

Deliberate mummification

The best-known practitioners were the ancient Egyptians, who from about 2600 BC embalmed the dead by removing internal organs, drying the body with natron salts, anointing it with oils and resins, and wrapping it in linen bandages, in accordance with religious beliefs about the afterlife. The Chinchorro culture of present-day Chile and Peru produced even older artificial mummies, dating to around 5050 BC. Mummies were also made by the Inca (including child sacrifices on high Andean peaks) and various other cultures.

Natural mummies

Natural mummies form in deserts, peat bogs, glaciers, and permafrost. Famous examples include Ötzi the Iceman (c. 3300 BC), preserved in Alpine ice; the bog bodies of northern Europe; and the naturally desiccated mummies of Guanajuato, Mexico.

Scientific study

Modern CT scanning, DNA analysis, and radiocarbon dating allow non-destructive study of mummies, revealing diet, disease, and ancestry. Egyptian mummies captured the European imagination from the nineteenth century onward and became a staple of horror literature and film.
