English pronouns

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The pronouns of the English language are a set of words that substitute for noun phrases, inflecting — in the personal pronouns especially — for person, number, gender and case. Modern English pronouns include the personal pronouns (I, you, he, she, it, we, they, with object forms me, him, her, us, them), possessives (my/mine, your/yours etc.), reflexives (myself, yourself, themselves), demonstratives (this, that, these, those), interrogatives and relatives (who, whom, whose, which, what), and a large class of indefinites (someone, anything, none, each, all...).

Personal pronouns

English retains case distinctions only in the pronouns: subject (nominative) forms are used for the subject of a clause, object (oblique) forms elsewhere, and the possessive forms function as determiners (my book) or independent pronouns (the book is mine). Third-person singular pronouns mark natural gender: masculine he, feminine she, and neuter it. The loss of the old singular thou/thee left you serving both numbers, a gap that dialects fill with forms like y'all and youse.

Singular they

They has been used with singular reference since the 14th century for an indefinite or unknown referent ("someone left their umbrella"); in recent decades it has also been adopted as the pronoun of specific individuals, including many non-binary people, and is now accepted by major style guides.

History

The pronoun system was much reduced from Old English, which marked four cases and three genders across a fuller paradigm. The Norse-derived they/them/their replaced the native third-person plural forms in Middle English, one of the most striking borrowings in English grammar.
