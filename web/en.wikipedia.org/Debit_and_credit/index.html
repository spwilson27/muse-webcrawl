Debit and credit


A ledger showing debit and credit columns

Debit and credit are terms used in bookkeeping and accounting to describe entries made on opposite sides of an account. Together they form the basis of the double-entry bookkeeping system, in which every financial transaction affects at least two accounts and total debits must equal total credits.

The words derive from the Latin debitum ("what is owed") and creditum ("something entrusted or loaned"). Whether a debit increases or decreases an account depends on the type of account: debits increase asset and expense accounts, while credits increase liability, equity, and revenue accounts.

History

Double-entry bookkeeping was described in print by Luca Pacioli in 1494 in his work Summa de arithmetica, although similar methods were used earlier by merchants in Italy and elsewhere. The system records the duality of every transaction: for example, buying equipment with cash debits the equipment account (an increase in assets) and credits cash (a decrease in assets).

The accounting equation

The system rests on the accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Equity. In a properly maintained set of books, the sum of all debit balances equals the sum of all credit balances in the trial balance.
