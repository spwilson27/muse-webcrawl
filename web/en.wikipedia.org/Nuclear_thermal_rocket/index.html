Nuclear thermal rocket

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A nuclear thermal rocket (NTR) is a type of thermal rocket in which the heat from a nuclear fission reactor replaces the chemical energy of the propellants in a conventional chemical rocket. In a nuclear thermal rocket, a working fluid, usually liquid hydrogen, is heated in the reactor core to very high temperatures and expanded through a rocket nozzle to create thrust. Because the propellant need only be heated, not burned, the light hydrogen exhaust gives a specific impulse roughly twice that of the best chemical rockets — typically 850–1,000 seconds rather than about 450 seconds.

History

The concept was studied in the United States from the 1950s in Project Rover at Los Alamos National Laboratory and the NERVA (Nuclear Engine for Rocket Vehicle Application) programme run by NASA and the Atomic Energy Commission. Between 1959 and 1972 a series of test reactors — Kiwi, Phoebus, and the NERVA NRX and XE engines — were fired at the Nevada Test Site, demonstrating thrust levels up to around 250,000 newtons (in Phoebus-2A) and run times approaching an hour. The programme was designed to power a crewed mission to Mars proposed after Apollo, but it was cancelled in 1973 when those missions were abandoned; no nuclear thermal rocket has ever flown. The Soviet Union developed its own RD-0410 engine in the 1960s–80s.

Design

In the classic solid-core design, hydrogen propellant flows through a graphite or ceramic reactor core containing uranium fuel, emerging at temperatures of 2,500 K or more, limited by the melting points of the core materials. More advanced concepts — liquid-core and gas-core ("nuclear lightbulb") designs — promise higher temperatures and specific impulses of up to several thousand seconds but pose severe containment challenges.

Applications and revival

Nuclear thermal propulsion is attractive for crewed deep-space missions because its higher efficiency shortens transit times to Mars and increases payload capability. NASA and DARPA resumed development in the 2020s with the DRACO programme, intended to demonstrate a nuclear thermal rocket in orbit later in the decade, using low-enriched uranium fuel to ease safety and regulatory concerns.
