The trivium is the lower division of the seven liberal arts and comprises grammar, logic (or dialectic), and rhetoric — the three arts of language and reasoning. Together with the quadrivium (arithmetic, geometry, music, and astronomy), it formed the standard curriculum of medieval education, inherited from late antique scholars such as Martianus Capella, Boethius, and Cassiodorus.

The three arts

Grammar is the art of correct language — reading, writing, and the study of literature. Logic (dialectic) is the art of reasoning and argument, concerned with valid inference and the detection of fallacy. Rhetoric is the art of persuasive and effective speech. A traditional summary holds that grammar teaches what a thing is, logic whether it is true, and rhetoric how to express it fittingly.

History

The trivium was the foundation of the medieval university: students first mastered the trivium before proceeding to the quadrivium and then to the higher faculties of theology, law, and medicine. The system was codified by Carolingian scholars such as Alcuin and remained central to European education for centuries. In the 20th century, Dorothy Sayers's essay "The Lost Tools of Learning" (1947) revived the trivium as a model for classical education, mapping its stages to children's development.
