Ludwig Josef Johann Wittgenstein (26 April 1889 – 29 April 1951) was an Austrian philosopher who worked primarily in logic, the philosophy of mathematics, the philosophy of mind, and the philosophy of language. He is regarded by many as the greatest philosopher of the 20th century. Only one book of his was published in his lifetime — the Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus (1921) — while his later thought, which substantially overturned the earlier work, appeared posthumously as Philosophical Investigations (1953).

Life

Wittgenstein was born in Vienna into one of Europe's wealthiest families; his father Karl was a steel magnate and patron of artists including Brahms and Mahler. Ludwig first studied engineering in Berlin and Manchester, where research in aeronautics led him to the foundations of mathematics, and in 1911 he went to Cambridge to study under Bertrand Russell. He served as an Austrian soldier throughout the First World War — on the front lines, carrying the draft of the Tractatus in his rucksack — and finished the book in an Italian prisoner-of-war camp.

Believing he had solved the problems of philosophy, he gave away his inherited fortune and worked as an elementary-school teacher in rural Austria, then as an architect in Vienna, designing a stark modernist house for his sister. In 1929 he returned to Cambridge, submitted the Tractatus as his doctoral thesis, and taught there (as professor from 1939) until 1947, holding famous, intense seminars for small circles of students. He died of prostate cancer in Cambridge in 1951; his reported last words were "Tell them I've had a wonderful life."

Early philosophy: the Tractatus

The Tractatus argues that the world is the totality of facts, that language pictures facts, and that a proposition has sense by sharing logical form with the state of affairs it depicts. What can be shown but not said — logic, ethics, aesthetics, the meaning of life — lies beyond meaningful language, and the book closes: "What we cannot speak about we must pass over in silence." It profoundly influenced the Vienna Circle's logical positivism.

Later philosophy: the Investigations

In the posthumous Philosophical Investigations, Wittgenstein rejected the picture theory, arguing instead that meaning is use: words function within countless "language-games" embedded in "forms of life," connected by family resemblances rather than essences. The book contains the famous private-language argument and an influential treatment of rule-following. His later, therapeutic conception — philosophy as dissolving confusion rather than building theories — shaped ordinary language philosophy and much subsequent analytic thought.
