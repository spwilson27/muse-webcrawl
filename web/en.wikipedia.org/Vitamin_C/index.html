Vitamin C

Vitamin C (also known as ascorbic acid and ascorbate) is a water-soluble vitamin found in citrus and other fruits, berries and vegetables. It is an essential nutrient for humans — who, unlike most animals, cannot synthesise it — and is required for the synthesis of collagen, L-carnitine and certain neurotransmitters, for protein metabolism, and as an antioxidant that also helps regenerate other antioxidants. Deficiency causes scurvy, once the scourge of long-distance sailors. Vitamin C is sold as a dietary supplement and is widely used as a food additive (E300).

Biological role

Ascorbic acid acts as a cofactor for a family of enzymes, including those that hydroxylate proline and lysine in collagen production; without it, connective tissue weakens, causing the bleeding gums, loose teeth, poor wound healing and anaemia of scurvy. It enhances absorption of non-heme iron from plant foods, participates in the synthesis of norepinephrine and peptide hormones, and scavenges free radicals, helping protect cells from oxidative damage. Humans, other primates, guinea pigs and a few other species carry a broken copy of the gene for L-gulonolactone oxidase, the final enzyme in the synthesis pathway.

History

Scurvy killed an estimated two million sailors between 1500 and 1800. In 1747 the Scottish surgeon James Lind conducted one of the first controlled clinical trials, showing that oranges and lemons cured the disease; the Royal Navy eventually issued lemon (later lime) juice, giving British sailors the nickname "limeys". The active factor was isolated and named ascorbic acid in the early 1930s by Albert Szent-Györgyi and by Charles Glen King; Norman Haworth determined its structure and achieved its synthesis, work recognised by Nobel Prizes in 1937.

Dietary aspects and supplements

Recommended intakes are around 75–90 mg per day for adults, easily met by a diet containing fruit and vegetables; smokers are advised to take extra. Vitamin C is destroyed by prolonged cooking and storage. Large supplemental doses popularised by Linus Pauling for the common cold are supported only for modest reductions in cold duration in the general population (with greater benefit in people under extreme physical stress), and high doses can cause gastrointestinal upset and may increase kidney-stone risk. Because the vitamin is water-soluble, excess is largely excreted in urine.
