Working time

Working time or laboring time is the period of time that a person spends at paid labor. Unpaid labor such as personal housework or caring for children or pets is not considered part of the working week.

Regulation of working hours developed alongside industrial factory labor in the nineteenth century.

History

Before industrialization, working patterns followed daylight and the seasons. The factory system introduced fixed shifts, often of 12 to 16 hours. Nineteenth-century labor movements campaigned for the eight-hour day, summarized in the slogan "eight hours labour, eight hours recreation, eight hours rest," popularized by Robert Owen. The International Labour Organization's first convention in 1919 established the eight-hour day and 48-hour week for industry.

Modern patterns

Many countries limit the standard workweek to between 35 and 40 hours, with overtime pay beyond that. France adopted a statutory 35-hour week in 2000. Annual average hours worked vary widely between countries, from under 1,400 in Germany to over 2,000 in some economies. Recent experiments with a four-day workweek have drawn renewed attention.

Health effects

Long working hours are associated with cardiovascular disease, stress, and reduced sleep. The World Health Organization and ILO estimated in 2021 that working 55 or more hours per week contributed to hundreds of thousands of deaths annually from stroke and heart disease.
