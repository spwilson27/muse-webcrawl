The Siege of Acre, also known as the Fall of Acre, took place in 1291 and resulted in the Crusaders losing control of Acre to the Mamluks. It is considered one of the most important battles of the period, and it marked the end of the Crusader presence in the Levant. When Acre fell, the Crusaders lost their last major stronghold of the Crusader Kingdom of Jerusalem; the remaining coastal cities were evacuated or captured within months, ending nearly two centuries of Crusader states in the Holy Land.

Background

By 1290 the Crusader states had shrunk to a strip of coastal cities. The Mamluk sultan Qalawun had taken Tripoli in 1289 and prepared to attack Acre, but died in November 1290. His son, al-Ashraf Khalil, succeeded him and vowed to complete the conquest. A massacre of Muslim merchants in Acre by newly arrived European crusaders gave the sultan a pretext. Khalil assembled a vast army with dozens of siege engines, including huge mangonels, assembled from across Egypt and Syria.

The siege

The Mamluk army arrived before Acre on 6 April 1291. The city's double walls were defended by the military orders — Templars under Grand Master William of Beaujeu, Hospitallers, and Teutonic Knights — together with the forces of King Henry II, who arrived from Cyprus in May with reinforcements. Mamluk sappers undermined the walls while artillery bombarded them. On 18 May a general assault breached the defences around the Accursed Tower; William of Beaujeu was mortally wounded leading a counterattack. Fighting continued street by street as thousands of inhabitants tried to escape by sea. On 28 May the Templars' fortress by the sea, where the last defenders had withdrawn, was undermined and collapsed, killing those inside.

Aftermath

Acre was systematically demolished, its walls and harbour destroyed so that the Franks could never return. Sidon, Beirut, Tyre, Haifa, and Atlit were abandoned by August 1291. The island of Ruad, held by the Templars until 1302/03, was the last Crusader outpost in the Holy Land. The fall of Acre provoked grief and recrimination in Europe, and although popes continued to plan new crusades, none recovered the mainland Levant.
