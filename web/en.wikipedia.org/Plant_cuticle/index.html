Plant cuticle

The plant cuticle is a waxy, water-repelling layer that covers the outer surface of the primary aerial tissues of land plants, including leaves, young shoots, and other organs without periderm. It is secreted by the epidermis and consists chiefly of cutin, a polyester polymer, impregnated and overlaid with waxes.

Structure and composition

The cuticle is composed of an insoluble membrane of cutin together with soluble cuticular waxes. In many species the surface wax forms crystals that give leaves a glaucous, bluish bloom, as in the leaves of many Eucalyptus species and members of the cabbage family.

Functions

The cuticle's primary function is to reduce water loss from the plant surface, a key adaptation that allowed plants to colonise dry land; gas exchange instead occurs mainly through adjustable pores called stomata. The cuticle also protects against ultraviolet radiation, mechanical damage, frost, and attack by pathogens and insects. Its properties influence how water droplets and applied chemicals, such as herbicides and foliar feeds, spread over and penetrate leaves.

Evolution

A cuticle is present in all embryophytes and is thought to have evolved as early land plants adapted to life out of water roughly 450 million years ago; even some hornworts and other bryophytes possess cuticle-like coverings.
