Contraceptive implant

A contraceptive implant is a long-acting reversible contraceptive device implanted under the skin of the upper arm. The most widely used type is a single flexible plastic rod, about the size of a matchstick, that releases a low dose of the progestin etonogestrel (marketed as Nexplanon or Implanon). Earlier systems, such as Norplant, used multiple capsules releasing levonorgestrel. The etonogestrel implant is effective for at least three years and is among the most effective methods of contraception, with a failure rate of about 0.05% per year.

Mechanism

The released progestin prevents pregnancy primarily by suppressing ovulation. It also thickens cervical mucus, hindering sperm passage, and thins the endometrial lining. The implant is inserted just under the skin with local anesthetic using a preloaded applicator, and is removed through a small incision; fertility returns promptly after removal. The current version is radiopaque (containing barium sulfate) so it can be located by X-ray or ultrasound if not palpable.

Effects and risks

The most common side effect is irregular menstrual bleeding, which is the leading reason for discontinuation; other reported effects include headaches, acne and weight gain. The implant offers no protection against sexually transmitted infections. Rare insertion complications include deep placement or migration. Certain enzyme-inducing medications, such as some anticonvulsants and rifampicin, can reduce effectiveness.
