Water dispute

A water dispute is a conflict over access to, control of, or allocation of water resources, occurring between countries, between regions or states within a country, or between competing users such as farmers, cities, and ecosystems. As populations grow and climate change alters rainfall and river flows, competition for fresh water has made such disputes increasingly prominent.

International disputes

More than 260 river basins are shared by two or more countries. Notable disputes include tensions over the Nile between Egypt, Sudan, and Ethiopia concerning the Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam; the Indus Waters Treaty between India and Pakistan, a rare durable agreement signed in 1960; disputes over the Jordan River in the Middle East; and disagreements over the Mekong, Tigris–Euphrates, and other transboundary rivers. The 1997 UN Convention on the Non-Navigational Uses of International Watercourses established principles of equitable and reasonable utilization.

Domestic disputes

Within countries, disputes commonly arise between upstream and downstream users, between agricultural and urban demand, and over groundwater depletion. Examples include the Cauvery water dispute between Indian states, and long-running litigation over the Colorado River and California water rights in the United States.

Resolution

Water disputes are addressed through treaties, river basin commissions, courts, and tribunals. Analysts note that despite frequent rhetoric about "water wars," cooperation and negotiated agreements have historically been far more common than violent conflict over water.
