DC fast charging

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

DC fast charging, also known as Level 3 charging, is a method of charging battery electric vehicles in which direct current (DC) power is supplied directly to the vehicle's battery, bypassing the car's onboard AC-to-DC converter. Because the conversion equipment sits in the charging station rather than the vehicle, DC fast chargers can deliver far higher power — commonly 50 kW to 350 kW, and higher for trucks — adding hundreds of kilometres of range in tens of minutes.

How it works

Unlike AC charging (Levels 1 and 2), where the onboard charger limits the rate, a DC fast charger communicates digitally with the vehicle, which sets the requested voltage and current through its battery management system. Charging speed follows the battery's charge curve: power peaks at low states of charge and tapers, typically sharply after roughly 80 percent, to protect cell longevity. Most charging sessions therefore target the 10–80 percent window.

Standards and connectors

Several incompatible connector and protocol families emerged:

  • CHAdeMO — developed in Japan, used by the Nissan Leaf and Mitsubishi Outlander PHEV.
  • Combined Charging System (CCS) — CCS1 in North America and CCS2 in Europe, extending the Type 1 and Type 2 AC connectors with two large DC pins.
  • North American Charging Standard (NACS) — Tesla's connector, standardised as SAE J3400 and adopted by most North American automakers from 2023 onward.
  • GB/T — the Chinese national standard used across China.
  • Megawatt Charging System (MCS) — under development for heavy trucks and buses, targeting power above 1 MW.

Networks and deployment

Major networks include Tesla Supercharger, Electrify America, EVgo, ChargePoint, and Ionity in Europe. Charging speeds have climbed from the original 50 kW CHAdeMO units to 350 kW CCS stations and Tesla V3/V4 Superchargers rated up to 250–325 kW for cars. Battery-buffered and grid-constrained sites increasingly pair chargers with stationary storage.

Effects on batteries

Frequent fast charging at high rates and high states of charge can accelerate battery degradation, particularly in hot conditions, though modern thermal management and conservative charge curves have made the effect modest in most studies. Manufacturers often recommend mixing AC and DC charging for daily use.
