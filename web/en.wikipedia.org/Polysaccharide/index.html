Polysaccharide

Polysaccharides are long-chain polymeric carbohydrates composed of monosaccharide units bound together by glycosidic linkages. They range in structure from linear to highly branched and are among the most abundant biomolecules on Earth. Polysaccharides may be homopolysaccharides, built from a single type of monosaccharide, or heteropolysaccharides, containing two or more types.

Storage polysaccharides

Starch is the principal energy-storage carbohydrate of plants, a mixture of the largely linear amylose and the branched amylopectin, both polymers of glucose. Animals and fungi store glucose as glycogen, a highly branched polymer similar to amylopectin, stored mainly in the liver and muscles.

Structural polysaccharides

Cellulose, a linear polymer of β(1→4)-linked glucose, is the main structural component of plant cell walls and the most abundant organic polymer on Earth. Its straight chains pack into hydrogen-bonded fibers of great tensile strength. Chitin, built from N-acetylglucosamine, forms the exoskeletons of arthropods and the cell walls of fungi. Other important examples include the pectins and hemicelluloses of plant walls and the glycosaminoglycans of animal connective tissue.

Functions and uses

Beyond storage and structure, polysaccharides serve in cell recognition (as parts of glycoproteins and glycolipids), lubrication, and immune defense; bacterial capsular polysaccharides are targets of several vaccines. Industrially, polysaccharides such as agar, alginate, carrageenan, and xanthan gum are widely used as thickeners, gelling agents, and stabilizers in food and pharmaceuticals.
