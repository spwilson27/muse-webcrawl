Habitual residence

Habitual residence is a legal concept used in private international law to connect a person to a jurisdiction. Unlike the older concept of domicile, habitual residence is a question of fact: the place where a person ordinarily and regularly lives, with a sufficient degree of continuity and stability, assessed on all the circumstances of the case.

Use in treaties and legislation

The Hague Conference on Private International Law adopted habitual residence as its principal connecting factor because it avoids the technicalities of domicile in national law. It is central to the Hague Child Abduction Convention of 1980, which turns on the child's habitual residence immediately before a wrongful removal or retention, and to European Union instruments such as the Brussels II and Rome regulations in family and civil law. It also appears in tax and social security contexts, though those fields often use their own statutory tests.

Characteristics

No minimum period of residence is fixed; a person can acquire a new habitual residence quickly if they move with settled intent, and children generally share the habitual residence of their carers. Courts look at factors such as duration, regularity of presence, family and social ties, and the reasons for being in a place.
