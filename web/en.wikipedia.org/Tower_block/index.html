A tower block is a tall residential building containing many apartments or flats. The term is principally used in the United Kingdom for high-rise public and private housing, typically of twelve or more storeys. Tower blocks transformed British city skylines in the post-war decades, when councils built them in large numbers to replace slum housing destroyed by bombing or cleared as unfit.

History in Britain

The first British tower blocks appeared in the early 1950s; Harlow's The Lawn (1951) is often cited as the first point block. Encouraged by subsidies that paid more for taller buildings, local authorities built thousands through the 1960s, using industrialised systems such as large panel construction. Influential examples include Ernő Goldfinger's Balfron and Trellick Towers in London and Park Hill in Sheffield, a vast "streets in the sky" scheme.

The partial collapse of Ronan Point in east London in 1968, after a gas explosion blew out a load-bearing panel, badly damaged public confidence in system-built towers. Later decades saw many blocks demolished or renovated, while some — notably Trellick Tower and Park Hill — became listed and fashionable.

Safety and the Grenfell Tower fire

On 14 June 2017, fire destroyed Grenfell Tower in North Kensington, London, killing 72 people. The rapid spread was driven by combustible aluminium composite cladding and failures in compartmentation. The disaster prompted a national inquiry, the removal of similar cladding from hundreds of buildings, and the Building Safety Act 2022, making tower-block fire safety a major political and regulatory issue in the UK.

Design and social debates

Tower blocks have been praised for efficient land use, views, and modernist design, and criticised for isolation, maintenance difficulties, and the breakdown of traditional street communities. Many continue to provide valued homes; their long-term future involves retrofit for energy efficiency and fire safety rather than wholesale demolition.
