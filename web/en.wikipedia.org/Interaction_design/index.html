Interaction design

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Interaction design, often abbreviated IxD, is the practice of designing interactive digital products, environments, systems, and services. Beyond traditional interaction with screens, it encompasses the way people interact with all kinds of digital and increasingly physical products, focusing on the behaviour of the interface over time and on the quality of the dialogue between user and system.

History

The term was coined by Bill Moggridge and Bill Verplank in the mid-1980s, when Moggridge was designing the GRiD Compass laptop. The discipline grew from human–computer interaction (HCI), industrial design, and cognitive psychology, and was shaped by works such as Donald Norman's The Design of Everyday Things (1988), which introduced concepts of affordances, feedback, and user-centred design.

Dimensions

A widely cited framework describes five dimensions of interaction design: 1D words (labels and text), 2D visual representations (graphics, typography, icons), 3D physical objects or space (devices and environments), 4D time (animation, sound, and behaviour), and 5D behaviour (the actions and reactions of users and systems).

Process and methods

Interaction designers research user needs through interviews, personas, and scenarios; explore solutions through sketching, wireframes, and prototypes; and evaluate them with usability testing. The process is iterative and overlaps with user experience (UX) design, information architecture, and visual interface design. Key principles include visibility of system status, consistency, error prevention, and designing for learnability and efficiency.

Related fields

Interaction design is closely related to HCI, human factors, service design, and software design, and is taught in dedicated programmes such as those at the Interaction Design Institute Ivrea (where the Arduino platform originated) and design schools worldwide.
