Flight dynamics (spacecraft)

Spacecraft flight dynamics is the application of mechanical dynamics to model how the external forces acting on a spacecraft determine its flight path. These forces are primarily of three types: propulsive force provided by the vehicle's engines; gravitational force exerted by the Earth and other celestial bodies; and aerodynamic lift and drag, when the vehicle is in the atmosphere.

Flight dynamics in spaceflight differs fundamentally from atmospheric flight dynamics because there is no significant aerodynamic force in the vacuum of space, and gravity acts as the dominant force after engine thrust ceases. A spacecraft in orbit is in continuous free fall around a celestial body, following a trajectory governed by Kepler's laws of planetary motion and described by orbital elements.

Orbital mechanics

The motion of a spacecraft under gravity alone follows a conic section — an ellipse, parabola or hyperbola — with the central body at one focus. Changing the orbit requires a change in velocity (delta-v) delivered by propulsive burns; the total delta-v capability of a spacecraft largely determines which missions it can perform. Common manoeuvres include raising or lowering an orbit, changing its inclination, performing a Hohmann transfer between orbits, and executing rendezvous and docking with another vehicle.

Attitude dynamics

Separately from its trajectory, a spacecraft's orientation — its attitude — must be controlled so that antennas point at Earth, solar panels at the Sun, and instruments at their targets. Attitude determination uses sensors such as Sun sensors, star trackers, gyroscopes and magnetometers, while control is achieved with reaction wheels, control moment gyroscopes, magnetic torquers and small thrusters. Disturbance torques arise from gravity gradients, solar radiation pressure, residual magnetic fields and atmospheric drag in low orbit.

Flight dynamics operations

In mission operations, flight dynamics is also the discipline and team responsible for orbit determination, manoeuvre planning, trajectory optimisation and conjunction assessment — tracking the spacecraft's actual path from radiometric and optical observations and predicting its future position.
