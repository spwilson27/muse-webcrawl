Soka Gakkai (Japanese: 創価学会, "Value-Creation Society") is a Japanese Buddhist lay organisation founded on the teachings of the 13th-century priest Nichiren. It is among the largest of Japan's new religious movements and, through Soka Gakkai International (SGI), claims members in some 190 countries and territories.

History

Educator Tsunesaburō Makiguchi founded the organisation in 1930 as the Sōka Kyōiku Gakkai, a society for educational reform based on his theory of value creation; he and his disciple Jōsei Toda were imprisoned during the Second World War for refusing to enshrine a Shinto talisman, and Makiguchi died in prison in 1944. Toda rebuilt the group after the war as Soka Gakkai, pursuing aggressive propagation (shakubuku). Under third president Daisaku Ikeda (from 1960), membership grew rapidly and the movement internationalised; in 1991 it split from the Nichiren Shōshū priesthood, which excommunicated the lay organisation.

Beliefs and practice

Members chant the daimoku, Nam-myoho-renge-kyo, before a mandala called the Gohonzon, and recite portions of the Lotus Sutra morning and evening (gongyo). Core concepts include the oneness of life and its environment (eshō funi) and "human revolution"—inner transformation as the basis of social change. The movement also promotes peace, culture, and education, founding Soka University and a network of schools.

Political involvement

In 1964 Ikeda founded the political party Kōmeitō, which has long been a coalition partner in Japanese governments; the party formally separated from the religious organisation in 1970. Soka Gakkai's political role and its rapid growth have periodically drawn public controversy and scholarly debate in Japan and abroad.
