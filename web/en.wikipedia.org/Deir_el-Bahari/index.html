Deir el-Bahari or Deir el-Bahri (Arabic: الدير البحري, "the Northern Monastery") is a complex of mortuary temples and tombs in a bay in the cliffs on the west bank of the Nile opposite Luxor, in the Theban Necropolis. The name derives from a Coptic monastery built among the ruins in the early medieval period. The site is famous above all for the terraced mortuary temple of the female pharaoh Hatshepsut.

Temple of Mentuhotep II

The earliest great monument at the site is the mortuary temple of Mentuhotep II (reigned c. 2061–2010 BCE), the Eleventh Dynasty pharaoh who reunified Egypt and founded the Middle Kingdom. Its unusual design combined terraced colonnades with a central structure — long debated as a pyramid or mastaba — above his tomb shaft, and served as the model for Hatshepsut's temple five centuries later.

Temple of Hatshepsut

The mortuary temple of Hatshepsut (c. 1479–1458 BCE), known in Egyptian as Djeser-Djeseru ("Holy of Holies"), was designed by her steward and architect Senenmut. Three ascending terraces of colonnades rise against the cliff face, connected by central ramps. Its reliefs depict the divine birth of the queen, the coronation by her father Thutmose I, and the celebrated trading expedition to the Land of Punt, whose ships brought back myrrh trees, gold, and exotic animals. The temple also contains chapels to Hathor and Anubis and a sanctuary of Amun.

Other remains

A smaller temple of Thutmose III stands between the two larger complexes. The site later received a Ptolemaic sanctuary at Hatshepsut's temple, where the deified sages Imhotep and Amenhotep son of Hapu were venerated for healing. In 1881 the royal cache (DB320) — a tomb containing the mummies of more than fifty kings, queens, and nobles, moved there by priests in antiquity — was officially discovered nearby, after the Abd el-Rassul family had been quietly selling its contents for years.

Luxor massacre

On 17 November 1997, six gunmen of the Egyptian Islamist group al-Jama'a al-Islamiyya attacked tourists at Hatshepsut's temple, killing 62 people, most of them foreign visitors, before being killed themselves. The massacre devastated Egypt's tourism industry for several years; the site was subsequently provided with much heavier security.
