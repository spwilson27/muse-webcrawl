The Oslo Accords are a pair of agreements between Israel and the Palestine Liberation Organization (PLO) — Oslo I (1993) and Oslo II (1995) — that launched the Oslo peace process aimed at resolving the Israeli–Palestinian conflict through a two-state solution. They resulted from secret negotiations in Oslo, Norway, facilitated by Norwegian officials including Terje Rød-Larsen and Mona Juul.

Oslo I: Declaration of Principles

The Declaration of Principles on Interim Self-Government Arrangements was signed in Washington, D.C. on 13 September 1993, following mutual recognition letters in which the PLO recognized Israel's right to exist and Israel recognized the PLO as the representative of the Palestinian people. It provided for the creation of the Palestinian Authority, Israeli withdrawal from parts of Gaza and Jericho, and negotiations on final-status issues — Jerusalem, refugees, settlements, borders, and security — within five years.

Oslo II: Interim Agreement

The Interim Agreement on the West Bank and the Gaza Strip, signed at Taba in 1995, divided the West Bank into Areas A (full Palestinian civil and security control), B (Palestinian civil, Israeli security control), and C (full Israeli control). Yitzhak Rabin, Shimon Peres, and Yasser Arafat received the 1994 Nobel Peace Prize. Rabin was assassinated by an Israeli extremist in November 1995, weeks after signing Oslo II.

Legacy

The final-status negotiations at Camp David in 2000 failed, and the Second Intifada followed. The Oslo framework — the Palestinian Authority, the Area A/B/C division, and security coordination — nonetheless still structures governance in the West Bank decades later, though the envisioned five-year interim period long ago expired without a final agreement.
