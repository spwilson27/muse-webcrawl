Triskaidekaphobia

Many buildings omit a thirteenth floor

Triskaidekaphobia (from Ancient Greek τρεισκαίδεκα treiskaídeka 'thirteen' and φόβος phóbos 'fear') is fear or avoidance of the number 13. It is also a reason for the fear of Friday the 13th, called paraskevidekatriaphobia (from Greek Παρασκευή Paraskeví 'Friday' + δεκατρία dekatría 'thirteen' + φόβος phóbos 'fear') or friggatriskaidekaphobia (from Old Norse Frigg).

Origins

The superstition surrounding 13 has several proposed sources. In Norse mythology, the god Loki was the disruptive 13th guest at a feast in Valhalla that led to the death of Balder. In Christian tradition, thirteen diners sat at the Last Supper, and Judas Iscariot — the thirteenth to sit at the table — betrayed Jesus, who was crucified the following day, a Friday. The Code of Hammurabi is sometimes said to omit a 13th law, though this is a misconception arising from early translations.

Examples

Many buildings skip the number 13 in floor numbering, going directly from 12 to 14 (sometimes labelling it 12A or M, the 13th letter). Airlines and theatres may omit a row 13, and some streets lack a house number 13. Estimates in the United States have suggested that hundreds of millions of dollars are lost annually as people avoid travel, business, and purchases on Friday the 13th.

Notable people reported to have suffered from triskaidekaphobia include composer Arnold Schoenberg, who feared the number throughout his life and, fittingly, died on Friday 13 July 1951, aged 76 (7 + 6 = 13). The Apollo 13 mission — numbered 13 and launched at 13:13 CST — suffered an explosion that forced its crew to abort their lunar landing, which some accounts linked in the public imagination to the superstition.

Similar phobias

Comparable number aversions include tetraphobia, the fear of the number 4, widespread in East Asia because the word for four sounds like the word for death, and hexakosioihexekontahexaphobia, fear of the number 666.
