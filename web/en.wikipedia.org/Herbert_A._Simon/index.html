Herbert Alexander Simon (June 15, 1916 – February 9, 2001) was an American scholar whose work ranged across political science, economics, sociology, psychology, and computer science. He received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economics in 1978 "for his pioneering research into the process of decision-making within economic organizations", and shared the Turing Award in 1975 with Allen Newell for contributions to artificial intelligence and the psychology of human cognition.

Career

Simon studied at the University of Chicago (PhD 1943) and spent most of his career at the Carnegie Institute of Technology — later Carnegie Mellon University — in Pittsburgh, where he helped found the departments of industrial administration and computer science. His first major book, Administrative Behavior (1947), analysed how decisions are actually made inside organisations.

Bounded rationality and satisficing

Simon challenged the classical economic picture of the perfectly rational "economic man". Real decision-makers, he argued, operate with incomplete information, limited time, and finite computational capacity — bounded rationality. Rather than optimising, they satisfice: they search until they find an option that meets an aspiration level, then stop. These ideas reshaped economics, management theory, and cognitive psychology, and are developed in works such as Models of Man (1957) and The Sciences of the Artificial (1969).

Artificial intelligence

With Allen Newell and J. C. Shaw, Simon built some of the earliest AI programs: the Logic Theorist (1955–56), often called the first artificial intelligence program, which proved theorems from Whitehead and Russell's Principia Mathematica, and the General Problem Solver (1957), which modelled human means–ends problem solving. At the 1956 Dartmouth workshop era he predicted that machines would within decades do any work a man can do — an optimism that defined early AI.

Legacy

Simon is one of the few scholars to receive both a Nobel Prize and the Turing Award. His concepts — bounded rationality, satisficing, problem solving as search, and the architecture of complexity — remain foundational across the social and computational sciences.
