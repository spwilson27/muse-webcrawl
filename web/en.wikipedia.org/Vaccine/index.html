Vaccine

TypeBiological preparation

A vaccine is a biological preparation that provides active acquired immunity to a particular infectious or malignant disease. Vaccines typically contain an agent that resembles a disease-causing microorganism, or a component of it, and stimulate the body's immune system to recognize and destroy the pathogen in future encounters.

Mechanism

Vaccines work by training the adaptive immune system: antigen-presenting cells display the vaccine antigen, prompting B cells to produce antibodies and T cells to develop memory. On later exposure to the real pathogen, memory cells mount a faster, stronger response that prevents or lessens disease. Widespread vaccination also produces herd immunity, indirectly protecting those who cannot be vaccinated.

Types

Major types include live-attenuated vaccines (e.g. MMR), inactivated vaccines (e.g. inactivated polio), toxoids (tetanus, diphtheria), subunit and conjugate vaccines, viral-vector vaccines, and mRNA vaccines, which came to prominence with the COVID-19 vaccines of 2020–2021. Related immune components are produced by white blood cells.

History

The term derives from Variolae vaccinae, the cowpox used by Edward Jenner in 1796 to protect against smallpox. Louis Pasteur extended the principle to rabies and anthrax in the 1880s. Mass vaccination campaigns eradicated smallpox in 1980 and have nearly eradicated polio. Nutrients such as vitamin B12 support normal immune function, though they are not vaccines themselves.
