Geostationary orbit

A geostationary orbit, also referred to as a geosynchronous equatorial orbit (GEO), is a circular orbit 35,786 kilometres (22,236 miles) above Earth's equator, with an orbital period equal to Earth's rotation period of one sidereal day. A satellite in such an orbit appears motionless in the sky as seen from the ground, returning to exactly the same position every day.

The geostationary ring, 35,786 km above the equator.

History

The concept was popularised by science-fiction author Arthur C. Clarke in a 1945 paper proposing the orbit for communications relays, and the ring is sometimes called the Clarke Belt. The first geosynchronous satellite was Syncom 2 (1963), and Syncom 3 (1964) became the first truly geostationary satellite, relaying television coverage of the Tokyo Olympics.

Uses

Because a geostationary satellite holds a fixed position relative to the ground, fixed dish antennas can track it without moving parts. The orbit is therefore ideal for communications satellites, direct-broadcast television, and meteorological satellites such as GOES and Meteosat, which image the same hemisphere continuously. Orbital slots in the belt are allocated internationally through the International Telecommunication Union, and defunct satellites are boosted into a higher "graveyard orbit" to keep the ring clear.
