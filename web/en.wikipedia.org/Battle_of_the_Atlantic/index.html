The Battle of the Atlantic was the longest continuous military campaign of the Second World War, running from 1939 to the defeat of Germany in 1945. At its core was the German U-boat blockade of Britain and the Allied effort to keep the sea lanes open: the tonnage war. Winston Churchill later wrote that the U-boat peril was the only thing that ever really frightened him during the war.

Course of the campaign

Germany began the war with too few U-boats for Karl Dönitz's planned campaign of 300 boats, but the fall of France in 1940 gave bases on the Atlantic coast and began the U-boats' first "Happy Time" against poorly escorted convoys. After the United States entered the war, a second Happy Time off the American East Coast in early 1942 sank shipping at catastrophic rates. The Allies responded with a complete convoy system, escort carriers, very-long-range aircraft closing the mid-Atlantic "air gap," centimetric radar, Huff-Duff direction finding, ahead-thrown weapons like Hedgehog, and signals intelligence from the breaking of Naval Enigma.

The turning point

The crisis came in March 1943, when U-boat wolfpacks nearly broke the convoy system around convoys HX 229 and SC 122. Two months later, in "Black May" 1943, the Germans lost 41 U-boats and Dönitz withdrew from the North Atlantic convoy routes. Allied shipbuilding—above all the Liberty ships—now outpaced sinkings. Renewed German efforts with snorkels and advanced Type XXI "electro-boats" came too late to alter the outcome.

Significance

The victory kept Britain supplied with food, fuel and American matériel, enabled the build-up for D-Day, and supplied the Soviet Union via the Arctic convoys. It was won by the narrowest of logistical margins and at immense cost, especially to the merchant seamen and to the U-boat crews, who suffered the highest loss rate of any service in the war.
