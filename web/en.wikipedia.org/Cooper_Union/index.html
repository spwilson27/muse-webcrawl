The Cooper Union

The Cooper Union for the Advancement of Science and Art, commonly known as Cooper Union, is a private college in New York City. Founded in 1859 by industrialist Peter Cooper, it offers programs in architecture, fine arts and engineering, and is renowned for its highly selective admissions and its historically tuition-free education.

History

Peter Cooper, a self-educated inventor and industrialist, founded the institution so that working-class New Yorkers could receive an education "equal to the best" free of charge. Its Foundation Building (1859), an early example of a rolled-iron-beam framed structure, houses the Great Hall, a famous lecture venue: Abraham Lincoln delivered his "Right Makes Might" address there in February 1860, and many later presidents and reformers spoke from its stage.

Academics

The college comprises the Irwin S. Chanin School of Architecture, the School of Art and the Albert Nerken School of Engineering, together with humanities and social sciences teaching. Admission is extremely competitive, with acceptance rates in the single digits in some years.

Tuition controversy

For more than a century and a half, every admitted student received a full-tuition scholarship, funded by Cooper's endowment, which includes the land under the Chrysler Building. Facing financial difficulties, the board announced in 2013 that it would begin charging tuition — charging half tuition from 2014 — provoking lawsuits, protests and a governance crisis. In 2018 the board adopted a plan intended to restore full-tuition scholarships for undergraduates over the following decade.
