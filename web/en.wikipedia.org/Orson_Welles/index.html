Orson Welles

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

George Orson Welles (May 6, 1915 – October 10, 1985) was an American director, actor, writer and producer who is remembered for his innovative work in film, radio and theatre. His first film, Citizen Kane (1941), is routinely cited as the greatest film ever made. Welles is celebrated for pioneering deep-focus cinematography, nonlinear narrative and chiaroscuro lighting in cinema, and for his distinctive baritone voice.

Theatre and radio

A child prodigy from Kenosha, Wisconsin, Welles found early success in New York with the Federal Theatre Project, including a 1936 all-Black-cast "Voodoo Macbeth", and in 1937 co-founded the Mercury Theatre. His 1938 radio adaptation of H. G. Wells's The War of the Worlds, presented as a news bulletin, made him famous — and notorious — for reportedly panicking some listeners, and won him a contract with RKO Pictures.

Film career

Citizen Kane, co-written with Herman J. Mankiewicz, was followed by The Magnificent Ambersons (1942), whose studio re-editing began a lifelong pattern of interference and unfinished projects. Later films include The Stranger (1946), The Lady from Shanghai (1947), Touch of Evil (1958), The Trial (1962) and Chimes at Midnight (1965). Welles also acted extensively, notably as Harry Lime in The Third Man (1949). He spent much of his later career in Europe, self-financing films while working as an actor and narrator, and died in Los Angeles in 1985. See also Citizen Kane and Mercury Theatre.
