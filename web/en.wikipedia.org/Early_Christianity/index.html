Early Christianity

Early Christianity generally covers the period from the ministry of Jesus (c. 27–30 AD) to the First Council of Nicaea in 325 AD. It is commonly divided into the Apostolic Age, when the first apostles were alive and spreading the message, and the Ante-Nicene Period, ending with the council convened by Constantine the Great.

Origins

Christianity began as a Jewish sect in Roman Judaea. Followers of Jesus believed him to be the Messiah promised in Hebrew scripture and held that he had been raised from the dead. After the crucifixion (c. 30 or 33 AD), the movement spread from Jerusalem to cities around the eastern Mediterranean, aided by the missionary journeys of Paul of Tarsus, who argued that Gentile converts need not follow the full Mosaic law.

Spread and communities

By the end of the first century, Christian communities existed in Rome, Corinth, Ephesus, Antioch and Alexandria. Worship centred on baptism, the Eucharist, prayer and readings from scripture and apostolic letters. Local churches were led by overseers (episkopoi), elders and deacons, and travelling teachers and prophets also played a role.

Persecution

Christians faced sporadic local persecution, most notoriously under Nero after the Great Fire of Rome in 64 AD, and empire-wide campaigns under Decius (250) and Diocletian (303–313). The Edict of Milan in 313 legalised Christianity, and in 380 the Edict of Thessalonica made Nicene Christianity the state religion of the Roman Empire.

Theology and texts

The writings that became the New Testament — four Gospels, the Acts of the Apostles, letters of Paul and others, and the Book of Revelation — were composed during the first and early second centuries. Debates over the nature of Christ and the Trinity occupied writers such as Ignatius of Antioch, Justin Martyr, Irenaeus and Tertullian, culminating in the Nicene Creed of 325.
