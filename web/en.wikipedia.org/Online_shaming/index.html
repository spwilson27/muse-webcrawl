Online shaming

Online shaming is a form of public shaming in which targets are humiliated on the Internet, for example through social media or online forums. It typically begins when a person's words, images, or behaviour are publicised online and attract mass criticism, ridicule, or condemnation from large numbers of strangers. Online shaming is closely related to call-out culture and so-called "cancel culture", and it differs from older forms of public shaming by its speed, scale, permanence, and the disproportion that can arise between the original act and the punishment.

Characteristics

Incidents often follow a pattern: a post, photograph, or video is shared out of its original context; it spreads virally; the target is identified by name; and an online crowd delivers judgement ranging from mockery to threats. Consequences can include job loss, business boycotts, reputational damage, and psychological harm. Because search engines preserve the material, the shaming can follow the target for years.

Notable cases and discussion

Widely discussed cases include the 2013 incident in which a public-relations executive's offensive tweet, posted before an international flight, became a global trending topic while she was in the air, costing her job — a story told in Jon Ronson's 2015 book So You've Been Publicly Shamed, which examined the phenomenon and its human costs. Other cases involve misidentification of people in breaking-news events, where crowds have accused innocent bystanders.

Debate

Defenders describe online shaming as a tool of accountability that allows otherwise powerless people to call out racism, abuse, or misconduct by the powerful, and compare it to a digital-age return of community sanction. Critics argue it resembles mob justice: there is no due process, penalties are uncontrolled and often grossly disproportionate, context and intent are disregarded, and the practice can chill speech. The debate overlaps with broader arguments about free expression and platform governance.
