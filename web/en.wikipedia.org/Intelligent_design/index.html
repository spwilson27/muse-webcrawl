Intelligent design

Intelligent design (ID) is a pseudoscientific argument for the existence of God, presented by its proponents as "an evidence-based scientific theory about life's origins". Proponents claim that "certain features of the universe and of living things are best explained by an intelligent cause, not an undirected process such as natural selection." ID is a form of creationism that lacks empirical support and offers no testable or usable hypotheses, and is therefore not science.

History

The term gained currency after the 1987 U.S. Supreme Court decision Edwards v. Aguillard barred teaching creation science in public schools. The 1989 textbook Of Pandas and People popularized "intelligent design" terminology. The Discovery Institute, founded in 1990, became the movement's principal promoter through its "wedge strategy" and campaigns such as "Teach the Controversy".

Arguments and criticism

Leading ID arguments include Michael Behe's "irreducible complexity" and William Dembski's "specified complexity". The scientific community responds that these arguments rest on misrepresentation of evolution, lack of testable mechanisms, and arguments from ignorance; examples of "irreducibly complex" systems have been shown to have plausible evolutionary pathways, and co-option of parts with other functions is well documented.

Kitzmiller trial

In Kitzmiller v. Dover Area School District (2005), a U.S. federal court ruled that intelligent design is not science, is essentially religious, and that the school board's policy violated the Establishment Clause of the First Amendment.
