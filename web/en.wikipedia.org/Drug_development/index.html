Drug development is the process of bringing a new pharmaceutical drug to market once a lead compound has been identified through drug discovery. It comprises preclinical research (in vitro and in animal models) and clinical trials in humans, together with the regulatory review required for marketing approval — by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the European Medicines Agency, and their counterparts.

Phases

After preclinical testing, an Investigational New Drug application permits human trials. Phase I tests safety and dosage in a small group of volunteers; Phase II evaluates efficacy and side effects in several hundred patients; Phase III involves large, often multi-thousand-patient randomized trials confirming effectiveness. If successful, a New Drug Application is filed. Post-marketing Phase IV studies continue surveillance for rare or long-term adverse effects. Attrition is severe: only about one in ten compounds entering Phase I wins approval, and development typically takes a decade or longer at costs often estimated in the billions of dollars, contributing to debate over drug pricing and the productivity of pharmaceutical R&D ("Eroom's law").
