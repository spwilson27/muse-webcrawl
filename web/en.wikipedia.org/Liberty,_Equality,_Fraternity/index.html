Liberty, Equality, Fraternity (French: Liberté, égalité, fraternité) is the national motto of France and an example of a tripartite motto. It originated during the French Revolution as one of many revolutionary slogans, though it was not officially adopted immediately; during the Reign of Terror the variant "Liberty, Equality, Fraternity — or Death" circulated. The motto was inscribed in the constitutions of 1946 and 1958 and remains a core symbol of the French Republic, appearing on coins, stamps and public buildings alongside Marianne.

Origins

The three terms circulated separately in Enlightenment thought before being combined. The precise formula is often credited to Maximilien Robespierre, who proposed in December 1790 that the words be inscribed on National Guard flags and uniforms. Antoine-François Momoro, a printer and member of the Cordeliers club, also promoted the triad. Under the Revolution the words condensed the programme of the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen: liberty of the person, equality before the law, and fraternity among citizens.

Later history

The motto fell in and out of official favour with successive regimes. Restored in 1848 during the Second Republic and celebrated at the centenary festivities of 1889, it was displaced under Vichy France by "Travail, famille, patrie" (Work, Family, Fatherland) and restored after the Liberation. Interpretations of the three terms — and of their tensions, especially between liberty and equality — remain a staple of French political debate.

Adoption elsewhere

Haiti, whose revolution was intertwined with France's, also uses the motto. The triad has influenced republican movements internationally and is echoed in the preamble of India's constitution, which proclaims liberty, equality and fraternity among the values secured to citizens.
