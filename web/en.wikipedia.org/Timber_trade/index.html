Timber trade

The timber trade is the global business of transporting timber — logs, sawn lumber, wood panels, pulpwood and fuelwood — from forests to sawmills, processors and consumers, both within countries and internationally. It is one of the oldest and largest commodity trades in the world: wood is harvested on every inhabited continent, and annual global roundwood removals exceed three billion cubic metres, roughly half of it burned as fuel and half entering industry.

Products and markets

The industrial trade handles softwood (from conifers such as pine, spruce and fir) for construction lumber, packaging and pulp, and hardwood (from broad-leaved trees such as oak, teak and mahogany) for furniture, flooring and joinery. Major exporting regions include Canada, Russia, Northern Europe, the United States, Brazil, Malaysia and Indonesia; major importers include China — the world's largest importer of logs and sawnwood — the United States, the European Union, Japan and India. Much trade is regional: logs are heavy and bulky relative to their value, so transport costs strongly shape trade routes.

History

Timber has been traded over long distances since antiquity — Phoenician and Roman builders imported cedar from Lebanon, and medieval navies consumed whole forests in oak. In the 19th century the colonial timber trade moved Baltic deals (sawn planks) to Britain, squared timber from British North America across the Atlantic, and teak from Burma and mahogany from Central America to imperial markets; timber was for a time Canada's leading export and a central driver of settlement. Steam sawmills, railways and, later, container shipping and plywood and panel industries globalised the trade in the 20th century.

Legality and sustainability

Illegal logging and the associated trade are estimated to account for a significant share of tropical timber production and are a major cause of deforestation. In response, consumer countries have adopted legality rules — the US Lacey Act amendments, the European Union Timber Regulation and its successor deforestation rules, and similar laws in Australia and Japan — requiring importers to exercise due diligence. Voluntary certification schemes such as the Forest Stewardship Council (FSC) and the Programme for the Endorsement of Forest Certification (PEFC) label timber from sustainably managed forests, and CITES lists certain heavily traded species such as big-leaf mahogany. Trade disputes also recur, most notably the long-running Canada–United States softwood lumber dispute over alleged subsidies.
