Toxic alcohol

Common toxic alcohols: methanol and ethylene glycol

Toxic alcohols are a group of alcohols that include methanol, ethylene glycol, and isopropyl alcohol, which are poisonous when ingested. Although the parent compounds themselves cause mainly intoxication and central nervous system depression, their metabolites produced by alcohol dehydrogenase are responsible for severe toxicity, including metabolic acidosis and end-organ damage. Toxic alcohol poisoning is a medical emergency; mass poisonings from adulterated or counterfeit alcoholic drinks recur worldwide.

Methanol

Methanol (wood alcohol), found in windshield-washer fluid, solvents, and illicit spirits, is metabolised to formaldehyde and then formic acid, which damages the retina and optic nerve — causing the classic "snowfield" vision and blindness — and produces a profound high-anion-gap metabolic acidosis. Outbreaks of methanol poisoning from tainted liquor have killed hundreds in single incidents.

Ethylene glycol

Ethylene glycol, the sweet-tasting main component of antifreeze, is metabolised through glycolic acid to oxalic acid, whose calcium oxalate crystals precipitate in the kidneys and other tissues, causing acute kidney failure, hypocalcaemia, and acidosis.

Isopropyl alcohol

Isopropanol (rubbing alcohol) is metabolised to acetone; it causes ketosis without acidosis and is primarily a CNS depressant roughly twice as potent as ethanol, with prominent gastritis.

Treatment

Management aims to block alcohol dehydrogenase before toxic metabolites form, using the competitive inhibitor fomepizole or, failing that, ethanol itself; haemodialysis removes both parent alcohols and metabolites and corrects acidosis. Cofactor therapy (folate for methanol, thiamine and pyridoxine for ethylene glycol) helps shunt metabolism to harmless products.
