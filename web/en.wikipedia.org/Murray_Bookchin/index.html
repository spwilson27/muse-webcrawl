Murray Bookchin (1921–2006) was an American social theorist, author, orator, historian, and political philosopher. A pioneer in the environmental movement, he founded the theory of social ecology and the political philosophy of communalism, arguing that nearly all ecological problems stem from social hierarchies and domination, and proposing a society organized through confederated, directly democratic municipal assemblies — a strategy he called libertarian municipalism.

Life

Born in New York City to Russian Jewish immigrant parents, Bookchin joined the Young Pioneers, the Communist youth organization, as a child, but broke with Stalinism over the Spanish Civil War and moved toward Trotskyism before becoming an anarchist. He worked as a foundry worker and autoworker and was active in the labour movement. From the 1950s he wrote on environmental dangers — his 1962 book Our Synthetic Environment, published under the pen name Lewis Herber, anticipated Rachel Carson's Silent Spring — and in the 1970s co-founded the Institute for Social Ecology in Vermont and taught at Ramapo College. He spent his final decades in Burlington, Vermont, where he advised local green politics.

Ideas

Bookchin held that the domination of humans by humans — hierarchy in its economic, ethnic, gender, and political forms — gave rise to the idea of dominating nature. His magnum opus, The Ecology of Freedom (1982), traced hierarchy from prehistory to capitalism. Politically, he proposed that activists contest municipal elections and build popular assemblies that could confederate across cities, progressively displacing the nation-state. In the 1990s he broke sharply with much of the anarchist movement, denouncing "lifestyle anarchism" and renaming his political program communalism.

Legacy

Bookchin's ideas influenced the anti-globalization movement, the Occupy movement's assembly practices, and — most concretely — the Kurdish freedom movement: Abdullah Öcalan read Bookchin in prison, and his "democratic confederalism," implemented in the autonomous administration of North and East Syria (Rojava), drew heavily on communalism and social ecology.
