Monstrance

A monstrance, also known as an ostensorium (or ostensory), is a vessel used in the Roman Catholic, Old Catholic, High Church Lutheran and Anglican traditions for the public display of the consecrated Eucharistic host, most commonly in the context of Eucharistic adoration and Benediction of the Blessed Sacrament. The name derives from the Latin monstrare, "to show", and its purpose is precisely to display the host to the faithful.

A typical sunburst monstrance; the host is visible at the centre.

Description

A monstrance usually consists of a stem or stand supporting a circular frame, often surrounded by rays resembling a sunburst, at the centre of which is a small glass-walled compartment. Within this compartment the consecrated host is held upright in a crescent-shaped holder called a luna or lunula. Many monstrances are made of gold or silver-gilt and richly ornamented; some incorporate a cross at the top and are decorated with figures of angels or saints.

Use

During Eucharistic adoration the host in the monstrance is placed on an altar so that the faithful may pray before it. At Benediction, the priest or deacon, wearing a humeral veil, uses the monstrance to bless the congregation with the Blessed Sacrament. Monstrances are also carried in processions, notably on the feast of Corpus Christi, when the Blessed Sacrament is borne through the streets under a canopy.

History

Monstrances developed in the later Middle Ages, after the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) affirmed the doctrine of transubstantiation and devotion to the reserved host grew. Early monstrances of the 13th and 14th centuries often resembled small towers or reliquaries; the sunburst form became standard in the Baroque period, after the Council of Trent reaffirmed Eucharistic devotion.
