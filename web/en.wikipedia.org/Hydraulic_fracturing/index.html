Hydraulic fracturing

Hydraulic fracturing, commonly known as fracking, is the injection of fluid — typically water mixed with sand and chemicals — into underground rock formations at high pressure to create and prop open fractures through which oil and natural gas can flow to a wellbore. Combined with horizontal drilling, the technique made it economically possible to produce hydrocarbons from low-permeability "tight" formations such as shale, and it drove the rapid growth of oil and gas production in the United States in the twenty-first century.

History

Wells were "shot" with explosives from the 1860s to increase production, but the first experimental hydraulic fracturing treatment was performed in 1947 in the Hugoton gas field of Kansas, with the first commercial treatments following in 1949 by Halliburton and Stanolind. Fracturing was long used mainly in vertical wells. The modern shale revolution began in the Barnett Shale of Texas in the 1990s, where Mitchell Energy, under George P. Mitchell, combined slickwater fracturing with horizontal drilling to unlock shale gas at commercial cost. The methods spread to the Marcellus, Bakken, Eagle Ford and Permian basins, transforming the United States into the world's largest oil and gas producer.

Process

A well is drilled vertically and then curved to run horizontally through the target formation, sometimes for several kilometres. Steel casing is cemented in place and perforated in stages; fracturing fluid — more than 90 percent water, with sand as proppant and a small fraction of additives such as friction reducers, biocides and scale inhibitors — is pumped at pressures sufficient to crack the rock. After each stage, plugs isolate the completed section. A single well may use millions of litres of water. Flowback and produced water are collected at the surface for reuse or disposal, often in deep injection wells.

Environmental and health debate

Hydraulic fracturing is controversial. Concerns include possible contamination of drinking-water aquifers, methane leaks, surface spills, air pollution, the large volumes of water consumed, and induced seismicity — the latter associated mainly with wastewater disposal wells rather than fracturing itself. Supporters point to economic benefits, energy security, and lower-carbon electricity when gas displaces coal, while noting that studies have generally found aquifer contamination to be uncommon and usually linked to surface handling or well integrity rather than the fracturing process itself. Several jurisdictions, including France, Germany (in part), New York State and Scotland, have banned or restricted the practice, while others have expanded it under regulation.
