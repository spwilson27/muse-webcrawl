Menominee (c. 1791 – 15 April 1841) was a Potawatomi chief and religious leader in Indiana during the 19th century. A convert to Roman Catholicism, he led his band's resistance to forced removal under the Indian Removal Act and became the central figure in the events leading to the Potawatomi Trail of Death of 1838.

Life

Menominee's village stood at the headwaters of the Yellow River near Twin Lakes in present-day Marshall County, Indiana. Under treaties in the 1830s the Potawatomi ceded most of their land; a reserve of two sections was confirmed to Menominee's band, but a further treaty in 1836 — which Menominee refused to sign, denying that the chiefs who signed had authority — purported to cede the reserve too and required removal within two years.

Removal

When the deadline passed in August 1838, Governor David Wallace sent the Indiana militia under General John Tipton. Menominee was seized when he came to negotiate, his chapel was burned, and about 850 Potawatomi were rounded up and marched west. Menominee famously appealed to the promise that his people could remain, declaring that he had never sold his land. The forced march to Kansas — the Potawatomi Trail of Death — killed more than 40 people, many of them children, from disease and hardship.

Death and legacy

Menominee died in Kansas in April 1841. A statue of him, erected in 1909 near the site of his village, is among the earliest monuments to a Native American in Indiana; a chapel (St. Mary of the Lake) marks the mission site, and the Trail of Death is commemorated by markers along the route in Indiana, Illinois, Missouri and Kansas.
