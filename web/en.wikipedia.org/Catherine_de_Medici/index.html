Catherine de' Medici (1519–1589) was an Italian noblewoman born into the powerful Medici family who became Queen of France as the wife of King Henry II. After her husband's death in 1559, she ruled France as regent for her young son Charles IX and remained the dominant political figure during the reigns of three sons — Francis II, Charles IX, and Henry III — throughout the French Wars of Religion.

Early life and marriage

The daughter of Lorenzo II de' Medici, Duke of Urbino, Catherine was orphaned in infancy and raised in convents amid the political turmoil of Florence. In 1533, at fourteen, she was married to Henri, Duke of Orléans, the future Henry II, in a match arranged by her kinsman Pope Clement VII. For a decade she was overshadowed by her husband's mistress, Diane de Poitiers, but after Henry's accession in 1547 she became queen and, on his death from a jousting wound in 1559, the central figure of the regency governments that followed.

The Wars of Religion

Catherine attempted to steer a middle course between militant Catholics, led by the Guise family, and the Protestant Huguenots. Her policies of limited toleration, such as the Edict of Saint-Germain (1562), alternated with repression. She bears a central and contested role in the St. Bartholomew's Day massacre of August 1572, in which thousands of Huguenots were killed in Paris and the provinces. Historians debate how far the massacre was premeditated and how far it escaped her control.

Patronage and legacy

Catherine was a major patron of the arts, credited with introducing Italian culinary and court fashions to France, enlarging the royal library, and building projects such as the Tuileries Palace and the Château de Chenonceau's bridge gallery. Protestant and later republican historiography cast her as a scheming Italian poisoner — the "black legend" — while modern scholarship presents her as a pragmatic stateswoman struggling to hold the French monarchy together amid civil war.
