Back-formation

In etymology, a back-formation is a word formed by removing an actual or supposed affix from an existing word, in the reverse direction of normal derivation. For example, the verb edit was back-formed from the older noun editor, and babysit from babysitter: speakers treated the ending as if it were a suffix and subtracted it to create a new, shorter word.

Back-formation
Field: Linguistics, etymology
Term coined: 1889 (James Murray)
Examples: edit ← editor; donate ← donation; pea ← pease

Examples

Back-formations are common in English. The noun pease (a mass noun for peas) was reanalysed as a plural, yielding the singular pea. Verbs such as sculpt (from sculptor), burgle (from burglar, chiefly British), televise (from television) and diagnose (from diagnosis) arose the same way. Some back-formations begin as humorous or erroneous coinages but become standard, such as gruntled (from disgruntled).

Relation to other processes

Back-formation differs from clipping (shortening without reanalysis, as in flu from influenza) and from folk etymology, in which an unfamiliar word is reshaped to resemble familiar elements. The term "back-formation" itself was introduced by lexicographer James Murray in 1889.
