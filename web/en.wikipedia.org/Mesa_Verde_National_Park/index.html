Mesa Verde National Park is an American national park and UNESCO World Heritage Site in Montezuma County, Colorado. It protects some of the best-preserved ancestral Puebloan archaeological sites in the United States, including nearly 5,000 known sites and 600 cliff dwellings built in the alcoves of the mesa canyons.

History

The ancestral Pueblo people lived in the Mesa Verde region from about 600 to 1300 CE, first in pit houses and then in multi-storey stone pueblos on the mesa tops. In the late 12th and 13th centuries they moved into dwellings built under overhanging cliffs — including the great Cliff Palace, the largest cliff dwelling in North America — probably for defence, shelter and access to water seeps. By about 1300, after prolonged drought, the population had migrated south to present-day Arizona and New Mexico, where their descendants are the modern Pueblo peoples.

Discovery and protection

Cowboys Richard Wetherill and Charlie Mason sighted Cliff Palace in 1888; the Wetherill family excavated and collected extensively, and the Swedish scholar Gustaf Nordenskiöld made the first scientific study in 1891. With the support of campaigners including Virginia McClurg, the park was created in 1906 — the first US national park set aside to preserve cultural rather than natural heritage — and was listed as a World Heritage Site in 1978.

Visiting

Major sites open to visitors include Cliff Palace, Balcony House and Long House (by ranger-guided tour), the mesa-top sites of Far View, and the Chapin Mesa museum. Wildfires remain a serious threat to the park's archaeology and piñon–juniper woodland.
