Right of self-defense under international law

The right of self-defense under international law is the entitlement of a state to use force in response to an armed attack against it. It is recognised both in customary international law and in the Charter of the United Nations, whose Article 51 provides that nothing in the Charter "shall impair the inherent right of individual or collective self-defence if an armed attack occurs against a Member of the United Nations, until the Security Council has taken measures necessary to maintain international peace and security."

Requirements

Customary law, as reflected in the 1837 Caroline affair correspondence, requires that defensive force be necessary and proportionate: the necessity must be "instant, overwhelming, leaving no choice of means, and no moment for deliberation," and the response must not be unreasonable or excessive. States exercising self-defense must immediately report their measures to the UN Security Council.

Scope and controversies

Major debates concern whether self-defense may be exercised anticipatorily against imminent attacks, preemptively against gathering threats, against non-state actors operating from another state's territory (the "unwilling or unable" doctrine), and in protection of nationals abroad. The International Court of Justice addressed the right in cases including Nicaragua v. United States (1986), the Nuclear Weapons advisory opinion (1996), Oil Platforms (2003), and Armed Activities on the Territory of the Congo (2005), generally requiring an armed attack of sufficient scale and effects attributable to the target of the response.

Collective self-defense

States may also act in collective self-defense of a victim state that requests assistance — the basis invoked, for example, for the defence of Kuwait in 1990–91 and for mutual-defence treaties such as NATO's Article 5, invoked once, after the September 11 attacks.
