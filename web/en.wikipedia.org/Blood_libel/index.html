Blood libel

Blood libel or ritual murder libel is an antisemitic canard: a false allegation that Jews murdered Christians in order to use their blood in the performance of religious rituals. The accusers claimed that the blood of Christian children was mixed into food eaten at Passover and other rituals. Blood libels typically led to violence against local Jewish communities, including massacres, expulsions, and trials ending in execution, and they persisted from the Middle Ages into the modern era despite repeated condemnation by popes, monarchs, and investigations that found no evidence.

History

The first recorded case in medieval Europe was the 1144 death of William of Norwich, after which local Jews were accused of ritual murder. The libel in the case of Hugh of Lincoln (1255) led to the execution of 18 Jews and was retold in Chaucer's "Prioress's Tale". Other notorious cases include Simon of Trent (1475), the Damascus affair of 1840, the Tiszaeszlár affair in Hungary (1882), and the Beilis trial in Kiev (1911–1913), in which Mendel Beilis was acquitted.

Official condemnations

Emperor Frederick II investigated the Fulda case in 1236 and declared the accusations baseless, and a series of popes, including Innocent IV in 1247 and Gregory X in 1272, issued bulls rejecting the blood libel. Nevertheless the myth survived for centuries in folklore, literature, and propaganda, including Nazi publications.

Modern era

Blood libel accusations occasionally surfaced in the 20th century, notably in the 1946 Kielce pogrom in Poland, and variants of the myth continue to circulate in extremist material. Jewish law strictly prohibits the consumption of blood, a fact repeatedly cited in refutations.
