Great Lakes region

The Great Lakes and the states and provinces surrounding them.

The Great Lakes region of Northern America is a binational Canadian–American region centred on the Great Lakes. It comprises the U.S. states of Illinois, Indiana, Michigan, Minnesota, New York, Ohio, Pennsylvania, and Wisconsin, together with the Canadian province of Ontario. The region is one of the most populous and economically productive areas of North America, home to more than 50 million people.

Geography

The five Great Lakes — Superior, Michigan, Huron, Erie, and Ontario — together hold roughly one-fifth of the world's fresh surface water. The lakes drain through the St. Lawrence River to the Atlantic Ocean and are connected to the Mississippi River basin by canals and to the Atlantic by the St. Lawrence Seaway and the New York State Canal System. Major metropolitan areas include Chicago, Toronto, Detroit, Cleveland, Buffalo, Milwaukee, and Hamilton.

Economy

The region developed as a manufacturing heartland — the historical core of the American "Rust Belt" — built on steel, automobiles, and shipping through the lakes. If it were a country, the Great Lakes economy would rank among the largest in the world, with a gross regional product of several trillion US dollars. Agriculture, higher education, research, and freshwater-related industries also play major roles, and the Great Lakes Megalopolis (or "ChiPitts" corridor) is one of North America's recognized megaregions.

History

Indigenous peoples, including the Anishinaabe, Haudenosaunee, and Wyandot, have lived around the lakes for thousands of years. French explorers and fur traders arrived in the 17th century, and the region was contested in the French and Indian War and the War of 1812 before the present international boundary was settled. The opening of the Erie Canal in 1825 and the Welland Canal spurred rapid settlement and industrial growth.
