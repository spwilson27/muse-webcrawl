Apatite is a group of phosphate minerals, usually hydroxyapatite, fluorapatite and chlorapatite, with high concentrations of OH−, F− and Cl− ions, respectively, in the crystal. The formula of the admixture of the three most common endmembers is written as Ca10(PO4)6(OH,F,Cl)2, and the crystal unit cell formulae of the individual minerals are written as Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2, Ca10(PO4)6F2 and Ca10(PO4)6Cl2.

Blue-green apatite crystal

The mineral was named apatite by the German geologist Abraham Gottlob Werner in 1786, although the specific mineral he had described was reclassified as fluorapatite in 1860 by the German mineralogist Karl Friedrich August Rammelsberg. Apatite is often mistaken for other minerals, a tendency reflected in its name, which is derived from the Greek word ἀπατάω (apatáō), meaning "to deceive."

Properties

Apatite defines a hardness of 5 on the Mohs scale. Hydroxyapatite, also known as hydroxylapatite, is the major component of tooth enamel and bone mineral. A relatively rare form of apatite in which most of the OH groups are absent and containing many carbonate and acid phosphate substitutions is a large component of bone material.

Uses

The primary use of apatite is as a source of phosphorus: it is mined as phosphate rock for the manufacture of fertilizer. Apatite is occasionally used as a gemstone. Transparent specimens of vivid green, blue, or purple color have been cut into gems, and chatoyant specimens are known as cat's-eye apatite. Apatite is also used in the manufacture of phosphoric acid and hydrofluoric acid.

Geology

Apatite is a common accessory mineral in igneous rocks and is also found in metamorphic and sedimentary rocks. Large deposits of phosphate rock, composed largely of apatite-group minerals, occur in Russia, Morocco, the United States (notably Florida and North Carolina), and elsewhere. Moon rock samples collected during the Apollo program were found to contain traces of apatite.
