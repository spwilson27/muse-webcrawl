Lipschitz continuity

In mathematical analysis, Lipschitz continuity, named after the German mathematician Rudolf Lipschitz, is a strong form of uniform continuity for functions. A function f defined on a subset of the real numbers is called Lipschitz continuous if there exists a real constant K ≥ 0 such that, for all x1 and x2 in the domain, |f(x1) − f(x2)| ≤ K|x1 − x2|.

Definition

The smallest constant K satisfying the inequality is called the Lipschitz constant. The condition implies that the slopes of all secant lines of the graph are bounded in absolute value. Every Lipschitz continuous function is continuous, and in fact uniformly continuous. A function that is Lipschitz continuous with constant K < 1 is called a contraction mapping, a concept central to the Banach fixed-point theorem.

Examples

Every continuously differentiable function on a closed bounded interval is Lipschitz continuous, since its derivative is bounded. The absolute value function |x| is Lipschitz continuous with constant 1 but is not differentiable at 0. By contrast, the function x2 is not Lipschitz continuous on the whole real line, though it is on every bounded interval.

Properties

By Rademacher's theorem, a Lipschitz function on an open subset of ℝn is differentiable almost everywhere. Lipschitz conditions are also fundamental in the Picard–Lindelöf theorem guaranteeing existence and uniqueness of solutions to ordinary differential equations.
