Living fossil

A living fossil is an extant taxon that cosmetically resembles related species known only from the fossil record. The term was coined by Charles Darwin in On the Origin of Species (1859), where he used it for forms such as the platypus and the South American lungfish that had persisted with little change in restricted habitats. To be called a living fossil, a species is generally a sole survivor or one of few survivors of an otherwise extinct lineage, exhibits exceptional morphological stasis, and typically shows low taxonomic diversity.

Famous examples

  • Coelacanths (Latimeria): lobe-finned fishes thought extinct for 66 million years until a live specimen was caught off South Africa in 1938.
  • Horseshoe crabs: chelicerates whose body plan has changed little in hundreds of millions of years.
  • Ginkgo biloba: the only living species of its division, nearly identical to Jurassic fossils.
  • Tuatara of New Zealand, the last rhynchocephalian reptiles.
  • Nautilus, lingula brachiopods, the dawn redwood (Metasequoia), and the Wollemi pine, the latter two rediscovered in the twentieth century.

Criticism of the concept

Biologists caution that "living fossil" can be misleading. Morphological stability does not imply genetic or molecular stasis: coelacanth and horseshoe crab genomes have continued to evolve, and surviving species are modern organisms adapted to modern environments, not literal ancestors preserved unchanged. Some researchers prefer terms such as "stabilomorph" or simply describe the traits — long-term stasis, bradytely (slow evolutionary rate), and relict distribution — separately. Others defend the term as a useful shorthand for a real phenomenon: lineages that persist for geological ages with low diversity and conservative body plans.
