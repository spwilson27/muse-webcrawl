Migrant Mother is a 1936 photograph by Dorothea Lange depicting Florence Owens Thompson, a mother of seven children, at a camp for destitute pea pickers in Nipomo, California. Taken while Lange was working for the Farm Security Administration (FSA), the image became one of the most iconic photographs of the Great Depression and of American documentary photography in general.

History

In March 1936, Lange was driving home from an assignment when she passed a sign for a pea-pickers' camp at Nipomo. She photographed Thompson and her children, taking a series of exposures that progressively moved closer; the final, most famous frame shows Thompson gazing into the distance with her hand to her chin, two children turned away on either side of her, and an infant in her lap. Lange later recalled that she did not ask the woman's name or history, and that Thompson told her they had been living on frozen vegetables and birds the children had killed.

Publication and impact

The photographs were published in the San Francisco News, prompting the federal government to rush food aid to the camp. The image came to symbolize the plight of migrant farm workers and the hardships of the Depression era. Thompson's identity was not widely known until the late 1970s, when she was located living in Modesto, California; she expressed ambivalence about the photograph, which had brought her no direct financial benefit. The Library of Congress holds the negative, and prints are held by major museums. In 1998, a print signed by Lange sold at auction, and Migrant Mother remains among the most reproduced photographs in history.
