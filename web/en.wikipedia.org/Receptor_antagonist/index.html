Receptor antagonist

A receptor antagonist is a type of receptor ligand or drug that blocks or dampens a biological response by binding to a receptor rather than activating it, as an agonist does. Antagonists mediate their effects by binding to the active (orthosteric) site or to allosteric sites on the receptor, preventing the binding or action of agonists, including endogenous neurotransmitters and hormones.

Types

Competitive antagonists bind reversibly to the same site as the agonist, so their blockade can be overcome by increasing the agonist concentration; they shift the agonist's dose–response curve to the right. Non-competitive antagonists bind at a separate allosteric site and reduce the maximum response the agonist can produce, regardless of agonist concentration. Irreversible antagonists form covalent bonds with the receptor, permanently inactivating it until new receptors are synthesised. Antagonists are also characterised by affinity and efficacy: a pure antagonist has affinity but zero intrinsic efficacy, whereas an inverse agonist reduces the receptor's constitutive activity below baseline.

Examples

Familiar examples include naloxone, an opioid receptor antagonist used to reverse opioid overdose; propranolol and other beta blockers, antagonists at beta-adrenergic receptors; antihistamines, antagonists at histamine H1 receptors; and losartan, an angiotensin II receptor blocker. Receptor antagonists form one of the largest classes of pharmaceutical drugs.
