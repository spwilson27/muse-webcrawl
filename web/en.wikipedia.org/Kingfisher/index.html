Kingfishers are a family, subfamily complex, and grouping of small to medium-sized, brightly coloured birds in the order Coraciiformes. They have a cosmopolitan distribution, occurring throughout the world's tropical and temperate regions, with the greatest diversity in the Old World tropics and Australasia. About 118 species are recognised, conventionally divided among three subfamilies or families: river kingfishers (Alcedinidae), tree kingfishers (Halcyonidae), and water kingfishers (Cerylidae).

Description

Kingfishers are characterised by large heads, long, sharp, pointed bills, short legs, and stubby tails. Many species are brilliantly coloured in combinations of blue, green, orange, and rufous — colours produced not by pigments but by the structure of the feathers. Their eyes have excellent vision, including the ability to compensate for refraction at the water's surface when hunting prey below it.

Behaviour and diet

Although the name implies fish-eating, many species — especially the tree kingfishers, including the Australian kookaburras — live away from water and feed on invertebrates, reptiles, and other terrestrial prey. Fishing species such as the common kingfisher perch over water and plunge-dive headfirst to seize fish; the pied kingfisher can hover before diving. Kingfishers are strongly territorial.

Breeding

Kingfishers are monogamous and cavity nesters: river kingfishers excavate tunnels in earthen banks, while tree kingfishers use tree holes or arboreal termite nests. The female lays a clutch of glossy white eggs, and both parents incubate the eggs and feed the young.

Cultural significance

In Greek myth the kingfisher was associated with Alcyone, whose seven days of calm weather around the winter solstice — the "halcyon days" — were when the bird was said to nest on the sea. Kingfishers appear in heraldry, literature, and art worldwide, and the bird's aerodynamics famously inspired the redesign of the Japanese Shinkansen bullet train's nose.
