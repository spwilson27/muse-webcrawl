Avermectin

[Image placeholder: chemical structure of an avermectin]

The avermectins are a series of macrocyclic lactone derivatives with potent antiparasitic properties, produced by fermentation of the soil bacterium Streptomyces avermitilis. They are widely used as insecticides, acaricides and anthelmintics in agriculture, veterinary medicine and human medicine.

Discovery

The avermectins were discovered in the 1970s from a soil sample collected near a golf course in Shizuoka, Japan, by researchers working with Satoshi Ōmura of the Kitasato Institute, in collaboration with William Campbell at Merck. Their semi-synthetic derivative ivermectin proved extraordinarily effective against parasitic worms and arthropods. Ōmura and Campbell shared half of the 2015 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for this work.

Mechanism of action

Avermectins bind glutamate-gated chloride channels found in invertebrate nerve and muscle cells, increasing chloride permeability and causing paralysis and death of the parasite. These channels are absent in vertebrates, and the drugs do not readily cross the mammalian blood–brain barrier, accounting for their wide margin of safety.

Uses

Ivermectin transformed the control of onchocerciasis (river blindness) and lymphatic filariasis through mass donation programmes, and is used against strongyloidiasis and scabies. Related compounds include abamectin and emamectin benzoate (agricultural insecticides), doramectin, selamectin and milbemycin derivatives (veterinary products).
