Hyde Park Aide-Mémoire

The aide-mémoire recorded the Anglo-American understanding on atomic research

The Hyde Park Aide-Mémoire was an agreement concluded between United States President Franklin D. Roosevelt and British Prime Minister Winston Churchill on 18–19 September 1944, during Churchill's visit to Roosevelt's home at Hyde Park, New York, following the Second Quebec Conference. The aide-mémoire governed post-war collaboration between the United States and the United Kingdom on the development of atomic weapons (then referred to under the code name "Tube Alloys").

Content

The short document recorded several points of agreement: that full collaboration between the two governments on Tube Alloys for military and commercial purposes should continue after the war unless terminated by joint agreement; that the weapon, when available, might after "mature consideration" be used against Japan, with warning to be given beforehand; and that there should be no discussion with third parties, in particular no information given to France or to the Soviet Union. It also rejected the proposal of the Danish physicist Niels Bohr that the project be internationalised and shared with the Russians.

Misplacement and consequences

The American copy of the aide-mémoire was misfiled — clerks, not recognising "Tube Alloys", filed it among naval papers relating to Hyde Park — and for a time the United States government effectively forgot the commitment, contributing to Anglo-American friction after the war, when cooperation was in fact curtailed by the McMahon Act of 1946. The British copy preserved by Churchill eventually brought the agreement to light, and it was formally acknowledged in subsequent negotiations that culminated in the 1958 US–UK Mutual Defence Agreement, which restored full nuclear cooperation.
