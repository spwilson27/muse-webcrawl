Ranavalona I (c. 1778–1782 – 16 August 1861) was sovereign of the Kingdom of Madagascar from 1828 to 1861. Having risen to succeed her husband Radama I, she pursued a policy of self-reliance and resistance to European influence: expelling most foreigners, persecuting Malagasy Christians, and ruling through the traditional institutions of the Merina kingdom. Her 33-year reign is remembered in sharply opposed ways — as cruel tyranny in much Western writing, and as defiant defence of Malagasy sovereignty in modern Madagascar.

Rise to power

Born Rabodoandrianampoinimerina, she was the daughter of a prince of Ambatomanoina whose warning saved King Andrianampoinimerina from an assassination plot; in gratitude the king married her to his son Radama, making her his principal wife. When Radama I died in 1828, she outmanoeuvred rival claimants with the support of the army and the powerful Andriana (noble) factions, took the throne name Ranavalona ("folded, kept aside"), and had competing heirs eliminated — a violent consolidation typical of Merina succession struggles.

Reign

Ranavalona annulled Radama's treaties with Britain, refused new concessions, and ended the British military training mission — though she retained and expanded the industrial works at Mantasoa directed by the French artisan Jean Laborde, which produced cannon, muskets, glass and soap for the crown. She reduced contact with foreign powers, expelled the London Missionary Society's missionaries in 1835, and from the mid-1830s banned Christianity for Malagasy subjects; converts who refused to recant were fined, enslaved, subjected to the tangena poison ordeal, or executed — the first martyrs, such as Rafaravavy Rasalama (1837), are still commemorated by Malagasy churches.

Her government rested on the fanompoana system of unpaid labour owed to the sovereign, used for armies, public works and industry; its burdens, together with constant military campaigns to hold the island's coastal peoples under Merina rule and outbreaks of disease, are blamed by historians for a severe population decline during her reign, though estimates of Madagascar's population in this era are very uncertain. In 1845 her forces repelled a joint British-French naval attack at Tamatave, a victory for her policy of armed isolation.

Death and assessment

Ranavalona died in 1861 and was succeeded briefly by her son Radama II, who reversed her policies before his assassination in 1863. Colonial-era histories, based largely on missionary sources, portrayed her as a bloodthirsty tyrant; modern scholarship offers a more mixed picture of a ruler who kept Madagascar independent for three decades while European empires absorbed the rest of Africa and Asia — at heavy cost to her own people. She remains a symbol of Malagasy sovereignty.
