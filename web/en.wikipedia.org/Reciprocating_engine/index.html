Reciprocating engine

In a reciprocating engine, pistons moving in cylinders drive a crankshaft through connecting rods.

A reciprocating engine, also often known as a piston engine, is a heat engine that uses one or more reciprocating pistons to convert pressure into a rotating motion. The term covers the steam engine and the familiar internal combustion engines, including petrol (gasoline) and diesel engines, as well as the Stirling engine.

Operating principle

In all reciprocating engines, expanding gas pushes a piston along a cylinder. A connecting rod joins the piston to a crankshaft, converting the piston's linear motion into rotation. In a four-stroke internal combustion engine the cycle consists of intake, compression, power and exhaust strokes; in a two-stroke engine the cycle is completed in a single revolution of the crankshaft. Steam engines admit steam alternately to both sides of the piston or use the steam expansively in successive cylinders (compounding).

History

The reciprocating steam engine powered the Industrial Revolution. Thomas Newcomen's atmospheric engine of 1712 pumped water from mines; James Watt's improvements in the 1770s, including the separate condenser, greatly increased efficiency. In the late nineteenth century the internal combustion engine — developed by engineers including Nikolaus Otto, Gottlieb Daimler and Rudolf Diesel — displaced steam in road vehicles because of its higher power-to-weight ratio and ready starting, although reciprocating steam engines remained in use in ships and locomotives into the twentieth century.

Configurations

Cylinders may be arranged inline, in a V, flat (boxer), radial — common in aircraft engines — or in rarer layouts such as the W and opposed-piston designs. Piston engines dominated aviation until the arrival of the jet engine, but remain universal in general aviation, automobiles, motorcycles, small generators and machinery. Their principal mechanical limitation is the vibration and stress arising from the reciprocating masses, mitigated by counterweights and balance shafts.

Efficiency

Typical automotive petrol engines convert about 20–35 per cent of fuel energy into work; modern diesels reach around 40 per cent, and the largest slow-speed marine diesels exceed 50 per cent, making them among the most efficient heat engines in practical use.
