Omaha Beach

Omaha Beach was one of five landing beaches of the Normandy landings in northern France on 6 June 1944 (D-Day) during World War II. The 8-kilometre (5 mi) beach, between Sainte-Honorine-des-Pertes and Vierville-sur-Mer on the Calvados coast, was assigned to the United States Army, and it was there that American troops suffered the heaviest losses of any landing beach — roughly 2,000 to 5,000 casualties.

The landings

Omaha's gently sloping beach was backed by bluffs up to 50 metres high, defended by strongpoints ("Widerstandsnester") with artillery, machine guns and mortars covering five narrow exits (draws). The preliminary naval and aerial bombardment largely missed the defences, and most of the amphibious DD tanks launched at sea swamped. The first wave landed from 06:30 into intense fire; many landing craft grounded on sandbars, and infantry units became disorganised and pinned down at the shingle bank.

Breakthrough

Small groups of soldiers, led by officers and NCOs improvising on the spot, gradually climbed the bluffs between strongpoints, while U.S. Navy destroyers closed to point-blank range to shell the defences. By late morning the beachhead was expanding; by nightfall about 34,000 troops had landed and penetrated up to 2.5 km inland, though all D-Day objectives were not reached. The adjacent Ranger assault scaled the cliffs at Pointe du Hoc to the west.

Legacy

Omaha, nicknamed "Bloody Omaha", is remembered as the hardest-fought of the invasion beaches. The Normandy American Cemetery at Colleville-sur-Mer, overlooking the beach, contains 9,388 graves. The landings were depicted in the films The Longest Day (1962) and Saving Private Ryan (1998).
