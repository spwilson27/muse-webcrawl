Ideomotor phenomenon

Ideomotor phenomenon

FieldPsychology
Key figuresWilliam Benjamin Carpenter; Michel Eugène Chevreul; Michael Faraday

The ideomotor phenomenon is a psychological phenomenon in which a subject makes motions unconsciously. Also called the ideomotor effect or ideomotor response, it occurs when merely thinking about a movement, or expecting it, triggers small involuntary muscular actions that can produce noticeable motion in a held object.

Explanation of purported paranormal effects

The effect is the accepted scientific explanation for a range of phenomena once attributed to spirits or hidden forces: the movements of the planchette on a Ouija board, the swinging of dowsing rods and pendulums, automatic writing, and table-turning. In 1853 Michael Faraday demonstrated experimentally that table-turning was caused by the sitters' own unconscious muscular action; the physiologist William Benjamin Carpenter introduced the term "ideo-motor action" in 1852, and the French chemist Michel Eugène Chevreul gave a similar account of the divining pendulum. In the 1990s the ideomotor effect was also implicated in "facilitated communication", in which facilitators unconsciously guided the hands of non-speaking people, leading to messages attributable to the facilitator rather than the subject.

Characteristics

The movements are genuine but involuntary and typically unnoticed by the person producing them, which is why participants sincerely attribute them to an external agency. Deliberately thinking of a motion primes the motor system — a principle related to the ideomotor theory of action in cognitive psychology, which holds that actions are represented in terms of their perceived effects. The effect is amplified when feedback (such as a moving pointer) confirms the expected direction, producing a self-reinforcing loop.
