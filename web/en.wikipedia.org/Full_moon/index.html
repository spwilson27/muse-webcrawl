Full moon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A full moon is the lunar phase that occurs when the Moon appears completely illuminated from Earth's perspective. This happens when Earth is located between the Sun and the Moon (more exactly, when the ecliptic longitudes of the Sun and Moon differ by 180°). A full moon occurs roughly once every 29.53 days, the length of a synodic month.

Occurrence and appearance

At full moon the Moon rises at about sunset and sets at about sunrise, shining all night. Its apparent brightness is the greatest of any lunar phase — partly because of the "opposition surge", the dramatic brightening as shadows on the lunar surface disappear at full phase. When a full moon coincides with perigee, the Moon appears slightly larger and brighter (popularly called a "supermoon"); a total lunar eclipse can only occur at full moon, when the Moon passes through Earth's shadow.

Names and culture

Traditional names for full moons in North American folklore include the Wolf Moon (January), Pink Moon (April), Harvest Moon (the full moon nearest the autumn equinox) and Hunter's Moon (October). Full moons structure many calendars and festivals: the Jewish Passover and Christian Easter are set by the spring full moon, the Buddhist Vesak falls on a full moon, and the Chinese Mid-Autumn Festival is held on the full moon of the eighth lunar month. Claims that the full moon influences human behaviour — the so-called "lunar effect" — are not supported by scientific evidence.
