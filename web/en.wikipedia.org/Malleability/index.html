Malleability

Malleability is a physical property of materials, most notably metals, that describes their ability to deform under compressive stress — that is, to be hammered, pressed, or rolled into thin sheets or other shapes without breaking or cracking. A material that exhibits a large degree of malleability is said to be malleable.

Overview

Malleability is closely related to ductility, which describes deformation under tensile stress (being drawn into a wire). Both properties are manifestations of plasticity: the permanent deformation of a material under load. Malleability arises from the metallic bond, in which positively charged metal ions sit in a "sea" of delocalised electrons; when a metal is hammered, layers of atoms can slide over one another while the bond is maintained, allowing the material to change shape rather than fracture.

At the crystal level, plastic deformation occurs mainly through the motion of dislocations within the crystal lattice. Metals with face-centred cubic structures, such as gold, silver, copper, and aluminium, tend to be especially malleable because they possess many slip systems along which dislocations can move.

Examples

Gold is the most malleable of all metals: a single gram can be beaten into a sheet covering about one square metre, and gold leaf can be made only a few hundred atoms thick — thin enough to be semi-transparent and appear greenish in transmitted light. Silver, copper, aluminium, tin, platinum, and lead are also notably malleable. Steel can be malleable, particularly at high temperatures, which is exploited in forging and rolling. In contrast, cast iron and bismuth are brittle and show little malleability at room temperature.

Factors affecting malleability

  • Temperature: Malleability generally increases with temperature, because dislocation motion becomes easier and new slip mechanisms activate. Many metals are worked hot for this reason; conversely, some metals (for example zinc) become brittle when cold or, for zinc, at certain intermediate temperatures.
  • Crystal structure: Face-centred cubic metals are typically more malleable than hexagonal close-packed or body-centred cubic metals at a given temperature.
  • Alloying and impurities: Alloying elements and impurities often pin dislocations, hardening the metal and reducing malleability; work hardening during deformation has a similar effect, which is why metals are periodically annealed during extensive working.
  • Grain size: Finer grains generally impede dislocation motion, trading some malleability for strength.

Applications

Malleability underlies essential manufacturing processes: rolling of sheet metal, forging, stamping, coin minting, and the production of foil and metal leaf. Historically, the malleability of gold and silver made them ideal for coinage and decorative metalwork; today, aluminium's malleability is exploited in beverage cans, aircraft skins, and packaging foil.
