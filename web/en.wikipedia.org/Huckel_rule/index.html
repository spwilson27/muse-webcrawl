Huckel rule

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Hückel’s rule, proposed by the German physical chemist Erich Hückel in 1931, predicts whether a planar ring molecule will have aromatic properties. A cyclic, planar, fully conjugated molecule is aromatic — and enjoys special stability — when it contains

4n + 2 π electrons

where n is a non-negative integer (0, 1, 2, …). Thus systems with 2, 6, 10, 14… π electrons are aromatic; benzene, with six π electrons, is the classic example.

Criteria

For the rule to apply, the molecule must be (1) cyclic, (2) planar, (3) fully conjugated, with a p orbital on every ring atom, and (4) contain 4n + 2 π electrons. Rings with 4n π electrons that otherwise meet the criteria, such as cyclobutadiene, are antiaromatic and especially unstable. Ions can obey the rule: the cyclopentadienyl anion and the tropylium cation each have six π electrons and are aromatic.

Background

Hückel derived the rule from quantum-mechanical molecular orbital calculations on monocyclic systems (Hückel molecular orbital theory), which show that the π energy levels of a ring consist of one lowest level followed by degenerate pairs; filling them with 4n + 2 electrons gives a closed shell. Extensions of the rule address Möbius topologies and excited states (Baird’s rule).
