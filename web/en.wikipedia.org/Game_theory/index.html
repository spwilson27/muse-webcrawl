Game theory

Game theory is the study of mathematical models of strategic interactions among rational agents. It has applications in economics, political science, biology, computer science, logic and military strategy. Modern game theory began with John von Neumann's proof of the minimax theorem in 1928 and his 1944 book with Oskar Morgenstern, Theory of Games and Economic Behavior.

Key concepts

A game is defined by its players, the strategies available to each, and the payoffs each combination of strategies produces. A central solution concept is the Nash equilibrium, introduced by John Forbes Nash in 1950: a profile of strategies from which no player can benefit by unilaterally changing their own strategy. Nash proved that every finite game has at least one equilibrium in mixed strategies.

The best-known example is the prisoner's dilemma, in which two suspects each do better by confessing whatever the other does, yet both end up worse off than if both had stayed silent — illustrating how individual rationality can produce collectively poor outcomes.

Types of games

Games are classified as cooperative or non-cooperative; simultaneous or sequential; zero-sum or non-zero-sum; and of perfect or imperfect information. Repeated games, in which the same players interact many times, can sustain cooperation through the threat of future punishment, as formalised in the folk theorem.

Applications

Game theory underlies auction design, bargaining models, voting theory and the study of oligopoly in economics; evolutionary game theory applies its tools to biology, where John Maynard Smith's concept of the evolutionarily stable strategy explains animal behaviour. It has also been used in nuclear strategy, cybersecurity and the design of matching markets. Numerous game theorists, including Nash, Reinhard Selten, John Harsanyi, Robert Aumann, Lloyd Shapley and Alvin Roth, have received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences.
