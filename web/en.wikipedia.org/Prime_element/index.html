Prime element

FieldAbstract algebra; ring theory
DefinitionNon-zero non-unit p such that p | ab implies p | a or p | b
Related notionsIrreducible element; prime ideal; prime number

In abstract algebra, a prime element of a commutative ring is a non-zero element that is not a unit and that satisfies the following property: whenever it divides a product of two elements, it divides at least one of them. Prime elements generalise the notion of a prime number from the integers to arbitrary rings.

Definition

An element p of an integral domain R is prime if p ≠ 0, p is not a unit, and for all a, b in R, if p divides ab then p divides a or p divides b. Equivalently, the principal ideal generated by p is a non-zero prime ideal.

Prime versus irreducible

Every prime element is an irreducible element, but the converse is not always true. In a unique factorisation domain (UFD) the two concepts coincide. A standard example of an irreducible element that is not prime occurs in the ring Z[√−5], where 6 = 2 · 3 = (1 + √−5)(1 − √−5) gives two genuinely different factorisations, and 2 is irreducible yet divides the product (1 + √−5)(1 − √−5) without dividing either factor.

Examples

In the integers, the prime elements are the prime numbers and their negatives. In a field there are no prime elements, since every non-zero element is a unit. In the Gaussian integers, primes such as 1 + i are prime elements, as are rational primes congruent to 3 modulo 4.
