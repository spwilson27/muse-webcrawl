Aramaic is a Semitic language, or group of languages, that originated among the Arameans of the ancient Near East and spread to become the lingua franca of much of Southwest Asia. First attested in inscriptions from the early first millennium BC, Aramaic was adopted by the Neo-Assyrian, Neo-Babylonian, and Achaemenid Persian empires; Imperial Aramaic served as an administrative language from Egypt to Central Asia.

History

Aramaic displaced many local languages and remained the everyday vernacular of the Levant and Mesopotamia through the Hellenistic and Roman periods; it was the native language of Jesus and the language of portions of the biblical books of Daniel and Ezra and of the later Jewish Talmuds. Following the Muslim conquests, Arabic gradually replaced Aramaic as the dominant spoken language, though Aramaic survives in liturgical use and in Neo-Aramaic varieties spoken by Assyrian, Mandaean, and other communities.

Dialects and scripts

Important varieties include Syriac, a major literary and liturgical language of Eastern Christianity, Jewish Babylonian Aramaic, and Mandaic. The Aramaic alphabet, written right to left in 22 letters, was the ancestor or source of the Hebrew, Syriac, and Arabic scripts, among others. Modern Neo-Aramaic languages, some of them endangered, are spoken in northern Iraq, southeastern Turkey, northwestern Iran, Syria, and a worldwide diaspora.
