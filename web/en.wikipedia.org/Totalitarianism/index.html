Totalitarianism is a political system and a form of government that prohibits opposition political parties, disregards and outlaws the political claims of individual and group opposition to the state, and controls the public sphere and the private sphere of society. It is the complete opposite of democracy in practically all aspects. In the field of political science, totalitarianism is the extreme form of authoritarianism, wherein all socio-political and economic policies and aspects of life are subject to the authority of the state's ideology and propaganda, enforced through a single mass party, a secret police, control of mass media, and often a cult of personality around the leader.

Origins of the term

The word emerged in 1920s Italy: critics of Mussolini's Fascist regime used totalitario pejoratively, while the regime's philosopher Giovanni Gentile and Mussolini himself adopted it approvingly, envisioning a "total state" in which "everything is within the state, nothing outside the state, nothing against the state". During the Cold War the concept was applied comparatively to Nazism and Stalinism.

Theories

Hannah Arendt's The Origins of Totalitarianism (1951) argued that totalitarian movements use ideology and terror to pursue total domination, atomizing society and destroying the space between people. Carl Friedrich and Zbigniew Brzezinski proposed a six-point syndrome: an official ideology, a single mass party, a terroristic police, a monopoly of communications, a monopoly of weapons, and a centrally directed economy. Critics have questioned the concept's Cold War polemics and whether any state achieved truly "total" control; historians debate applying the term to later or contemporary regimes, and scholars such as Juan Linz distinguished sultanistic and merely authoritarian systems from genuinely totalitarian ones.
