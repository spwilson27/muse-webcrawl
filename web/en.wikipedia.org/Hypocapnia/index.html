Hypocapnia

Hyperventilation washes out carbon dioxide, producing hypocapnia.

Hypocapnia (from the Greek words υπό meaning below normal and καπνός kapnós meaning smoke), also known as hypocarbia, sometimes incorrectly called acapnia, is a state of reduced carbon dioxide in the blood. Hypocapnia usually results from deep or rapid breathing, known as hyperventilation. It is defined by an arterial partial pressure of CO2 (PaCO2) below 35 mmHg.

Effects

Hypocapnia causes respiratory alkalosis, which lowers ionised calcium and produces symptoms including light-headedness, dizziness, tingling of the lips and extremities (paraesthesia), muscle cramps and carpopedal spasm, and in severe cases confusion or fainting. Carbon dioxide is a potent cerebral vasodilator, so hypocapnia constricts cerebral blood vessels; rapid hyperventilation can reduce cerebral blood flow enough to cause syncope. This effect is exploited therapeutically, as brief controlled hyperventilation can lower dangerously raised intracranial pressure, though prolonged use risks cerebral ischaemia.

Causes

Voluntary or anxiety-driven hyperventilation is the classic cause, but hypocapnia also occurs with pain, fever, sepsis, pulmonary embolism, asthma exacerbations, pregnancy (a mild physiological hypocapnia), high altitude, and over-ventilation of mechanically ventilated patients. Free-divers who hyperventilate before a dive dangerously suppress their urge to breathe, risking shallow-water blackout.

Treatment

Treatment addresses the underlying cause. Reassurance and slowed breathing suffice for most hyperventilation episodes; the traditional practice of rebreathing into a paper bag is no longer generally recommended because it can cause hypoxia if the diagnosis is wrong.
