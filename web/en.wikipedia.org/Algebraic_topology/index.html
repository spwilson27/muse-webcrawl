Algebraic topology is a branch of mathematics that uses tools from abstract algebra to study topological spaces. The basic goal is to find algebraic invariants that classify topological spaces up to homeomorphism, though usually most invariants classify up to homotopy equivalence. If two spaces have different invariants — a different fundamental group, say, or different homology — then they cannot be homeomorphic, which lets algebra answer purely geometric questions.

History

The subject began with Henri Poincaré's paper Analysis Situs (1895) and its supplements, which introduced the fundamental group and (simplicial) homology and posed the question — settled only in 2003 by Grigori Perelman — of whether a simply connected closed 3-manifold must be a sphere. In the 1920s and 1930s Emmy Noether's influence recast homology in terms of groups rather than numerical invariants (Betti numbers and torsion coefficients), and Heinz Hopf and others developed homotopy theory. The Eilenberg–Steenrod axioms (1945) axiomatized homology theories; Samuel Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane created category theory largely to formalize the subject's notion of functoriality and naturality. From the 1950s, algebraic topology fed into and drew upon algebraic geometry, differential geometry and mathematical physics.

Basic constructions

The fundamental group

The fundamental group π₁(X, x₀) of a space records equivalence classes of loops based at a point x₀, with composition given by concatenation. It is the circle's group that is infinite cyclic (Z), the torus's that is Z × Z, and a simply connected space (such as a sphere of dimension ≥ 2) has trivial fundamental group. The fundamental group of the figure-eight is the free group on two generators, illustrating that the invariant need not be abelian.

Homology and cohomology

Homology associates to a space a sequence of abelian groups Hₙ(X), built from chains of simplices or singular simplices modulo boundaries, which detect "holes" of each dimension. The Euler characteristic is the alternating sum of the ranks of these groups. Cohomology groups are defined dually and carry a ring structure (the cup product) that contains more information than homology alone. Landmark computations include the homology of spheres and projective spaces, and landmark theorems include the Brouwer fixed point theorem — every continuous map from a disk to itself has a fixed point — which follows from the functoriality of homology.

Methods and applications

Principal computational tools include CW complexes, the Mayer–Vietoris sequence, the Künneth formula, spectral sequences, and obstruction theory. Algebraic topology underlies results across mathematics — the hairy ball theorem, the Borsuk–Ulam theorem, and the classification of vector bundles — and has applications in data analysis (persistent homology), robotics (configuration spaces), sensor networks, condensed matter physics (topological phases) and string theory.
