Antiarrhythmic agent

Antiarrhythmic agents, also known as cardiac dysrhythmia medications, are a group of pharmaceuticals used to suppress abnormal rhythms of the heart (arrhythmias), such as atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, ventricular tachycardia and ventricular fibrillation.

Classification

The widely used Vaughan Williams classification divides the drugs by their principal mechanism on the cardiac action potential:

  • Class I: sodium-channel blockers (e.g. flecainide, procainamide, lidocaine)
  • Class II: beta blockers (e.g. metoprolol)
  • Class III: potassium-channel blockers that prolong repolarisation (e.g. amiodarone, sotalol)
  • Class IV: non-dihydropyridine calcium-channel blockers (e.g. verapamil, diltiazem)
  • Class V: agents acting by other mechanisms, such as adenosine and digoxin

Use and risks

Antiarrhythmics may restore or maintain normal sinus rhythm or control heart rate, but many carry a risk of provoking new arrhythmias (proarrhythmia) — trials such as CAST showed some drugs increase mortality when used after heart attacks — so their use is balanced against alternatives including catheter ablation and implantable devices.
