Comparative method

Comparative method
FieldHistorical linguistics
PurposeReconstruction of proto-languages; establishing language families
Key figuresWilliam Jones, Jacob Grimm, Karl Verner, the Neogrammarians

In linguistics, the comparative method is a technique for studying the development of languages by performing a feature-by-feature comparison of two or more languages with common descent from a shared ancestor and then extrapolating backwards to infer the properties of that ancestor. Together with the method of internal reconstruction, it constitutes the principal methodology of historical linguistics.

Principles

Genetic relatedness and cognates

The method rests on the observation that related languages share cognates — words descended from a common ancestor — which correspond to one another by regular patterns of sound correspondence. For example, English father, German Vater, Latin pater and Sanskrit pitā all descend from Proto-Indo-European *ph₂tḗr.

Regularity of sound change

Sound changes are assumed to apply regularly across the vocabulary: the Neogrammarian hypothesis holds that sound laws admit no exceptions unexplained by further conditioning. Grimm's law described the systematic shift of Proto-Indo-European stop consonants in Proto-Germanic, and Verner's law explained its apparent exceptions by reference to the position of the accent.

Reconstruction

By aligning cognate sets and determining which sound in each correspondence set is most plausibly ancestral, linguists reconstruct proto-forms, marked with an asterisk. Repeated application yields a picture of the proto-language's phonology, morphology and lexicon, and a family tree (Stammbaum) of its descendants.

Applications and limits

The method has been applied most famously to the Indo-European family, reconstructing Proto-Indo-European, and also to Uralic, Austronesian, Bantu, Sino-Tibetan and many other families. It can reliably reach back several thousand years, but its power fades with time depth as vocabulary is replaced; claims of very deep relationships (such as Nostratic) remain controversial. The method is weaker where heavy borrowing has occurred, since loans can mimic cognacy, and it traditionally models divergence as clean splits, whereas dialect continua and wave-like diffusion (the wave model) also occur.
