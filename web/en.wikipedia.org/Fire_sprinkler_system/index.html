A fire sprinkler system is an active fire protection method, consisting of a water supply system providing adequate pressure and flowrate to a water distribution piping system, onto which fire sprinklers are connected. Although historically only used in factories and large commercial buildings, systems for homes and small buildings are now available at a cost-effective price.

Fire sprinkler systems have been extensively studied: worldwide statistics show that in about 96 percent of cases where sprinklers were installed, they controlled or extinguished the fire. Each sprinkler head operates independently, triggered by heat — contrary to popular depiction, smoke alone does not activate sprinklers, and only the heads nearest the fire discharge water.

How sprinklers work

Most sprinkler heads are sealed by a glass bulb filled with a heat-sensitive liquid, or by a fusible metal link. When the temperature around the head reaches its rated value, the bulb shatters (or the link melts), opening the orifice and spraying water in a deflected pattern over the area below. Systems are broadly classed as wet pipe (pipes always filled with water), dry pipe (filled with pressurised air, used where pipes might freeze), deluge, and pre-action systems.

History

Leonardo da Vinci designed an early sprinkler system in the fifteenth century. The first practical automatic sprinkler system was patented by Henry S. Parmelee of New Haven, Connecticut, in 1874, and Frederick Grinnell's improvements in the 1880s and 1890s produced the modern sprinkler head.

Effectiveness and regulation

Building codes increasingly require sprinklers in high-rise, commercial, care and — in some jurisdictions — all new residential buildings. The U.S. National Fire Protection Association has no record of a fire with more than two deaths in a completely sprinklered building where the system operated properly.
