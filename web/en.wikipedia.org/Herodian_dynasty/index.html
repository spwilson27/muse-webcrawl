Herodian dynasty

The Herodian dynasty was a royal dynasty of Idumaean (Edomite) descent, ruling the Herodian Kingdom of Judea and later the Herodian Tetrarchy as a vassal state of the Roman Empire. The dynasty began with Herod Antipater (Antipas), who was appointed procurator of Judea by Julius Caesar in 47 BCE, and rose to prominence under his son Herod the Great, who was made king by the Roman Senate in 40 BCE (or 39 BCE in some chronologies) and ruled until his death in 4 BCE.

Herod the Great

Herod the Great consolidated power after conquering Jerusalem in 37 BCE. A gifted administrator and monumental builder sponsored by Rome, he rebuilt the Second Temple in Jerusalem, founded the port city of Caesarea Maritima, and constructed fortresses such as Masada and the Herodium. His reign was also marked by paranoia and the execution of family members, including his wife Mariamne I and three of his sons.

The tetrarchy

On Herod's death, Augustus divided the kingdom among three of his sons: Archelaus received Judea, Samaria, and Idumaea as ethnarch (he was deposed in 6 CE and Judea came under direct Roman prefects); Herod Antipas became tetrarch of Galilee and Peraea — he is the ruler who, in the New Testament accounts, executed John the Baptist and encountered Jesus; and Philip ruled the northeastern territories until 34 CE.

Later Herodians

Herod Agrippa I, a grandson of Herod the Great, was granted the kingship by Caligula and Claudius and briefly ruled a reunited kingdom from 41 to 44 CE. His son Agrippa II was the last significant Herodian ruler; during the First Jewish–Roman War (66–73 CE) he sided with Rome. The dynasty also intermarried with the royalty of neighboring client kingdoms and produced figures such as Herodias and Salome, known from the Gospels.

Sources

The principal ancient source for the dynasty is the Jewish historian Josephus, whose Jewish War and Jewish Antiquities describe Herodian politics in detail. Archaeology — including excavations at Caesarea, Masada, and the Herodium, where Herod's tomb was announced discovered in 2007 — has confirmed much of the scale of Herodian building.
