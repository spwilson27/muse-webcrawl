The Volkswagen Beetle, officially the Volkswagen Type 1 and known in Germany as the Käfer, is an economy car produced by the German manufacturer Volkswagen from 1938 until 2003. With more than 21.5 million built, it is the best-selling single car platform in history and one of the most recognisable automobiles ever made.

Origins

The Beetle was conceived in the 1930s as a "people's car" (Volkswagen) for Nazi Germany. Ferdinand Porsche led the design team, producing a simple, robust car with a rear-mounted, air-cooled flat-four engine, torsion-bar suspension, and a distinctive streamlined body. Mass production was interrupted by the Second World War, during which the Wolfsburg plant built military derivatives such as the Kübelwagen.

Postwar success

Under British occupation officer Ivan Hirst, production restarted after 1945. The Beetle became the cornerstone of West Germany's postwar economic miracle and a global export success. In the United States, clever advertising by Doyle Dane Bernbach ("Think Small", "Lemon") made it a countercultural icon of the 1960s, beloved for its economy, durability, and anti-fashion styling.

Design

The Beetle's air-cooled engine eliminated the radiator and simplified maintenance; its sealed floorpan let it float briefly on water, a fact shown in period advertising. The design changed little over decades: engine displacement grew from 1,131 cc to 1,584 cc, and the "Super Beetle" of 1971 added MacPherson-strut front suspension and a larger boot.

End of production

German production ended in 1978 as the water-cooled, front-wheel-drive Golf took over, but the Beetle continued in Brazil and Mexico. The last original Beetle, number 21,529,464, rolled off the line in Puebla, Mexico, on 30 July 2003. Volkswagen later sold two retro-styled successors, the New Beetle (1997) and the Beetle A5 (2011).
