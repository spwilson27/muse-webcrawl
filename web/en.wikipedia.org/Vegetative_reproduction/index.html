Vegetative reproduction

Vegetative reproduction (also known as vegetative propagation, vegetative multiplication or cloning) is a form of asexual reproduction occurring in plants in which a new plant grows from a fragment or cutting of the parent plant or specialised reproductive structures, which are sometimes called vegetative propagules. Because no fusion of gametes is involved, offspring are genetically identical to the parent.

Natural mechanisms

Many plants reproduce vegetatively by means of specialised structures: rhizomes (horizontal underground stems, as in ginger), stolons or runners (as in the strawberry), bulbs (onions, tulips), tubers (potatoes), corms, suckers, and plantlets formed along leaf margins, as in Kalanchoe. Plants such as the quaking aspen can form vast clonal colonies from a single root system.

Artificial propagation

Horticulturists exploit vegetative reproduction deliberately through cuttings, grafting, layering, division, and tissue culture (micropropagation). These methods preserve desirable traits of a cultivar exactly, which seed propagation cannot guarantee.

Advantages and disadvantages

Vegetative reproduction allows rapid colonisation and preserves favourable genotypes, but the resulting genetic uniformity leaves populations vulnerable to disease and environmental change.
