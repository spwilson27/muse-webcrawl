Applied behavior analysis (ABA) is a research discipline and set of therapeutic techniques based on the principles of operant and respondent conditioning, originating in the behaviourism of B. F. Skinner. Practitioners analyse the functional relationship between behaviour and environment — summarised in the three-term contingency of antecedent, behaviour, and consequence — and systematically apply interventions such as reinforcement to increase socially significant behaviour or decrease harmful behaviour. ABA is best known as an early intervention for children with autism, but it is also used in education, organisational behaviour management, animal training, substance-abuse treatment, and dementia care.

History

Skinner's experimental analysis of behaviour (1930s–1950s) supplied the science; in the 1960s researchers including Donald Baer, Montrose Wolf, and Todd Risley translated it into an applied discipline, founding the Journal of Applied Behavior Analysis in 1968 and defining the field's seven dimensions (applied, behavioural, analytic, technological, conceptually systematic, effective, and generalisable). Ivar Lovaas at UCLA reported in 1987 that intensive early behavioural intervention (the "Lovaas method," using discrete trial training) produced large gains in some young autistic children — a study that drove the field's expansion, though its methods and claims remain debated.

Methods

Core procedures include functional behaviour assessment; positive reinforcement and shaping; prompting and prompt fading; task analysis and chaining; discrete trial training; natural environment teaching and pivotal response treatment; and token economies. Interventions are data-driven: behaviour is defined measurably, recorded continuously, and programmes adjusted on the evidence. Professional practice is certified in many jurisdictions, e.g., by the Behavior Analyst Certification Board (BCBA and RBT credentials), and ABA services are widely funded by health insurers in the United States.

Criticism

ABA is controversial, particularly within parts of the autistic self-advocacy community. Critics argue that historical programmes aimed at making autistic children "indistinguishable" from peers, sometimes used aversives (early Lovaas work employed punishment, and shock devices were used at one US institution until regulators intervened), and can prioritise compliance over autonomy. Many practitioners respond that modern ABA is assent-based and naturalistic and reject aversive methods; the debate continues over goals, evidence quality, and whose definition of "socially significant" should prevail.
