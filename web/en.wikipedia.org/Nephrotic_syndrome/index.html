Nephrotic syndrome

Nephrotic syndrome is a kidney disorder characterized by heavy loss of protein in the urine (proteinuria exceeding about 3.5 grams per day in adults), low serum albumin, generalized edema, and elevated blood lipids. It results from damage to the glomerular filtration barrier — particularly the podocytes and their slit diaphragms — rather than from inflammation of the kidney in the ordinary sense. It is distinguished from nephritic syndrome, in which hematuria, hypertension, and reduced kidney function predominate.

Causes

Primary (idiopathic) glomerular diseases causing nephrotic syndrome include minimal change disease (the commonest cause in children), focal segmental glomerulosclerosis (FSGS, commonest in adults of African ancestry), and membranous nephropathy (a leading cause in white adults), as well as membranoproliferative patterns. Secondary causes include diabetic kidney disease (the most common cause overall in adults), systemic lupus erythematosus, amyloidosis, infections (hepatitis B and C, HIV), malignancy, and drugs. Inherited defects of podocyte proteins — such as nephrin (NPHS1) and podocin (NPHS2) — cause congenital and childhood steroid-resistant forms.

Pathophysiology and features

Injury to the filtration barrier lets albumin and other plasma proteins escape into the urine. Falling serum albumin lowers plasma oncotic pressure, causing edema — typically periorbital on waking and pitting in dependent areas — and prompting compensatory hepatic protein synthesis, including lipoproteins (hence hyperlipidemia and lipiduria). Loss of immunoglobulins and complement factors increases susceptibility to encapsulated bacterial infections, while urinary loss of antithrombin and altered clotting factor balance creates a hypercoagulable state with risk of venous thromboembolism, including renal vein thrombosis.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on urinalysis and quantification of proteinuria (urine protein–creatinine ratio or 24-hour collection), serum albumin and lipids, kidney function, and tests for secondary causes; kidney biopsy usually establishes the histological diagnosis in adults and in atypical or steroid-resistant childhood cases. Treatment addresses the underlying cause — corticosteroids are first-line in minimal change disease, with other immunosuppressants (calcineurin inhibitors, cyclophosphamide, rituximab) for resistant or relapsing disease — plus supportive measures: sodium restriction and diuretics for edema, ACE inhibitors or ARBs to reduce proteinuria, statins, vaccination, and anticoagulation for thrombosis in high-risk patients.

Prognosis

Childhood minimal change disease usually responds to steroids with a relapsing course but preserved kidney function. FSGS and untreated membranous nephropathy carry substantial risks of progression to kidney failure; diabetic nephropathy progresses as part of systemic diabetes management.
