Irish whiskey (Irish: fuisce or uisce beatha, "water of life") is whiskey made on the island of Ireland. It is among the oldest distilled drinks in Europe, with a tradition commonly dated to monastic distillation around the twelfth century. Irish whiskey must be distilled from a mash of cereal grains, aged in wooden casks for at least three years in Ireland, and bottled at no less than 40% alcohol by volume.

Styles

The signature style is single pot still whiskey, made from a mixture of malted and unmalted barley distilled in a traditional copper pot still — exemplified by Redbreast and Green Spot — giving a spicy, oily character. Ireland also produces single malts (such as Bushmills and Connemara, the latter peated), grain whiskeys made in column stills, and blends combining them; Jameson, the world's best-selling Irish whiskey, is a blend dominated by pot still and grain whiskeys. Irish whiskey is often — though not by definition — triple distilled, and the barley is typically dried without peat smoke, giving a smoother, lighter profile than most Scotch.

History

In the nineteenth century Irish whiskey dominated world markets, but a combination of the temperance movement, independence-era trade disputes, Prohibition in the United States and competition from blended Scotch reduced the industry to a handful of distilleries consolidated under Irish Distillers by the 1970s, with just two operating whiskey distilleries on the island at the low point. Since the late 1980s the industry has undergone a dramatic revival — sales multiplied many times over, brands such as Jameson became global leaders, and dozens of new distilleries (Teeling, Dingle, Waterford and many others) have opened, making whiskey one of Ireland's fastest-growing exports.
