Blood–brain barrier

The blood–brain barrier (BBB) is a highly selective semipermeable border of endothelial cells that regulates the transfer of solutes between the circulating blood and the central nervous system. It protects the brain from toxins, pathogens and fluctuations in blood composition, while permitting the passage of essential nutrients such as glucose and amino acids.

Structure

Unlike capillaries elsewhere in the body, brain capillaries are sealed by tight junctions between endothelial cells, lack fenestrations, and are wrapped by pericytes and the end-feet of astrocytes. Dedicated transporter proteins carry nutrients in and actively pump many foreign molecules back out, notably via efflux transporters such as P-glycoprotein.

History

The barrier was inferred in the 1880s from Paul Ehrlich's observations that injected dyes stained every organ except the brain, and confirmed in 1913 when Edwin Goldmann showed the complementary experiment. Electron microscopy in the 1960s localised the barrier to the capillary endothelium.

Clinical significance

The BBB is a major obstacle in treating brain diseases: the great majority of small-molecule drugs and nearly all large biologics cannot cross it, complicating therapy for tumours, infection and neurodegeneration. Conversely, breakdown of the barrier is implicated in multiple sclerosis, stroke, meningitis and Alzheimer's disease. Strategies to bypass it include focused ultrasound, intranasal delivery and receptor-mediated transport.
