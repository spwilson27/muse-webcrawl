Public–private partnership

Roads are among the most common assets delivered through PPP concessions.

A public–private partnership (PPP or P3) is a long-term contractual arrangement between a government (or other public authority) and a private-sector consortium for the delivery of infrastructure or public services. Under a PPP the private partner typically designs, builds, finances, and operates the asset — a road, hospital, school, water system, or railway — for a concession period of 20 to 30 years or more, in return for payments from the government, charges to users such as tolls, or a combination of both. Risks are allocated to the party best able to manage them, in contrast to traditional procurement, where the public sector contracts separately for construction and retains operating responsibility.

Forms and rationale

Common models include build–operate–transfer (BOT), design–build–finance–operate (DBFO), and availability-payment concessions, in which the state pays a periodic fee conditional on the asset being available and meeting performance standards. The United Kingdom's Private Finance Initiative (PFI), launched in 1992, became the most influential template and was applied to hundreds of hospitals, schools, prisons, and defence facilities. Supporters argue PPPs deliver projects on time and on budget, harness private management expertise, and transfer construction and availability risk. Critics counter that private borrowing costs more than sovereign debt, contracts are inflexible and opaque, profits are privatised while ultimate risk returns to the state, and the accounting motive of keeping liabilities off the government balance sheet distorted decision-making.

Worldwide use

PPPs are used on every continent: Australia and Canada run large programmes for transport and social infrastructure; Spain and France have long traditions of toll-road concessions; and development banks promote PPPs for energy, water, and transport in low- and middle-income countries. High-profile difficulties — such as the collapse of the UK contractor Carillion in 2018 and renegotiated Latin American toll-road concessions — have led to tighter guidance, standardised contracts, and value-for-money tests.
