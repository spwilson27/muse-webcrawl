The Investiture Controversy, also called the Investiture Contest (German: Investiturstreit), was a conflict between the church and the state in medieval Europe over the ability to choose and install bishops (investiture) and abbots of monasteries and the pope himself. By the 11th century, kings and emperors across Europe had come to appoint bishops — often their own relatives or officials — investing them with the ring and crosier of spiritual office in return for loyalty and service, a practice entangled with simony and clerical marriage that the Gregorian Reform movement sought to end.

Course of the conflict

In 1075/76 Pope Gregory VII's Dictatus papae asserted sweeping papal claims, and Gregory forbade lay investiture. The German king Henry IV responded by declaring Gregory deposed; Gregory excommunicated and deposed Henry in return, releasing his subjects from their oaths. Facing rebellion by the German princes, Henry crossed the Alps in winter and stood as a penitent in the snow outside the castle of Canossa in January 1077, obtaining absolution — an episode that became a byword for humiliation before ecclesiastical power. The struggle resumed, however: Henry installed an antipope, took Rome in 1084, and drove Gregory into exile, where he died at Salerno in 1085 with the words "I have loved justice and hated iniquity, therefore I die in exile." Civil war and rival popes prolonged the contest into the next century.

Settlement

The Concordat of Worms (23 September 1122), agreed between Pope Callixtus II and Emperor Henry V, ended the controversy in the Empire: the emperor renounced investiture with ring and crosier and conceded free canonical elections, while retaining the right to invest bishops with their temporal estates (regalia) by the sceptre and to be present at elections in Germany. Similar settlements were reached in England (1107) and France. The long-term effects were profound: a church institutionally distinct from lay power, a strengthened papal monarchy, and an early legal separation of spiritual and temporal authority in the Latin West.
