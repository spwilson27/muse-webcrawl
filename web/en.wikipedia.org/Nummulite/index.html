Nummulite

DomainEukaryota
PhylumForaminifera
FamilyNummulitidae
GenusNummulites Lamarck, 1801
RangePalaeogene (esp. Eocene) to present-day relatives

Nummulites is a genus of large, coin-shaped foraminifera whose fossil tests are extremely abundant in Eocene marine limestones around the Mediterranean and across the former Tethys Ocean. The name comes from the Latin nummulus, "little coin", reflecting their disc-like shape; ancient authors including Herodotus and Strabo remarked on the coin-like fossils in the limestone of the Egyptian pyramids, which legend held to be the petrified lentils or coins of the pyramid builders.

Description

Although single-celled, nummulites grew to remarkable sizes — commonly 1–2 cm across and exceptionally up to about 15 cm — making them among the largest protists known. The calcareous test is a planispirally coiled disc divided into numerous chambers arranged in whorls, with complex internal partitions. Like other larger benthic foraminifera, living nummulitids host photosynthetic algal symbionts and inhabit warm, shallow, sunlit seas.

Geological significance

Nummulites flourished in the Eocene epoch, and their rapid evolution makes them valuable index fossils for dating and correlating Palaeogene marine strata. Nummulitic limestones were quarried in antiquity: the casing and core blocks of the Giza pyramids include stone packed with nummulite fossils. The genus declined sharply at the end of the Eocene, though related nummulitids survive in modern Indo-Pacific seas.
