Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary

Cretaceous–Paleogene (K–Pg) boundary
Age: 66 million years ago
Also called: K–T boundary
Signature: Global iridium-rich clay layer

The Cretaceous–Paleogene (K–Pg) boundary, formerly the K–T boundary, is a geological signature dated to 66 million years ago that marks the end of the Mesozoic Era and one of the largest mass extinctions in Earth's history, including the extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs.

Evidence

The boundary is visible worldwide as a thin layer of clay unusually rich in iridium, a metal rare in Earth's crust but common in asteroids. In 1980 Luis and Walter Alvarez proposed that a large asteroid impact caused the extinction. The buried Chicxulub crater beneath Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula, about 180 km wide, was identified as the impact site.

Extinction

Roughly 75% of species perished, including ammonites, pterosaurs, and all non-avian dinosaurs, while birds, mammals, and many other groups survived and diversified in the Paleogene. The role of the contemporaneous Deccan Traps volcanism remains debated.
