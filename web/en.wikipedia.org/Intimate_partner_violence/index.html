Intimate partner violence

Intimate partner violence (IPV) is domestic violence by a current or former spouse or partner in an intimate relationship against the other spouse or partner. IPV can take a number of forms, including physical, verbal, emotional, economic and sexual abuse. The World Health Organization (WHO) defines IPV as behaviour by an intimate partner or ex-partner that causes physical, sexual or psychological harm, including physical aggression, sexual coercion, psychological abuse and controlling behaviours.

Prevalence

Global estimates published by the WHO indicate that about 1 in 3 (30%) of women worldwide have been subjected to physical and/or sexual violence by an intimate partner or non-partner sexual violence in their lifetime, and that most of this violence is intimate partner violence. Men are also victims of IPV, although prevalence estimates and patterns differ; violence also occurs in same-sex relationships at comparable rates.

Forms and dynamics

Researchers distinguish between coercive controlling violence ("intimate terrorism"), in which one partner uses violence as part of a pattern of control, and situational couple violence arising from conflicts that escalate. Common forms include physical assault, sexual violence, stalking, psychological aggression and economic control. Risk factors include prior exposure to violence, alcohol misuse, economic stress and social norms condoning violence.

Health effects and responses

IPV is associated with injuries, chronic pain, mental health disorders including depression and PTSD, and increased risk of homicide. Responses include criminal justice measures and protection orders, shelters and advocacy services, screening in health care, and prevention programmes targeting attitudes and relationship skills among young people.
