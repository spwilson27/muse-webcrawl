Seeker (Quaker)

The Seekers were a seventeenth-century English Protestant dissenting group who believed that the true church, ministry and ordinances had been lost through apostasy and that they must wait — "seek" — for God to restore them. They are significant chiefly because many Seekers became the first converts of George Fox and formed the nucleus of the Religious Society of Friends (Quakers) in the 1650s.

Beliefs

Emerging amid the religious ferment of the English Civil War, Seekers concluded that no existing church possessed a valid commission from God. They therefore met together in silence or in discussion, without appointed ministers, sacraments or formal worship, awaiting a new dispensation. Their rejection of outward ordinances and their expectation of divine restoration made them receptive to the Quaker message of the inward Light of Christ available to all. John Saltmarsh and William Erbery are among the writers associated with Seeker ideas, and groups of Seekers were noted in London and the north-west of England.

Relation to the Quakers

When Fox preached in Lancashire, Westmorland and Yorkshire in 1652, whole Seeker meetings were "convinced" and joined the Friends, giving the early Quaker movement an organised base — sometimes called the Valiant Sixty's recruiting ground. Thereafter "Seeker" survived within Quakerism as a descriptive term: in modern usage, a "seeker" is a person exploring Quaker worship, and meetings often hold "seeker" sessions for enquirers who are not members.
