Santería

Santería (Spanish for "worship of saints"), also known as Regla de Ocha or La Regla de Lukumi, is an Afro-Caribbean religion that developed in Cuba among West African descendants. It combines beliefs and rituals of the Yoruba people with elements of Roman Catholicism, arising as enslaved Africans preserved their traditional religion by identifying their deities, the orishas, with Catholic saints.

Beliefs and practice

Practitioners, called santeros (priests) and santeras (priestesses), believe in a remote creator, Olodumare/Olorun, and in the orishas — spirits governing aspects of nature and human life, such as Yemaya (the sea), Shango (thunder), Oshun (rivers and love) and Obatala (creation and peace). Rituals include drumming ceremonies (toques de santo), divination systems such as Ifá and diloggún (cowrie-shell reading), initiations, and offerings including, in some rites, animal sacrifice — a practice upheld as constitutionally protected religious exercise by the U.S. Supreme Court in Church of the Lukumi Babalu Aye v. City of Hialeah (1993).

History

The religion coalesced in colonial Cuba as Yoruba people (called Lucumí) were brought as slaves from the early nineteenth century. After the Cuban Revolution of 1959 many practitioners emigrated to the United States and elsewhere, spreading Santería through the Caribbean diaspora. Interest in Afro-Cuban culture, and relaxed state attitudes in Cuba itself, have since made the tradition more publicly visible.
