Credit crunch

A credit crunch (also known as a credit squeeze, credit tightening or credit crisis) is a sudden reduction in the general availability of loans (or credit) or a sudden tightening of the conditions required to obtain a loan from banks.

A credit crunch generally involves a reduction in the availability of credit independent of any rise in official interest rates. Lenders become more risk-averse, demand more collateral, and cut credit lines, which can starve businesses and households of finance and deepen a recession. Credit crunches often follow a period of careless lending and rising asset prices, when losses and uncertainty about borrowers' solvency cause banks to rein in lending.

Examples

The most prominent modern example is the credit crunch of 2007–2008, triggered by losses on US subprime mortgages, which froze interbank lending markets and led to the global financial crisis. Earlier episodes include the US savings and loan crisis of the late 1980s and Japan's credit contraction in the 1990s.
