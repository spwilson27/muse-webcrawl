Satrap

A satrap was the governor of a province, or satrapy, in the ancient Iranian empires, above all the Achaemenid Persian Empire. The word derives, through Greek satrapēs, from Old Persian khshathrapāvan, "protector of the realm". The satrapal system allowed the King of Kings to administer a vast, multi-ethnic empire through regional governors who combined civil, fiscal, and often judicial authority.

Organisation

Under Darius I (r. 522–486 BC), the empire was organised into around twenty satrapies, each owing a fixed annual tribute recorded by Herodotus. Satraps were typically members of the Persian nobility or royal family, appointed by the king and serving at his pleasure. To check their power, the king appointed royal secretaries and military commanders independently of the satrap in many provinces, and itinerant inspectors known as the "king's eyes" reported on provincial affairs. Satraps maintained courts modelled on the royal court, collected taxes, administered justice, suppressed revolts, and raised troops for the royal army.

Later history and usage

Although powerful satraps occasionally rebelled — most notably in the Great Satraps' Revolt of the 370s–360s BC — the institution proved durable: Alexander the Great retained it after conquering Persia, and it was continued by the Hellenistic kingdoms, the Parthians, and the Sasanians. In modern political language, "satrap" is used pejoratively for a subordinate or client ruler who governs a territory on behalf of a greater power.
