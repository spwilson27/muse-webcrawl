Rigid designator

In the philosophy of language and modal logic, a rigid designator is a term that designates the same object in every possible world in which that object exists. The notion was introduced by Saul Kripke in his 1970 Princeton lectures, published as Naming and Necessity (1980).

A rigid designator picks out the same object in every possible world where the object exists.

Examples and contrasts

Proper names such as "Aristotle" are held to be rigid designators: they refer to Aristotle even in counterfactual situations where he never did any of the things described by associated descriptions. By contrast, definite descriptions such as "the teacher of Alexander the Great" are typically non-rigid (flaccid) designators, since in another possible world someone else might have taught Alexander. Kripke argued that mathematically, terms like "the square root of 9" are rigid, and that natural-kind terms such as "water" are rigid as well, designating the same substance (H2O) in every world.

Consequences

Rigid designation is central to Kripke's arguments for necessary a posteriori truths—for example, "Hesperus is Phosphorus"—and to the causal theory of reference against descriptivism. It also underpins debates about essentialism and the necessity of identity and origins.
