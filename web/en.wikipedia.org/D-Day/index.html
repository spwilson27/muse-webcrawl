D-Day

D-Day most commonly refers to 6 June 1944, the day of the Normandy landings in the Second World War, when Allied forces launched Operation Neptune, the seaborne assault phase of Operation Overlord, against German-occupied France. It was the largest seaborne invasion in history.

Meaning of the term

In military planning, "D-Day" is the day on which an operation begins; the "D" simply stands for "day". Planners also use H-Hour for the time a combat operation starts, and expressions such as D+3 for three days after the start. The most famous D-Day is that of Normandy, and the term has become attached to it in popular usage.

The Normandy landings

In the early hours of 6 June 1944, Allied airborne divisions landed by parachute and glider behind the beaches, followed by the largest amphibious assault ever mounted. Some 156,000 American, British, Canadian and other Allied troops landed on five beaches — Utah, Omaha, Gold, Juno and Sword — supported by nearly 7,000 vessels and more than 11,000 aircraft. The fortified "Atlantic Wall" defences were breached at heavy cost, most severely at Omaha Beach, but by nightfall the Allies had established a foothold in Normandy.

Aftermath

The landings opened the long-awaited second front in Western Europe. Allied forces broke out of Normandy later that summer, liberated Paris in August, and advanced into Germany, which surrendered in May 1945.
