Naturalization

Naturalization (or naturalisation) is the legal act or process by which a non-citizen of a country acquires the nationality of that country. Rules vary by state but typically involve a period of lawful residence, language and civic-knowledge requirements, good-character checks, and an oath or declaration of allegiance.

Requirements

Common conditions include minimum residence (for example, five years in the United States, with a shorter period for spouses of citizens), physical presence, English-language and civics tests in the US case, and renunciation or retention of prior citizenship depending on the laws of both states. Many countries naturalise through descent, marriage, military service, or exceptional achievement on accelerated terms.

History

The concept descends from Roman grants of citizenship and was systematised in the 19th and 20th centuries as nationality law was codified. Mass naturalisation ceremonies became a hallmark of settler societies such as the United States, Canada, and Australia, while many other states remain more restrictive.

Effects

Naturalised citizens generally gain full political rights, including voting and eligibility for office, though some constitutions reserve certain offices (such as the US presidency) to citizens by birth.
