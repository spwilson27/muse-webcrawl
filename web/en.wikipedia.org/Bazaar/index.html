Bazaar

A bazaar (from Persian bāzār) is a permanently enclosed marketplace or a network of streets lined with small shops and stalls where goods and services are exchanged or sold. The term originates in Persia and spread across the Islamic world, South Asia, and beyond. Historically, bazaars formed the commercial heart of cities along trade routes such as the Silk Road, combining retail and wholesale trade with workshops, caravanserais, mosques, and bathhouses, often arranged by trade — goldsmiths in one vaulted lane, carpet-sellers in another. Bargaining over prices is a traditional feature of bazaar commerce.

History

Markets existed in the ancient Near East from the earliest cities, but the covered bazaar as an institution matured under the Islamic caliphates and flourished under the Seljuks, Ottomans, Safavids, and Mughals. Istanbul's Grand Bazaar, begun in 1455 shortly after the Ottoman conquest, grew to thousands of shops under vaulted streets. Bazaars were frequently endowed through the waqf system, their rents supporting mosques and charitable foundations, and merchant guilds (and the market inspector, the muhtasib) regulated quality and conduct.

Organisation

Traditional bazaars cluster similar trades together, which aided comparison and reputation. Around them grew caravanserais for travelling merchants, warehouses (khans), and artisan workshops producing textiles, metalwork, carpets, ceramics, and jewellery. In modern usage "bazaar" also describes periodic charity sales and, by extension, any eclectic marketplace; in South Asia it simply means a market or market town quarter.
