Brainstem

SystemCentral nervous system

The brainstem is the stalk-like posterior part of the brain that connects the cerebrum with the spinal cord. It consists of three parts, from top to bottom: the midbrain, the pons, and the medulla oblongata. Despite comprising only a small fraction of the brain's mass, it is indispensable: nearly all information passing between the brain and body travels through it, and it houses the centres that keep the body alive.

Anatomy and functions

The medulla contains the cardiac, respiratory, and vasomotor centres that regulate heart rate, blood pressure, and breathing rhythm, as well as reflex centres for swallowing, coughing, sneezing, and vomiting. The pons ("bridge") links the cerebellum with the rest of the brain and helps regulate sleep and breathing. The midbrain contains structures involved in vision and hearing reflexes (the superior and inferior colliculi), eye movement, and motor pathways including the substantia nigra.

Ten of the twelve cranial nerves (III–XII) emerge from the brainstem, and its core contains the reticular formation, a network that regulates arousal and consciousness; damage to it can produce coma.

Clinical significance

Because so many vital functions are packed into a small area, brainstem strokes, haemorrhages, or tumours can be devastating or fatal. Extensive irreversible destruction of the brainstem forms the basis of brainstem death criteria used in many countries to determine legal death. Locked-in syndrome, classically from a pontine stroke, leaves a patient conscious but almost completely paralysed.
