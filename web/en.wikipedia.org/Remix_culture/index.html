Remix culture

Remix culture treats existing media as raw material for new work.

Remix culture, also known as read-write culture, is a term describing a culture that allows and encourages derivative works by combining or editing existing materials to produce new creative works. By default, a remix culture allows efforts to improve upon, change, integrate, or otherwise remix the work of other creators. The concept was popularised by law professor Lawrence Lessig, who contrasted the "read/write" culture of participatory creation with the "read-only" culture of passive media consumption.

Lessig argued in his book Remix (2008) that human culture has always developed through remixing — folk tales, music, and literature were continually retold and reworked — but that twentieth-century copyright law and one-way mass media narrowed the space for amateur creativity. Digital technologies, he argued, restore the possibility of read/write culture, as ordinary users can edit video, music, and text with the same tools professionals use.

Manifestations

Remix culture encompasses music remixes and mashups, fan fiction, fan films, internet memes, anime music videos, software mods, and the re-use of material under free-culture licences such as those of Creative Commons. Platforms for user-generated content have made remixing a mainstream mode of expression, while raising continuing disputes over attribution, commercial exploitation, and the boundaries of fair use.

Criticism

Critics have questioned whether remixing constitutes genuine creativity, argued that it can devalue original authorship, and warned that commercial platforms profit from unpaid creative labour. Others defend remix as a form of literacy essential to participation in contemporary culture.
