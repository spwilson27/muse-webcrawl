Kennedy–Thorndike experiment

The Kennedy–Thorndike apparatus used an interferometer with arms of deliberately unequal length.

The Kennedy–Thorndike experiment, first performed in 1932 by Roy J. Kennedy and Edward M. Thorndike, is a modified form of the Michelson–Morley experiment that tests the foundations of special relativity. Where the Michelson–Morley experiment tests the isotropy of the speed of light with respect to the orientation of the apparatus, the Kennedy–Thorndike experiment tests its independence from the velocity of the apparatus.

Description

Kennedy and Thorndike used an interferometer whose two arms differed in length by about 16 cm. Unlike the Michelson–Morley arrangement, in which the arms are equal, the unequal arms make the interference pattern sensitive to any change in the laboratory's velocity relative to a hypothetical preferred frame as the Earth rotates and revolves. The apparatus was mounted in a sealed, temperature-controlled vacuum chamber and observed over many months.

Results and significance

No fringe shift corresponding to a change in the Earth's velocity was observed, apart from a very small residual effect the experimenters attributed to imperfections. The null result, combined with the Michelson–Morley result and the later Ives–Stilwell experiment, forms a classic triad of tests of special relativity: together they empirically establish Lorentz invariance — length contraction, time dilation and the absence of a detectable preferred frame. Modern repetitions of the experiment using cryogenic optical resonators and laser frequency standards have tightened the limits on any violation by many orders of magnitude.
