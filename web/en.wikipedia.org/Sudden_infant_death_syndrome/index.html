Sudden infant death syndrome

Sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS), also known as cot death or crib death, is the sudden unexplained death of a child of less than one year of age. Diagnosis requires that the death remain unexplained after a thorough investigation, including autopsy and review of the death scene and clinical history. SIDS typically occurs during sleep, most often between two and four months of age.

Risk factors and the triple-risk model

The leading explanatory framework is the triple-risk model: SIDS strikes when a vulnerable infant (for example, one with subtle brainstem abnormalities affecting arousal and breathing) passes through a critical developmental period while exposed to an external stressor such as sleeping face-down, overheating, or exposure to tobacco smoke. Established risk factors include prone (stomach) sleeping, soft bedding, bed-sharing, maternal smoking during pregnancy, and prematurity; breastfeeding and pacifier use appear protective.

Prevention

Public health campaigns beginning in the early 1990s — notably the "Back to Sleep" campaign (now "Safe to Sleep") promoting supine sleeping on a firm, bare surface — were followed by declines of more than half in SIDS rates in many countries. Despite this, SIDS remains a leading cause of post-neonatal infant mortality in developed countries, with roughly 1,400 deaths annually in the United States.
