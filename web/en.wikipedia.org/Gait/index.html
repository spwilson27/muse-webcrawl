Gait

Gait is the pattern of movement of the limbs of animals, including humans, during locomotion over a solid substrate. Most animals use a variety of gaits, selecting gait based on speed, terrain, the need to maneuver, and energetic efficiency. Different animal species naturally use different proportions of gaits because of anatomical differences.

Classification

Gaits are typically categorized as either natural or trained. Natural gaits include walking, running, trotting, pacing, cantering, and galloping in quadrupeds. A gait is defined by the sequence of footfalls and the proportion of the cycle during which each foot is in contact with the ground (the duty factor). Gaits with a period in which all feet are off the ground are classified as running gaits.

Human gait

Human gait is bipedal and biphasic: each cycle consists of a stance phase (about 60% of the cycle) and a swing phase (about 40%). The gait cycle begins and ends with the heel strike of the same foot. Analysis of gait is used in medicine to diagnose neurological and musculoskeletal disorders, in biomechanics and sports science, and in gait analysis for identification, since an individual's walking pattern is partly distinctive.

Horse gaits

Horses naturally perform the walk (four-beat), trot (two-beat diagonal), canter (three-beat), and gallop (four-beat). Certain breeds perform additional "ambling" gaits such as the pace, running walk, and tölt. The study of animal locomotion was advanced by Eadweard Muybridge's photographic sequences of the 1870s, which settled the question of whether all four hooves leave the ground during a gallop.
