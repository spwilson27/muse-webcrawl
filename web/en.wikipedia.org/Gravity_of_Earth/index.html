The gravity of Earth, denoted g, is the acceleration imparted to objects by Earth's gravitational attraction combined with the centrifugal effect of its rotation. At the surface it averages about 9.8 m/s². The conventional standard value, gn = 9.80665 m/s² exactly, was adopted in 1901 for use in defining units such as the kilogram-force and the standard atmosphere.

Variation over the surface

Gravity is not constant over Earth. It is stronger at the poles (≈ 9.832 m/s²) than at the equator (≈ 9.780 m/s²), a difference of about 0.5%, because the equatorial bulge places surface points farther from the centre and because rotation produces an outward centrifugal acceleration at the equator. Gravity decreases with altitude (by roughly 0.3% at the height of Everest's summit region, relative to sea level) and varies locally with the density of underlying rock; these small variations, called gravity anomalies, are measured with gravimeters and used in geophysics and geodesy.

Modelling

The International Gravity Formula and the normal gravity of reference ellipsoids (such as GRS80) give the smooth latitude- and height-dependent component of gravity, while the geoid represents the equipotential surface that mean sea level would follow. Newton's law of universal gravitation, refined by general relativity, provides the theoretical framework; for most purposes g ≈ GM/r², with Earth's mass M and radius r.

Comparison

Earth's surface gravity defines the unit "gee" used for accelerations in aviation and spaceflight. It is about 2.6 times the gravity of Mars and about one sixth that experienced at the surface of Jupiter relative to its visible cloud tops.
