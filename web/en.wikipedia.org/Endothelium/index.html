Endothelium

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Endothelium
SystemCardiovascular, lymphatic
Cell typeSimple squamous epithelium (endothelial cells)
LocationInner lining of blood vessels, lymphatic vessels and heart chambers

The endothelium is a single layer of squamous endothelial cells that lines the interior surface of blood vessels, lymphatic vessels, and the heart (where it is continuous with the endocardium). It forms the interface between circulating blood or lymph and the vessel wall, and with a total surface area of several thousand square metres in an adult human, it functions as a vast, distributed organ system.

Functions

The endothelium regulates vascular tone by releasing vasodilators such as nitric oxide and prostacyclin, and vasoconstrictors such as endothelin. It controls the passage of materials and white blood cells into and out of tissues, maintains a non-thrombogenic surface that balances clotting and fibrinolysis, modulates inflammation, and drives the formation of new blood vessels (angiogenesis). It also participates in blood pressure regulation and presents antigens in some vascular beds.

Structure

Endothelial cells are held together by tight and adherens junctions and sit on a basement membrane. Their structure varies across organs: continuous endothelium in most tissues, fenestrated endothelium in the kidneys and endocrine glands (with pores allowing rapid exchange), and discontinuous (sinusoidal) endothelium in the liver, spleen and bone marrow.

Dysfunction and disease

Endothelial dysfunction — a state of impaired vasodilation and a pro-inflammatory, pro-thrombotic surface — is an early event in atherosclerosis and is associated with hypertension, diabetes, smoking and hypercholesterolaemia. Damage to the endothelium contributes to sepsis, thrombosis and the vascular complications of many diseases.
