Politics of Germany

The politics of Germany takes place within a framework of a federal, parliamentary, representative democratic republic, where the Chancellor is the head of government, and of a pluriform multi-party system. The President is the head of state with largely ceremonial functions. Executive power is exercised by the government, legislative power is vested in both the government and the two chambers of parliament, the Bundestag and the Bundesrat, and the judiciary is independent of the executive and the legislature.

The political system is laid out in the 1949 constitution, the Grundgesetz (Basic Law), which remained in effect with minor amendments after German reunification in 1990.

Constitution

The Basic Law guarantees fundamental rights, establishes Germany as a democratic and social federal state, and entrenches human dignity as inviolable. Because of the Basic Law's "eternity clause", the principles of democracy, federalism and the rule of law cannot be abolished even by constitutional amendment. The Federal Constitutional Court (Bundesverfassungsgericht) in Karlsruhe guards the constitution and can strike down legislation.

Executive

The Chancellor is elected by the Bundestag on the proposal of the President and is the most powerful political figure, setting policy guidelines (Richtlinienkompetenz) and appointing federal ministers. The Bundestag can remove a Chancellor only by simultaneously electing a successor — the constructive vote of no confidence — a stability mechanism designed in reaction to the Weimar Republic's instability.

Legislature

The Bundestag, elected for four years by a mixed-member proportional system combining constituency seats with party lists, is the directly elected chamber. The Bundesrat consists of delegates of the sixteen Länder (federal states) governments and must approve laws affecting state competences, including most tax and administrative legislation.

Political parties

Dominant parties since 1949 have included the Christian Democratic Union (CDU) and its Bavarian sister party the CSU, the Social Democratic Party (SPD), the Free Democratic Party (FDP), Alliance 90/The Greens, The Left, and, since 2013, the Alternative for Germany (AfD). Parties receiving less than five percent of the vote are normally excluded from the Bundestag. Because no party usually wins an outright majority, Germany is governed by coalition governments.

Federalism

The sixteen Länder retain substantial powers, notably over education, policing and culture, and administer most federal law. Germany's federal structure was designed to disperse power in reaction to the centralised Nazi state.
