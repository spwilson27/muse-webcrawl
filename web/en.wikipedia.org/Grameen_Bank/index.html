Grameen Bank (Bengali: গ্রামীণ ব্যাংক, "rural bank") is a microfinance organisation and community development bank founded in Bangladesh by Muhammad Yunus. It makes small loans—known as microcredit—to poor people without requiring collateral, based on the principle that the poor have underused skills and that even tiny amounts of credit can help them start income-generating activities.

History

Yunus, an economics professor at Chittagong University, began the project in 1976 in the village of Jobra, lending US$27 of his own money to 42 women who made bamboo stools. The experiment expanded with support from Bangladesh's central bank, and in October 1983 the project was transformed by government ordinance into an independent bank. The vast majority of its borrowers—over 95 percent—are women, and the borrowers themselves own most of the bank's shares.

Method

Grameen's model organises borrowers into groups of five, whose members support and monitor one another, though loans are individual and not formally jointly guaranteed. Borrowers commit to the "Sixteen Decisions," a social charter covering practices from building sanitary latrines to educating children. Repayment rates have historically been reported above 95 percent, though the bank suffered difficulties after the 1998 floods and restructured its system ("Grameen II") with more flexible products.

Recognition and influence

In 2006, Grameen Bank and Yunus jointly received the Nobel Peace Prize "for their efforts to create economic and social development from below." The model inspired microfinance institutions worldwide. It has also attracted criticism—over interest rates, borrower indebtedness, and the evidence for microcredit's poverty impact—and Yunus was removed as managing director by Bangladesh Bank in 2011 amid tensions with the government, a decision he contested.
