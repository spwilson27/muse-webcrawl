Wild Weasel

Wild Weasel is a code name given by the United States Air Force to an aircraft, of any type, equipped with anti-radiation missiles and tasked with the suppression of enemy air defenses (SEAD): destroying the radar and surface-to-air missile (SAM) installations of enemy air defense systems. The task of a Wild Weasel aircraft is to bait enemy anti-aircraft defenses into targeting it with their radars, whereupon the radar waves are traced back to their source, allowing the Weasel or its teammates to precisely target it for destruction.

History

The Wild Weasel concept was developed by the United States Air Force in 1965, during the Vietnam War, after the introduction of Soviet SAM missiles and their downing of American strike aircraft participating in Operation Rolling Thunder. The program was headed by General Kenneth Dempster. The first Wild Weasel aircraft were F-100 Super Sabres, which flew their first missions in November 1965.

The modified two-seat F-105F and F-105G Thunderchiefs, and the F-4 Phantom II, soon became the standard Wild Weasel platforms. Wild Weasel crews developed tactics in which they would deliberately fly ahead of strike packages to draw fire and locate SAM sites, a role encapsulated in the unofficial motto "First in, Last out" and the sardonic crew patch abbreviation YGBSM ("You Gotta Be Shittin' Me"), said to be the reaction of the first electronic warfare officer assigned to the duty.

Equipment and tactics

Wild Weasel aircraft carried radar homing and warning receivers that detected enemy radar emissions, together with anti-radiation missiles such as the AGM-45 Shrike and the later AGM-78 Standard ARM and AGM-88 HARM, which home in on radar signals. The electronic warfare officer in the rear seat operated the detection equipment and identified threat systems while the pilot flew the aircraft.

Later aircraft

After Vietnam, the F-4G Phantom II "Wild Weasel V" became the dedicated platform, serving through the Gulf War of 1991. It was replaced by specially configured F-16 Fighting Falcons, which continue the SEAD mission with HARM missiles and targeting pods. The mission remains a central element of modern air campaigns, in which suppression of air defenses precedes and protects strike operations.
