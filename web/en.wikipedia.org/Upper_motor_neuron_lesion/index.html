Upper motor neuron lesion

An upper motor neuron lesion (also known as a pyramidal insufficiency) is a lesion of the neural pathway above the anterior horn cell of the spinal cord or the motor nuclei of the cranial nerves — that is, damage to the upper motor neurons of the corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts, which descend from the motor cortex to synapse on lower motor neurons. Such lesions produce a characteristic pattern of weakness and altered reflexes distinct from lower motor neuron lesions.

The corticospinal tract carries upper motor neurons from the cortex to the spinal cord.

Signs and symptoms

The classic features of an upper motor neuron lesion include:

  • Weakness in a "pyramidal" distribution — in the upper limbs the extensors are weaker than the flexors, while in the lower limbs the flexors are weaker than the extensors.
  • Spasticity — velocity-dependent increased muscle tone, often with a "clasp-knife" quality.
  • Hyperreflexia — brisk deep tendon reflexes, sometimes with clonus.
  • Extensor plantar response (Babinski sign) — upward movement of the great toe on stroking the sole.
  • Loss of fine, skilled voluntary movement, with relatively preserved bulk — there is little or no muscle wasting and no fasciculations.

Immediately after an acute lesion, such as a stroke or spinal cord injury, there may be an initial period of flaccidity and absent reflexes ("cerebral shock" or spinal shock) before spasticity develops over days to weeks.

Causes

Common causes include stroke and intracerebral haemorrhage, multiple sclerosis, traumatic brain and spinal cord injury, cerebral palsy, tumours compressing the motor pathways, and hereditary spastic paraplegia. In amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (motor neuron disease), upper and lower motor neuron signs coexist in the same patient. Lesions of the corticobulbar tract may additionally produce pseudobulbar palsy, with dysarthria, dysphagia and emotional lability.

Anatomy

Upper motor neurons originate chiefly in layer V of the primary motor cortex (Betz cells) and premotor areas. Their axons pass through the corona radiata, the posterior limb of the internal capsule, the cerebral peduncle, the pons and the medullary pyramids, where most fibres decussate to form the lateral corticospinal tract; a minority continue in the anterior corticospinal tract. Damage anywhere along this pathway produces upper motor neuron signs below the level of the lesion — on the opposite side of the body for lesions above the pyramidal decussation, and on the same side for lesions in the spinal cord below it.
