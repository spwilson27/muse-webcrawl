Neoconservatism

Neoconservatism is a political movement that began among a group of mostly liberal ("hawkish") American intellectuals in the late 1960s and 1970s who became disenchanted with the Democratic Party's domestic and foreign policies. Characterized by an assertive, interventionist foreign policy aimed at promoting democracy abroad, skepticism toward international institutions, free-market economics combined with acceptance of a moderated welfare state, and a moralistic defense of Western values, neoconservatism became especially influential in the Republican Party during the presidency of George W. Bush.

Origins

Key early figures included Irving Kristol — often called the "godfather of neoconservatism" — Norman Podhoretz, Daniel Patrick Moynihan, and Jeane Kirkpatrick, many of them former liberals or even Trotskyists who "were mugged by reality," in Kristol's phrase. Journals such as Commentary and The Public Interest articulated critiques of Great Society programs and détente. Kirkpatrick's 1979 essay "Dictatorships and Double Standards" influenced the Reagan administration's anti-communist stance.

Influence and the Iraq War

Neoconservatives advocated a foreign policy of American primacy; the Project for the New American Century (1997) promoted this agenda. Following the September 11 attacks, neoconservative officials and intellectuals were leading proponents of the 2003 invasion of Iraq and the "Bush Doctrine" of preventive war and democracy promotion. The war's difficult aftermath diminished the movement's standing, and under Donald Trump the Republican Party moved toward a more nationalist, non-interventionist orientation, though neoconservative ideas persist in debates over China, Russia, and support for Ukraine and Israel.

Criticism

Critics across the spectrum have accused neoconservatism of reckless militarism, naïve faith in democratization, and disregard for international law; supporters counter that it defends liberal democracy against authoritarian and Islamist threats. The term is often used pejoratively and its boundaries are contested.
