35 mm film is a film gauge used in filmmaking and still photography. It was created in 1892 by William Dickson and Thomas Edison, who split 70 mm film lengthwise and added perforations. For much of the 20th century it was the dominant format in both the cinema and the amateur and professional still-photography markets.

History

Edison's laboratory standardized the 35 mm width with four pairs of sprocket holes per frame for motion pictures. In still photography, Oskar Barnack at Leica adapted the format in the 1910s, doubling the cinema frame to 24 × 36 mm; the Leica I of 1925 popularized the "miniature" camera. Kodak's 135 cartridge of 1934 made loading simple and cemented the format's dominance until digital photography displaced it in the 2000s.

Motion pictures

In cinema, 35 mm runs vertically through the camera with a standard four-perforation pulldown. Aspect ratios evolved from the Academy ratio of 1.375:1 to widescreen formats achieved with anamorphic lenses or by cropping (Super 35). A soundtrack area sits between the perforations and the image.

Still photography

135 film is supplied in cartridges of 12, 24, or 36 exposures. The 36 × 24 mm frame ("full frame") survives as the reference sensor size in digital photography. Half-frame cameras exposed a 24 × 18 mm frame, doubling capacity.
