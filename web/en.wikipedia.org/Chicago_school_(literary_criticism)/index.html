Chicago school (literary criticism)

The Chicago School of literary criticism is a form of criticism of English literature begun at the University of Chicago in the 1930s and continuing into the 1950s. It is sometimes called Neo-Aristotelianism, after its foundational reliance on the literary theory of Aristotle, especially the Poetics.

Approach

The Chicago critics — among them Ronald S. Crane, Elder Olson, Wayne C. Booth and Norman Maclean — emphasised the analysis of literary works as constructed wholes, attending to plot as the organising principle of a work and to genre as an essential guide to interpretation. They were pluralists, holding that several legitimate critical methods exist, and they reacted against the close-reading monism of the New Criticism. Booth's The Rhetoric of Fiction (1961), with its concepts of the implied author and unreliable narration, is the school's best-known later product.
