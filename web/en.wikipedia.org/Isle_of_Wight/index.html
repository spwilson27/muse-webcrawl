The Isle of Wight is an island and ceremonial county off the south coast of England, in the English Channel, separated from Hampshire by the Solent. It is the largest and second most populous island in England, with Newport as its county town and Ryde its largest town. It has been a popular holiday destination since Victorian times.

Geography

The diamond-shaped island measures about 37 km east to west and 21 km north to south. A ridge of chalk downs runs across its centre, reaching 241 metres at St Boniface Down, and ends in the west at the famous chalk stacks of the Needles. About half the island is designated an Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty. Its mild climate and varied coastline make it noted for sailing — Cowes hosts the world-famous Cowes Week regatta.

History

The island was inhabited in the Palaeolithic and was part of Roman Britain, with villas at Brading and Newport. It was conquered by the Saxon king Cædwalla in the 680s and by the Normans after 1066; Carisbrooke Castle later held Charles I before his execution. Queen Victoria built Osborne House near Cowes as her summer residence and died there in 1901. The Isle of Wight Festival, first held in 1968–70 and revived in 2002, drew one of the largest rock audiences of its era in 1970.

Transport and economy

Ferries and hovercraft link the island to Portsmouth, Southampton and Lymington. Tourism, marine industries and agriculture dominate the economy. The island returns one Member of Parliament (two from 2024) and, unusually for England, its county council covers the whole island as a unitary authority.
