Big Five personality traits

The Big Five personality traits, sometimes known as the five-factor model (FFM), are a widely used taxonomy of human personality. The model describes personality in terms of five broad dimensions: openness to experience, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness, and neuroticism — often remembered by the acronyms OCEAN or CANOE. Each dimension represents a spectrum along which individuals vary continuously, rather than a set of discrete types.

History

The model grew out of the lexical hypothesis, the idea that the most socially relevant personality differences become encoded in everyday language. Beginning with the work of Gordon Allport and Henry Odbert in the 1930s, who catalogued thousands of trait-descriptive words, researchers such as Raymond Cattell reduced the list using factor analysis. Cattell's sixteen-factor model was later shown by Donald Fiske, and subsequently by Ernest Tupes and Raymond Christal in 1961, to contain five recurrent factors. Lewis Goldberg popularised the "Big Five" label in the 1980s, and Paul Costa and Robert McCrae developed the NEO inventories that made the model a standard research instrument.

The five dimensions

Openness to experience

Openness reflects imagination, aesthetic sensitivity, intellectual curiosity, and a preference for variety. High scorers tend to be creative and unconventional; low scorers are more pragmatic, conventional, and data-driven.

Conscientiousness

Conscientiousness describes self-discipline, dutifulness, and goal-directed behaviour. It is the Big Five trait most consistently associated with academic and occupational performance.

Extraversion

Extraversion involves sociability, assertiveness, positive emotionality, and energy level. Introverts, at the low end, prefer quieter and less stimulating environments.

Agreeableness

Agreeableness captures tendencies toward compassion, politeness, trust, and cooperation rather than antagonism.

Neuroticism

Neuroticism is the tendency to experience negative emotions such as anxiety, anger, and depression. Its opposite pole is emotional stability.

Measurement

The traits are most commonly measured with self-report questionnaires such as the NEO-PI-R and its shorter public-domain counterpart, the International Personality Item Pool (IPIP). Each broad factor is subdivided into six narrower facets; the NEO model, for example, divides neuroticism into anxiety, angry hostility, depression, self-consciousness, impulsiveness, and vulnerability.

Applications and criticism

The model is used in clinical psychology, organisational behaviour, and behavioural genetics, where twin studies typically estimate the heritability of the traits at roughly 40–60%. Critics note that the five factors may not capture all of personality (the HEXACO model adds an honesty–humility dimension), that factor structures replicate imperfectly across cultures and languages, and that broad trait scores explain behaviour less well in specific situations than trait theorists originally hoped.
