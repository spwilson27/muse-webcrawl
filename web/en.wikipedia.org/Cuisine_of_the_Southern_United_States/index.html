Cuisine of the Southern United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The cuisine of the Southern United States comprises the foodways of the American South, a tradition shaped by Indigenous, West African, British, French, Spanish and Caribbean influences. It ranges from the Lowcountry cooking of the Carolina and Georgia coasts to Creole and Cajun Louisiana, Appalachian mountain food, Tex-Mex border cooking and the barbecue traditions of the interior South.

History and influences

Native American staples — corn, beans, squash, and pork from introduced hogs — formed the base. Enslaved West Africans brought okra, black-eyed peas, yams, peanuts and techniques of slow cooking and one-pot meals that became central to "soul food". British and Scots-Irish settlers contributed frying, baking and cured meats; French and Spanish rule left marks on Louisiana's Creole cuisine, while the Acadian exiles created Cajun cooking in the bayous.

Characteristic dishes and ingredients

Signature dishes include fried chicken, pulled-pork and brisket barbecue, shrimp and grits, gumbo and jambalaya, country ham, biscuits and gravy, cornbread, hushpuppies, collard and turnip greens, fried green tomatoes, pecan pie and sweet tea. Cornmeal, rice, pork, chicken, catfish and greens recur across the region.

Regional varieties

Lowcountry, Creole, Cajun, Appalachian, Floribbean and Texan cooking are recognised sub-traditions. Barbecue itself is fiercely regional: whole-hog with vinegar sauce in eastern North Carolina, tomato-based sauces in the west of the state, white sauce in northern Alabama, dry-rubbed ribs in Memphis and beef brisket in central Texas.
