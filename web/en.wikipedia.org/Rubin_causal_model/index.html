Rubin causal model

The Rubin causal model (RCM), also known as the potential outcomes framework, is a framework for causal inference in statistics developed principally by Donald Rubin, building on earlier work by Jerzy Neyman (1923) on randomised experiments. It defines a causal effect as the comparison between the outcomes a unit would experience under treatment and under control — two potential outcomes, of which only one can ever be observed. This is sometimes called the fundamental problem of causal inference.

Framework

For each unit i and a binary treatment, the framework posits potential outcomes Yi(1) (if treated) and Yi(0) (if not treated). The individual causal effect is their difference, which is unobservable because a unit receives only one treatment condition. Research therefore targets averages such as the average treatment effect (ATE) over a population.

Key assumptions that make estimation possible include the stable unit treatment value assumption (SUTVA) — no interference between units and no hidden versions of treatment — and, in observational studies, ignorability: treatment assignment is independent of potential outcomes conditional on observed covariates. Rubin and collaborators developed tools that rest on the framework, notably the propensity score (with Paul Rosenbaum), matching, and Bayesian approaches to missing data, since the unobserved potential outcome can be viewed as a missing-data problem.

Influence

The Rubin causal model underpins much modern practice in econometrics, epidemiology, political science and medicine, and stands alongside Judea Pearl's structural causal models and graphical approaches as one of the two dominant frameworks for causal reasoning. Extensions address mediation, dynamic treatment regimes, and interference between units.
