Gene duplication

Gene duplication is any process by which a region of DNA containing a gene is copied, producing an extra copy of that gene. It is regarded as a major engine of evolution, because duplicated genes are freed from selective constraint and can accumulate mutations that yield new functions.

Mechanisms

Duplications arise through unequal crossing-over during meiosis, retrotransposition of messenger RNA, replication slippage, and whole-genome duplication (polyploidy), which has occurred in the ancestry of plants, yeasts, and vertebrates — the "2R hypothesis" proposes two rounds of whole-genome duplication early in vertebrate evolution.

Fates of duplicated genes

Most duplicates are lost (pseudogenisation) when one copy accumulates disabling mutations. Some are retained by subfunctionalisation, in which each copy performs part of the ancestral function, or neofunctionalisation, in which one copy evolves a genuinely new function — Susumu Ohno's classic 1970 argument in Evolution by Gene Duplication. Duplicates may also be retained for dosage reasons. Families of related genes, such as the globin genes and the Hox genes, are products of successive duplications.

Role in disease

Copy-number changes from duplication (and reciprocal deletion) underlie genomic disorders; for example, duplication of PMP22 causes Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease type 1A, and amplification of oncogenes such as HER2 occurs in cancers.
