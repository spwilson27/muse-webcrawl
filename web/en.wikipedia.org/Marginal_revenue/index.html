Marginal revenue

In microeconomics, marginal revenue (MR) is the additional total revenue generated by increasing product sales by one unit. It equals the change in total revenue divided by the change in quantity sold, or, in calculus terms, the first derivative of the total revenue function with respect to quantity.

Marginal revenue and market structure

Under perfect competition a firm is a price taker, so each additional unit sells at the market price and marginal revenue equals price (and average revenue). For a firm with market power facing a downward-sloping demand curve, selling an extra unit requires lowering the price on all units sold, so marginal revenue lies below the price and below the demand (average revenue) curve. With a linear demand curve, the marginal revenue curve has the same price intercept and twice the slope.

Profit maximisation

A profit-maximising firm produces the quantity at which marginal revenue equals marginal cost (MR = MC), provided price covers average variable cost. If marginal revenue exceeds marginal cost, expanding output raises profit; if it is less, contracting output raises profit. Marginal revenue can become negative when the price cut needed to sell more outweighs the added sales — the inelastic portion of the demand curve, where a monopolist never willingly operates.

Relationship to elasticity

Marginal revenue is linked to the price elasticity of demand ε by the formula MR = P(1 + 1/ε). When demand is elastic (ε < −1), marginal revenue is positive; when demand is unit elastic, marginal revenue is zero and total revenue is maximised; when demand is inelastic, marginal revenue is negative.
