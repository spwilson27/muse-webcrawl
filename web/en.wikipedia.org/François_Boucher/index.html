François Boucher (29 September 1703 – 30 May 1770) was a French painter, draughtsman, and engraver, the most celebrated exponent of the Rococo style. His idyllic mythological scenes, pastorals, and portraits epitomized the taste of the court of Louis XV.

Career

Boucher won the Prix de Rome in 1723 and spent four years in Italy. Back in Paris he was admitted to the Royal Academy in 1734 with Rinaldo and Armida. Winning the patronage of Madame de Pompadour, the king's mistress, he became the most fashionable artist in France: he was appointed First Painter to the King (Premier peintre du Roi) in 1765 and director of the Academy.

Work

Boucher produced an enormous oeuvre — paintings, tapestry cartoons for the Gobelins and Beauvais manufactories, stage designs for the Opéra, porcelain designs, and book illustrations. Famous works include The Triumph of Venus (1740), Diana Leaving the Bath (1742), and the portrait of Madame de Pompadour (1756). His art is marked by luminous flesh tones, feathery brushwork, and playful eroticism.

Criticism and reputation

Enlightenment critics such as Denis Diderot attacked Boucher's art as frivolous and immoral, and his reputation fell with the rise of Neoclassicism. He was rehabilitated in the nineteenth century by the Goncourt brothers, and today he is recognized as a defining artist of eighteenth-century French taste.
