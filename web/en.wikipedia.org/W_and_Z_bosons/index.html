W and Z bosons

The W and Z bosons are elementary particles that mediate the weak nuclear force, one of the four fundamental interactions. The W bosons are electrically charged (W+ and W−, mass about 80.4 GeV/c²), while the Z boson is neutral (mass about 91.2 GeV/c²). Their great masses — roughly 80–90 times that of the proton — give the weak force its extremely short range.

Role in the Standard Model

In the electroweak theory of Glashow, Salam, and Weinberg, the W and Z arise from the gauge symmetry SU(2)×U(1), acquiring mass through the Higgs mechanism. The W boson changes the flavour of quarks and leptons: in beta decay, a down quark converts to an up quark by emitting a virtual W−, which then decays to an electron and an antineutrino. Neutral-current interactions mediated by the Z — predicted before they were observed at CERN in 1973 (the Gargamelle experiments) — allow neutrino scattering without charge exchange.

Discovery

The W and Z were directly discovered in 1983 at CERN's Super Proton Synchrotron, converted into a proton–antiproton collider, by the UA1 and UA2 experiments led respectively by Carlo Rubbia and Pierre Darriulat. Rubbia and engineer Simon van der Meer received the 1984 Nobel Prize in Physics. Later, the LEP collider at CERN measured the Z mass and width with extraordinary precision, counting the number of light neutrino species (three) and testing the Standard Model to high accuracy.

Properties

Both bosons are spin-1 vector bosons with very short lifetimes (~3×10−25 s). The Z decays into pairs of fermions and antifermions, with about 20% of decays to each charged-lepton family, roughly 70% to quarks, and about 20% "invisible" decays to neutrinos. Precision measurements of the W mass are an active test of the Standard Model; a 2022 measurement by the CDF collaboration at Fermilab reported a value in tension with the Standard Model prediction, prompting intense scrutiny.
