Iron Curtain

The Iron Curtain was the political, military, and ideological barrier erected by the Soviet Union after World War II to seal itself and its dependent Eastern and Central European allies off from open contact with the West and other non-communist areas. The term symbolized the efforts by the Soviet Union to block itself and its satellite states from open contact with the West, its allies, and neutral states.

Origins of the term

The phrase had been used earlier — notably by Ethel Snowden in 1920 and by Joseph Goebbels in 1945 — but it was popularized by former British prime minister Winston Churchill in his "Sinews of Peace" address at Westminster College in Fulton, Missouri, on 5 March 1946: "From Stettin in the Baltic to Trieste in the Adriatic, an iron curtain has descended across the Continent."

The physical barrier

On the ground, the Iron Curtain took the form of border defenses between the countries of Western and Eastern Europe, most notably the inner German border and the Berlin Wall. These defenses included minefields, watchtowers, barbed wire, and heavily patrolled "death strips" designed to prevent migration from East to West.

Political division

To the east of the curtain lay the Soviet Union and the states of the Warsaw Pact; to the west, the members of NATO and neutral states such as Austria, Switzerland, Sweden, and Finland. Yugoslavia, though communist, remained outside the Soviet bloc after the Tito–Stalin split of 1948, and Albania later aligned with China.

Fall

The curtain began to lift in 1989, when Hungary dismantled its border fence with Austria and opened a border crossing, prompting an exodus of East Germans. The fall of the Berlin Wall on 9 November 1989 and the revolutions of 1989 ended the division of Europe; the Cold War formally concluded with the dissolution of the Soviet Union in December 1991.
