Swift v. Tyson, 41 U.S. (16 Pet.) 1 (1842), was a United States Supreme Court decision in which Justice Joseph Story held that federal courts exercising diversity jurisdiction need not follow state court interpretations of general commercial common law, but could instead apply an independent body of federal general common law.

Background

The case arose from a disputed bill of exchange connected to a Maine land transaction tainted by fraud. Under New York precedent, the holder's claim would have failed for want of consideration; Story's opinion instead treated the question as one of general commercial law, on which federal courts could exercise independent judgment under section 34 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 (the Rules of Decision Act), which he read as covering only state statutes and strictly local matters.

Doctrine and consequences

For nearly a century Swift sustained a parallel federal common law, encouraging forum shopping: litigants could obtain more favourable law by suing in federal court. The Southern Pacific Company's tax litigation and cases such as Black & White Taxicab Co. v. Brown & Yellow Taxicab Co. illustrated the manipulation the doctrine invited.

Overruling

In Erie Railroad Co. v. Tompkins (1938), Justice Brandeis declared Swift unconstitutional, holding that "there is no federal general common law" and that federal courts must apply the law of the states, including their common law, except where the federal Constitution or statutes govern.
