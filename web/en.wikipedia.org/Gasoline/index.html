Gasoline (North American English) or petrol (Commonwealth English) is a petrochemical product characterised as a transparent, petroleum-derived flammable liquid consisting primarily of organic compounds obtained by the fractional distillation of petroleum, enhanced with a variety of additives. It is used mostly as a fuel in internal combustion engines; it is one of the primary fuels consumed globally and a major product of the petroleum industry.

Composition and production

Gasoline is a mixture of hydrocarbons containing roughly 4 to 12 carbon atoms per molecule — including paraffins, naphthenes and aromatics such as benzene, toluene and xylene. Refineries produce gasoline blendstocks through distillation, catalytic cracking, reforming and alkylation, then blend them with additives: detergents, antioxidants, and oxygenates such as ethanol. The octane rating measures the fuel's resistance to engine knock; historically tetraethyl lead was used for this purpose, but leaded gasoline was phased out worldwide (completed globally in 2021) because of lead's toxicity.

Use and impact

Gasoline powers most light-duty vehicles worldwide, as well as motorcycles, small aircraft (in the form of avgas), boats and garden equipment. Burning gasoline produces about 2.3 kg of carbon dioxide per litre, making road transport a major source of greenhouse gas emissions; engines also emit nitrogen oxides, carbon monoxide and particulates, controlled in modern vehicles by catalytic converters and fuel standards. Many jurisdictions tax gasoline heavily and are phasing it out in favour of electric vehicles.

History

Petroleum fractions were burned in lamps for centuries, but gasoline became commercially important only with the internal combustion engine in the late 19th century. The Burton thermal cracking process (1913) greatly increased yields from crude oil, and the mid-20th century saw the spread of the service station and the high-compression engine.
