Trouvère

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A trouvère (French pronunciation: [tʁuvɛʁ]) was a poet-composer of northern France during the High Middle Ages, writing lyric verse in the langue d'oïl (Old French). The trouvères were the northern counterparts of the troubadours of southern France, who wrote in Occitan.

History and themes

The tradition flourished from the late twelfth to the end of the thirteenth century. Following the troubadour example, trouvères composed principally the grand chant courtois — refined songs of courtly love — but their repertoire was broader, including crusade songs, dawn songs (aubes), pastourelles, dance songs and devotional songs to the Virgin. More than two thousand trouvère poems survive, many with melodies preserved in manuscripts called chansonniers.

Notable trouvères

Among the earliest was Chrétien de Troyes, better known for his Arthurian romances. Famous later figures include Thibaut IV, Count of Champagne and King of Navarre; the Châtelain de Couci; Gace Brulé; Colin Muset; and Adam de la Halle of Arras, whose Jeu de Robin et Marion is an early form of comic opera. Several kings and great lords practised the art, and there were also women trouvères (trobairitz analogues), though few of their works survive under their names.

Decline

By the fourteenth century the living lyric tradition gave way to the fixed forms cultivated by the rhétoriqueurs and to polyphonic composition, in which poet and composer were no longer necessarily the same person.
