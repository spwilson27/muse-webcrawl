The Palace of Culture and Science (Polish: Pałac Kultury i Nauki, PKiN) is a monumental high-rise building in central Warsaw, Poland. At 237 metres including its spire, it was for decades the tallest building in Poland and remains one of the tallest in the European Union. Erected in 1952–1955 as an official "gift of the Soviet peoples to Poland" under Joseph Stalin, it is the most prominent survivor of Stalinist architecture outside the former USSR — and one of the most debated buildings in Poland.

History

The project was agreed in 1951 between the Polish communist government and the Soviet Union. The design, by Soviet architect Lev Rudnev, adapted the style of Moscow's "Seven Sisters" skyscrapers, grafted with details borrowed from Polish historic architecture — attics and parapets echoing Krakow and Zamość — at the insistence of Polish officials. Some 3,500 Soviet workers were brought to Warsaw for the construction, housed in a purpose-built estate; at least 16 died in accidents. The building opened on 21 July 1955, initially named the Joseph Stalin Palace of Culture and Science; Stalin's name was removed during the de-Stalinisation thaw of 1956.

The Palace was built on cleared land in the city centre, in a district that had been the Jewish quarter and then part of the wartime ghetto zone, destroyed after 1943 — a layering of histories that adds to the building's contested symbolism.

The building

The complex contains more than 3,000 rooms over 123,000 m²: four theatres, cinemas (including the historic Kinoteka), museums, university and Polish Academy of Sciences offices, a congress hall seating nearly 3,000 — famous for concerts from the Rolling Stones (1967) to Leonard Cohen — a swimming pool, and youth cultural institutions. A viewing terrace on the 30th floor, 114 m up, is one of Warsaw's most popular tourist attractions. Four statues in niches represent the building's namesakes: Copernicus and Maria Skłodowska-Curie for science, and Shakespeare and Mickiewicz for culture.

Controversy and legacy

For many Poles the Palace long symbolised Soviet domination; a popular joke held that the terrace offered "the best view in Warsaw — because it is the only place you cannot see the Palace". Demolition was seriously debated after 1989, and in 2017–2018 some politicians again proposed razing it. Instead it was entered into the register of monuments in 2007, protecting it, and it has been gradually reclaimed as a functioning cultural centre and an icon — resented and beloved — of the capital. Since 2000 its clock tower has marked the millennium, and since 2022 the area around it has been redeveloped with the new Museum of Modern Art.
