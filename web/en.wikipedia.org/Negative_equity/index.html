Negative equity

Negative equity occurs when the value of an asset used to secure a loan is less than the outstanding balance on the loan. In real estate, a property whose market value has fallen below the mortgage balance is described as being underwater or upside down.

Causes

Negative equity typically results from a decline in property values after purchase, from borrowing with a very small deposit, or from loans in which the balance grows over time, such as interest-only or negative-amortization mortgages. Vehicles commonly fall into negative equity because they depreciate quickly.

Consequences

Homeowners in negative equity cannot sell or refinance without bringing cash to the transaction or negotiating a short sale. Widespread negative equity was a central feature of the late-2000s financial crisis, when falling United States house prices left millions of borrowers underwater and contributed to high rates of default and foreclosure.

Measurement

Analytics firms such as CoreLogic publish regular estimates of the share of mortgaged properties in negative equity, which peaked at roughly a quarter of United States homes around 2009–2011 before declining as prices recovered.
