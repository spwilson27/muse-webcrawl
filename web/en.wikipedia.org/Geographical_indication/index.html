Geographical indication

A geographical indication (GI) is a name or sign used on products which correspond to a specific geographical location or origin (e.g., a town, region, or country). The use of a geographical indication, as an indication of the product's source, is intended to act as a certification that the product possesses certain qualities, is made according to traditional methods, or enjoys a certain reputation, due to its geographical origin.

History

The concept of protecting geographical names for foodstuffs and beverages dates back to ancient times, when certain wines and crafts acquired reputations tied to their place of origin. French appellation systems, developed in the early 20th century, were foundational: the Appellation d'origine contrôlée (AOC) system regulated wines such as Champagne, whose name may only be used for sparkling wine produced in the Champagne region of France using specified methods.

International protection

The TRIPS Agreement of 1994, administered by the World Trade Organization, obliges member countries to provide legal means to prevent the use of geographical indications that mislead the public. The Lisbon Agreement of 1958 and its 2015 Geneva Act provide an international registration system administered by WIPO.

Examples

Well-known protected geographical indications include Parmigiano Reggiano cheese, Darjeeling tea, Tequila from Mexico, and Scotch whisky. In the European Union, products are registered under the Protected Designation of Origin (PDO) and Protected Geographical Indication (PGI) schemes.

Criticism

Critics argue that geographical indications can function as trade barriers, and disputes have arisen over names that have become generic in some markets, such as "feta" and "parmesan".
