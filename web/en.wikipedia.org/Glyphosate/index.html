Glyphosate

Glyphosate is the world's most widely used herbicide.

Glyphosate (IUPAC name: N-(phosphonomethyl)glycine) is a broad-spectrum systemic herbicide and crop desiccant. It is an organophosphorus compound, specifically a phosphonate. It is used to kill weeds, especially annual broadleaf weeds and grasses known to compete with commercial crops grown around the world. Glyphosate was discovered by Monsanto chemist John E. Franz in 1970 and brought to market in 1974 under the trade name Roundup.

Mode of action

Glyphosate kills plants by inhibiting the enzyme 5-enolpyruvylshikimate-3-phosphate synthase (EPSPS) in the shikimate pathway, which plants and many microorganisms use to synthesize aromatic amino acids. The pathway is absent in animals, which was long the basis for claims of low animal toxicity. Genetically modified "Roundup Ready" crops carry a bacterial EPSPS variant that is insensitive to glyphosate, allowing whole fields to be sprayed; these crops drove a dramatic increase in glyphosate use from the 1990s onward, and the herbicide became the most used in history.

Controversy

Glyphosate's safety has been intensely debated. In 2015, the International Agency for Research on Cancer classified glyphosate as "probably carcinogenic to humans" (Group 2A), while the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency and the European Food Safety Authority concluded it is not likely to be carcinogenic at real-world exposures. Thousands of lawsuits alleged that Roundup caused non-Hodgkin lymphoma; Bayer, which acquired Monsanto in 2018, agreed to large settlements while maintaining the product's safety. Regulators have repeatedly renewed approvals, the European Union doing so for ten years in 2023. Glyphosate-resistant weeds have proliferated, driving development of new herbicide-tolerant crop systems.
