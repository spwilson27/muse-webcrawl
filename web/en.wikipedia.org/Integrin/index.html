Integrin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Integrins are transmembrane receptors that facilitate cell–cell and cell–extracellular matrix (ECM) adhesion. Upon ligand binding, integrins activate signal transduction pathways that mediate cellular responses such as regulation of the cell cycle, organisation of the intracellular cytoskeleton, and movement of new receptors to the cell membrane. But the presence of integrins allows rapid and flexible responses to events at the cell surface — for example, signalling platelets to initiate an interaction with coagulation factors.

Integrins thus link the ECM outside the cell to the actin cytoskeleton inside, and they signal in both directions: "outside-in" signalling reports the chemical and mechanical composition of the cell's environment, while "inside-out" signalling regulates the receptor's affinity for its ligands. They are central to cell migration, tissue organisation, embryonic development, immune function, wound healing and haemostasis.

Structure

An integrin is an obligate heterodimer composed of one alpha (α) and one beta (β) subunit, each a type I transmembrane protein with a large extracellular domain, a single membrane-spanning helix and a short cytoplasmic tail. In vertebrates, 18 α and 8 β subunits combine to form 24 distinct integrins with different ligand specificities and tissue distributions. The extracellular "head" contains the ligand-binding site; many integrins recognise short peptide motifs in their ligands, most famously the Arg–Gly–Asp (RGD) sequence found in fibronectin and other ECM proteins. Ligand binding typically depends on divalent cations (Mg²⁺, Ca²⁺, Mn²⁺) coordinated at metal-ion-dependent adhesion sites.

Integrins switch between bent (low-affinity, inactive), extended (primed) and extended-open (high-affinity, ligand-bound) conformations. Intracellular proteins such as talin and kindlin bind the β cytoplasmic tail to trigger activation from inside the cell.

Function

Integrin-mediated adhesion assemblies — focal adhesions in cultured cells — couple ECM binding to the actin cytoskeleton through adaptor proteins including talin, vinculin, paxillin and focal adhesion kinase (FAK). Through these complexes integrins:

  • anchor cells to basement membranes and connective tissue (e.g. β1 integrins binding collagen and laminin);
  • enable cell migration during development, immune surveillance and wound healing;
  • mediate leukocyte adhesion to endothelium and trafficking into tissues (β2 integrins such as LFA-1 and Mac-1);
  • support platelet aggregation via integrin αIIbβ3 binding fibrinogen — the final common step of clot formation;
  • transduce mechanical forces (mechanotransduction) and survival signals; loss of appropriate adhesion triggers a form of apoptosis called anoikis.

Clinical significance

Defects in integrins cause human disease: mutations in αIIb or β3 cause Glanzmann thrombasthenia, a bleeding disorder; defects in β2 integrins cause leukocyte adhesion deficiency, with recurrent infections; mutations in α6β4 integrin cause a form of junctional epidermolysis bullosa with skin blistering. Integrins are also important drug targets: antagonists of αIIbβ3 (abciximab, eptifibatide, tirofiban) are used as antiplatelet agents; natalizumab (anti-α4 integrin) and vedolizumab (anti-α4β7) are used in multiple sclerosis and inflammatory bowel disease; and integrin inhibitors have been investigated in cancer, where integrins promote tumour invasion, angiogenesis and metastasis.

History

The integrin family was defined in the 1980s through work by Richard Hynes, Erkki Ruoslahti, Timothy Springer and others, who identified a family of cell-surface receptors recognising fibronectin and other matrix proteins; the name "integrin" was proposed in 1986 to reflect the receptors' role in integrating the extracellular and intracellular environments.
