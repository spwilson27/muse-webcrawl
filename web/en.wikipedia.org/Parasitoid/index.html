Parasitoid

Parasitoid Definition: Organism that lives in or on a single host and ultimately kills it
Main groups: Parasitic wasps (Hymenoptera), tachinid flies (Diptera)
Estimated diversity: 10–20% of all insect species
Uses: Biological pest control

A parasitoid is an organism that lives in close association with its host at the host's expense, eventually resulting in the death of the host. Parasitoidism is one of six major evolutionary strategies within parasitism, distinguished by the fact that the parasitoid's life cycle is completed by killing a single host animal, usually an insect or other invertebrate. The term was coined by the Finnish zoologist Odo Reuter in 1913.

Life cycle and strategies

Most parasitoids are insects. An adult female parasitoid wasp locates a host — for example a caterpillar — and stings it to paralyse it, or stings through its body wall, laying one or more eggs on or inside it. The larva develops by consuming the host from within, typically sparing vital organs until the end so the host remains alive and fresh; when fully grown it kills the host, pupates in or beside the empty husk, and emerges as a free-living adult. Parasitoids are classified as ectoparasitoids (feeding externally) or endoparasitoids (feeding internally), and as idiobionts (which arrest host development at parasitisation) or koinobionts (which allow the host to continue growing). Some manipulate host behaviour — a phenomenon popularised by the emerald cockroach wasp, which stings a cockroach into compliant docility and leads it to the wasp's burrow.

Diversity and importance

Parasitoids comprise an enormous fraction of biodiversity: estimates suggest that parasitoid wasps alone may number over a million species, and parasitoid strategies have evolved independently many times, in at least eight insect orders and in nematodes and fungi. Many species deploy polydnaviruses, symbiotic viruses injected with the egg that suppress the host's immune system.

Biological control

Because each female can kill many hosts and populations track their hosts closely, parasitoids are among the most successful agents of classical biological pest control. The vedalia beetle's rescue of California citrus and, among parasitoids, the release of Encarsia formosa against greenhouse whitefly and of Anagyrus lopezi against the cassava mealybug in Africa are classic cases.

In culture

The lifestyle horrified Charles Darwin, who wrote to Asa Gray in 1860 that he could not persuade himself that a beneficent God "would have designedly created the Ichneumonidae with the express intention of their feeding within the living bodies of caterpillars." Parasitoids later inspired the life cycle of the creature in the film Alien (1979).
