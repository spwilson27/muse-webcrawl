Subgroup

In group theory, a branch of mathematics, a subgroup is a subset of a group that is itself a group under the same operation. Formally, given a group G under a binary operation ∗, a subset H of G is called a subgroup of G if H also forms a group under the operation ∗.

Definition

A subset H of a group G is a subgroup if it satisfies the group axioms: it contains the identity element of G, it is closed under the group operation, and it is closed under taking inverses. The subgroup test states that a nonempty subset H is a subgroup if and only if for all a, b in H, the element a ∗ b−1 is also in H.

Examples

The even integers form a subgroup of the integers under addition, while the odd integers do not, since they are not closed under addition. In the symmetric group S3, the alternating group A3 is a subgroup of order 3. Every group has at least two subgroups: the trivial subgroup consisting only of the identity element, and the group itself, called the improper subgroup.

Properties

The identity element of a subgroup is the identity element of the whole group, and inverses in the subgroup agree with inverses in the group. The intersection of any family of subgroups is again a subgroup. Subgroups can be arranged in a lattice ordered by inclusion, known as the lattice of subgroups.

Cosets and Lagrange's theorem

Given a subgroup H of G, a left coset of H is a set of the form gH = {gh : h ∈ H} for some g in G. The cosets partition the group, and all cosets of H have the same cardinality as H. This yields Lagrange's theorem: the order of a finite subgroup divides the order of the group. The number of distinct cosets is called the index of the subgroup.

Normal subgroups

A subgroup H is called normal if its left and right cosets coincide, that is, gH = Hg for all g in G. Normal subgroups are exactly those subgroups that are kernels of group homomorphisms, and they are precisely the subgroups for which a quotient group can be defined.
