Venn diagram

A Venn diagram is a diagram that shows all possible logical relations between a finite collection of different sets. Depicted as overlapping closed curves (usually circles) inside a rectangle representing the universal set, each region of overlap corresponds to elements belonging to the sets whose curves cover it. Venn diagrams were conceived around 1880 by John Venn, a British logician and philosopher, in his paper "On the Diagrammatic and Mechanical Representation of Propositions and Reasonings".

Overview

In a Venn diagram for two sets, two overlapping circles divide the plane into four regions: elements in the first set only, the second set only, both sets (the intersection), and neither set. Three circles produce eight regions. Unlike the related Euler diagrams, which omit regions that contain no elements, a Venn diagram shows every possible combination of membership, with empty regions shaded or marked as needed. For more than three sets, circles alone cannot produce all 2n regions, so ellipses or other curves are used.

History

John Venn introduced the diagrams in 1880, building on similar schemes by Gottfried Leibniz and Leonhard Euler. Venn himself called them "Eulerian circles", but the term "Venn diagram" became standard in the early 20th century, promoted by Clarence Irving Lewis. The diagrams became a staple of elementary set theory teaching in the 1960s under the "new math" movement.

Applications

Venn diagrams are used in set theory, probability (to visualise events and their unions and intersections), logic, statistics, linguistics, and computer science. In business and popular media they are widely used, sometimes loosely, to illustrate overlapping categories.
