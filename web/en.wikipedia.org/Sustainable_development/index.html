Sustainable development is an organizing principle that aims to meet the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs — the formulation made famous by the 1987 Brundtland Report, Our Common Future. It couples economic and social development with protection of the environment, and underpins the United Nations' global policy agenda.

History

The concept grew out of earlier ideas about sustainable forestry (Nachhaltigkeit) in 18th-century Germany and 20th-century conservation thought. The 1972 Stockholm Conference on the Human Environment first placed environment and development on the same international agenda, the Brundtland Commission formalized the concept in 1987, and the 1992 Rio Earth Summit produced Agenda 21 and the framework conventions on climate change and biodiversity.

Sustainable Development Goals

In 2015 the UN General Assembly adopted the 2030 Agenda, built around 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) — including ending poverty and hunger, ensuring clean water and affordable clean energy, climate action, and sustainable cities — succeeding the earlier Millennium Development Goals with 169 associated targets monitored by indicators.

Criticism and debates

Critics question whether "sustainable development" is a coherent goal or a political compromise masking a contradiction between endless economic growth and finite ecological limits. Debates include the relative weight of weak versus strong sustainability (whether natural capital can be substituted by manufactured capital), the role of population, degrowth critiques, and problems of implementation and measurement.
