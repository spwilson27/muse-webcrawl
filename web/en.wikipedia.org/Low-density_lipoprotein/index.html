Low-density lipoprotein

Low-density lipoprotein

Classification: Lipoprotein
Density: 1.019–1.063 g/mL
Diameter: ~22 nm
Key protein: Apolipoprotein B-100

Low-density lipoprotein (LDL) is one of the five major groups of lipoproteins that transport fat molecules, including cholesterol, around the body in extracellular water. LDL delivers cholesterol to peripheral tissues and is popularly called "bad cholesterol" because elevated LDL cholesterol is associated with atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease, although cholesterol itself is essential for cell membranes and steroid hormones.

Structure and function

Each LDL particle contains a single molecule of apolipoprotein B-100, a cholesteryl ester-rich core, phospholipids and free cholesterol on its surface. LDL particles are about 22 nanometres in diameter and are formed in the circulation from very-low-density lipoproteins (VLDL) after triglycerides are removed by lipoprotein lipase, passing through an intermediate-density (IDL) stage.

Physiology

Cells take up LDL via the LDL receptor, which recognises apoB-100. Receptor-mediated endocytosis delivers cholesterol for membrane synthesis and suppresses endogenous cholesterol production. Mutations in the LDL receptor cause familial hypercholesterolaemia. See Cholesterol and Atherosclerosis.

Clinical significance

Guidelines use LDL cholesterol concentration as a primary target for lipid-lowering therapy with statins, ezetimibe and PCSK9 inhibitors. Risk increases continuously with LDL level, and lowering LDL reduces major vascular events.

Testing

LDL cholesterol was traditionally estimated by the Friedewald equation from total cholesterol, HDL cholesterol and triglycerides; direct assays and newer equations are now also used, especially at high triglycerides or low LDL levels.
