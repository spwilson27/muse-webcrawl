IBM 1403

An IBM 1403 printer, cover raised.

The IBM 1403 line printer was introduced on October 2, 1959 with the IBM 1401 data processing system. It was the most successful and longest-lived of IBM's chain printers, and for decades was the workhorse impact printer of mainframe data centres. Later models served systems through the System/360 and System/370 lines; the 1403 remained in IBM's catalogue until 1983, and its forms-control conventions influenced printer design for years.

Technology

The 1403 used a horizontally rotating chain of type slugs—240 characters in repeating sets—behind which ribbon and paper passed. A bank of 132 hammers, one per print column, fired at the precise instant the desired slug passed, pressing paper and ribbon against the chain. Early models printed 600 lines per minute; the Model N1 reached 1,100 lines per minute. A carriage control tape (a punched paper loop) governed form skips and page ejects, synchronising paper movement with the program.

Character and culture

The 1403 printed a 48-character set (letters, digits, and limited symbols) across 132 columns on wide green-bar fanfold paper—the visual signature of mainframe-era computing. Its chain produced a distinctive roar; operators famously could "play music" on it by printing patterns that made hammer strikes beat in rhythm. Because printing stopped the processor on many small systems, the 1403's speed set the effective pace of the 1401 installations that made computing affordable to mid-sized businesses in the 1960s.
