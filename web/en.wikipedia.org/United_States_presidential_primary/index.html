United States presidential primary

A United States presidential primary is an election in which voters in a state choose delegates to a political party's national convention, where the party formally nominates its candidate for president. Along with caucuses — local meetings at which supporters are counted — primaries form the first stage of the U.S. presidential election process. The modern system, in which most delegates are won in binding primaries rather than selected by party leaders in "smoke-filled rooms," dates from reforms after the 1968 Democratic National Convention.

History

For most of the nineteenth century, nominees were chosen by congressional caucuses and then conventions dominated by party organizations. Progressive-era reformers introduced presidential primaries beginning with Florida (1901) and Wisconsin (1905); by 1916 a dozen-plus states held them, though conventions still decided — in 1912 Theodore Roosevelt won most primaries yet lost the Republican nomination to William Howard Taft. The televised chaos of the 1968 Democratic convention, where Hubert Humphrey won the nomination without entering primaries while anti-war candidates had won them, produced the McGovern–Fraser reforms: from 1972 onward, most delegates were pledged through primaries and caucuses.

Process

Parties set their own rules. Democrats allocate delegates proportionally to candidates winning at least 15% statewide or in a congressional district, and include unpledged "superdelegates" (whose power to contravene pledged results was sharply curtailed in 2018). Republicans allow winner-take-all allocation after mid-March. States choose open primaries (any voter may participate), closed primaries (registered party members only), or hybrid systems. The calendar traditionally opens with the Iowa caucuses and the first-in-the-nation New Hampshire primary — protected by state law — followed by Nevada and South Carolina, and a multi-state "Super Tuesday" in early March. For 2024 the Democratic Party reordered its calendar to start with South Carolina, though New Hampshire held its primary first anyway under state law.

significance of early states

Because most campaigns end after poor showings in the first contests, the early states exert outsized influence ("momentum"), a feature criticized as unrepresentative of the national party. Nevertheless, since the modern system's creation, nearly every major-party nominee has emerged from the primary process, and the conventions have become largely ceremonial ratifications.
