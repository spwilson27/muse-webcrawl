Panic of 1819

The Panic of 1819 was the first major peacetime financial crisis in the United States, marking the end of the economic expansion that followed the War of 1812. It caused widespread bank failures, foreclosures, unemployment, and a collapse in agricultural and land prices, and it eroded the confident mood of the so-called Era of Good Feelings.

Causes

After the war, state-chartered banks proliferated and issued large volumes of paper money, fuelling a speculative boom in western land and cotton. The Second Bank of the United States, chartered in 1816, initially contributed to the credit expansion, but in 1818, under pressure to build specie reserves and fearing overextension, it abruptly contracted credit and demanded that state banks redeem their notes in gold and silver. At the same time, European demand for American cotton fell as post-Napoleonic markets stabilised, and commodity prices dropped sharply.

Effects

Banks failed across the country, western farmers lost their land to foreclosure, and urban unemployment rose steeply; contemporary estimates suggested hundreds of thousands were affected. Debtors' relief movements flourished in states such as Kentucky, where the "Old Court–New Court" controversy erupted over relief legislation. The crisis deepened public resentment of banks and the Second Bank of the United States, setting the stage for the Jacksonian Bank War of the 1830s.

Recovery

The economy recovered gradually in the early 1820s. The Land Act of 1820 lowered the price and minimum purchase of public land while ending credit sales. The panic remains a landmark in American economic history, demonstrating the young republic's vulnerability to credit cycles and the political potency of banking issues.
