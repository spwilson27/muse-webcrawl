Cytochrome c oxidase

Cytochrome c oxidase
EC number7.1.1.9 (formerly 1.9.3.1)
Respiratory complexComplex IV
LocationInner mitochondrial membrane
Reaction4 cytochrome c (reduced) + O₂ + 8 H⁺ → 4 cytochrome c (oxidised) + 2 H₂O + 4 H⁺ (pumped)

Cytochrome c oxidase (also known as Complex IV) is a large transmembrane protein complex found in bacteria and in the mitochondria of eukaryotes. It is the last enzyme in the respiratory electron transport chain, located in the mitochondrial (or bacterial) membrane. It receives electrons from cytochrome c and transfers them to oxygen, reducing oxygen to water, while pumping protons across the membrane to contribute to the electrochemical gradient used by ATP synthase to make ATP.

Structure

The mammalian enzyme contains 13 subunits, three of which (I, II, and III) are encoded by mitochondrial DNA and form the catalytic core; the remaining ten are encoded in the nucleus. The complex contains two haem groups (a and a₃), two copper centres (Cuₐ and Cuₕ), and ions of magnesium and zinc. The binuclear centre formed by haem a₃ and Cuₕ is the site of oxygen binding and reduction.

Mechanism

For each oxygen molecule reduced, the enzyme takes up eight protons from the matrix: four are consumed in forming two water molecules and four are pumped across the membrane into the intermembrane space. The reaction thus couples the exergonic reduction of oxygen to the generation of the proton-motive force.

Inhibitors and clinical aspects

Cytochrome c oxidase is inhibited by cyanide, carbon monoxide, azide, and hydrogen sulfide, which bind at the oxygen-reducing centre; this blockade of cellular respiration underlies much of the toxicity of these poisons. Inherited defects in complex IV subunits or assembly factors cause mitochondrial diseases, including forms of Leigh syndrome.
