Reform Judaism

Reform Judaism, also known as Liberal Judaism or Progressive Judaism, is a major Jewish denomination that emphasises the evolving nature of the faith, the superiority of its ethical aspects over its ceremonial ones, and a belief in a continuous revelation, not centred on the theophany at Mount Sinai alone but open to new interpretations. Its defining statements, beginning with the Pittsburgh Platform of 1885, stress ethical monotheism, social justice (tikkun olam), and the autonomy of the individual in deciding which practices to observe.

History

The movement arose in early 19th-century Germany amid Jewish emancipation: Israel Jacobson's temple at Seesen (1810) and the Hamburg Temple (1818) introduced vernacular prayer, sermons, organ music and decorum modelled on Protestant services, while rabbis such as Abraham Geiger and Samuel Holdheim gave the reforms theological grounding. Reform crossed the Atlantic with German Jewish immigrants; Isaac Mayer Wise founded the Union of American Hebrew Congregations (1873) and Hebrew Union College (1875) in Cincinnati, making North America the movement's centre of gravity.

Beliefs and practice

Reform Judaism regards Jewish law (halakha) as a valuable guide but not binding in its entirety, affirms gender equality in religious life (ordaining women rabbis since 1972), recognises patrilineal descent (1983), and has been at the forefront of LGBTQ inclusion. Liturgy historically minimised references to the restoration of sacrifices, a personal messiah and bodily resurrection, though later platforms (Columbus 1937, San Francisco 1976, Pittsburgh 1999) re-embraced Hebrew, ritual and Zionism. Today the Union for Reform Judaism counts roughly 850 congregations in North America, with sister movements worldwide under the World Union for Progressive Judaism.
