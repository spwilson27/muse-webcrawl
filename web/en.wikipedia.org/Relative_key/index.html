Relative key

Each major key shares its key signature with its relative minor.

In music theory, the relative key of a given key is the key that shares the same key signature. Every major key has a relative minor, and every minor key has a relative major. The tonic of the relative minor lies a minor third (three semitones) below the tonic of the major key; conversely, the relative major lies a minor third above the minor tonic.

Examples

C major and A minor are relative keys — both have no sharps or flats. G major (one sharp) pairs with E minor; F major (one flat) with D minor; D major (two sharps) with B minor. On the circle of fifths, the relative minor is conventionally written inside the circle opposite its major. A complete list runs through all fifteen major and fifteen minor key signatures used in practice.

Use in composition

Because relative keys share all their diatonic notes, modulation between them is smooth and extremely common: a piece in a major key often moves to its relative minor for contrast (for example in the development of sonata form), and the minor key frequently borrows its raised sixth and seventh degrees from the relative major's scale (melodic and harmonic minor). Relative keys contrast with parallel keys, which share the same tonic but differ in mode and signature (C major versus C minor).
