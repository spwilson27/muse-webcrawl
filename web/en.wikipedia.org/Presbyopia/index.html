Presbyopia is the age-related loss of the eye's ability to focus on near objects. It results from the gradual hardening of the crystalline lens and changes in the ciliary muscle, which reduce the amplitude of accommodation. The word comes from the Greek presbys ("old man") and ōps ("eye"). Nearly everyone develops presbyopia with age, typically noticing symptoms in their early to mid-forties.

Mechanism

In a young eye, the ciliary muscle contracts to release tension on the zonular fibers, letting the elastic lens round up and increase its optical power for near vision. With age the lens grows continuously, its proteins stiffen, and its capsule loses elasticity, so this shape change diminishes. The near point recedes from about 10 cm in childhood to beyond comfortable reading distance by the fifties; by around age 55 the loss of accommodation is essentially complete.

Symptoms and course

Typical complaints are blurred near vision, needing to hold reading material at arm's length, eyestrain, and headaches after close work, often worse in dim light or when tired. People with myopia may find they can still read by removing their distance glasses; people with hyperopia often notice presbyopia earlier.

Correction

Presbyopia is corrected with convex ("plus") lenses: reading glasses, bifocals, trifocals, or progressive lenses, and multifocal or monovision contact lenses. Surgical options include multifocal and accommodating intraocular lenses, corneal inlays, and laser procedures. In 2021 the US FDA approved pilocarpine hydrochloride 1.25% eye drops (Vuity) as a pharmacologic treatment that improves near vision by constricting the pupil; other drops are in development.

Epidemiology

Presbyopia is one of the most common vision conditions worldwide, affecting an estimated 1.8 billion people as of the mid-2010s, with the number growing as populations age. Uncorrected presbyopia is a significant cause of near-vision impairment, particularly in low-income regions with poor access to spectacles.
