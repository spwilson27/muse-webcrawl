A protease (also called a peptidase, proteinase, or proteolytic enzyme) is an enzyme that catalyzes proteolysis, breaking down proteins into smaller polypeptides or single amino acids, and spurring the formation of new protein products. It does this by cleaving the peptide bonds within proteins by hydrolysis, a reaction in which water molecules are consumed. Proteases are involved in countless biological processes, including digestion, blood clotting, immune function, cell signaling, and the recycling of damaged proteins.

Classification

Proteases are classified by the site of cleavage (endopeptidases cleave internal bonds; exopeptidases, including aminopeptidases and carboxypeptidases, remove terminal amino acids) and by their catalytic mechanism. The MEROPS database groups proteases into families and clans based on sequence and structure. The main catalytic types are:

  • Serine proteases – use a serine residue in the active site; examples include trypsin, chymotrypsin, elastase, and the subtilisins.
  • Cysteine proteases – use a cysteine thiol; examples include papain, caspases, and cathepsins.
  • Aspartic proteases – use two aspartate residues and a water molecule; examples include pepsin, renin, and HIV-1 protease.
  • Metalloproteases – require a metal ion, usually zinc; examples include matrix metalloproteinases and thermolysin.
  • Threonine proteases – the proteasome is the principal example.

Biological roles

In the digestive system, proteases such as pepsin in the stomach and trypsin and chymotrypsin from the pancreas break dietary proteins into absorbable peptides and amino acids; they are secreted as inactive zymogens to protect the tissues that produce them. In the blood coagulation cascade, the serine proteases thrombin and factor Xa regulate clot formation. Caspases execute programmed cell death (apoptosis), and the proteasome degrades ubiquitin-tagged proteins. Many viruses encode essential proteases; the HIV protease is the target of a major class of antiretroviral drugs, the protease inhibitors.

Inhibition and uses

Organisms produce protease inhibitors (such as serpins) to control proteolytic activity, and failure of this control contributes to diseases including emphysema and thrombosis. Industrial proteases, especially alkaline serine proteases from Bacillus, are widely used in laundry detergents, food processing, leather treatment, and molecular biology.
