Germanic peoples

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Germanic peoples were a group of Indo-European peoples identified by their use of the Germanic languages, whose range, from at least the Early Roman era, was in northern Europe from the west bank of the Rhine to the Vistula in the east and from the Danube and the North Sea and Baltic seas in the south and north. Their languages are thought to derive from a Proto-Germanic language spoken in the late Bronze Age and early Iron Age in southern Scandinavia and northern Germany.

Origins and expansion

Archaeologists associate early Germanic culture with the Nordic Bronze Age and the Jastorf culture of the Pre-Roman Iron Age. By the 1st century BC Germanic groups were pressing against the Roman frontier on the Rhine and Danube. Rome's attempts to conquer the territory east of the Rhine ended with the annihilation of three legions under Varus at the Battle of the Teutoburg Forest in AD 9, after which the Rhine remained the frontier for centuries.

The Roman historian Tacitus, in his Germania (c. AD 98), and Julius Caesar before him, described peoples such as the Suebi, Cherusci, Chatti, and Marcomanni. Later sources name the Goths, Vandals, Burgundians, Lombards, Franks, Saxons, Angles, and Alamanni.

Migration Period

From the late 4th century AD, the movement of the Huns westward triggered large-scale migrations. Germanic groups crossed the frontiers of the Western Roman Empire: the Visigoths sacked Rome in 410; the Vandals crossed to North Africa; the Ostrogoths under Theodoric ruled Italy; the Franks under Clovis founded the Merovingian kingdom in Gaul; and Angles, Saxons, and Jutes settled in post-Roman Britain, laying the foundations of England. These successor kingdoms transformed the map of Europe and transmitted much of Roman law and culture into the early Middle Ages.

Society and religion

Early Germanic society was organised around kinship groups and assemblies (things), with kings and war-leaders whose authority rested on success in battle. Their pre-Christian religion, known from archaeology and later Norse texts, centred on gods such as Wodan (Odin), Donar (Thor), and Tiwaz (Týr). Between the 4th and 11th centuries the Germanic peoples converted to Christianity, first largely in its Arian form among the Goths, then in its Catholic form among the Franks, and last in Scandinavia.

Legacy

Modern peoples speaking Germanic languages include the Germans, English, Dutch, Flemings, Danes, Norwegians, Swedes, Icelanders, and Afrikaners. Germanic languages today are spoken natively by roughly 500 million people, and by well over a billion including second-language speakers of English.
