Transistor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A transistor is a semiconductor device used to amplify or switch electrical signals and power. It is one of the basic building blocks of modern electronics. A voltage or current applied to one pair of the transistor's terminals controls the current through another pair of terminals. Because the controlled (output) power can be higher than the controlling (input) power, a transistor can amplify a signal.

History

The transistor was invented at Bell Telephone Laboratories in December 1947 by John Bardeen, Walter Brattain, and William Shockley, who shared the 1956 Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery. The first working device was a point-contact transistor; Shockley's bipolar junction transistor (BJT) followed, and the metal–oxide–semiconductor field-effect transistor (MOSFET), proposed earlier by Julius Edgar Lilienfeld, became practical by 1960 through the work of Mohamed Atalla and Dawon Kahng.

Types

The MOSFET is the most widely used type: by the early 21st century it accounted for the overwhelming majority of all transistors manufactured. Other types include bipolar junction transistors, junction field-effect transistors (JFETs), and insulated-gate bipolar transistors (IGBTs) used in power electronics.

Importance

Transistors are the fundamental active components of integrated circuits. The number of transistors per chip grew exponentially for decades, a trend described by Moore's law. Modern processors contain tens of billions of transistors. Miniaturisation has been driven by fabrication advances closely tied to chemistry and materials science, and to photolithographic processes.

Operation

In a field-effect transistor, current flows through a semiconductor channel between source and drain terminals; a voltage on the gate terminal modulates the channel's conductivity. In a bipolar transistor, a small base current controls a larger collector current. Both families can be configured as amplifiers or as switches, the latter underpinning digital logic.
