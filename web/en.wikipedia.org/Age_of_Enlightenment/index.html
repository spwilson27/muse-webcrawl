Age of Enlightenment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Age of Enlightenment (also the Age of Reason) was an intellectual and philosophical movement that dominated Europe in the 17th and 18th centuries. It emphasised reason, individualism, scepticism of traditional authority, and the scientific method, and it profoundly influenced politics, science, philosophy and society.

Ideas

Enlightenment thinkers argued that human knowledge could be attained through rationality and empirical observation. Central ideas included liberty, progress, toleration, fraternity, constitutional government, and the separation of church and state. John Locke's theories of natural rights and the social contract, Montesquieu's separation of powers, Voltaire's advocacy of civil liberties, and Jean-Jacques Rousseau's general will shaped modern political thought.

Science and knowledge

Building on the Scientific Revolution, figures such as Isaac Newton provided a model of knowledge grounded in mathematics and observation. Denis Diderot and Jean le Rond d'Alembert's Encyclopédie (1751–1772) sought to gather and disseminate all human knowledge. Academies, salons, coffeehouses and a growing print culture spread Enlightenment ideas across Europe and the Americas.

Political impact

Enlightenment ideas inspired the American Revolution and the French Revolution, and informed the US Declaration of Independence (1776), the US Constitution, and the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789). "Enlightened absolutists" such as Frederick the Great of Prussia, Catherine the Great of Russia and Joseph II of Austria adopted some reforms while retaining royal power.

Criticism and legacy

Critics, both contemporary and later, questioned the movement's universalism, its role in justifying colonialism, and its faith in reason. Nevertheless, the Enlightenment's legacy underlies modern liberal democracy, human rights discourse, and the organisation of modern science and education. Immanuel Kant famously defined it in 1784 as humanity's emergence from its self-imposed immaturity: Sapere aude — "dare to know."
