The Meselson–Stahl experiment is an experiment by Matthew Meselson and Franklin Stahl in 1958 which supported Watson and Crick's hypothesis that DNA replication was semiconservative. In semiconservative replication, when the double-helical DNA is replicated, each of the two new double-helical DNA molecules consists of one strand from the original molecule and one newly synthesised strand. The experiment has been called "the most beautiful experiment in biology".

The three hypotheses

After the discovery of the double helix in 1953, three models of replication were conceivable: conservative (the parental molecule stays intact and an entirely new copy is made), semiconservative (each daughter molecule keeps one parental strand), and dispersive (parental material is scattered through both strands of both daughters).

Method

Meselson and Stahl grew E. coli for many generations in a medium containing the heavy nitrogen isotope 15N, so that all DNA was "heavy". The bacteria were then transferred to a medium with ordinary 14N, and DNA samples taken after each generation were separated by equilibrium density-gradient centrifugation in caesium chloride, which sorts DNA molecules by density.

Results

  • Generation 0: a single band of heavy (15N) DNA.
  • Generation 1: a single band of exactly intermediate density — hybrid molecules with one old and one new strand. This eliminated the conservative model.
  • Generation 2: two bands, one hybrid and one light (14N), in equal amounts — exactly as predicted by semiconservative replication, and incompatible with the dispersive model, which would have given a single, ever-lighter band.

The results, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences in 1958, confirmed the semiconservative mechanism implied by the Watson–Crick structure and remain a classic demonstration of a decisive experiment.
