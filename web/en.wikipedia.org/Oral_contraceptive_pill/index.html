Combined oral contraceptive pill

The combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP), often simply called the pill, is a type of birth control taken by mouth that contains both an estrogen and a progestin. It prevents pregnancy primarily by suppressing ovulation, and also thickens cervical mucus and thins the uterine lining. With perfect use it is more than 99% effective; typical-use effectiveness is lower, around 91–93%, mainly because of missed pills.

History

Developed in the 1950s through the work of biologist Gregory Pincus, clinician John Rock, and funders Margaret Sanger and Katharine McCormick, the first pill, Enovid, was approved for contraceptive use in the United States in 1960. It rapidly became one of the most widely used contraceptives worldwide and is credited with a major role in the sexual revolution and in expanding women's educational and professional opportunities. Early formulations contained much higher hormone doses than modern pills.

Use

Pills are usually taken daily for 21 days followed by a 7-day break (or placebo week) during which withdrawal bleeding occurs; some regimens use 24 active pills or continuous dosing. Besides contraception, COCPs are prescribed for acne, heavy or painful periods, endometriosis, and polycystic ovary syndrome, and long-term use reduces the risk of ovarian and endometrial cancers.

Risks

Combined pills slightly increase the risk of venous thromboembolism and, in some users, arterial events such as stroke and heart attack; risk is higher in smokers over 35. They do not protect against sexually transmitted infections. They are distinct from the progestin-only "minipill".
