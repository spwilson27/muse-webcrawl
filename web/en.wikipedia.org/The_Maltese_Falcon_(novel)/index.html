The Maltese Falcon is a 1930 detective novel by American writer Dashiell Hammett, originally serialized in the magazine Black Mask beginning with the September 1929 issue. The story is told entirely through external, objective description — dialogue and physical action — without access to any character's thoughts, a technique often called the "camera eye" or behaviorist style. Its protagonist, Sam Spade, became the archetype of the hardboiled private detective.

Plot summary

San Francisco private investigator Sam Spade and his partner Miles Archer are hired by a woman calling herself Ruth Wonderly to shadow Floyd Thursby. Archer is shot dead while on the job, and Thursby is killed the same night. Wonderly, whose real name is Brigid O'Shaughnessy, admits she is entangled in the hunt for a statuette of a black falcon: according to the obese schemer Kasper Gutman, the falcon is a golden, jewel-encrusted gift the Knights of Malta made to Charles V of Spain in 1530, later covered in black enamel to disguise it. The statuette that finally changes hands is a fake made of lead. Spade, who has had an affair with Archer's widow and is drawn to Brigid, nevertheless gives Brigid to the police for Archer's murder: "When a man's partner is killed he's supposed to do something about it."

Background

Hammett drew on his own experience as a Pinkerton detective operative. The novel first appeared in five installments in Black Mask. The falcon's back-story — a gold statuette given as tribute by the Knights Hospitaller of Malta — is Hammett's invention, though the Order did pay a nominal tribute of a falcon annually for Malta.

Adaptations

The novel has been adapted for film three times, most famously in the 1941 version directed by John Huston and starring Humphrey Bogart, which follows the novel closely. Earlier adaptations appeared in 1931 and 1936.

Legacy

The novel is consistently ranked among the greatest English-language novels of the 20th century and is a foundational text of hardboiled crime fiction. The Modern Library placed it on its list of the 100 best English-language novels of the 20th century.
