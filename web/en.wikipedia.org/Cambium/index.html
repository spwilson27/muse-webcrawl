Cambium

Cambium
TypeLateral meristem (plant tissue)
Main formsVascular cambium; cork cambium (phellogen)
ProductsSecondary xylem (wood) and secondary phloem; periderm
FunctionSecondary (thickening) growth of stems and roots

A cambium (plural cambiums or cambia) is a layer of actively dividing cells in the stems and roots of plants that produces growth in thickness, known as secondary growth. The two principal cambia are the vascular cambium and the cork cambium.

Vascular cambium

The vascular cambium is a cylindrical sheath of meristematic tissue lying between the primary xylem and primary phloem. In woody plants it produces secondary xylem (wood) toward the inside and secondary phloem toward the outside. Because far more xylem than phloem is formed, the cambium is steadily pushed outward as the stem thickens. Seasonal variation in the size of the xylem cells produced creates the annual growth rings used in dendrochronology. The vascular cambium contains two kinds of initials: fusiform initials, which generate the axial system of elongated cells, and ray initials, which generate the radial parenchyma rays.

Cork cambium

The cork cambium (phellogen) arises further out in the stem and produces the periderm, the protective outer tissue that replaces the epidermis in woody plants. It generates cork cells (phellem) outward and phelloderm inward; together these tissues make up the bark. Lenticels, pores in the cork, allow gas exchange with the living tissues beneath.
