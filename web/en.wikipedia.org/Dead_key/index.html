Dead key

A dead key is a special kind of modifier key on a typewriter or computer keyboard that does not generate a character by itself, but modifies the character produced by the following keystroke—most commonly adding a diacritical mark or accent.

Mechanism

On mechanical typewriters, a dead key typed an accent without advancing the carriage, so the typist could overstrike it with a letter to form characters such as é or è. On computer keyboards, pressing a dead key stores the accent in a pending state; the next character typed is combined with it, and pressing the space bar yields the accent alone.

Usage

Dead keys are standard on many European keyboard layouts for accents (acute, grave, circumflex, tilde, diaeresis) and on the US-International layout, which turns the apostrophe, quote, grave, tilde, and circumflex keys into dead keys. Compose keys and input method editors serve similar purposes on other systems.

Chained dead keys

Some layouts support chaining two dead keys before a letter, allowing combinations such as the Vietnamese and Romanian alphabets require, or doubly-accented characters in phonetic transcription.
