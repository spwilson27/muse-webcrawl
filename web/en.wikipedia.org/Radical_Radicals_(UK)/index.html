Radical Radicals (UK)

The London Corresponding Society, one of the popular radical organisations of the 1790s.

The term Radical Radicals is used by historians for the ultra-radical wing of the British Radical movement of the late 18th and early 19th centuries — reformers who pressed beyond parliamentary reform toward universal manhood suffrage, annual parliaments, and, in some cases, republicanism and equal political rights, at a time when the vote was confined to a small propertied minority. The label is applied retrospectively, above all to the circles around figures such as Thomas Spence, and to the activists prosecuted in the era of the French Revolutionary Wars and the post-war repression.

Background

British radicalism drew on the Commonwealthman tradition, the American and French Revolutions, and Thomas Paine's Rights of Man (1791–92), which sold in unprecedented numbers. The London Corresponding Society (founded 1792) and similar societies organised working-class demands for reform, prompting the government's Treason Trials of 1794 and the Gagging Acts. Within the movement a spectrum emerged: moderate reformers around figures like Francis Burdett and Henry Hunt sought parliamentary reform within the constitution, while "ultra" or "Radical Radicals" — Spence and his followers, the Spencean Philanthropists, and later Thomas Evans and the younger Arthur Thistlewood — advocated fundamental change, including Spence's plan for common ownership of land.

Post-war radicalism

After 1815, economic distress revived mass radicalism: the Spa Fields meetings (1816), the Pentridge rising (1817), the Peterloo Massacre (1819) and the Cato Street conspiracy (1820) — a plot by Thistlewood's circle to murder the Cabinet — marked the high point of ultra-radical direct action. The government's use of spies and agents provocateurs, and the Six Acts of 1819, largely crushed the movement, and leadership of reform passed to more cautious parliamentary campaigners, culminating in the Reform Act 1832. Many Radical Radicals' demands — universal suffrage, vote by ballot, equal electoral districts — were later taken up by Chartism.
