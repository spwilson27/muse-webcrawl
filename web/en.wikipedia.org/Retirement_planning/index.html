Retirement planning

Retirement planning, in a financial context, refers to the allocation of savings or revenue that a person puts aside during their working life, designated for use in their retirement years. It involves determining retirement income goals and the decisions and actions needed to achieve those goals. Retirement planning includes identifying sources of income, sizing up expenses, implementing a savings program, and managing assets and risk. Cash flows are estimated to determine if the retirement income goal can be achieved.

In a general sense, retirement planning is the planning one does to be prepared for life after paid work ends, not just financially but in all aspects of life. The non-financial aspects include lifestyle choices such as how to spend time in retirement, where to live, and when to quit working, among other things.

Planning process

Financial planners and advisers typically begin by estimating how much income a retiree will need, often expressed as a replacement ratio of pre-retirement income (commonly 70–85 percent, since some expenses such as commuting and retirement saving itself fall away, while others such as healthcare rise). The planner then projects the growth of savings using assumptions about investment returns, inflation, and life expectancy, and recommends a savings rate and asset allocation. Because compounding rewards early saving, beginning contributions early in a career greatly reduces the required saving rate.

As retirement approaches, plans typically shift toward reducing portfolio volatility, deciding when to claim public pension benefits, planning for taxation of withdrawals, and arranging insurance for health and long-term care.

Income sources

Retirement income commonly comes from a combination of sources, sometimes described as a "three-legged stool": public pensions (such as Social Security in the United States), employer-sponsored plans (defined-benefit pensions, 401(k), 403(b) and similar accounts), and personal savings and investments (IRAs, taxable brokerage accounts, annuities, rental property). Tax-advantaged accounts have contribution limits and rules about when withdrawals may or must begin; in the United States, required minimum distributions force withdrawals from most tax-deferred accounts beginning in a retiree's seventies.

Withdrawal strategies

A widely discussed rule of thumb is the "4% rule," derived from the Trinity study and the work of William Bengen, which found that a retiree withdrawing 4 percent of the initial portfolio value, adjusted annually for inflation, historically had a high probability of not exhausting a diversified portfolio over 30 years. Other strategies include dynamic withdrawal rules that adjust spending to market performance, bucket strategies that hold near-term spending in cash and bonds, and annuitization of part of the portfolio to guarantee lifetime income.

Risks

Key risks in retirement planning include longevity risk (outliving one's assets), inflation eroding purchasing power, sequence-of-returns risk (poor market returns early in retirement permanently impairing a portfolio), healthcare and long-term care costs, and cognitive decline affecting financial decision-making. Planning aims to balance these risks rather than eliminate any single one.
