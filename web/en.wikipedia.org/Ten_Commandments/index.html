Ten Commandments

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Ten Commandments (known in Judaism as the Aseret ha-Dibrot, "the Ten Statements," and in Christian tradition also as the Decalogue) are a set of biblical principles relating to ethics and worship that play a fundamental role in Judaism and Christianity. The text appears twice in the Hebrew Bible: in Exodus 20:2–17 and Deuteronomy 5:6–21.

Text and numbering

According to the Book of Exodus, the commandments were revealed by God to Moses on Mount Sinai and inscribed on two stone tablets kept in the Ark of the Covenant. The list, in a common paraphrase, is:

  1. You shall have no other gods before me.
  2. You shall not make idols (graven images).
  3. You shall not take the name of the Lord your God in vain.
  4. Remember the Sabbath day, to keep it holy.
  5. Honour your father and your mother.
  6. You shall not murder.
  7. You shall not commit adultery.
  8. You shall not steal.
  9. You shall not bear false witness against your neighbour.
  10. You shall not covet.

The numbering differs between traditions: Jewish, Catholic and Lutheran, and Reformed arrangements divide the statements differently, particularly at the beginning and end of the list.

Interpretation and influence

The commandments form the core of the covenant between God and Israel and summarise obligations toward God (the first table) and toward other people (the second). In Christian teaching, Jesus summarised the law in the two "great commandments" of love of God and love of neighbour. The Decalogue has had a lasting influence on Western law and ethics, and public displays of the commandments have been the subject of church–state litigation in the United States. Similar lists of divine commands appear in other religious traditions, though the Ten Commandments are specific to the Abrahamic scriptures.
