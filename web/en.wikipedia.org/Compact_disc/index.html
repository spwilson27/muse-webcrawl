The compact disc (CD) is a digital optical disc data storage format that was co-developed by Philips and Sony to store and play digital audio recordings. It uses a 120 mm polycarbonate disc with a reflective metal layer; data is stored as a spiral track of microscopic pits and lands read by a 780 nm infrared laser diode. The CD was released in Japan in October 1982 and became the dominant medium for recorded music through the 1990s before being displaced by downloads and streaming in the 21st century.

History

The compact disc evolved from Philips' optical videodisc work on the LaserDisc in the 1970s. Philips and Sony formed a task force in 1979 and published the Red Book standard in 1980, specifying 16-bit PCM audio sampled at 44,100 Hz. The sampling rate was chosen to fit video equipment used for mastering, being derived from the NTSC and PAL line rates. The standard disc diameter of 120 mm and playing time of 74 minutes were set so that the disc could hold Beethoven's Ninth Symphony, a stipulation often credited to Sony executive Norio Ohga.

The first commercial CD, a recording of ABBA's The Visitors, was produced in August 1982, and the format launched in Japan on 1 October 1982 with players such as Sony's CDP-101. Billy Joel's 52nd Street was among the first albums released on CD.

Technical details

Audio CDs store stereo sound as uncompressed PCM with a bit rate of 1,411.2 kbit/s. Reed–Solomon error correction (CIRC) allows scratches and fingerprints to be tolerated. A standard 120 mm disc holds up to about 74–80 minutes of audio or roughly 650–700 MiB of data in the CD-ROM (Yellow Book) variant. Smaller 80 mm "mini CDs" were also produced.

Variants and legacy

The CD family grew to include CD-ROM, CD-R, CD-RW, Video CD, Super Video CD, and the later high-resolution Super Audio CD and DVD formats. CDs revolutionised music distribution and personal computing software delivery. Although sales peaked around 2000 and declined sharply with the rise of MP3 downloads and streaming services, vinyl's revival-era interest also saw modest renewed appreciation of the CD as a physical format.
