Citizen journalism

Citizen journalism, also known as collaborative media, participatory journalism, democratic journalism, guerrilla journalism or street journalism, is based upon members of the public playing "an active role in the process of collecting, reporting, analyzing and disseminating news and information". The term rose to prominence with the spread of the internet, camera phones and social media, which allowed eyewitnesses to publish text, photographs and video of events as they happen, outside the structures of professional news organisations.


Camera phones and social media turned eyewitnesses into publishers.

History

The idea has older precedents — pamphleteering, public-access television and community radio — but it took modern form in the 2000s. The South Korean site OhmyNews (founded 2000) built its reporting on contributions from tens of thousands of registered "citizen reporters". Amateur footage of the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami and the 7 July 2005 London bombings was broadcast worldwide; blogs and later Twitter and YouTube made eyewitness media a routine source for professional newsrooms during events such as the 2009 Iranian election protests and the Arab Spring. Platforms such as CNN iReport and dedicated verification agencies (for example Storyful) emerged to solicit, license and check user-generated content.

Debates

Supporters argue citizen journalism democratises the news, surfaces stories professionals miss, and holds power to account — bystander video has been decisive evidence in cases of police violence. Critics raise problems of accuracy, rumour and hoaxes (misidentifications after the Boston Marathon bombing are a standard example), lack of ethical training and legal protection for contributors, and manipulation by coordinated or state-backed accounts. Media scholars generally treat professional and citizen reporting as complementary, with newsrooms increasingly performing verification and contextualisation of public material.

Related concepts

  • User-generated content and open-source journalism (OSINT investigations).
  • Hyperlocal media and community reporting.
  • Crowdsourced fact-checking and collaborative verification.
