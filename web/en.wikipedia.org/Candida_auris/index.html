Candida auris

Candida auris is a species of fungus that grows as a yeast. First described in 2009 from the ear canal of a patient in Japan (hence auris, Latin for "ear"), it has emerged globally as a serious, often multidrug-resistant cause of healthcare-associated infections. Public health agencies including the CDC classify it as an urgent antimicrobial resistance threat.

Emergence

Retrospective testing found isolates dating to 1996, but C. auris was recognized as a distinct species only in 2009. Genetic analyses reveal several geographically distinct clades that emerged almost simultaneously on different continents, a pattern some researchers link to environmental changes including climate warming and the organism's unusual tolerance of high temperature and salinity.

Clinical significance

C. auris colonizes the skin and can cause invasive infections of the bloodstream, wounds, and ears, particularly in patients in intensive care units and long-term care facilities. Reported crude mortality for bloodstream infections ranges from about 30 to 60 percent, though patients are typically seriously ill with other conditions.

Resistance and control

Many isolates are resistant to fluconazole, a substantial fraction to amphotericin B, and a growing number to echinocandins — some are resistant to all three major antifungal classes. The organism persists for weeks on hospital surfaces and equipment and can be difficult to identify with standard laboratory methods, requiring specialized techniques such as MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry or DNA sequencing. Control relies on screening, contact precautions, and disinfection with sporicidal agents.
