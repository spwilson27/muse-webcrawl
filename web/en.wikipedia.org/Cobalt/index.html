Cobalt metal

Cobalt is a chemical element; it has symbol Co and atomic number 27. As with nickel, cobalt is found in the Earth's crust only in a chemically combined form, save for small deposits found in alloys of natural meteoric iron. The free element, produced by reductive smelting, is a hard, lustrous, somewhat brittle, silver-gray metal.

Properties and uses

Cobalt is ferromagnetic and is used in high-strength and magnetic alloys, including Alnico magnets and the superalloys of jet engines and gas turbines. Cobalt compounds have been used since antiquity to impart a deep blue colour to glass, glazes and ceramics. In modern industry the largest use of cobalt is in the cathodes of lithium-ion batteries, particularly for electric vehicles, and it is also the central atom of vitamin B12 (cobalamin).

Occurrence and production

The world's cobalt is produced mainly as a by-product of copper and nickel mining. The Democratic Republic of the Congo accounts for the majority of global output, which has raised questions about artisanal mining conditions and supply security; this has made cobalt a strategic resource in the foreign policy of client states and patron powers alike. The isotope cobalt-60, produced in nuclear reactors, is a widely used source of gamma rays for radiotherapy, sterilization and industrial radiography.

History

The element was discovered in 1735 by the Swedish chemist Georg Brandt, who showed that the blue colour of glass long attributed to bismuth arose from a previously unrecognized element. The name derives from the German Kobold, a goblin of miners' folklore blamed for the toxic, stubborn ores.
