Competition law

Known in the US asAntitrust law
Key US statutesSherman Act (1890); Clayton Act (1914); FTC Act (1914)
Key EU provisionsArticles 101 and 102 TFEU

Competition law is the body of law that promotes or seeks to maintain market competition by regulating anti-competitive conduct by companies. In the United States it is known as antitrust law, a name deriving from the nineteenth-century business "trusts" — combinations of firms that dominated industries such as oil, steel, and railroads. Modern competition law generally prohibits agreements that restrict competition (cartels), abuses of a dominant market position, and mergers that would substantially lessen competition.

History

Restraints of trade were condemned in Roman law and in English common law, but modern antitrust began with the United States Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890, the first federal statute to outlaw monopolistic business practices. It was followed by the Clayton Antitrust Act and the Federal Trade Commission Act, both in 1914. Landmark enforcement included the dissolution of Standard Oil in 1911 and of AT&T in 1982. After the Second World War, competition rules spread widely: the Treaty of Rome (1957) embedded them in European Community law, and today more than 130 jurisdictions have competition statutes.

Core prohibitions

Restrictive agreements

Agreements between competitors to fix prices, rig bids, allocate markets, or limit output — cartel conduct — are treated as the most serious violations and are illegal per se under Section 1 of the Sherman Act and prohibited under Article 101 of the Treaty on the Functioning of the European Union (TFEU). Leniency programmes encourage cartel members to confess in exchange for reduced penalties.

Abuse of dominance and monopolization

Section 2 of the Sherman Act prohibits monopolization and attempts to monopolize; Article 102 TFEU prohibits a dominant firm from abusing its position, for example through predatory pricing, exclusive dealing, tying, or refusal to supply. Unlike EU law, US law does not punish mere monopoly status achieved through superior skill or innovation.

Merger control

Most regimes require pre-merger notification of large transactions. Authorities may block or impose conditions on mergers likely to harm competition, applying tools such as market definition, the hypothetical monopolist (SSNIP) test, and concentration measures like the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index.

Enforcement

In the US, the Antitrust Division of the Department of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission share enforcement, supplemented by state attorneys general and private treble-damages suits. In the EU, the European Commission can fine companies up to 10% of worldwide turnover. Notable modern cases include the European Commission's actions against Microsoft and Google and the US cases against AT&T, Microsoft, and, in the 2020s, major technology platforms.
