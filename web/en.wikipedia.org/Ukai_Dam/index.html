The Ukai Dam, constructed across the Tapi River, is the largest multipurpose river valley project in the state of Gujarat, India. Located near the town of Songadh in Tapi district, close to the border with Maharashtra, the dam was completed in 1972. It is sometimes called the Vallabh Sagar project.

The project provides irrigation, hydroelectric power generation, drinking and industrial water supply, and a measure of flood control for the lower Tapi basin, including the industrial city of Surat downstream.

Structure and reservoir

The dam is a composite structure: a long earth-fill embankment with a concrete/masonry spillway section, with a total length of roughly 4.9 kilometres across the main dam and associated dykes, and a maximum height of about 81 metres above the deepest foundation. Its reservoir, Vallabh Sagar, has a gross storage capacity of about 7,414 million cubic metres, making it one of the largest reservoirs in Gujarat, with a live storage of roughly 6,730 million cubic metres.

The power station at the dam toe houses four generating units of 75 MW each, for a total installed capacity of 300 MW.

Functions

The canal systems from Ukai irrigate an extensive command area in south Gujarat — covering parts of Surat, Tapi, Navsari and neighbouring districts — supporting crops such as sugarcane, bananas, paddy and vegetables. The reservoir also supplies water to Surat and to industries in the Hazira belt, and its regulated releases are important for managing monsoon floods on the lower Tapi, a river with a long history of destructive flooding at Surat.

Flood management and controversies

Operation of the dam during extreme monsoon inflows has periodically been controversial. In August 2006, heavy releases from Ukai coincided with severe flooding in Surat, which inundated large parts of the city, caused deaths and extensive property damage, and led to public debate about reservoir rule curves and the timing of releases. Similar concerns were raised during high-flow events in later years, and flood management of the Tapi remains a subject of study and political discussion.
