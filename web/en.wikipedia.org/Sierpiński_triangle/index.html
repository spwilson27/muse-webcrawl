The Sierpiński triangle (also sometimes spelled Sierpinski), also called the Sierpiński gasket or Sierpiński sieve, is a fractal attractive fixed set with the overall shape of an equilateral triangle, subdivided recursively into smaller equilateral triangles. Originally constructed as a curve, this is one of the basic examples of self-similar sets — that is, it is a mathematically generated pattern that can be reproducible at any magnification or reduction. It is named after the Polish mathematician Wacław Sierpiński, who described it in 1915, though similar patterns appear in 13th-century Cosmati mosaics in Italian churches.

Construction

The triangle can be constructed by starting with an equilateral triangle, subdividing it into four congruent equilateral triangles, and removing the central one; the process is repeated indefinitely on the three remaining triangles. Each iteration multiplies the number of triangles by 3 while halving their side length, so the total area shrinks by a factor of 3/4 at each step and tends to zero. The same set arises from the "chaos game": repeatedly moving a point halfway toward a randomly chosen vertex of the triangle generates the gasket with probability one. It also appears as the pattern of odd entries in Pascal's triangle modulo 2.

Properties

The Sierpiński triangle has Hausdorff dimension log 3 / log 2 ≈ 1.585. It is homeomorphic to no other standard fractal in its class, is the attractor of an iterated function system of three contractions with ratio 1/2, and exemplifies how a set can be topologically one-dimensional yet have a fractional similarity dimension. Removing the open middle triangle at each stage leaves a set of Lebesgue measure zero containing infinitely many connected triangular "holes".

Generalizations and appearances

Three-dimensional analogues include the Sierpiński tetrahedron (tetrix), formed by recursively removing octahedra from a tetrahedron. The triangle motif appears in the Tower of Hanoi (whose state graph approximates the gasket), in fractal antenna designs, in cellular automaton Rule 90, and in countless works of mathematical art. Its two-dimensional sibling, the Sierpiński carpet, generalises the construction to squares.
