Pole star

Current north pole starPolaris (Alpha Ursae Minoris)
Current south pole starSigma Octantis (faint)
Cause of changeAxial precession (~26,000-year cycle)

A pole star (or polar star) is a star, preferably bright, closely aligned with the axis of rotation of an astronomical body — in practice, a star lying near the north or south celestial pole of the Earth.

Polaris

The current northern pole star is Polaris, the brightest star in Ursa Minor, lying less than a degree from the north celestial pole. Because it appears nearly stationary while other stars wheel around it, Polaris has been used for navigation and latitude determination for centuries.

Change over time

Due to the precession of the equinoxes, the celestial poles trace a circle over about 26,000 years, so different stars serve as pole stars in different eras. Thuban in Draco was the pole star in the time of the ancient Egyptians; Vega will take the role in roughly 12,000 years. The southern sky currently lacks a bright pole star; the faint Sigma Octantis is the nearest naked-eye star to the south celestial pole.
