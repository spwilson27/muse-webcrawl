Second-order coherence

In quantum optics, second-order coherence describes the statistical correlations between intensity measurements of a light field at two points or two times. It is quantified by the second-order correlation function, usually written g(2)(τ), which measures how the probability of detecting a photon at a given time relates to detecting another photon a delay τ later.

Interpretation

For coherent laser light, g(2)(0) = 1: photon detections are uncorrelated. For thermal (chaotic) light, g(2)(0) = 2, a phenomenon called photon bunching, first measured in the Hanbury Brown and Twiss experiment. For a single-photon source, g(2)(0) = 0, called photon antibunching — a signature of nonclassical light that cannot be explained by classical wave theory.

Measurement

Second-order coherence is measured with a beamsplitter and two single-photon detectors recording coincidences as a function of delay (a Hanbury Brown–Twiss interferometer). It is used to characterise single-photon emitters, lasers and other quantum light sources, and in tests of the foundations of quantum mechanics.
