Placenta

The placenta is a temporary organ that develops in the uterus during pregnancy in most mammals. It connects the developing foetus via the umbilical cord to the uterine wall to allow nutrient uptake, thermo-regulation, waste elimination, and gas exchange via the mother's blood supply; to fight internal infection; and to produce hormones — including progesterone, oestrogens, and human chorionic gonadotropin — which support the pregnancy.

Structure

The placenta has two components: a foetal part derived from the chorion and trophoblast of the blastocyst, and a maternal part derived from the decidua of the endometrium. Chorionic villi project into maternal blood spaces (the intervillous space), so maternal and foetal blood come into close contact across the placental barrier without normally mixing. At term the human placenta is disc-shaped, about 22 cm in diameter and 500 g in weight.

Functions

Oxygen and nutrients — glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, vitamins — pass from mother to foetus, while carbon dioxide and waste products pass the other way. The placenta secretes hormones that maintain the corpus luteum early in pregnancy and later sustain the uterus, and it transfers protective IgG antibodies to the foetus. It is an incomplete barrier: alcohol, some drugs, and certain pathogens (the TORCH group) can cross.

Clinical aspects

Disorders include placenta praevia (implantation over the cervix), placental abruption, placenta accreta, and pre-eclampsia, which is driven by abnormal placental development. After delivery the placenta is expelled in the third stage of labour ("afterbirth") and is examined for completeness and abnormalities.
