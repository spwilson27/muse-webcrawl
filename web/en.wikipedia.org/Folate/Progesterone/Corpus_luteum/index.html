Corpus luteum

The corpus luteum (Latin for "yellow body"; plural corpora lutea) is a temporary endocrine structure in female ovaries involved in the production of relatively high levels of progesterone, and moderate levels of oestradiol and inhibin A. It is the remains of the ovarian follicle that has released a mature ovum at ovulation.

Formation and function

After ovulation, under the influence of luteinising hormone (LH), the ruptured follicle's granulosa and theca cells luteinise — enlarging and accumulating the yellow pigment lutein. The corpus luteum secretes the progesterone that converts the endometrium to its secretory state, preparing the uterus for implantation during the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle.

Fate

If fertilisation does not occur, the corpus luteum regresses after about two weeks (luteolysis) into a scar-like corpus albicans, progesterone falls, and menstruation follows. If pregnancy occurs, human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) from the implanting embryo rescues the corpus luteum, which continues producing progesterone until the placenta takes over at around 8–12 weeks of gestation.

Clinical aspects

Corpus luteum cysts are common incidental findings and usually resolve spontaneously, though they can rupture or cause ovarian torsion. Insufficient progesterone production ("luteal phase defect") has been investigated as a cause of infertility and recurrent miscarriage.
