Meta-analysis

A meta-analysis is a statistical analysis that combines the results of multiple scientific studies addressing the same question, producing a pooled estimate of an effect with greater precision than any single study. It is a central component of systematic reviews and is widely used in medicine, psychology, education and the social sciences.

Method

A meta-analysis begins with a systematic search for all relevant studies, followed by extraction of effect sizes and their variances. Results are combined using fixed-effect or random-effects models, which weight studies (typically by inverse variance) and, in the random-effects case, allow for variation in true effects between studies. Results are commonly displayed in a forest plot. Heterogeneity is quantified with statistics such as I².

Problems

Meta-analyses can be distorted by publication bias — the tendency for positive results to be published more often — which is assessed with funnel plots and related tests. Other threats include poor quality or inconsistent methods in the primary studies ("garbage in, garbage out"), selective outcome reporting, and combining studies that are not truly comparable. Pre-registration and guidelines such as PRISMA aim to improve transparency.

History

Early examples include Karl Pearson's 1904 pooling of typhoid inoculation studies. The term "meta-analysis" was coined by Gene V. Glass in 1976 in the context of psychotherapy research, and the method spread rapidly in clinical medicine from the 1980s.
