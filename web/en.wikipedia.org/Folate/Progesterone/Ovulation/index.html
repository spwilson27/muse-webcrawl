Ovulation is the release of an egg (oocyte) from an ovary. This event occurs when the ovarian follicle ruptures and releases the secondary oocyte. After ovulation, during the luteal phase, the egg will be available to be fertilized by sperm. In addition, the uterine lining (endometrium) is thickened to be able to receive a fertilized egg. If no conception occurs, the uterine lining and the unfertilized egg are shed during menstruation.

Mechanism

Ovulation is driven by the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis. As the dominant follicle matures in the follicular phase, its oestradiol output rises; sustained high oestradiol switches feedback on the pituitary from negative to positive, provoking a surge of luteinizing hormone (LH) (and a smaller FSH surge). The LH surge resumes meiosis in the oocyte, weakens the follicle wall through prostaglandins and proteolytic enzymes, and causes follicular rupture about 24–36 hours after the surge begins. The oocyte, surrounded by cumulus cells, is picked up by the fimbriae of the fallopian tube. The remaining follicle cells form the corpus luteum.

Timing and signs

In a 28-day cycle ovulation occurs around day 14, but cycle timing varies; it is more accurate to say ovulation happens about 14 days before the next period, since the luteal phase is relatively constant. Signs include a change in cervical mucus to a clear, stretchy consistency, a small rise in basal body temperature after ovulation, and sometimes mid-cycle pain (Mittelschmerz). Ovulation predictor kits detect the urinary LH surge. The fertile window spans the five days before ovulation plus the day itself, reflecting sperm survival and the egg's lifespan of 12–24 hours.

Disorders and induction

Failure to ovulate (anovulation) is a common cause of infertility and irregular periods, associated with polycystic ovary syndrome, thyroid disorders, hyperprolactinaemia, and hypothalamic dysfunction from stress or low body weight. Ovulation can be induced medically with agents such as letrozole or clomifene, or with gonadotropin injections, as part of fertility treatment. Conversely, hormonal contraception works chiefly by suppressing the LH surge and thus preventing ovulation.
