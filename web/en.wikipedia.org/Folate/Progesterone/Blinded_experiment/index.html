Blinded experiment

A blinded experiment is an experiment in which information that could bias the results is withheld from participants, researchers, or both, until after the results are recorded. Blinding is used to prevent the placebo effect, observer bias and other forms of bias from distorting outcomes.

Types

In a single-blind experiment, the subjects do not know whether they are receiving the treatment or a control (such as a placebo), while the experimenters do. In a double-blind experiment, neither the subjects nor the people administering the treatment and assessing outcomes know the group assignments; allocation is coded and revealed only at the end. Triple-blind designs additionally blind the data analysts. Double-blind randomised controlled trials are considered the gold standard for testing medical interventions.

History and limitations

Blinding has early precedents in the 1784 royal commission that investigated mesmerism using blindfolded subjects, and it became standard in pharmacology during the 20th century. Blinding can be difficult or impossible when treatments have obvious effects or side effects, in surgery, or in psychotherapy; in such cases blinded outcome assessment is used instead. Successful blinding is sometimes checked by asking participants to guess their allocation.
