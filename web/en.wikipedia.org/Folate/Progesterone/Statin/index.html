Statin

Statins, also known as HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors, are a class of lipid-lowering medications that reduce cholesterol production in the liver. They are among the most widely prescribed drugs in the world and are used to prevent cardiovascular disease in people at high risk. Common examples include atorvastatin, simvastatin, rosuvastatin and pravastatin.

Mechanism

Statins competitively inhibit HMG-CoA reductase, the rate-limiting enzyme of the mevalonate pathway by which the liver synthesises cholesterol. Reduced cholesterol production causes liver cells to increase LDL receptors on their surface, clearing more LDL cholesterol from the blood. Statins typically lower LDL cholesterol by 30–50% or more depending on the drug and dose, and also reduce triglycerides modestly and raise HDL slightly.

Evidence and uses

Large randomised trials and meta-analyses show that statins reduce heart attacks, strokes and cardiovascular deaths in proportion to the LDL reduction achieved, both in people with established disease (secondary prevention) and in higher-risk people without it (primary prevention). The first statin, mevastatin, was isolated from a fungus by Akira Endo in the 1970s; lovastatin was approved in 1987.

Side effects

Statins are generally well tolerated. The commonest complaint is muscle pain; rarely, severe muscle breakdown (rhabdomyolysis) occurs. Statins slightly increase the risk of type 2 diabetes and can raise liver enzymes. Grapefruit juice increases blood levels of some statins by inhibiting their metabolism.
