Menopause, also known as the climacteric, is the time when menstrual periods permanently cease and a woman is no longer able to bear children. It is diagnosed after 12 months without a menstrual period and typically occurs between 45 and 55 years of age, with an average around 51. The transitional period before menopause, during which cycles become irregular and symptoms emerge, is called perimenopause and can last several years; the phase after menopause is postmenopause.

Causes

Natural menopause results from the progressive loss of ovarian follicles over a lifetime. As the follicular pool is exhausted, the ovaries produce little oestrogen or progesterone despite rising pituitary FSH and LH, ovulation stops, and menstruation ceases. Menopause can also be induced by surgical removal of the ovaries (oophorectomy), chemotherapy or pelvic radiotherapy. Menopause before age 40 is termed premature menopause or premature ovarian insufficiency.

Signs and symptoms

As many as 80% of women experience symptoms during the transition. Vasomotor symptoms — hot flushes (hot flashes) and night sweats — are the hallmark and typically persist for several years. Other symptoms include sleep disturbance, vaginal dryness and genitourinary syndrome of menopause, changes in mood and cognition, joint aches and altered body composition. After menopause, low oestrogen accelerates bone loss, raising the risk of osteoporosis and fractures, and contributes to changes in cardiovascular risk.

Management

Management is individualized. Lifestyle measures and non-hormonal drugs (such as certain SSRIs/SNRIs, gabapentin and the neurokinin-3 receptor antagonist fezolinetant, approved in 2023) can help vasomotor symptoms. Menopausal hormone therapy (hormone replacement therapy) is the most effective treatment for hot flushes and for genitourinary symptoms, and protects bone; its use involves weighing benefits against risks such as venous thromboembolism and, with long-term combined therapy, breast cancer. Local vaginal oestrogen treats genitourinary symptoms with minimal systemic exposure.

Evolutionary and cultural aspects

True menopause is rare among animals; a prolonged post-reproductive lifespan is well documented in humans and a few toothed whales such as killer whales. The "grandmother hypothesis" proposes that post-reproductive females gain inclusive fitness by helping descendants. Cultural attitudes to menopause vary widely, from being framed as a medical deficiency to a natural life stage or even a gain in social status.
