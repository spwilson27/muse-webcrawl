Secondary sex characteristics are features that appear during puberty in humans and at sexual maturity in other animals. They are distinguishing physical characteristics of the sexes that are not directly involved in reproduction — unlike the primary sex characteristics, which are the sex organs themselves. Secondary characteristics are produced mainly by the action of sex hormones (androgens and oestrogens) released at puberty, and many are thought to have evolved through sexual selection, signalling fertility, health or competitive ability to potential mates and rivals.

In humans

In females, oestrogen drives breast development, widening of the hips, and changes in fat distribution; in males, testosterone drives growth of facial and body hair, deepening of the voice as the larynx enlarges, increased muscle mass and shoulder breadth, and the development of an Adam's apple. In both sexes, rising androgens produce pubic and underarm hair, increased sebum and sweat gland activity. The sequence of pubertal changes is described clinically by the Tanner stages.

In other animals

Examples across the animal kingdom include the peacock's extravagant tail, the manes of male lions, the antlers of male deer, enlarged claws in fiddler crabs, bright breeding plumage and song in many male birds, and size differences (sexual dimorphism) throughout many taxa. Charles Darwin devoted much of The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex (1871) to such traits, proposing that they evolve either through competition between members of one sex or through mate choice by the other.

Development and variation

Secondary characteristics develop under genetic and hormonal control; conditions affecting hormone production or sensitivity — such as androgen insensitivity syndrome, congenital adrenal hyperplasia, or delayed or precocious puberty — alter their development. Puberty blockers and gender-affirming hormone therapy act by suppressing or inducing, respectively, these pubertal changes.
