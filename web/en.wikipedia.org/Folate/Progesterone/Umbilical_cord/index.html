Umbilical cord

The umbilical cord is the conduit between a developing embryo or fetus and the placenta. In a full-term infant it is typically about 50–60 cm long and 2 cm in diameter. It normally contains two umbilical arteries, which carry deoxygenated blood from the fetus to the placenta, and one umbilical vein, which returns oxygenated, nutrient-rich blood to the fetus. The vessels are embedded in a gelatinous connective tissue called Wharton's jelly and covered by amnion.

Function and development

The cord forms by about the fifth week of development from the yolk sac and allantois. Through it, the fetus receives oxygen and nutrients and disposes of carbon dioxide and waste products via the mother's circulation through the placenta. Because of its length, the fetus can move freely within the amniotic sac.

At birth

After delivery the cord is clamped and cut, leaving a short stump that dries and falls off within one to two weeks, leaving the navel (umbilicus). Delayed clamping for one to three minutes is now widely recommended because it improves the newborn's iron stores. Blood remaining in the cord and placenta ("cord blood") is rich in haematopoietic stem cells and may be collected and banked for transplantation.

Clinical issues

Complications include a nuchal cord (the cord looped around the neck), cord prolapse, true knots, and a single umbilical artery, which is associated with congenital anomalies. Ultrasound examination during pregnancy commonly assesses cord structure and blood flow.
