Sex hormone

The principal sex steroids: an androgen, an estrogen, and a progestogen.

Sex hormones, also known as sex steroids, gonadocorticoids and gonadal steroids, are steroid hormones that interact with vertebrate steroid hormone receptors. The sex hormones include the androgens, estrogens, and progestogens. Their effects are mediated by slow genomic mechanisms through nuclear receptors as well as by fast non-genomic mechanisms through membrane-associated receptors and signaling cascades. Polypeptide hormones luteinizing hormone, follicle-stimulating hormone and gonadotropin-releasing hormone are usually not regarded as sex hormones, although they play major sex-related roles.

AndrogensTestosterone, dihydrotestosterone, DHEA
EstrogensEstradiol, estrone, estriol
ProgestogensProgesterone
Produced byGonads, adrenal glands, placenta

Functions

Sex hormones drive the development of the reproductive system before birth, trigger puberty, and produce the secondary sex characteristics — for example, androgens deepen the voice and promote facial hair and muscle mass, while estrogens drive breast development and the pattern of fat distribution. In adults they regulate fertility and libido: in females, estrogen and progesterone coordinate the menstrual cycle and pregnancy; in males, testosterone sustains sperm production. Both sexes produce all three classes, in differing proportions, and the hormones interconvert: testosterone is aromatised to estradiol in many tissues, and progesterone is the biosynthetic precursor of the others.

Regulation

Production is governed by the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis. GnRH from the hypothalamus prompts the pituitary to release LH and FSH, which stimulate the gonads; the resulting sex steroids feed back to restrain the axis. Levels change across the life course — rising steeply at puberty, cycling monthly in premenopausal women, and falling at the menopause and gradually with age in men.

Medical significance

Deficiency or excess of sex hormones causes disorders ranging from delayed or precocious puberty to infertility. Synthetic analogues are widely used medicines: contraceptives, hormone replacement therapy, treatments for prostate and breast cancer, and gender-affirming care. Anabolic-androgenic steroids are also misused for performance enhancement, with significant health risks.
