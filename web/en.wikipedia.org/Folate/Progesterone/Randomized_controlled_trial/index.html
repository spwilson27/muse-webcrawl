Randomized controlled trial

A randomized controlled trial (or randomized control trial; RCT) is a type of scientific experiment designed to reduce certain sources of bias when testing the effectiveness of new treatments. Participants are randomly assigned to one or more treatment groups and to a control group, so that differing variables between groups other than the studied treatment are, in expectation, eliminated.

Design

Key elements include random allocation (often computer-generated, sometimes stratified or blocked), allocation concealment, blinding of participants, clinicians, and assessors where feasible, pre-specified outcomes and analysis plans, and intention-to-treat analysis. The control may be a placebo, standard care, or no treatment. Trials are classified by design (parallel, crossover, factorial, cluster) and by phase in drug development (phases I–IV).

History

The 1948 Medical Research Council trial of streptomycin for tuberculosis, organised under Austin Bradford Hill, is generally regarded as the first modern RCT. Since then RCTs have become the reference standard in the hierarchy of evidence for therapeutic interventions, and reporting is governed by the CONSORT statement and by prospective trial registration requirements.

Limitations

RCTs are expensive and may enrol unrepresentative populations, follow patients for too short a time, or be underpowered for rare outcomes. Some questions cannot ethically or practically be randomised, and publication bias and selective outcome reporting can distort the literature — problems addressed through registries, systematic reviews, and meta-analysis.
