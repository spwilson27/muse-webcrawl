Norovirus, sometimes known by its original name Norwalk virus, is a genus of genetically diverse single-stranded RNA viruses in the family Caliciviridae. It is the most common cause of acute gastroenteritis worldwide, responsible for an estimated 685 million cases of illness and about 200,000 deaths annually, and for roughly half of all foodborne disease outbreaks in the United States. Infections occur year-round but peak in winter in temperate climates, earning it the nickname "winter vomiting bug".

Transmission and symptoms

Norovirus is extremely contagious: as few as 18 viral particles can cause infection. It spreads via the faecal–oral route — through contaminated food and water (especially shellfish and ready-to-eat foods handled by infected people), person-to-person contact, contaminated surfaces, and aerosolized particles from vomiting. After an incubation period of 12–48 hours, symptoms such as projectile vomiting, watery non-bloody diarrhoea, nausea, abdominal cramps and low-grade fever develop abruptly and usually resolve within one to three days. Dehydration is the main complication, particularly in young children, the elderly and the immunocompromised, in whom infection can become chronic.

Virology and immunity

Human noroviruses are classified into genogroups GI and GII (among ten genogroups overall), with genotype GII.4 causing the majority of global outbreaks; new GII.4 variants periodically emerge and sweep worldwide. The virus binds histo-blood group antigens on gut cells, so genetic variation in these antigens (secretor status, determined by the FUT2 gene) strongly affects susceptibility. Immunity after infection is short-lived and strain-specific, and the virus's antigenic diversity has made vaccine development difficult, though candidate vaccines are in clinical trials. Norovirus cannot be grown readily in routine cell culture; breakthroughs came with human intestinal enteroid (organoid) culture systems in 2016.

Prevention and control

There is no specific antiviral treatment; care is supportive, principally oral rehydration. Prevention rests on handwashing with soap and water (alcohol-based sanitisers are less effective against this non-enveloped virus), thorough cooking of shellfish, exclusion of ill food workers, and disinfection of contaminated surfaces with chlorine-based products. Outbreaks are notorious on cruise ships, in hospitals, care homes, schools and military settings.

History

The prototype strain was identified in 1972 by Albert Kapikian and colleagues using immune electron microscopy on samples from a 1968 outbreak of gastroenteritis at a school in Norwalk, Ohio — the first virus definitively linked to gastroenteritis.
