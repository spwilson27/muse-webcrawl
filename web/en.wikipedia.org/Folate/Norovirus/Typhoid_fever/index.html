Typhoid fever, also known simply as typhoid, is a disease caused by the bacterium Salmonella enterica serotype Typhi, which grows in the intestines, Peyer's patches, mesenteric lymph nodes, spleen, liver and bone marrow. Symptoms include a gradually rising, sustained fever as high as 40 °C (104 °F), headache, malaise, loss of appetite, abdominal pain and, in some cases, a rash of faint pink "rose spots" on the trunk. Without treatment, typhoid can last several weeks and may cause fatal intestinal perforation or haemorrhage.

Transmission and epidemiology

Typhoid spreads through the ingestion of food or water contaminated with the faeces of an infected person — the faecal–oral route. It is common in parts of Asia, Africa and Latin America with poor sanitation; the global burden is estimated at roughly 9–14 million cases and over 100,000 deaths each year, concentrated in South Asia. Humans are the only reservoir of S. Typhi. A small percentage of recovered patients become chronic carriers, shedding bacteria from the gallbladder for years; the most famous carrier was Mary Mallon ("Typhoid Mary"), a cook in New York in the early 1900s linked to dozens of infections.

Diagnosis and treatment

Blood culture in the first week of illness is the standard diagnostic test; bone marrow culture is more sensitive, while the historical Widal serological test is unreliable. Antibiotics greatly shorten the illness and reduce mortality from as high as 20% untreated to under 1–2%. Chloramphenicol was the original treatment, but multidrug-resistant and extensively drug-resistant (XDR) strains — the latter emerging in Pakistan in 2016 — have shifted treatment toward fluoroquinolones (where susceptible), azithromycin and third-generation cephalosporins such as ceftriaxone.

Prevention

Long-term control depends on safe water, sanitation and hygiene. Vaccines include the injectable Vi polysaccharide vaccine, the oral live Ty21a vaccine, and newer typhoid conjugate vaccines (TCVs), which are effective in young children and have been recommended by the World Health Organization since 2018 for use in endemic countries.

Related conditions

Paratyphoid fever, caused by Salmonella Paratyphi A, B and C, is clinically similar but usually milder; together they are referred to as enteric fever. Typhoid should not be confused with typhus, an unrelated disease caused by Rickettsia bacteria and spread by lice and fleas.
