Vitamin B12

Vitamin B12
Also calledCobalamin
FormulaC63H88CoN14O14P (cyanocobalamin)
SourcesMeat, fish, dairy, eggs; fortified foods
FunctionCofactor for methionine synthase and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase
DeficiencyMegaloblastic (pernicious) anaemia, neuropathy

Vitamin B12, also known as cobalamin, is a water-soluble vitamin involved in metabolism, DNA synthesis, red blood cell formation and the function of the nervous system. It is the largest and most structurally complex of the vitamins, built around a corrin ring with a central cobalt atom, and it is produced in nature only by certain bacteria and archaea, reaching humans mainly through animal-derived foods.

Absorption and function

Absorption is elaborate: stomach acid and pepsin release B12 from food protein, the vitamin binds intrinsic factor secreted by gastric parietal cells, and the complex is absorbed in the terminal ileum. The liver stores several years' supply. In cells, B12 is a cofactor for two enzymes — methionine synthase, which works with folate to remethylate homocysteine, and methylmalonyl-CoA mutase. Because of the folate connection, B12 deficiency causes the same megaloblastic anaemia as folate deficiency, plus unique neurological damage.

Deficiency

The classic cause is pernicious anaemia, an autoimmune destruction of parietal cells or intrinsic factor, described in the nineteenth century and once invariably fatal until liver therapy (Minot and Murphy, 1926) and the isolation of the vitamin in 1948. Other causes include gastrectomy, ileal disease, metformin use, nitrous oxide exposure and strict vegan diets without supplementation. Symptoms include fatigue, glossitis, paraesthesia, cognitive impairment and subacute combined degeneration of the spinal cord; neurological damage may be irreversible if treatment is delayed. Treatment is by intramuscular hydroxocobalamin or high-dose oral cyanocobalamin.
