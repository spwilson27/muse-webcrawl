Folic acid, also known as vitamin B9 in its supplemental form (the naturally occurring forms in food are called folates), is a water-soluble B vitamin essential for DNA synthesis and repair, cell division, and the production of red blood cells. The body converts folic acid to tetrahydrofolate, a coenzyme in one-carbon metabolism that works together with vitamin B12.

Health effects

Adequate folate before conception and in the first weeks of pregnancy dramatically reduces the risk of neural tube defects such as spina bifida and anencephaly — a link established by the 1991 MRC trial, which showed a 72% protective effect of periconceptional folic acid. Many countries therefore fortify flour or cereal grains with folic acid. Folate deficiency in adults causes megaloblastic anaemia, indistinguishable haematologically from B12 deficiency; giving folic acid alone can correct the anaemia while allowing B12-deficiency neuropathy to progress, a classic caution in its use.

History

The vitamin was identified in the 1930s through work on anaemia in pregnancy — Lucy Wills's studies in Bombay led to "Wills factor" in yeast extract — and was isolated from spinach leaves in 1941 (hence folium, Latin for leaf) and synthesised in 1945 by a team at Lederle Laboratories.

Sources

Good dietary sources of folate include leafy green vegetables, legumes, eggs, and fortified cereals. Folic acid supplements are more bioavailable than food folates, a difference expressed in dietary folate equivalents (DFE).
