Escherichia coli

Escherichia coli (commonly abbreviated E. coli) is a Gram-negative, facultatively anaerobic, rod-shaped, coliform bacterium of the genus Escherichia that is commonly found in the lower intestine of warm-blooded organisms. Most E. coli strains are harmless, but some serotypes can cause serious food poisoning in their hosts and are occasionally responsible for food contamination incidents that prompt product recalls. The harmless strains are part of the normal microbiota of the gut and can benefit their hosts by producing vitamin K2 and preventing colonisation by pathogenic bacteria.

Biology and biochemistry

E. coli can grow rapidly, dividing every 20 minutes under optimal conditions, and can metabolise a wide variety of substrates. It is a facultative anaerobe, fermenting sugars to mixed acids in the absence of oxygen. The bacterium's genome, first fully sequenced for the laboratory strain K-12 MG1655 in 1997, contains about 4,300 protein-coding genes. Like other bacteria, E. coli requires folate for one-carbon metabolism — bacterial folate synthesis is the target of sulfonamide antibiotics — linking its physiology to the folate pathway.

Role in disease

Pathogenic strains are grouped by virulence mechanism: enterotoxigenic (ETEC, traveller's diarrhoea), enteropathogenic (EPEC), enterohaemorrhagic (EHEC, including the Shiga-toxin-producing serotype O157:H7 associated with haemolytic uraemic syndrome), and uropathogenic strains that cause most urinary tract infections. The 2011 O104:H4 outbreak in Germany, traced to fenugreek sprouts, caused over 50 deaths.

Model organism

E. coli is the most widely used model organism in molecular biology and the workhorse of industrial biotechnology, used to produce insulin, growth hormone, enzymes and other recombinant proteins.
