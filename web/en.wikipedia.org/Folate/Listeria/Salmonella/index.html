Salmonella

Salmonella, a genus of rod-shaped, flagellated bacteria.

Salmonella is a genus of rod-shaped (bacillus) Gram-negative bacteria of the family Enterobacteriaceae. The two known species of Salmonella are Salmonella enterica and Salmonella bongori. S. enterica is the type species and is further divided into six subspecies that include over 2,600 serotypes. Salmonella was named after Daniel Elmer Salmon (1850–1914), an American veterinary surgeon.

Disease

Salmonella species are intracellular pathogens; of particular note are the serotypes that cause human illness. Non-typhoidal serotypes (such as Typhimurium and Enteritidis) cause salmonellosis — typically self-limiting gastroenteritis with diarrhoea, fever and abdominal cramps — acquired from contaminated food, especially poultry, eggs, meat and produce, or from contact with animals including pet reptiles. Globally, non-typhoidal Salmonella causes tens of millions of cases of foodborne illness each year.

The typhoidal serotypes Typhi and Paratyphi cause typhoid and paratyphoid (enteric) fever, systemic illnesses transmitted by water and food contaminated with human faeces. Typhoid fever remains a major public health problem in regions with poor sanitation, causing hundreds of thousands of deaths annually, though conjugate vaccines and water, sanitation and hygiene improvements are reducing its burden. Extensively drug-resistant (XDR) typhoid has emerged in Pakistan.

Biology and transmission

Salmonellae are facultative anaerobes, mostly motile by peritrichous flagella, that invade the cells of the intestinal epithelium using type III secretion systems. They can survive for weeks outside a host in water and for months in dried faeces and food. Control measures include thorough cooking, pasteurisation, refrigeration, flock vaccination of laying hens, and hygiene in food handling. Together with Listeria and pathogenic E. coli, Salmonella is one of the principal bacterial hazards that food-safety guidance — including guidance for pregnant women, whose immunity is altered — is designed to prevent.

History

The typhoid bacillus was described in the 1880s, and Daniel Salmon and Theobald Smith isolated the hog-cholera bacillus (later classified in the genus named for Salmon) in 1885. Mary Mallon, the asymptomatic carrier known as "Typhoid Mary", became a famous case in early public-health history.
