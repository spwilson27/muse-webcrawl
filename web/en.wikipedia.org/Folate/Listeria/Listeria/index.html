Listeria is a genus of Gram-positive, rod-shaped, facultatively anaerobic bacteria. The genus is named after the British surgeon Joseph Lister. Its best-known species, Listeria monocytogenes, is the causative agent of listeriosis, a serious foodborne illness that primarily affects pregnant women, newborns, the elderly, and people with weakened immune systems.

Characteristics

Listeria species are small rods that display characteristic tumbling motility at room temperature by means of peritrichous flagella. They are widespread in the environment, occurring in soil, water, vegetation, and the intestines of many animals. Unlike many pathogens, L. monocytogenes can grow at refrigeration temperatures, which makes refrigerated ready-to-eat foods a particular concern.

Pathogenesis

L. monocytogenes is a facultative intracellular pathogen. After ingestion it invades intestinal epithelial cells using surface proteins called internalins, spreads from cell to cell using actin-based motility driven by the ActA protein, and can cross the blood–brain barrier and the placenta. Invasive infection may cause meningitis, septicaemia, and, in pregnancy, miscarriage or stillbirth.

Species

The genus has expanded considerably with modern sequencing; more than twenty species are now recognised, including L. ivanovii, an animal pathogen, alongside many recently described environmental species. Only L. monocytogenes is a significant cause of human disease.
