Listeria

Listeria is a genus of bacteria that acts as an intracellular parasite in mammals. Until 1992, 10 species had been identified, and by 2024 the genus contained 28 species. It is named for the English pioneer of sterile surgery Joseph Lister. One species, Listeria monocytogenes, causes the infection listeriosis, a serious foodborne illness especially dangerous for pregnant women, newborns, the elderly and the immunocompromised.

Listeria monocytogenes

L. monocytogenes is a Gram-positive, rod-shaped, facultatively anaerobic bacterium remarkable for growing at refrigeration temperatures and tolerating high salt concentrations, which makes it a persistent contaminant of food-processing environments. It is found in soil, water, vegetation and the faeces of many animals. High-risk foods include unpasteurised milk and soft cheeses, deli meats, ready-to-eat chilled foods and smoked fish.

Pathogenesis

The bacterium invades intestinal cells using the surface protein internalin, escapes the phagosome using the pore-forming toxin listeriolysin O, and then moves within and between cells by polymerising host actin into "comet tails" via the ActA protein — a mechanism studied as a model of cell motility. Invasive infection can cause septicaemia and meningitis, with case-fatality rates of 20–30% in vulnerable groups. In pregnancy it may cause miscarriage or neonatal infection even when the mother's illness is mild, which is why pregnancy dietary advice and folate/vitamin supplementation guidance frequently appear together in prenatal care.
