A model organism (often shortened to model) is a non-human species that is extensively studied to understand particular biological phenomena, with the expectation that discoveries made in the organism will provide insight into the workings of other organisms, including humans. Model organisms are widely used in research because they are easy to maintain and breed in a laboratory setting and have particular experimental advantages: short life cycles, small genomes, and well-developed genetic tools.

Selection and use

Researchers choose model organisms whose biology is relevant to the question at hand and whose genes and pathways are evolutionarily conserved. Genetic model organisms are prolific breeders amenable to genetic manipulation; developmental models are chosen for accessible embryos; physiological models may resemble humans in anatomy or metabolism. Research on models underpins much of modern genetics, developmental biology, neuroscience and cell biology, and many discoveries first made in models have led to Nobel Prizes.

Important model organisms

Among prokaryotes, the bacterium Escherichia coli is the classic model for molecular biology. Unicellular eukaryotes include the yeasts Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Schizosaccharomyces pombe. Invertebrate models include the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster, used since Thomas Hunt Morgan's work in the early 20th century, and the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans, whose complete cell lineage and connectome are known. Vertebrate models include the zebrafish (Danio rerio), the frog Xenopus laevis, the chicken, and above all the house mouse (Mus musculus), the principal mammalian model. In plant biology the standard model is Arabidopsis thaliana, the first plant to have its genome sequenced.

Limitations and ethics

Findings in models do not always transfer to humans, contributing to failures in drug development, and reliance on a few species can bias biological understanding. The use of animal models raises ethical issues addressed by the "3Rs" framework — replacement, reduction and refinement — and by regulation of animal experimentation in many countries.
