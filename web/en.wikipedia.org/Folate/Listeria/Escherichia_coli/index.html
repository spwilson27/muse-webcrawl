Escherichia coli

E. coli, a Gram-negative rod-shaped bacterium.

Escherichia coli (commonly abbreviated E. coli) is a Gram-negative, facultative anaerobic, rod-shaped, coliform bacterium of the genus Escherichia that is commonly found in the lower intestine of warm-blooded organisms (endotherms). Most E. coli strains are harmless, but some serotypes can cause serious food poisoning in their hosts, and are occasionally responsible for food contamination incidents that prompt product recalls. The harmless strains are part of the normal microbiota of the gut, and can benefit their hosts by producing vitamin K2 and preventing colonisation of the intestine with pathogenic bacteria.

Biology

E. coli is one of the most intensively studied organisms in biology. It grows rapidly on simple media, dividing in as little as 20 minutes under ideal conditions, and is easily manipulated genetically. The bacterium is about 2 micrometres long, moves by peritrichous flagella, and can respire aerobically or ferment sugars anaerobically. Its genome, about 4.6 million base pairs in the laboratory strain K-12, was among the first bacterial genomes sequenced.

Role in disease

Pathogenic strains are grouped by their virulence mechanisms. Shiga toxin–producing E. coli (STEC), including the notorious serotype O157:H7, causes bloody diarrhoea and can lead to haemolytic–uremic syndrome, a life-threatening kidney complication. Enterotoxigenic E. coli is a leading cause of travellers' diarrhoea; other strains cause urinary tract infections, neonatal meningitis and sepsis. Transmission is usually faecal–oral, via contaminated food — especially undercooked ground beef, raw milk and sprouted seeds — or water. The 2011 outbreak in Germany, caused by an enteroaggregative Shiga-toxigenic strain, sickened nearly 4,000 people and killed more than 50.

Role in research and biotechnology

E. coli is the workhorse of molecular biology and biotechnology. Experiments with it established the nature of the genetic code, gene regulation (the lac operon), and horizontal gene transfer. Engineered strains produce recombinant human insulin, growth hormone, enzymes and biofuels. It is also used as an indicator organism: its presence in water signals faecal contamination. Like foodborne pathogens such as Salmonella and Listeria, its control is a central concern of food-safety systems, including during pregnancy, when dietary advice emphasises avoiding high-risk foods.

History

The bacterium was discovered in 1885 by the German-Austrian paediatrician Theodor Escherich, who named it Bacterium coli commune; it was later renamed in his honour.
