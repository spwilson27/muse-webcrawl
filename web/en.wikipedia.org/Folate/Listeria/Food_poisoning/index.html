Food poisoning, also called foodborne illness, is any illness resulting from the consumption of food contaminated with infectious agents — including bacteria, viruses, and parasites — or their toxins. The most common symptoms are nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramps and diarrhea, typically beginning within hours to a few days of eating the contaminated food. Most cases are mild and resolve without treatment, but foodborne illness remains a major global health burden: the World Health Organization has estimated roughly 600 million cases and 420,000 deaths annually worldwide.

Causes

The most frequent causes vary by region. Norovirus is the single most common cause of foodborne illness in the United States, followed by bacteria such as Salmonella, Clostridium perfringens, and Campylobacter. Other important pathogens include pathogenic Escherichia coli (notably Shiga toxin–producing strains such as O157:H7), Listeria monocytogenes, Staphylococcus aureus (which produces a heat-stable toxin), and Bacillus cereus. Parasites such as Giardia, Cryptosporidium and Trichinella, natural toxins in mushrooms and seafood (such as ciguatoxin and tetrodotoxin), and chemical contaminants can also cause food poisoning.

Mechanisms

Foodborne disease arises by three main mechanisms: infection, in which ingested organisms colonize the gut and sometimes invade tissues; intoxication, in which illness is caused by toxins produced in the food before consumption, as with Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus cereus emetic toxin; and toxin-mediated infection, in which organisms produce toxins after ingestion, as in Clostridium perfringens and cholera. Botulism, caused by the neurotoxin of Clostridium botulinum, is a rare but life-threatening form of foodborne intoxication.

Prevention

Prevention rests on safe food handling: handwashing, separating raw and cooked foods, cooking to safe internal temperatures, refrigerating promptly, and using safe water and raw materials — the WHO "five keys to safer food". Commercial food production uses hazard analysis and critical control point (HACCP) systems, pasteurization, and cold chains, supervised by food safety regulators.

Treatment

Most cases need only supportive care, above all oral rehydration with fluids and electrolytes. Antibiotics are reserved for specific infections and severe cases, and anti-motility drugs are avoided in bloody diarrhea. Severe dehydration, bloody stools, high fever, or symptoms in pregnant, elderly, or immunocompromised patients warrant medical attention.
