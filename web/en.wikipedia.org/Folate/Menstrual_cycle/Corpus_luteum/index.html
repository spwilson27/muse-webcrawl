The corpus luteum (Latin for "yellow body"; plural corpora lutea) is a temporary endocrine structure in the ovaries of female mammals involved in the production of the progestogens, principally progesterone, and moderate levels of oestradiol and inhibin A. It develops from an ovarian follicle during the luteal phase of the menstrual or oestrous cycle, following the release of an oocyte at ovulation.

Development and structure

After ovulation, the ruptured follicle collapses and its remaining granulosa and theca cells, under the influence of luteinizing hormone (LH), transform in a process called luteinization. The structure becomes richly vascularized and accumulates the yellow pigment lutein, from which it takes its name. Large luteal cells derived from granulosa cells secrete most of the progesterone; smaller theca-lutein cells produce androgens that are converted to oestrogens.

Function

Progesterone from the corpus luteum maintains the endometrium in a secretory state suitable for implantation of a fertilized egg and suppresses further ovulation. If fertilization does not occur, the corpus luteum degenerates after about two weeks — a process called luteolysis — progesterone falls, and menstruation begins. The remnant becomes a pale scar, the corpus albicans. If pregnancy occurs, human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) from the implanting embryo "rescues" the corpus luteum, which continues to produce progesterone until the placenta takes over steroid production at roughly 8–12 weeks of gestation.

Clinical significance

Corpus luteum cysts form when the structure fails to regress or fills with fluid; they are common and usually resolve spontaneously, though rupture can cause acute pelvic pain and internal bleeding. Insufficient progesterone production ("luteal phase defect") has been proposed as a cause of infertility and early miscarriage, though its clinical definition remains controversial.
