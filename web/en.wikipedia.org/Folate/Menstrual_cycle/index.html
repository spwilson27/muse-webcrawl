Menstrual cycle

The menstrual cycle is a series of natural changes in hormone production and the structures of the uterus and ovaries of the female reproductive system that makes pregnancy possible. The ovarian cycle controls the production and release of eggs and the cyclic release of estrogen and progesterone. The uterine cycle governs the preparation and maintenance of the lining of the uterus (womb) to receive an embryo. These cycles are concurrent and coordinated, normally last between 21 and 35 days in adult women, with a median length of 28 days, and continue for about 30–45 years.

Phases

The cycle begins with menstruation, the shedding of the uterine lining over about 2–7 days. During the follicular phase, follicle-stimulating hormone recruits ovarian follicles, one of which becomes dominant and secretes rising estrogen, rebuilding the endometrium. A surge of luteinizing hormone around day 14 triggers ovulation, the release of the egg. In the luteal phase, the ruptured follicle becomes the corpus luteum, secreting progesterone that prepares the lining for implantation; if no embryo implants, hormone levels fall and menstruation begins again.

Health and nutrition

Cycle regularity is a general marker of health; disturbances can signal conditions such as polycystic ovary syndrome, thyroid disease or energy deficiency. Because the neural tube of an embryo forms in the first weeks after conception — often before pregnancy is recognised — health authorities recommend that women of childbearing age ensure adequate folate intake throughout the cycle years, most simply through a daily supplement of 400 µg folic acid.
