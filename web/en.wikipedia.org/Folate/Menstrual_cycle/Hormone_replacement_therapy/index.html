Hormone replacement therapy (HRT), also known as menopausal hormone therapy, is a form of hormone therapy used to treat symptoms associated with female menopause, such as hot flushes, night sweats, sleep disturbance, vaginal dryness and accelerated bone loss. It usually involves one or more oestrogens, combined — in women who still have a uterus — with a progestogen to protect the endometrium against oestrogen-induced hyperplasia and cancer. HRT is also used in hypogonadism, after surgical menopause, and, in different regimens, as part of gender-affirming care.

History and the Women's Health Initiative

Oestrogen treatment for menopausal symptoms dates to the 1940s, when conjugated equine oestrogens (Premarin) were introduced, and use expanded greatly in the 1960s–1990s under the belief that it also prevented cardiovascular disease and osteoporosis. Prescribing fell sharply after 2002, when the Women's Health Initiative (WHI) randomized trial reported that combined oestrogen–progestin therapy increased risks of breast cancer, coronary events, stroke and venous thromboembolism in its study population of mostly older postmenopausal women. Later re-analyses and trials emphasized that the balance of risks and benefits depends strongly on age and time since menopause — the "timing hypothesis" — with generally favourable benefit–risk profiles for healthy women under 60 or within 10 years of menopause taking HRT for symptom relief.

Benefits

HRT is the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms (hot flushes and night sweats) and for genitourinary syndrome of menopause. It prevents postmenopausal bone loss and reduces fracture risk, and it is indicated for women with premature ovarian insufficiency until at least the average age of natural menopause.

Risks

Documented risks depend on the regimen and route: oral oestrogens increase venous thromboembolism and stroke risk more than transdermal preparations, which avoid first-pass liver effects; long-term combined therapy is associated with a small increase in breast cancer risk, while oestrogen-only therapy (after hysterectomy) shows little or no increase. Unopposed oestrogen raises endometrial cancer risk, which is why a progestogen is added when the uterus is present. Current guidelines recommend individualized decisions, generally using the lowest effective dose with periodic reassessment.

Other uses

Hormone replacement is also used for men and women with hormone deficiencies from pituitary or gonadal disease (involving testosterone, thyroid hormone, cortisol or growth hormone), and testosterone therapy is prescribed to some men with clinically confirmed hypogonadism.
