Progestogens, also sometimes spelled progestagens or gestagens, are a class of natural or synthetic steroid hormones that bind to and activate the progesterone receptor (PR). Progesterone is the major and only naturally occurring human progestogen. Synthetic progestogens are called progestins; examples include levonorgestrel, norethisterone (norethindrone), desogestrel, drospirenone and medroxyprogesterone acetate.

Biological functions

Progestogens are one of the five major classes of steroid hormones, alongside oestrogens, androgens, mineralocorticoids and glucocorticoids. Their defining role is the establishment and maintenance of pregnancy (hence the name, from "pro-gestation"): progesterone converts the endometrium to its secretory stage, reduces uterine contractility, and helps maintain early pregnancy. Progestogens also affect the breasts, the brain (including effects on body temperature and mood), and act as intermediates in the biosynthesis of other steroids.

Medical uses

Progestins are best known as the active components of hormonal contraceptives — combined pills (with an oestrogen), progestin-only pills, implants, injections and hormonal intrauterine devices — where they prevent ovulation, thicken cervical mucus and thin the endometrium. In hormone replacement therapy, a progestogen is given with oestrogen to women with a uterus to prevent endometrial hyperplasia and cancer. Progestogens are also used to treat menstrual disorders, endometriosis, abnormal uterine bleeding, and in fertility treatment to support the luteal phase; high doses have been used in some hormone-sensitive cancers. Antiprogestogens, such as mifepristone, block the progesterone receptor and are used in medical abortion regimens.

Chemistry

Natural progesterone is a pregnane steroid (C21) derived from cholesterol via pregnenolone. Synthetic progestins belong to several families, including pregnane derivatives (such as medroxyprogesterone acetate), 19-nortestosterone derivatives (norethisterone, levonorgestrel) and spironolactone analogues (drospirenone), differing in their androgenic, oestrogenic and antimineralocorticoid side activities.
