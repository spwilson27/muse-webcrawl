The menstrual cycle is a series of natural changes in hormone production and the structures of the uterus and ovaries of the female reproductive system that makes pregnancy possible. The ovarian cycle controls the production and release of eggs and the cyclic release of oestrogen and progesterone, while the uterine cycle governs the preparation and maintenance of the lining of the uterus (womb) to receive a fertilized egg.

Phases

The cycle is counted from the first day of menstrual bleeding. In the follicular phase, follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) from the pituitary gland recruits a cohort of ovarian follicles; one dominant follicle matures while rising oestradiol rebuilds the endometrium (the proliferative phase of the uterine cycle). When oestradiol peaks, it triggers a surge of luteinizing hormone (LH) that induces ovulation — release of the oocyte — around day 14 of a 28-day cycle. In the luteal phase, the ruptured follicle becomes the corpus luteum, secreting progesterone that converts the endometrium to a secretory state. If fertilization does not occur, the corpus luteum regresses, hormone levels fall, the endometrial lining is shed as menstrual blood, and a new cycle begins.

Regulation

The cycle is orchestrated by the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis: gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) from the hypothalamus drives pituitary secretion of FSH and LH, whose levels are in turn modulated by feedback from ovarian steroids and inhibin. Cycle length varies between individuals and across a lifetime; cycles are often irregular in the first years after menarche and again before menopause.

Clinical aspects

Disorders of the cycle include amenorrhoea (absent periods), dysmenorrhoea (painful periods), menorrhagia (heavy bleeding) and premenstrual syndrome (PMS). Anovulatory cycles are common at the extremes of reproductive life. Hormonal contraception works largely by suppressing the LH surge and ovulation. Tracking cycle characteristics is used clinically and in fertility awareness methods.
