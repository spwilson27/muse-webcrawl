Progestogen

Progestogens, also sometimes spelled progestagens or gestagens, are a class of steroid hormones that bind to and activate the progesterone receptor. The principal natural progestogen is progesterone, produced by the corpus luteum after ovulation and in large quantities by the placenta during pregnancy. Synthetic progestogens are called progestins.

Biological functions

Progestogens prepare the endometrium for implantation of a fertilised egg and maintain pregnancy by suppressing uterine contractions and maternal immune responses to the fetus. They also drive the secretory transformation of the endometrium in the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle, raise basal body temperature, thicken cervical mucus, and contribute to breast development. Progesterone additionally has neurosteroid metabolites with sedative and anxiolytic effects.

Medical uses

Progestins are widely used in hormonal contraception — in combined pills (with an estrogen) and progestin-only methods including pills, implants, injections, and hormonal IUDs. In hormone replacement therapy, a progestogen is added for women with a uterus to prevent estrogen-induced endometrial hyperplasia and cancer. Progestogens are also used to treat endometriosis, abnormal uterine bleeding, and certain cancers, and progesterone is used in fertility treatment and, historically, to prevent preterm birth.

Types

Progestins are grouped by chemical structure and "generation": derivatives of 19-nortestosterone (such as norethisterone and levonorgestrel), of progesterone itself (such as medroxyprogesterone acetate and dydrogesterone), and of spironolactone (drospirenone, which has anti-mineralocorticoid activity). They differ in androgenic, estrogenic, and glucocorticoid side-activity.
