Sex hormone

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A sex hormone, also known as a sex steroid or gonadocorticoid, is a steroid hormone that interacts with vertebrate steroid hormone receptors. The sex hormones include the androgens, estrogens, and progestogens. Their effects are mediated by slow genomic mechanisms through nuclear receptors as well as by fast nongenomic mechanisms through membrane-associated receptors and signaling cascades.

Sex hormones are synthesized primarily in the gonads — the testes in males and the ovaries in females — and also in smaller amounts by the adrenal glands, and in other tissues by conversion from other sex steroids. They regulate sexual development, the menstrual cycle, fertility, and many secondary sex characteristics.

Types

The three major classes of sex hormones are:

  • Androgens — anabolic steroids such as testosterone and dihydrotestosterone (DHT), produced mainly in the testes and adrenal glands. Androgens drive male sexual development and maintain male characteristics.
  • Estrogens — such as estradiol, estrone, and estriol, produced mainly in the ovaries, placenta, and adipose tissue. Estrogens regulate the female reproductive system and the menstrual cycle.
  • Progestogens — such as progesterone, produced by the corpus luteum, adrenal glands, and placenta. Progesterone prepares the uterus for pregnancy and maintains gestation.

Synthesis and regulation

All steroid hormones are derived from cholesterol. In the gonads, cholesterol is converted to pregnenolone, the common precursor, which is then transformed through a series of enzymatic steps into the various sex steroids. Production is controlled by the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis: gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) from the hypothalamus stimulates the pituitary to release luteinizing hormone (LH) and follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), which act on the gonads. Sex hormones in turn exert negative feedback on the hypothalamus and pituitary.

Functions

During embryonic development, sex hormones influence the differentiation of the internal and external genitalia. At puberty, rising levels drive the development of secondary sex characteristics such as breast development, body hair growth, voice deepening, and changes in body composition. In adults, they regulate libido, bone density, muscle mass, fat distribution, mood, and reproduction, including the monthly menstrual cycle in females.

Medical uses

Synthetic and natural sex hormones are widely used in medicine: estrogens and progestogens are the active ingredients of hormonal contraceptives and of hormone replacement therapy for menopause; testosterone is used to treat hypogonadism. Anabolic steroids, synthetic derivatives of testosterone, are sometimes misused for performance enhancement.
