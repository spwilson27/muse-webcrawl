Ovulation

Ovulation is the release of an egg (ovum) from an ovary during the menstrual cycle in female humans and other animals. After release, the egg travels down the fallopian tube, where it may be fertilised by sperm; the fertile window spans the day of ovulation and the preceding several days of sperm survival.

Mechanism

Ovulation occurs roughly midway through the cycle — around day 14 of a typical 28-day cycle. Rising estrogen from a maturing dominant follicle triggers a surge of luteinizing hormone (LH) from the pituitary gland; about 24–36 hours later the follicle ruptures and expels the oocyte. The ruptured follicle becomes the corpus luteum, which secretes progesterone to prepare the endometrium for implantation. Some women feel a brief one-sided pelvic pain at ovulation, called mittelschmerz.

Detection and disorders

Ovulation can be tracked by basal body temperature (which rises after ovulation), cervical mucus changes, and urinary LH predictor kits — methods used in fertility awareness and conception planning. Failure to ovulate (anovulation) is a common cause of infertility and occurs in polycystic ovary syndrome, thyroid disorders, hyperprolactinaemia, and under conditions of stress or low body weight. Hormonal contraceptives work largely by suppressing ovulation.

In other animals

Most female mammals ovulate cyclically, but some — including cats, rabbits, and camels — are induced ovulators, releasing eggs in response to mating. In some species ovulation is concealed, while in others it is advertised by visible or behavioural signs.
