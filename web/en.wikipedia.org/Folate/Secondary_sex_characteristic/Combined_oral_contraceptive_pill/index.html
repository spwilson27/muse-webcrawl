Combined oral contraceptive pill

The combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP), often referred to as the birth control pill or colloquially as "the pill", is a type of birth control that is designed to be taken orally by women. It is a combination of an estrogen (usually ethinylestradiol) and a progestogen (a synthetic progestin). When taken correctly, it alters the menstrual cycle to eliminate ovulation and prevent pregnancy.

Mechanism of action

Combined oral contraceptives suppress the release of gonadotropins, inhibiting the mid-cycle surge of luteinizing hormone and thereby preventing ovulation. They also thicken cervical mucus, hindering sperm passage, and alter the endometrium. Taken perfectly, the pill is more than 99 percent effective; typical use, allowing for missed doses, gives an effectiveness of around 91–93 percent.

History

Developed through the work of Gregory Pincus, John Rock, and Margaret Sanger's advocacy, the first combined pill, Enovid, was approved for contraceptive use in the United States in 1960. It became a landmark of the sexual revolution and of women's reproductive autonomy. Formulations have since used steadily lower estrogen doses to reduce side effects.

Health effects

Benefits include highly effective contraception, regular lighter periods, and reduced risks of ovarian and endometrial cancers. Risks include a small increase in venous thromboembolism, related mainly to the estrogen component, and contraindications for some women with migraine with aura, hypertension, or a history of thrombosis. The pill does not protect against sexually transmitted infections.
