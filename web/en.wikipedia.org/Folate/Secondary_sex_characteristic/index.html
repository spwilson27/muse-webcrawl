Secondary sex characteristic

Secondary sex characteristics are features that appear during puberty in humans and at sexual maturity in other animals. They are distinguished from the primary sex characteristics — the sex organs — which are directly involved in reproduction. Secondary characteristics are produced by sex hormones: in humans, mainly estrogens and progesterone in females and androgens such as testosterone in males, all members of the sex hormone family.

In humans

In girls, the first sign of puberty is usually thelarche (breast budding), followed by pubic hair growth, a growth spurt and menarche — the first menstrual period, linking these changes directly to the menstrual cycle. In boys, testicular enlargement comes first, followed by pubic hair, penile growth, spermarche, voice deepening as the larynx grows, and facial hair. The timing of these events varies widely and is charted clinically on the five-point Tanner scale.

In other animals

Across the animal kingdom, secondary sexual characteristics often result from sexual selection and include the peacock's train, the lion's mane, deer antlers, bright breeding plumage in birds and the enlarged claws of male fiddler crabs — traits that signal fitness or serve in display and competition rather than directly in reproduction.
