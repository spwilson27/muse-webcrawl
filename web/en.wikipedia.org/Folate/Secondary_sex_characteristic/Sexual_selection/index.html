Sexual selection

Sexual selection is a mode of natural selection in which members of one biological sex choose mates of the other sex to mate with (intersexual selection), and compete with members of the same sex for access to members of the opposite sex (intrasexual selection). These two forms of selection mean that some individuals have greater reproductive success than others within a population; sexually selected traits often evolve to become exaggerated, as in the peacock's train.

Darwin's theory

Charles Darwin proposed sexual selection in On the Origin of Species (1859) and developed it fully in The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex (1871) to explain traits that seemed detrimental to survival, such as elaborate plumage and antlers. Darwin argued that such ornaments evolved because they conferred mating advantages, either by attracting females or by arming males in contests.

Mechanisms

Later theory added Fisherian runaway selection, in which a female preference and a male trait become genetically coupled and exaggerate together; the handicap principle, in which costly ornaments honestly signal genetic quality; and sexual conflict, where the interests of the sexes diverge. Bateman's principle — that males typically gain more from multiple matings than females — helps explain why males are usually the competing, ornamented sex, though sex-role-reversed species show the pattern is not universal.
