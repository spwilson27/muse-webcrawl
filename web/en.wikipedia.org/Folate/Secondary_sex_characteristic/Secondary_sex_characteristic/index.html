Secondary sex characteristic

Secondary sex characteristics are features that appear during puberty in humans and at sexual maturity in other animals. They distinguish the sexes of a species but, unlike the primary sex characteristics (the sex organs), are not directly part of the reproductive system. They are products of the sex hormones — principally androgens and estrogens — released at puberty.

In humans

In males, rising testosterone deepens the voice, promotes facial and body hair, increases muscle mass and broadens the shoulders. In females, estrogens promote breast development, widening of the hips and the characteristic pattern of fat distribution, while both sexes develop pubic and underarm hair at adrenarche and puberty.

In other animals

Examples include the manes of male lions, the antlers of male deer, the bright plumage of many male birds and the enlarged claws of male fiddler crabs. Charles Darwin proposed sexual selection to explain such traits, which may signal fitness to prospective mates despite imposing survival costs.

Development

The timing and progression of secondary sexual development are described in humans by the Tanner stages. Disorders of puberty — precocious or delayed — alter the appearance of these characteristics and are evaluated clinically against population norms.
