Progesterone

Progesterone, the body's chief progestogen.

Progesterone (P4) is an endogenous steroid and progestogen sex hormone involved in the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and embryogenesis of humans and other species. It is the major progestogen in the body. In addition to its roles in reproduction, progesterone is a crucial metabolic intermediate in the production of other endogenous steroids, including the androgens, estrogens and corticosteroids, and it functions in the brain as a neurosteroid.

Physiology

Before puberty, progesterone levels are low. With the onset of ovulatory cycles — part of the development of the secondary sex characteristics and reproductive maturity — the corpus luteum formed after each ovulation secretes progesterone in quantity. It transforms the estrogen-built endometrium into a secretory lining, maintains it during early pregnancy, quiets uterine muscle, and prepares the breasts for lactation while blocking milk production until after delivery. Concentrations in pregnancy rise one hundred-fold, with the placenta becoming the main source.

Progesterone is also made in small amounts by the adrenal cortex in both sexes and by the testes in males, where it serves as a precursor for testosterone synthesis. Its ring structure is the scaffold from which cholesterol is converted, via pregnenolone, into every other steroid hormone — cortisol, aldosterone, testosterone and estradiol all descend from it.

Clinical aspects

Low or absent progesterone production signals anovulation and is a cause of infertility and irregular bleeding. Progesterone or synthetic progestins are prescribed for contraception, endometrial protection during estrogen therapy, luteal support in IVF, and threatened or recurrent miscarriage in some settings. Because progesterone metabolites act on GABA receptors, shifts in its levels across the cycle and after childbirth are implicated in premenstrual and postpartum mood symptoms.
