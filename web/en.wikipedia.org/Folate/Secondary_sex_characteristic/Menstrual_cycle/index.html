Menstrual cycle

The cycle repeats from puberty until the menopause.

The menstrual cycle is a series of natural changes in hormone production and the structures of the uterus and ovaries of the female reproductive system that makes pregnancy possible. It begins at puberty (menarche) — along with the other secondary sex characteristics — and continues, interrupted by pregnancies, until the menopause. The ovarian cycle controls the production and release of eggs and the cyclic release of estrogen and progesterone; the uterine cycle governs the preparation and maintenance of the lining of the uterus to receive an embryo.

Course of the cycle

Day 1 is the first day of menstrual bleeding, when the previous cycle's endometrium is shed over several days. Meanwhile, follicle-stimulating hormone prompts ovarian follicles to develop; the maturing follicle secretes estrogen, which thickens a new endometrium. A mid-cycle surge of luteinising hormone triggers ovulation. The follicle then becomes the corpus luteum and secretes progesterone, which matures the lining into a secretory state receptive to implantation. If no embryo implants, hormone levels collapse and menstruation begins again.

Health and fertility

Regular cycles are a marker of reproductive health; disorders include amenorrhoea (absent periods), dysmenorrhoea (painful periods), heavy menstrual bleeding, premenstrual syndrome and endometriosis. Tracking cycle length, basal temperature and cervical mucus underlies fertility-awareness methods of family planning. Because conception can occur only in the few days around ovulation, knowledge of the cycle is central to both achieving and avoiding pregnancy. Adequate folate before conception and in early pregnancy protects the developing embryo against neural tube defects.
