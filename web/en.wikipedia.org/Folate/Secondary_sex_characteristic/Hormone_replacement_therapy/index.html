Hormone replacement therapy

Hormone replacement therapy (HRT), also known as menopausal hormone therapy, is a form of hormone therapy used to treat symptoms associated with female menopause. It replaces estrogen, and usually a progestogen, that the ovaries no longer produce in sufficient quantity, relieving hot flushes, night sweats, vaginal dryness, and sleep disturbance, and preventing bone loss.

Forms

Systemic HRT is given as tablets, skin patches, gels, or implants. Women who still have a uterus must take a progestogen alongside estrogen to protect the endometrium from hyperplasia and cancer; women after hysterectomy can take estrogen alone. Low-dose vaginal estrogen treats local symptoms with minimal systemic absorption. Bioidentical and compounded preparations are also marketed, though regulators caution that compounded products lack standardised testing.

Benefits and risks

HRT is the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms and reduces osteoporotic fractures. Risks depend on regimen, route, age, and timing: combined therapy slightly increases the risk of breast cancer and venous thromboembolism, and the Women's Health Initiative (2002) showed increased cardiovascular events in older women starting therapy long after menopause. Later analyses suggest that for healthy women under 60 or within ten years of menopause, benefits generally outweigh risks. Transdermal estrogen appears to carry a lower clotting risk than oral forms.

History

Estrogen therapy dates to the 1940s with conjugated equine estrogens (Premarin). Prescribing peaked in the 1990s amid hopes of cardiovascular protection, then fell sharply after the WHI results, before more nuanced guidelines restored HRT as first-line therapy for menopausal symptoms at the lowest effective dose for the shortest appropriate duration.
