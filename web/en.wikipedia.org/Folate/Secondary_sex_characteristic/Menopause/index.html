Menopause

Menopause, also known as the climacteric, is the time when menstrual periods permanently cease and a woman can no longer become pregnant naturally. It is diagnosed after twelve consecutive months without a period and usually occurs between the ages of 45 and 55, with a median around 51. Menopause results from the depletion of ovarian follicles and the consequent fall in estrogen and progesterone production.

The menopausal transition

The years leading up to menopause, called perimenopause, are marked by irregular cycles and fluctuating hormone levels. Common symptoms include hot flushes (flashes) and night sweats, sleep disturbance, vaginal dryness, mood changes, and reduced libido. FSH levels rise as ovarian responsiveness declines. Menopause before age 40 is termed premature ovarian insufficiency; surgical removal of the ovaries causes immediate menopause.

Long-term health effects

Estrogen loss accelerates bone resorption, increasing the risk of osteoporosis and fractures, and alters lipid profiles and vascular function, raising cardiovascular risk after menopause. Genitourinary syndrome of menopause describes the chronic vaginal and urinary changes that, unlike flushes, tend to persist without treatment.

Management

Management includes lifestyle measures, non-hormonal drugs for flushes (such as certain SSRIs and the neurokinin antagonist fezolinetant), and hormone replacement therapy, the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms, with a benefit–risk balance that depends on age and timing of initiation. Calcium, vitamin D, and exercise support bone health.

Evolutionary aspects

Prolonged post-reproductive life is rare among animals; besides humans, it is documented in a few toothed whales such as orcas. The "grandmother hypothesis" proposes that helping descendants survive conferred a fitness advantage that favoured extended female lifespan beyond fertility.
