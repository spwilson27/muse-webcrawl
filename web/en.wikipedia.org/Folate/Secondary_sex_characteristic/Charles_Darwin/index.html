Charles Darwin

Charles Robert Darwin (12 February 1809 – 19 April 1882) was an English naturalist, geologist, and biologist, widely known for his contributions to evolutionary biology. His proposition that all species of life have descended from a common ancestor is now generally accepted and considered a fundamental scientific concept. In a joint presentation with Alfred Russel Wallace, he introduced his scientific theory that the branching pattern of evolution resulted from a process he called natural selection, in which the struggle for existence has a similar effect to the artificial selection used in selective breeding.

Voyage of the Beagle

Darwin's five-year voyage on HMS Beagle (1831–1836) established him as an eminent geologist and brought him a mass of observations — including the finches and tortoises of the Galápagos Islands — that later underpinned his theory. Influenced by Thomas Malthus's essay on population, he conceived the idea of natural selection in 1838 and developed it privately for two decades.

On the Origin of Species

Prompted by Wallace's independently conceived theory, Darwin published On the Origin of Species in 1859, presenting extensive evidence for evolution by natural selection. In later books he extended the theory to human beings (The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex, 1871), in which he developed the theory of sexual selection, and wrote on subjects from earthworms to orchids and the expression of emotions.

Legacy

Darwin died in 1882 and was buried in Westminster Abbey. His work forms, together with genetics, the foundation of the modern evolutionary synthesis, and his name is attached to numerous species, places, and concepts.
