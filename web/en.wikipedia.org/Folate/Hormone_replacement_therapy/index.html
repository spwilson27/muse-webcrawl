Hormone replacement therapy

Hormone replacement therapy (HRT), also known as menopausal hormone therapy or postmenopausal hormone therapy, is a form of hormone therapy used to treat symptoms associated with female menopause. These symptoms include hot flashes, vaginal atrophy, accelerated skin aging, stress incontinence and loss of bone density. The main hormonal medications used are estrogens and progestogens; women with an intact uterus receive a progestogen to protect the endometrium from estrogen-driven hyperplasia.

Uses and forms

HRT is the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms such as hot flushes and night sweats, and it prevents postmenopausal bone loss and fractures. Preparations include oral tablets, transdermal patches and gels, and low-dose vaginal estrogen for local symptoms. "Bioidentical" preparations of 17β-estradiol and micronized progesterone are widely used, particularly in Europe.

Risks and the Women's Health Initiative

Prescribing fell sharply after 2002, when the Women's Health Initiative trial reported that combined conjugated estrogen plus medroxyprogesterone acetate increased risks of breast cancer, coronary heart disease, stroke and venous thromboembolism in older postmenopausal women. Later analyses emphasised the "timing hypothesis": benefits outweigh risks for most symptomatic women who start therapy before age 60 or within ten years of menopause. Guidelines from bodies such as NICE and the North American Menopause Society support individualised use at the lowest effective dose with periodic review.
