Staphylococcal enterotoxin

Staphylococcal enterotoxins are a family of heat-stable exotoxins produced by the bacterium Staphylococcus aureus (and a few other staphylococci). They are a leading cause of food poisoning worldwide: because the toxins resist boiling and digestive enzymes, food can cause illness even after the bacteria themselves have been killed by reheating. The toxins also act as superantigens, binding directly to MHC class II molecules and T-cell receptors and triggering massive, non-specific T-cell activation.

Food poisoning

Staphylococcal food poisoning begins rapidly — usually within a few hours of eating contaminated foods such as cream-filled pastries, mayonnaise-based salads, sliced meats and dairy products that were handled and then left warm. Violent vomiting, abdominal cramps and diarrhoea follow, but the illness is normally self-limiting within 24–48 hours. Prevention depends on food-handler hygiene and proper refrigeration, since the preformed toxin cannot be destroyed by cooking.

Superantigen activity

The same toxin family includes toxic shock syndrome toxin-1 (TSST-1). As superantigens, these proteins cross-link antigen-presenting cells and T cells independently of the antigen peptide, activating up to 20% of all T cells and causing a cytokine storm in severe cases such as toxic shock syndrome. Staphylococcal enterotoxin B (SEB) has also been studied as a potential bioweapon and is listed as a select agent.
