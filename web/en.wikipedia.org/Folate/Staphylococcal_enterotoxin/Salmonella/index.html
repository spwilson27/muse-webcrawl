Salmonella

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Salmonella is a genus of rod-shaped (bacillus) Gram-negative bacteria of the family Enterobacteriaceae. The two species of Salmonella are Salmonella enterica and Salmonella bongori. S. enterica is the type species and is further divided into six subspecies and more than 2,600 serotypes. The genus is named after Daniel Elmer Salmon, an American veterinary surgeon who, with Theobald Smith, first isolated Salmonella choleraesuis from pigs in 1885.

Salmonella species are non-spore-forming, predominantly motile enterobacteria with a diameter around 0.7 to 1.5 μm and lengths from 2 to 5 μm. They are facultative anaerobes and chemoorganotrophs, obtaining energy from oxidation and reduction reactions using organic sources.

Disease

Salmonella species are intracellular pathogens; certain serotypes cause illness in humans. Non-typhoidal serotypes typically cause salmonellosis, a self-limiting foodborne gastroenteritis with diarrhoea, fever, and abdominal cramps beginning 6 to 72 hours after ingestion of contaminated food or water. Typhoidal serotypes — S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi — cause typhoid and paratyphoid fever, systemic illnesses transmitted mainly through contaminated water and food in regions with poor sanitation.

Transmission and prevention

Non-typhoidal Salmonella are zoonotic and are commonly associated with raw or undercooked eggs, poultry, meat, unpasteurized milk, and produce contaminated with animal waste. Thorough cooking, safe food handling, handwashing, and preventing cross-contamination are the main preventive measures. Outbreaks are frequently traced to egg- and poultry-production chains.

Epidemiology

Salmonellosis is among the most common foodborne infections worldwide, causing tens of millions of human cases annually, with the great majority transmitted through food. Typhoid fever causes an estimated 11–21 million cases per year, concentrated in South Asia and sub-Saharan Africa; vaccines exist for typhoid but not for non-typhoidal salmonellosis.

Classification

The nomenclature of Salmonella is complex: serotypes of S. enterica subspecies enterica are given names (e.g., Enteritidis, Typhimurium) that are often treated informally as species names. Identification in the laboratory relies on culture, biochemical tests, and serotyping of O (somatic) and H (flagellar) antigens by the Kauffmann–White scheme.
