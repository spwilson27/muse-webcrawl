Estrogen (also spelled oestrogen in British English) is a category of sex hormone responsible for the development and regulation of the female reproductive system and secondary sex characteristics. There are three major endogenous estrogens that have estrogenic hormonal activity: estrone (E1), estradiol (E2), and estriol (E3). Estradiol, an estrane, is the most potent and prevalent.

Estrogens are synthesized in all vertebrates and some insects. Their presence in both vertebrates and insects suggests that estrogenic sex hormones have an ancient evolutionary history. Quantitatively, estrogens circulate at lower levels than androgens in both men and women. While estrogen levels are significantly lower in males than in females, estrogens nevertheless have important physiological roles in males.

Types

  • Estrone (E1) — predominant estrogen after menopause
  • Estradiol (E2) — predominant during reproductive years; the most potent
  • Estriol (E3) — produced in large amounts by the placenta in pregnancy
  • Estetrol (E4) — fetal estrogen, used in some contraceptives

Biosynthesis

Estrogens are produced primarily by the ovaries (developing follicles and corpus luteum) and, during pregnancy, the placenta. They are synthesized from androgen precursors by the enzyme aromatase; adipose tissue aromatization is a major source after menopause and in men.

Function

Estrogens drive pubertal development (breast development, growth of the pelvis and reproductive tract), regulate the menstrual cycle, support pregnancy, and maintain bone density, skin and vascular health. They also have neuroprotective and metabolic effects in both sexes.

Medical uses

Synthetic and natural estrogens are used in hormonal contraception, menopausal hormone therapy, treatment of hypogonadism, and as part of gender-affirming hormone therapy. Selective estrogen receptor modulators (SERMs) such as tamoxifen act as estrogen agonists or antagonists depending on tissue.

Risks and environmental estrogens

Excess estrogen exposure is associated with increased risk of breast and endometrial cancers and thromboembolism; hormone therapy balances these risks against symptom relief and bone protection. Environmental xenoestrogens (e.g., some plasticizers) can mimic estrogen action and are studied as endocrine disruptors.
