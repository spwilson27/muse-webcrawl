Sex hormone

Sex hormones, also known as sex steroids, gonadocorticoids, and gonadal steroids, are steroid hormones that interact with vertebrate steroid hormone receptors and regulate sexual development and reproduction. The principal classes are androgens (such as testosterone), estrogens (such as estradiol), and progestogens (such as progesterone). All are synthesised from cholesterol, mainly in the gonads and adrenal glands.

Types and production

Androgens are produced chiefly by the testes and, in smaller amounts, by the ovaries and adrenal cortex; they drive male sexual differentiation and secondary sexual characteristics. Estrogens are produced mainly by the ovaries (and by the placenta in pregnancy) and are responsible for female secondary sexual characteristics and regulation of the menstrual cycle. Progestogens, above all progesterone, prepare and maintain the uterus for pregnancy. Production is controlled by the hypothalamic–pituitary–gonadal axis through GnRH, LH, and FSH.

Functions

Sex hormones govern embryonic sexual differentiation, puberty, gamete production, the menstrual cycle, pregnancy, and sexual behaviour, and also exert wider effects on bone, muscle, fat distribution, cardiovascular function, and the brain. Both sexes produce all three classes, in differing proportions.

Medical applications

Synthetic sex hormones and their analogues underpin hormonal contraception, hormone replacement therapy, treatments for infertility and hormone-sensitive cancers, and gender-affirming hormone therapy. Anabolic-androgenic steroids, derived from testosterone, are also misused for performance enhancement.
