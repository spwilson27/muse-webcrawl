Secondary sex characteristic

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Secondary sex characteristics are features that appear during puberty in humans and at sexual maturity in other animals. They are distinguishing physical characteristics of the sexes that are not directly involved in reproduction, in contrast to the primary sex characteristics (the gonads and reproductive organs). Examples in humans include facial and body hair growth and a deepened voice in males, and breast development and widened hips in females.

Development

Secondary sex characteristics develop under the influence of the sex hormones: androgens, principally testosterone, in males, and estrogens, principally estradiol, in females. Gonadotropin-releasing hormone from the hypothalamus triggers pituitary release of LH and FSH, which stimulate the gonads to produce sex steroids. The timing and sequence of changes are described by Tanner staging.

In males

Testosterone causes growth of facial, axillary, and pubic hair; enlargement of the larynx with deepening of the voice; increased muscle mass and bone density; broadening of the shoulders; and increased activity of sebaceous and sweat glands, sometimes with acne.

In females

Estrogens cause breast development, widening of the pelvis and hips, redistribution of body fat to the hips and thighs, and growth of pubic and axillary hair (partly driven by adrenal androgens). Menarche, the first menstruation, is a late event of female puberty.

In other animals

Across species, secondary sex characteristics are often the product of sexual selection. They include the lion's mane, the peacock's train, the antlers of stags, bright plumage in male birds, and the enlarged claws of male fiddler crabs. Charles Darwin discussed such traits in The Descent of Man, and Selection in Relation to Sex (1871), proposing that they evolve because they confer advantages in competition for mates or in attracting them.
