Hormone replacement therapy

Hormone replacement therapy (HRT), also known as menopausal hormone therapy, is a form of hormone therapy used to treat symptoms associated with female menopause, including hot flushes, night sweats, vaginal dryness, and bone loss. It typically involves administration of estrogen, combined with a progestogen in women who still have a uterus, to protect the endometrium from estrogen-induced hyperplasia.

Uses and forms

HRT is available as oral tablets, transdermal patches, gels, and vaginal preparations. Systemic estrogen is the most effective treatment for vasomotor symptoms, and therapy also prevents postmenopausal osteoporosis and fractures. HRT is also used in premature ovarian insufficiency and as part of feminizing hormone therapy.

Risks and evidence

The Women's Health Initiative trials, reported in 2002, found that combined estrogen–progestin therapy increased the risks of breast cancer, coronary heart disease, stroke, and venous thromboembolism, leading to a sharp decline in HRT use. Later analyses suggested a more favourable risk–benefit balance for women who start therapy before age 60 or within ten years of menopause (the "timing hypothesis"), and current guidance recommends individualised decisions using the lowest effective dose.
