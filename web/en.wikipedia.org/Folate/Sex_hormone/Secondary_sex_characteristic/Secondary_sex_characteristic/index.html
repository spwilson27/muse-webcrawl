Secondary sex characteristic

Secondary sex characteristics are features that appear during puberty in humans and during sexual maturity in other animals. They are distinguishing characteristics of the two sexes of a species, but unlike primary sex characteristics — the sex organs — they are not directly part of the reproductive system. Secondary sex characteristics are believed to be the product of sexual selection for traits that display fitness and give an individual an advantage in courtship and competitive interactions.

In humans

In females, driven chiefly by estrogen, secondary sex characteristics include breast development, widening of the hips, and the distribution of body fat and pubic hair. In males, driven chiefly by testosterone, they include growth of facial and body hair, deepening of the voice as the larynx enlarges, increased muscle mass and bone density, and broadening of the shoulders.

In other animals

Examples across the animal kingdom include the manes of male lions, the bright plumage of male birds, antlers in deer, and the enlarged claws of male fiddler crabs. Such traits commonly function as signals in mate choice or as weapons and displays in competition between males.
