Drosophila melanogaster

Drosophila melanogaster is a species of fly (the taxonomic order Diptera) in the family Drosophilidae. The species is often referred to as the fruit fly or vinegar fly. Starting with Charles W. Woodworth's 1901 proposal of its use as a model organism, D. melanogaster continues to be widely used for biological research in genetics, physiology, microbial pathogenesis, and life-history evolution.

Life cycle and genetics

The fly develops from egg through larva and pupa to adult in about 8–10 days at 25 °C, produces hundreds of offspring, and has only four pairs of chromosomes. Males do not undergo meiotic recombination, simplifying genetic analysis. Its giant polytene chromosomes in larval salivary glands made gene mapping visible under the microscope.

Research history

Thomas Hunt Morgan's "fly room" at Columbia University used Drosophila to establish the chromosome theory of heredity, beginning with the white-eyed mutant in 1910; Hermann Muller showed that X-rays induce mutations. Later work founded developmental genetics — the homeotic (Hox) genes patterning the body plan were discovered in the fly — and the species' genome was sequenced in 2000. Roughly 60–75% of known human disease genes have fly counterparts, sustaining its use in medicine as well as basic biology, including in animal testing contexts where invertebrates raise fewer ethical concerns.
