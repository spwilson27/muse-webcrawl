Model organism

A model organism (often shortened to model) is a non-human species that is extensively studied to understand particular biological phenomena, with the expectation that discoveries made in the model will provide insight into the workings of other organisms, including humans. Model organisms are widely used in genetics, developmental biology, neuroscience, and drug discovery.

Selection criteria

Good model organisms are easy and inexpensive to keep in the laboratory, have short generation times and large numbers of offspring, are amenable to genetic manipulation, and share conserved genes and pathways with the organisms they are meant to illuminate. A well-characterised genome and a community of researchers sharing stocks and databases add to a model's value.

Major models

Classic models include the bacterium Escherichia coli; the yeasts Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Schizosaccharomyces pombe; the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans; the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster; the zebrafish Danio rerio; the thale cress Arabidopsis thaliana; and the house mouse Mus musculus, the principal mammalian model. Viruses such as bacteriophage lambda and plants such as maize have also been historically central.

Contributions and limitations

Work in model organisms has revealed the genetic code's universality, the operon, programmed cell death, circadian clock genes, and countless disease mechanisms, earning many Nobel Prizes. However, results may not always extrapolate to humans — many drugs successful in mice fail in clinical trials — prompting debate about over-reliance on a few standard models and interest in a broader range of species.
