Arabidopsis thaliana

Arabidopsis thaliana, the thale cress, mouse-ear cress or simply arabidopsis, is a small plant in the mustard family (Brassicaceae) related to cabbage and mustard. It is a popular model organism in plant biology and genetics: although of little agricultural significance itself, A. thaliana offers important advantages for basic research in genetics and molecular biology.

Characteristics

The plant has a relatively short life cycle of about six weeks from germination to mature seed, a small stature convenient for laboratory growth, prolific seed production, and ready self-fertilisation. Its genome is small — about 135 megabase pairs on five chromosomes, roughly 27,000 genes — and it was the first plant genome to be sequenced, in 2000.

Research use

Arabidopsis is easily transformed using Agrobacterium tumefaciens (the "floral dip" method), and large collections of insertion mutants and natural accessions (ecotypes) such as Columbia (Col-0) and Landsberg erecta are maintained in stock centres. Research with it has illuminated flower development (the ABC model), hormone signalling, pathogen defence, circadian rhythms, and epigenetic silencing, with findings often translating to crop species.

Distribution

Native to Europe, Asia, and Africa, A. thaliana is a winter annual weed of disturbed ground and has naturalised widely in North America and elsewhere. Despite its weediness it is not a significant agricultural pest.
