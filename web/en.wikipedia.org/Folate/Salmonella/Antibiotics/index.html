Antibiotic

An antibiotic is a type of antimicrobial substance active against bacteria. Antibiotics are widely used in the treatment and prevention of bacterial infections; they may kill bacteria (bactericidal) or inhibit their growth (bacteriostatic). They are not effective against viruses such as those causing the common cold or influenza.

History

Although antibacterial compounds such as arsphenamine (Salvarsan, 1910) came earlier, the antibiotic era began with Alexander Fleming's discovery of penicillin in 1928 and its development into a drug by Howard Florey, Ernst Chain and colleagues in the early 1940s. Many antibiotics are natural products of fungi and bacteria, or semi-synthetic derivatives of them; classes include penicillins, cephalosporins, macrolides, tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones and aminoglycosides.

Mechanisms and resistance

Antibiotics work by targeting structures or processes specific to bacteria: cell-wall synthesis, protein synthesis, DNA replication, or metabolic pathways such as folate synthesis. Misuse and overuse — in medicine and agriculture — have driven the evolution of antibiotic resistance, in which bacteria acquire genes or mutations that neutralise a drug. Resistant organisms such as MRSA and multidrug-resistant tuberculosis are major public-health threats, and stewardship programmes aim to preserve antibiotic effectiveness.
