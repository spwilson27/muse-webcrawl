Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura

Thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP) is a rare blood disorder that results in blood clots forming in small blood vessels throughout the body. These clots cause a low platelet count (thrombocytopenia), destruction of red blood cells (microangiopathic hemolytic anemia), and varying degrees of organ damage, particularly affecting the brain, kidneys, and heart. Symptoms may include bruising and purpura, fever, fatigue, shortness of breath, confusion, and headache.

Cause and mechanism

TTP occurs when the activity of the enzyme ADAMTS13 is severely reduced. ADAMTS13 normally cleaves large multimers of von Willebrand factor; without it, unusually large multimers persist and promote spontaneous platelet aggregation in the microcirculation. Most cases are acquired and autoimmune, caused by autoantibodies that inhibit ADAMTS13; a rarer congenital form (Upshaw–Schülman syndrome) results from inherited mutations in the ADAMTS13 gene. TTP must be distinguished from the related condition haemolytic–uremic syndrome, which is more often triggered by Shiga toxin–producing infections.

Treatment

TTP is a medical emergency. Untreated, it was historically fatal in more than 90 percent of cases; with prompt plasma exchange, which removes the autoantibody and replenishes ADAMTS13, survival exceeds 80 percent. Modern therapy adds immunosuppression with corticosteroids and rituximab, and the anti–von Willebrand factor agent caplacizumab, which blocks platelet binding while the underlying autoimmune process is brought under control.
