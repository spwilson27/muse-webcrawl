Kidney dialysis

Kidney dialysis is the process of artificially removing excess water, solutes and toxins from the blood in people whose kidneys have failed. It is a form of renal replacement therapy used for kidney failure, both acute and chronic, until kidney function recovers or a transplant can be performed. Millions of people worldwide depend on dialysis for survival.

Types

In haemodialysis, blood is pumped from the body through a dialyser — an artificial filter of semipermeable membranes — where waste products diffuse into a dialysis fluid, and the cleaned blood is returned. Sessions typically last about four hours, three times a week, usually in a clinic, and require vascular access such as an arteriovenous fistula. In peritoneal dialysis, the lining of the patient's own abdomen (the peritoneum) serves as the filter: dialysis fluid is instilled into the abdominal cavity through a permanent catheter and exchanged several times a day, or overnight by a machine, allowing treatment at home.

Principles and complications

Dialysis works by diffusion and ultrafiltration across a semipermeable membrane. It replaces only part of normal kidney function, so patients also need dietary restrictions, fluid limits and medicines for anaemia and bone disease. Complications include low blood pressure during treatment, infection of access sites, and long-term cardiovascular disease. The first successful clinical haemodialysis was performed by Willem Kolff in the Netherlands in the 1940s.
