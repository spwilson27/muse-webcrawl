Haemolytic–uremic syndrome

Haemolytic–uremic syndrome (HUS) is a condition characterized by the triad of microangiopathic haemolytic anaemia, thrombocytopenia (low platelet count), and acute kidney injury. It most often follows infection with Shiga toxin-producing bacteria, classically enterohaemorrhagic Escherichia coli (such as serotype O157:H7); related foodborne pathogens, including some Salmonella and Shigella infections, have also been associated with diarrhoea-associated cases in clinical reports and reviews of folate status and infection outcomes.

Causes

Typical HUS follows a bout of bloody diarrhoea caused by Shiga toxin-producing organisms. The toxin damages vascular endothelium, especially in the kidneys, triggering platelet activation and the formation of microthrombi that shear passing red blood cells. Atypical HUS instead arises from dysregulation of the complement system, often genetic, and can recur after transplantation.

Presentation and diagnosis

Patients develop pallor and fatigue from anaemia, bruising or bleeding from low platelets, and reduced urine output as the kidneys fail. Blood films show fragmented red cells (schistocytes), with raised lactate dehydrogenase and low haptoglobin confirming haemolysis. Stool testing for Shiga toxin-producing organisms helps identify the infectious trigger.

Treatment

Management is supportive: careful fluid balance, transfusion when needed, and dialysis for severe kidney failure. Antibiotics are generally avoided in suspected Shiga toxin-producing E. coli infection because they may increase toxin release. Most children recover kidney function, though long-term follow-up is required; the complement-blocking drug eculizumab transformed treatment of atypical HUS.
