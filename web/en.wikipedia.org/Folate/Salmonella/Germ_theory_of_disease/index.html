Germ theory of disease

The germ theory of disease is the currently accepted scientific theory for many diseases. It states that microorganisms known as pathogens or "germs" can cause disease. These small organisms, too small to be seen without magnification, invade humans, other animals, and other living hosts, and their growth and reproduction within the host can cause illness. The term germ refers not only to bacteria but to any type of microorganism, such as protists, fungi, and non-cellular pathogens such as viruses and prions.

History

Ideas resembling the germ theory were proposed for centuries — Girolamo Fracastoro suggested in 1546 that epidemic diseases were caused by transferable seed-like entities — but the dominant framework remained the miasma theory, which attributed disease to bad air. In the 1850s and 1860s, Louis Pasteur demonstrated that fermentation and spoilage were caused by microorganisms and disproved spontaneous generation. Joseph Lister applied the theory to surgery with antiseptic technique, and Robert Koch formulated his postulates for linking a specific microbe to a specific disease, identifying the organisms responsible for tuberculosis and cholera.

Evidence and acceptance

Ignaz Semmelweis had shown in 1847 that hand disinfection dramatically reduced puerperal fever in maternity wards, and John Snow's investigation of the 1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak traced the disease to contaminated water. Acceptance of the germ theory transformed medicine, public health, and food safety, leading to practices such as pasteurization, sterilisation of surgical instruments, and vaccination programmes.
