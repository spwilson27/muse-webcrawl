Food safety

Food safety (or food hygiene) is a scientific method and discipline describing the handling, preparation, and storage of food in ways that prevent foodborne illness. The occurrence of two or more cases of a similar illness resulting from the ingestion of a common food is known as a food-borne disease outbreak. Food safety includes a number of routines that should be followed to avoid potential health hazards, from the farm and factory through transport, retail, and the consumer's kitchen.

Key principles

Public health agencies commonly summarise food safety around a small set of core practices: cleaning hands and surfaces, separating raw and cooked foods to prevent cross-contamination, cooking to safe internal temperatures, and chilling perishable food promptly. In the United States these are promoted as the "four steps to food safety": clean, separate, cook, and chill. Potentially hazardous foods should be kept out of the "danger zone" of roughly 5 °C to 60 °C (41 °F to 140 °F), in which bacteria such as Salmonella can multiply rapidly.

Regulation and standards

Food safety is governed by national and international law. The Codex Alimentarius, maintained by the Food and Agriculture Organization and the World Health Organization, provides international standards, while systems such as Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points (HACCP) require producers to identify points in a process where contamination can be prevented, eliminated, or reduced to safe levels. In the United States the Food and Drug Administration and the Department of Agriculture share oversight; in the European Union the European Food Safety Authority performs risk assessment.

Microbial hazards

The most common causes of foodborne illness include norovirus, Salmonella, Clostridium perfringens, Campylobacter, and Staphylococcus aureus. Control measures include pasteurization of milk and juices, thorough cooking, and the germ theory-based understanding of contamination that emerged in the nineteenth century.
