Caenorhabditis elegans

Caenorhabditis elegans is a free-living, transparent nematode (roundworm), about 1 mm in length, that lives in temperate soil environments. It is the type species of its genus and a premier model organism, chosen in 1963 by Sydney Brenner for the study of developmental biology and neurobiology.

Biology

The species is mainly hermaphroditic, with rare males. Adults consist of a fixed, known number of cells — 959 somatic cells in the hermaphrodite — whose complete lineage was mapped by John Sulston and colleagues. The nervous system has exactly 302 neurons, and its full connectome was reconstructed by electron microscopy. Under adverse conditions, larvae enter the long-lived dauer stage.

Research significance

C. elegans was the first multicellular organism to have its whole genome sequenced (1998) and the first with a complete cell lineage and connectome. Studies of its programmed cell death genes (work recognised by the 2002 Nobel Prize to Brenner, Sulston, and Horvitz), the discovery of RNA interference by Fire and Mello (Nobel Prize 2006), and the first use of green fluorescent protein as a reporter in a whole organism all made it central to modern biology. It is also used to study ageing, since mutations in insulin-signalling genes can double its roughly two-to-three-week lifespan.
