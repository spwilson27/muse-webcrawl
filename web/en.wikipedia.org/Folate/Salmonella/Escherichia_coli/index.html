Escherichia coli

E. coli, a normal gut inhabitant and occasional pathogen.

Escherichia coli (E. coli) is a Gram-negative, facultative anaerobic, rod-shaped, coliform bacterium of the genus Escherichia that is commonly found in the lower intestine of warm-blooded organisms. Most strains are harmless members of the gut microbiota, where they aid digestion, produce vitamin K2, and exclude pathogens. Certain strains, however, have acquired virulence genes that make them important causes of foodborne and urinary disease — alongside fellow foodborne bacteria Salmonella and Listeria.

Pathogenic strains

Pathotypes of E. coli are classified by mechanism. Shiga toxin–producing E. coli (STEC) causes haemorrhagic colitis and haemolytic–uremic syndrome; enterotoxigenic strains (ETEC) produce heat-labile and heat-stable toxins causing watery travellers' diarrhoea; enteropathogenic, enteroinvasive, enteroaggregative and uropathogenic strains cause infant diarrhoea, dysentery-like illness, and urinary tract infections respectively. Cattle are the principal reservoir of O157:H7, and outbreaks have been traced to ground beef, raw milk, leafy greens, sprouts and contaminated water.

Laboratory and industrial importance

Strain K-12 and its derivatives made E. coli the central model organism of molecular genetics: experiments on it revealed the operon model, messenger RNA, the genetic code's universality, and the mechanisms of conjugation, transduction and transformation. Recombinant E. coli manufactures insulin and many other protein drugs, and engineered strains are used in research on biofuels and fine chemicals. Because it is specific to faecal contamination, counting E. coli is the standard microbiological test of drinking-water safety.

Prevention of infection

Control mirrors that for salmonellosis: thorough cooking of minced meat, pasteurisation, washing raw vegetables, safe water, and scrupulous hand and kitchen hygiene. Antibiotic treatment is generally withheld in suspected STEC infection because it may increase toxin release and the risk of haemolytic–uremic syndrome.
