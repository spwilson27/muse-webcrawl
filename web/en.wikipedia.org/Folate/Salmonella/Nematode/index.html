Nematode

Nematodes, also called roundworms, are animals of the phylum Nematoda. They are among the most abundant and diverse animals on Earth: tens of thousands of species have been described, and the true total may exceed a million. Nematodes inhabit virtually every environment — soil, fresh water, the oceans, and the bodies of plants and animals — and Nathan Cobb famously remarked that if all matter except nematodes disappeared, the world would still be recognisable in outline from the nematodes covering it.

Anatomy

Nematodes are unsegmented worms with slender, cylindrical bodies tapering at both ends, covered by a tough collagenous cuticle that is shed during growth. They have a complete digestive tract with a mouth and anus, and longitudinal muscles only, producing a characteristic thrashing movement. Most are microscopic, though some parasites reach over a metre in length.

Ecology and significance

Free-living nematodes feed on bacteria, fungi and other small organisms and are vital to nutrient cycling in soils. Many species are parasites: plant-parasitic nematodes cause major crop losses, while animal and human parasites include hookworms, pinworms, Ascaris and the filarial worms causing elephantiasis and river blindness. The free-living species Caenorhabditis elegans is one of biology's most important model organisms, the first multicellular animal to have its genome sequenced (1998).
