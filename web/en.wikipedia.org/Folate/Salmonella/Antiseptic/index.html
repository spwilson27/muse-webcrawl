Antiseptic

An antiseptic is an antimicrobial substance or compound that is applied to living tissue to reduce the possibility of infection, sepsis, or putrefaction. Antiseptics are generally distinguished from antibiotics, which destroy bacteria within the body, and from disinfectants, which destroy microorganisms found on non-living objects.

History

The modern practice of antisepsis began with Joseph Lister's use of carbolic acid (phenol) in surgery from 1867, building on Pasteur's germ theory and earlier observations by Ignaz Semmelweis on hand disinfection in maternity wards. Lister's methods dramatically reduced post-operative infection and were gradually adopted, later giving way to aseptic technique, which excludes microbes rather than killing them in the wound.

Common agents

Widely used antiseptics include alcohols (ethanol, isopropanol) in hand rubs; chlorhexidine for skin preparation and mouthwash; iodophors such as povidone-iodine; hydrogen peroxide; and quaternary ammonium compounds. They act by denaturing proteins, disrupting membranes, or oxidising microbial components, and are generally broad-spectrum, though bacterial spores are resistant to many.

Use

Antiseptics are used for hand hygiene, preoperative skin preparation, wound care, and mucous-membrane disinfection. Overuse in consumer products has raised concerns about contributing to antimicrobial resistance and about toxicity, leading regulators to review the safety of some agents such as triclosan.
