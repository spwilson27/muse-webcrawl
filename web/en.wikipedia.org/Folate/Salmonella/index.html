Salmonella

Salmonella is a genus of rod-shaped (bacillus) Gram-negative bacteria of the family Enterobacteriaceae. The two known species are Salmonella enterica and Salmonella bongori, divided into more than 2,600 serotypes. Salmonella species are non-spore-forming, predominantly motile enterobacteria and are major causes of foodborne illness worldwide. The genus is named after Daniel Elmer Salmon, an American veterinary surgeon.

Disease

Non-typhoidal Salmonella serotypes such as Enteritidis and Typhimurium typically cause self-limiting gastroenteritis with diarrhoea, abdominal cramps and fever beginning 6–72 hours after ingestion of contaminated food — commonly poultry, eggs, meat, unpasteurised milk and produce. Typhoidal serotypes (Typhi and Paratyphi) invade from the gut to cause enteric fever, a systemic and potentially fatal illness still common where sanitation is poor. Treatment of uncomplicated gastroenteritis is supportive; antibiotics are reserved for invasive disease and high-risk patients, partly because of rising antimicrobial resistance.

Transmission and control

The bacteria live in the intestinal tracts of animals and humans and are shed in faeces. Control rests on farm hygiene, refrigeration, thorough cooking, avoiding cross-contamination, and surveillance systems that fingerprint isolates to detect outbreaks. Like other enteric bacteria, Salmonella synthesises folate for growth — one reason folate-pathway antibiotics are effective against it in vitro — and it is a standard organism in food-safety testing.
