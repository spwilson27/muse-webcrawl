Listeriosis

SpecialtyInfectious disease
CauseListeria monocytogenes
Risk factorsPregnancy, older age, immunosuppression
SourcesDeli meats, unpasteurised dairy, soft cheeses

Listeriosis is a bacterial infection most commonly caused by Listeria monocytogenes, usually contracted by eating contaminated food. While in healthy people it may cause only a mild, self-limited gastroenteritis or flu-like illness, in pregnant women, newborns, older adults, and immunocompromised people it can invade the bloodstream and central nervous system, causing meningitis, septicaemia, miscarriage, and death.

Transmission

Listeria is widespread in soil and water and can contaminate raw milk, soft cheeses, deli meats, hot dogs, smoked seafood, and ready-to-eat foods. Uniquely among common foodborne pathogens, it can grow at refrigeration temperatures, so prolonged storage of refrigerated ready-to-eat foods increases risk. Outbreaks have been traced to cantaloupes, ice cream, caramel apples, and frozen vegetables.

Clinical features

Invasive listeriosis typically presents with fever, muscle aches, confusion, and neck stiffness when the meninges are involved; blood cultures are usually positive. In pregnancy, maternal illness may be mild while infection of the fetus or newborn (granulomatosis infantiseptica) can be devastating. The case-fatality rate for invasive disease is high, around 20–30%.

Prevention and treatment

Prevention rests on food safety: avoiding unpasteurised dairy, heating deli meats, and observing use-by dates; at-risk people are advised to avoid high-risk foods. The bacterium is named after Joseph Lister. Treatment is with antibiotics, classically high-dose ampicillin, often combined with gentamicin.
