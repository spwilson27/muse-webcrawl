Acute kidney injury

Acute kidney injury (AKI), previously called acute renal failure, is a sudden episode of kidney failure or kidney damage that happens within a few hours or a few days. It causes a build-up of waste products in the blood and makes it hard for the kidneys to keep the right balance of fluid in the body. AKI is common in hospitalised patients, affecting roughly 1 in 5 admissions, and is associated with substantially increased mortality.

Diagnosis and staging

The KDIGO criteria define AKI by a rise in serum creatinine (≥26.5 µmol/L within 48 hours, or ≥1.5 times baseline within 7 days) or by reduced urine output (<0.5 mL/kg/h for 6 hours). Severity is staged 1–3 according to the magnitude of the creatinine rise or oliguria, stage 3 including the need for renal replacement therapy.

Causes

Causes are grouped as prerenal (reduced perfusion: hypovolaemia, sepsis, heart failure), intrinsic renal (acute tubular necrosis from ischaemia or nephrotoxins such as aminoglycosides and contrast media; glomerulonephritis; interstitial nephritis), and postrenal (obstruction of the urinary tract). In practice, sepsis and hypoperfusion are the dominant triggers in critically ill patients.

Management

Treatment is directed at the cause: restoring perfusion with fluids and vasopressors, stopping nephrotoxic drugs, relieving obstruction, and managing complications such as hyperkalaemia, acidosis, and fluid overload. Severe cases require dialysis or continuous renal replacement therapy. Many patients recover renal function, but an episode of AKI increases the later risk of chronic kidney disease.
