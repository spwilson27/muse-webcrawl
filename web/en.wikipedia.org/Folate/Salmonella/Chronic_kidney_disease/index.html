Chronic kidney disease

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a long-term condition in which the kidneys are damaged and cannot filter blood as well as they should, with abnormalities of kidney structure or function present for more than three months. It affects roughly one in ten adults worldwide and is usually progressive, though progression can often be slowed.

Causes and stages

The commonest causes are diabetes mellitus and high blood pressure; others include glomerulonephritis, polycystic kidney disease and prolonged obstruction of the urinary tract. CKD is classified into five stages by estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), from stage 1 (normal filtration with kidney damage) to stage 5, kidney failure. Protein in the urine (albuminuria) is an important marker of damage and risk.

Symptoms and complications

Early CKD usually has no symptoms and is detected by blood and urine tests. As function declines, complications develop: fluid retention, anaemia, bone and mineral disorders, acidosis, high potassium, and a greatly increased risk of cardiovascular disease, which is the leading cause of death in people with CKD.

Treatment

Management focuses on treating the cause, controlling blood pressure (often with ACE inhibitors or ARBs), blood-sugar control in diabetes, dietary measures, and newer protective drugs such as SGLT2 inhibitors. Kidney failure is treated with dialysis or kidney transplantation.
