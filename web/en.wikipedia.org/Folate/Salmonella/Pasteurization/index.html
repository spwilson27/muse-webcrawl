Pasteurization

Pasteurization is a process of food preservation in which packaged foods (such as milk and fruit juices) are treated with mild heat, usually to less than 100 °C (212 °F), to eliminate pathogens and extend shelf life. Pasteurization is intended to make food safe by reducing the number of viable disease-causing microorganisms to levels unlikely to cause disease, assuming proper refrigeration and consumption before expiry.

History

The process is named after the French microbiologist Louis Pasteur, who in the 1860s showed that heating wine and beer prevented them from souring by killing the microbes responsible. Pasteur's work provided key support for the germ theory of disease. Pasteurization of milk was adopted widely in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries as a public health measure against tuberculosis, brucellosis, diphtheria, scarlet fever, and Q-fever transmitted through raw milk.

Methods

Common methods include high-temperature short-time (HTST or "flash") pasteurization, in which milk is heated to about 72 °C (162 °F) for at least 15 seconds, and ultra-high-temperature (UHT) processing, in which milk is heated to around 135 °C (275 °F) for a few seconds and sealed aseptically, allowing storage without refrigeration for months. Unlike sterilization, pasteurization is not intended to kill all microorganisms; it reduces their numbers so that they are unlikely to cause disease and slows spoilage, contributing to overall food safety.
