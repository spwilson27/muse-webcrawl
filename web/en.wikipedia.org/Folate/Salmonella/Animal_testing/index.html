Animal testing

Animal testing, also known as animal experimentation, animal research and in vivo testing, is the use of non-human animals in experiments that seek to control the variables that affect the behaviour or biological system under study. It is used in basic biological and medical research, in assessing the safety and efficacy of drugs and other products, and in education.

Scope and species

The vast majority of animals used are rodents, especially mice and rats bred for the purpose; fish and fruit flies such as Drosophila melanogaster are also used in large numbers, while dogs, cats, and non-human primates form a small fraction. Estimates of annual worldwide use range from tens of millions to over 100 million vertebrates.

Regulation and the 3Rs

Most countries regulate animal research through licensing of premises, projects, and personnel, guided by the 3Rs proposed by Russell and Burch in 1959: replacement of animals where possible, reduction in numbers, and refinement of procedures to minimise suffering. The European Union bans animal testing for cosmetics, and several jurisdictions have ended particular practices such as great-ape research.

Debate

Supporters cite animal research's contribution to vaccines, insulin, antibiotics, and surgical techniques, and the legal requirement to test medicines for safety. Opponents question its predictive value for humans, point to failures and to painful procedures, and campaign for alternatives such as cell culture, organoids, organ-on-a-chip devices, and computer modelling, which are steadily replacing some uses.
