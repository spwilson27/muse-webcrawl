Meningitis

Meningitis is acute or chronic inflammation of the protective membranes covering the brain and spinal cord, collectively called the meninges. The most common symptoms are fever, intense headache, vomiting, and neck stiffness. Other symptoms include confusion or altered consciousness, nausea, and an inability to tolerate light or loud noises. Young children often exhibit only nonspecific symptoms, such as irritability, drowsiness, or poor feeding.

Causes

Meningitis is most often caused by infection with viruses, bacteria, or other microorganisms, though it may also result from certain drugs and other diseases. Bacterial meningitis is the most dangerous form: common causative organisms include Neisseria meningitidis, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Haemophilus influenzae, and group B streptococci in newborns. Viral meningitis is usually milder and resolves without specific treatment. The inflammation is typically triggered by the immune response to the infection in the subarachnoid space.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is made by analysing cerebrospinal fluid obtained by lumbar puncture. Bacterial meningitis is a medical emergency: antibiotics must be given as early as possible, often together with corticosteroids, because untreated bacterial meningitis is nearly always fatal and even with treatment carries a significant risk of death and of sequelae such as hearing loss and brain damage. Vaccines exist against several bacterial causes, including meningococcal, pneumococcal, and Hib vaccines, and have greatly reduced the incidence of the disease in countries where they are routinely used.
