Listeria

Listeria monocytogenes, the species that infects humans.

Listeria is a genus of bacteria that acted as an intracellular parasite until 2009, when L. grayi was found to be non-pathogenic. As of 2024 the genus contains 28 species. Named for the British surgeon and antisepsis pioneer Joseph Lister, the genus received its current name in 1940. The species Listeria monocytogenes causes the infection listeriosis, a rare but serious foodborne illness.

Listeriosis

Listeriosis most often strikes pregnant women, newborns, the elderly and the immunocompromised. In pregnancy it may cause only mild flu-like illness in the mother but can lead to miscarriage, stillbirth, premature delivery or life-threatening infection of the newborn — which is why dietary guidance in pregnancy warns against the foods that carry it. In other vulnerable people it can invade the bloodstream and central nervous system, causing sepsis and meningitis, with a case-fatality rate of 20–30%, among the highest of foodborne diseases. Healthy adults usually experience only brief gastroenteritis or no symptoms.

Biology and food safety

L. monocytogenes is unusually hardy: it tolerates salt and acidity and — uniquely among major foodborne pathogens — grows slowly at refrigerator temperatures. It is widespread in soil, water, silage and the intestines of animals, and contaminates ready-to-eat foods such as soft cheeses, pâté, deli meats, smoked fish and pre-packed salads, often via biofilms in food-processing plants. Once inside the body it invades cells, moves from cell to cell using actin-based "comet tails", and crosses the intestinal, blood–brain and placental barriers. Control relies on hygiene in processing plants, shelf-life management, cooking, and the avoidance of high-risk foods by vulnerable groups — measures that complement those used against Salmonella and pathogenic E. coli.

History

The organism was first described in 1926 by E. G. D. Murray in laboratory rabbits and named Bacterium monocytogenes for the characteristic rise in monocytes it produced. Its role as a foodborne pathogen was firmly established by investigations of the 1981 Maritime provinces outbreak in Canada traced to coleslaw, followed by the 1985 California outbreak linked to Mexican-style cheese.
