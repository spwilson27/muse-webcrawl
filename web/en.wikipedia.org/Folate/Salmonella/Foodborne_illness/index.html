Foodborne illness

Foodborne illness (also foodborne disease or food poisoning) is any illness resulting from the contamination of food by pathogenic bacteria, viruses, parasites, or by toxins and chemicals. Contamination usually arises from improper handling, preparation or storage of food. Symptoms most commonly include vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, fever and nausea.

Causes

The most common bacterial causes include Salmonella, Campylobacter, Escherichia coli O157:H7, Listeria monocytogenes, Staphylococcus aureus and Clostridium perfringens. Viral causes are dominated by norovirus, while parasites such as Giardia and Toxoplasma also contribute. Some illnesses are caused not by the organisms themselves but by toxins they produce, as in botulism.

Transmission

Foodborne pathogens spread when food is undercooked, stored at unsafe temperatures, cross-contaminated between raw and cooked items, or handled by infected food workers. High-risk foods include raw or undercooked meat, poultry, eggs, unpasteurised dairy products, raw shellfish and ready-to-eat foods.

Scale and prevention

The World Health Organization estimates that unsafe food causes about 600 million cases of illness and 420,000 deaths worldwide each year. Prevention rests on the basic principles of food hygiene: clean, separate, cook and chill — washing hands and surfaces, avoiding cross-contamination, cooking to safe temperatures, and refrigerating promptly. Regulatory systems such as HACCP (hazard analysis and critical control points) apply these principles across the food industry.

Outbreaks and investigation

Public health authorities investigate clusters of illness using epidemiological interviews, laboratory typing of isolates and, increasingly, whole-genome sequencing, which can link cases across wide areas to a common food source and trigger recalls.
