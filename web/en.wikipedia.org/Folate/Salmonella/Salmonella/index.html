Salmonella is a genus of rod-shaped, Gram-negative bacteria of the family Enterobacteriaceae. The genus is named after the American veterinarian Daniel Elmer Salmon. Its two species, Salmonella enterica and Salmonella bongori, encompass more than 2,600 serovars, many of which cause disease in humans and animals.

Disease

Non-typhoidal Salmonella serovars are among the most common causes of foodborne gastroenteritis worldwide, typically acquired from contaminated poultry, eggs, meat, and produce. Symptoms include diarrhoea, fever, and abdominal cramps, usually resolving without antibiotics. By contrast, S. Typhi and S. Paratyphi cause typhoid and paratyphoid fever, systemic illnesses spread through contaminated water and food that can be fatal without treatment.

Biology

Salmonella are facultative anaerobes and are generally motile by peritrichous flagella. They invade intestinal epithelial cells and can survive within macrophages, using type III secretion systems encoded on pathogenicity islands to manipulate host cells.

Prevention

Control rests on food hygiene, thorough cooking, clean water, and surveillance of food production chains. Vaccines exist against typhoid fever, and poultry vaccination programmes have sharply reduced human infections in several countries.
