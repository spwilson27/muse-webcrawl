Joseph Lister, 1st Baron Lister (5 April 1827 – 10 February 1912) was a British surgeon, medical scientist, and experimental pathologist who pioneered antiseptic surgery and preventive medicine. Building on Louis Pasteur's work on fermentation and germ theory, Lister introduced the use of carbolic acid (phenol) to sterilise surgical instruments and clean wounds, dramatically reducing post-operative infection and death.

Antiseptic surgery

As professor of surgery at Glasgow, Lister read Pasteur's papers on micro-organisms and hypothesised that wound sepsis — "hospital gangrene" and erysipelas, which killed a large share of surgical patients — was caused by microbes entering wounds from the air. In 1865 he began dressing wounds with lint soaked in carbolic acid, spraying the operating field, and sterilising instruments and ligatures. In his famous series of compound fracture cases, nine of eleven patients recovered without suppuration, and amputation mortality in his wards fell from about 46% to 15%.

Career and recognition

Lister published his results in The Lancet in 1867 ("On the Antiseptic Principle in the Practice of Surgery"). Antisepsis spread slowly in Britain but faster in Germany and the United States; Lister later adopted and promoted steam sterilisation and the broader aseptic methods that superseded chemical antisepsis. He held chairs in Glasgow, Edinburgh, and at King's College London, served as president of the Royal Society (1895–1900), and was raised to the peerage in 1897. The bacterial genus Listeria and the antiseptic mouthwash Listerine are named for him.

Legacy

Lister is widely regarded as the father of modern surgery. His work, alongside that of Pasteur and Robert Koch, established germ theory at the centre of medicine and made elective surgery acceptably safe for the first time.
