Louis Pasteur

Louis Pasteur (27 December 1822 – 28 September 1895) was a French chemist and microbiologist renowned for his discoveries of the principles of vaccination, microbial fermentation, and pasteurization, the last of which is named after him. His research in chemistry led to remarkable breakthroughs in the causes and preventions of diseases, which laid the foundations of hygiene, public health, and modern medicine.

Early life and chemistry

Pasteur was born in Dole, in the Jura region of France, the son of a tanner. He studied at the École Normale Supérieure in Paris, where his early work on the molecular asymmetry of tartaric acid crystals founded the science of stereochemistry. Appointed dean of the science faculty at Lille in 1854, he turned to the practical problems of local industries, including the souring of wine and beer.

Germ theory and pasteurization

Pasteur showed that microorganisms were responsible for fermentation and for the spoilage of beverages, and that heating could destroy them — the process of pasteurization. His swan-neck flask experiments refuted the doctrine of spontaneous generation and gave decisive support to the germ theory of disease. He later applied these ideas to the diseases of silkworms, saving the French silk industry.

Vaccines

Pasteur developed vaccines against anthrax and rabies by weakening, or attenuating, the responsible microbes. In 1885 he successfully treated Joseph Meister, a boy bitten by a rabid dog, with a course of injections of attenuated rabies virus. The Pasteur Institute, founded in Paris in 1887, became a world centre for microbiology. Pasteur died in 1895 and was buried in a crypt beneath the institute.
