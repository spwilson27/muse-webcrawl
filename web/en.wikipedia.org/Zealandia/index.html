Zealandia (pronounced /ziːˈlændiə/), also known as Te Riu-a-Māui or Tasmantis, is an almost entirely submerged mass of continental crust in the south-west Pacific Ocean that subsided after breaking away from Gondwana between about 85 and 60 million years ago. At roughly 4.9 million square kilometres, it is larger than the Indian subcontinent; about 94 per cent of it lies beneath the sea. Its emerged portions form New Zealand, New Caledonia and several smaller islands.

Geology

Zealandia consists of stretched and thinned continental crust, typically 10–30 km thick, composed of Palaeozoic and Mesozoic basement rocks similar to those exposed in New Zealand. It rifted from eastern Gondwana during the Cretaceous as the supercontinent broke up, and gradually subsided below sea level, reaching near its present depth by roughly 25 million years ago.

Recognition as a continent

The name Zealandia was proposed by geophysicist Bruce Luyendyk in 1995. A 2017 paper by Nick Mortimer and colleagues argued that Zealandia satisfies all the criteria for a continent — elevation above the surrounding ocean floor, distinctive geology, well-defined area and crustal thickness — and should be recognised as Earth's eighth and smallest continent. In 2023, geologists published the first complete geological map of the entire continent.

Biogeography

Because of its long isolation and partial submergence, Zealandia's biota has been the subject of debate: some researchers have argued that its endemic species survived in place on emergent land, while others proposed a near-total drowning around 25 million years ago followed by recolonisation.
