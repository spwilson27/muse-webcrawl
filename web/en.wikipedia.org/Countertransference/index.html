Countertransference

Countertransference is defined as the redirection of a therapist's feelings toward a patient, or more generally as a therapist's emotional entanglement with a patient. The concept arose within psychoanalysis as the counterpart to transference, in which the patient redirects feelings originally directed at figures from childhood onto the analyst.

Freudian origins

Sigmund Freud introduced the term in 1910, in "The Future Prospects of Psycho-Analytic Therapy," warning that countertransference arises from the patient's influence on the analyst's unconscious feelings and must be recognized and overcome, lest the analyst's own unresolved conflicts distort treatment. In the classical view, countertransference was purely an obstacle, and the analyst was to maintain neutrality and abstinence.

Broader conceptions

From the 1950s, analysts such as Paula Heimann and Heinrich Racker argued that the analyst's emotional responses could be a valuable instrument: feelings evoked in the therapist may mirror the patient's inner world and thus provide diagnostic information. The "totalistic" view counts all of the therapist's reactions as potentially meaningful. Contemporary psychodynamic practice treats countertransference as an inevitable and often useful feature of the therapeutic relationship, to be managed through self-awareness, supervision, and personal analysis rather than eliminated.

In modern practice

Beyond psychoanalysis, the concept is used in counselling, nursing, and medicine to describe clinicians' emotional reactions to patients. Unmanaged countertransference can produce boundary violations, avoidance, or over-involvement; recognized and reflected upon, it can deepen empathy and understanding.
