Scordatura

Scordatura (Italian for "mistuning") is the practice of tuning a string instrument differently from the standard tuning, in order to change its resonance, timbre, range or the chords it can play. It has been used for lute, guitar, viola d'amore and the violin family since the Renaissance, and remains common in folk fiddling and some contemporary music.

Baroque and classical use

The most famous repertoire is Heinrich Ignaz Franz Biber's Mystery (Rosary) Sonatas (c. 1676) for violin, in which each sonata prescribes a different tuning — the strings retuned to the notes of the chord associated with the mystery — and the part is written as if in standard tuning, so the sounding pitches differ from the written ones. Composers including Vivaldi, Telemann and Mozart (the Sinfonia Concertante, where the viola is tuned a semitone higher for brilliance) used scordatura for special colour. Tartini and Paganini also exploited altered tunings.

Folk and modern practice

Folk fiddlers in Appalachian, Scandinavian and Cajun traditions use "cross-tuning" (for example AEAE on the violin) to create ringing drones in specific keys. Hardanger fiddle music depends on sympathetic-string tunings. Guitarists routinely employ drop-D, open and DADGAD tunings, and modern composers such as John Cage and bands in rock and metal employ extensive retunings. Because string tension changes, scordatura can alter an instrument's brightness — loosened strings sound darker, tightened strings brighter — and makes certain chords and harmonics physically possible that standard tuning forbids.

Notation

Two conventions exist: writing the actual sounding pitches, or writing the notes as fingered in standard tuning ("tablature effect"), leaving the player to read positions rather than sounds. Editions differ, and performers must know which convention applies.
