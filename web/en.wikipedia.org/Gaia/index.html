Gaia

In Greek mythology, Gaia (Greek: Γαῖα, "land" or "earth") is the personification of Earth and one of the primordial deities. Mother of the sky god Uranus, the mountains and the sea (Pontus), she is the ancestral mother of the Titans, the Olympian gods and ultimately all life. As Terra or Tellus she was likewise venerated in Roman religion.

Mythology

In Hesiod's Theogony, Gaia emerges after Chaos and bears Uranus, the Ourea (mountains) and Pontus. With Uranus she produces the Titans; when Uranus imprisons their children within her, Gaia fashions a sickle and persuades Cronus to castrate his father. Later she aids Zeus against Cronus, but then, angered by the imprisonment of the Titans, produces the Giants and the monster Typhon to challenge the Olympians.

Worship

Gaia was worshipped across Greece, notably at Delphi, where she was said to have held the oracle before Apollo, and at Athens and Olympia, where oaths were sworn by her name. Sacrifices to her sought fertility of the soil.

Modern usage

Her name was adopted by James Lovelock for the Gaia hypothesis, the idea that Earth's biosphere behaves as a self-regulating system, and by the European Space Agency for its star-mapping space observatory Gaia.
