James Buchanan Duke (23 December 1856 – 10 October 1925) was an American tobacco and electric power industrialist, best known for building the American Tobacco Company into a near-monopoly and for endowing Duke University. He was a central figure in the mechanisation and mass marketing of cigarettes.

Tobacco empire

Duke entered the family tobacco firm founded by his father, Washington Duke, in Durham, North Carolina. In the 1880s he bet the company's future on cigarettes and on the Bonsack cigarette-rolling machine, securing favourable royalty terms that gave W. Duke, Sons & Co. a decisive cost advantage. Aggressive advertising and price cuts followed. In 1890 he merged the five largest cigarette makers into the American Tobacco Company, with himself as president, controlling some 90 percent of the US cigarette market. He later led the "tobacco trust" abroad, fighting Britain's Imperial Tobacco in a trade war that ended in the formation of the jointly owned British-American Tobacco in 1902. The US Supreme Court dissolved American Tobacco as an illegal combination in 1911.

Electric power

From 1905 Duke developed hydroelectric power on the Catawba River in the Carolinas through the Southern Power Company, later Duke Power (now Duke Energy), which electrified the region's textile mills and towns and grew into one of America's largest utilities.

Philanthropy

In December 1924 Duke established The Duke Endowment, a $40 million trust (with further bequests at his death) supporting hospitals, churches, and colleges in the Carolinas. Its most famous provision transformed Trinity College in Durham into Duke University, renamed in honour of his father and family. Duke died in New York in 1925 and is buried in Duke Chapel. His daughter Doris Duke became a noted philanthropist and heiress.
