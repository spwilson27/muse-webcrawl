Hemostasis

Hemostasis (from Ancient Greek haima, "blood", and stasis, "standing still") is a process to prevent and stop bleeding, meaning to keep blood within a damaged blood vessel. It is the first stage of wound healing and the opposite of haemorrhage. Hemostasis involves three main steps: vasoconstriction, the temporary blockage of a break by a platelet plug (primary hemostasis), and blood coagulation, the formation of a fibrin clot (secondary hemostasis).

Mechanism

When endothelium is injured, the normally anticoagulant surface becomes procoagulant. Platelets adhere to exposed collagen via von Willebrand factor, become activated, release ADP and thromboxane A2, and aggregate to form a soft plug. Simultaneously, tissue factor initiates the coagulation cascade, generating thrombin, which converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin strands that stabilise the plug into a durable clot. As healing proceeds, the clot is dissolved by fibrinolysis, driven by plasmin.

Regulation and disorders

Hemostasis is tightly balanced by natural anticoagulants (antithrombin, protein C and S) and proper blood flow. Insufficient hemostasis causes excessive bleeding, as in haemophilia, von Willebrand disease, and thrombocytopenia; excessive or misplaced clotting causes thrombosis — deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, stroke, and heart attack. Disseminated intravascular coagulation combines both, consuming clotting factors while forming widespread micro-clots.

Assessment and medicine

Clinical tests include platelet count, prothrombin time (PT/INR), activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), and viscoelastic tests such as thromboelastography. Anticoagulant and antiplatelet drugs (heparin, warfarin, direct oral anticoagulants, aspirin, clopidogrel) deliberately impair hemostasis to treat or prevent thrombosis, while in surgery the same word denotes any technique — pressure, ligature, cautery, topical agents — used to stop bleeding.
