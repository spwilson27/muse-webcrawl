Endoplasmic reticulum

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is a type of organelle found in eukaryotic cells that forms an interconnected network of flattened, membrane-enclosed sacs or tube-like structures known as cisternae. The membranes of the ER are continuous with the outer nuclear membrane. The ER occurs in most eukaryotic cell types but is absent from red blood cells and spermatozoa.

Structure

The ER consists of the nuclear envelope and two connected forms: the rough endoplasmic reticulum (RER), whose surface is studded with ribosomes giving it a studded appearance in electron micrographs, and the smooth endoplasmic reticulum (SER), which lacks ribosomes and is more tubular. The internal space enclosed by ER membranes is called the lumen or cisternal space.

Functions

The rough ER is the site of synthesis of proteins destined for secretion, for insertion into membranes, or for delivery to lysosomes. Newly made polypeptides enter the lumen, where they fold with the help of chaperone proteins, are modified (for example by N-linked glycosylation), and are checked for quality. Transport vesicles bud from special exit sites and carry cargo to the Golgi apparatus.

The smooth ER is involved in lipid synthesis, including phospholipids and steroids; in carbohydrate metabolism; in detoxification of drugs and poisons (abundant in liver cells); and in the storage and release of calcium ions. In muscle cells a specialised form, the sarcoplasmic reticulum, regulates calcium release that triggers contraction.

Stress responses

When misfolded proteins accumulate in the ER lumen, cells activate the unfolded protein response, which slows translation, expands folding capacity, and can trigger apoptosis if stress is unresolved. ER dysfunction is implicated in diabetes, neurodegenerative diseases, and other disorders.
