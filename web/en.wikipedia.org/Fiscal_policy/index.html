Fiscal policy

In economics, fiscal policy is the use of government revenue collection (taxation) and expenditure to influence a country's economy. It is one of the two main macroeconomic policy tools, the other being monetary policy, which is conducted by central banks through interest rates and the money supply. Fiscal policy is used, in the Keynesian tradition, to smooth the business cycle: governments increase spending or cut taxes to stimulate aggregate demand during recessions, and restrain spending or raise taxes to cool an overheating economy.

Stances

An expansionary (or loose) fiscal policy raises government spending, cuts taxes, or both, increasing the budget deficit; it is intended to boost demand and employment. A contractionary (or tight) policy does the opposite, reducing deficits or building surpluses, usually to control inflation or reduce public debt. A neutral stance aims to leave the fiscal impulse on the economy broadly unchanged. The cyclically adjusted or structural balance is used to distinguish deliberate policy changes from the automatic effects of the economic cycle.

Automatic stabilisers and discretionary policy

Some fiscal effects occur automatically without new legislation: in a downturn, tax receipts fall and spending on unemployment benefits rises, cushioning the fall in household income. These are called automatic stabilisers. Discretionary policy consists of deliberate changes, such as stimulus packages, infrastructure programmes, or tax reforms. Discretionary measures are subject to lags — recognition, decision, and implementation lags — which can limit their usefulness for fine-tuning the economy.

Debates

Economists debate the size of fiscal multipliers: how much output changes per unit of tax cut or spending increase. Critics in the neoclassical tradition raise the possibility of Ricardian equivalence, in which households save tax cuts in anticipation of future taxes, and of crowding out, in which government borrowing raises interest rates and displaces private investment. Sustained deficits raise questions of debt sustainability; the ratio of public debt to GDP evolves with interest rates, growth, and the primary balance. The Keynesian framework associated with John Maynard Keynes's General Theory (1936) provided the modern rationale for countercyclical fiscal policy, which was widely applied after the 2008 financial crisis and again during the COVID-19 pandemic.
