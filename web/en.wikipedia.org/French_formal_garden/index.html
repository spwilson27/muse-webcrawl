French formal garden

The French formal garden, also known as the jardin à la française, is a style of garden based on symmetry and the principle of imposing order on nature. Its epitome is generally considered to be the Gardens of Versailles designed by André Le Nôtre for Louis XIV in the 17th century.

Characteristics

The style features a central axis extending from the residence to the horizon, along which the other elements are arranged symmetrically: terraces, broad avenues, canals and basins of water, and parterres — ornamental flowerbeds and clipped box hedges arranged in scrolls and geometric patterns (broderie). Topiary trees are clipped into cones and balls, and bosquets (formal groves) enclose fountains and statuary. The whole is designed like a carpet to be viewed from the raised windows and terraces of the house, expressing the owner's dominion over territory.

History

The style grew out of the Italian Renaissance gardens imported to France in the late 15th and 16th centuries, which were adapted to flatter French landscapes. Nicolas Fouquet's garden at Vaux-le-Vicomte (1656–1661), created by Le Nôtre with the architect Louis Le Vau and painter Charles Le Brun, first brought the elements together at grand scale; after Fouquet's fall, the same team created Versailles. The formal French style dominated European court gardens for a century — examples include Peterhof in Russia, Schönbrunn in Vienna, and Het Loo in the Netherlands — before being challenged in the 18th century by the more naturalistic English landscape garden.
