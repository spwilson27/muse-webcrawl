General strike

A general strike is a strike action in which participants cease all work across many industries simultaneously, in a city, region, or entire country, rather than employees of a single employer or industry. General strikes are usually organised by trade union federations in pursuit of economic or political goals.

History

The idea of a general strike was advanced in early 19th-century Britain by figures such as William Benbow, who proposed a month-long "grand national holiday" in 1832. Chartists attempted general strikes in 1842. In the 20th century, general strikes became major national events: the 1905 and 1917 strikes in Russia, the Winnipeg general strike of 1919 in Canada, the British general strike of 1926, and the May 1968 events in France, in which some ten million workers participated.

Purposes

General strikes have been called to win specific demands such as wage increases or shorter hours, to protest government policy, and in some cases to challenge the political order itself. Revolutionary syndicalists, notably theorised by Georges Sorel, regarded the general strike as the decisive weapon by which workers could seize control of production; reformist unions more often treated it as an instrument of pressure within collective bargaining and politics.

Legal status

Legality varies widely. Some countries recognise political or solidarity strikes in law; others, including the United Kingdom after legislation of the 1980s, restrict strike action to disputes with an immediate employer. Secondary and general strikes are prohibited in several jurisdictions, though enforcement against mass action has often proved impractical.
