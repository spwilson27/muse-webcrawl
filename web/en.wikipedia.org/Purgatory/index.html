Purgatory (Latin: purgatorium, from purgare, "to purge") is, in Catholic theology, an intermediate state after physical death for those bound for heaven who are not yet fully purified of the effects of sin. According to the doctrine, those who die in God's grace and friendship but are still imperfectly purified undergo purification so as to achieve the holiness necessary to enter heaven. The purification is distinct from the punishment of the damned in hell: everyone in purgatory is saved and will eventually reach heaven.

Doctrine and practice

The Catholic Church holds that the living can assist the souls in purgatory by prayers, almsgiving, works of penance and especially the offering of Masses for the dead, and through indulgences applied to them. Scriptural support is traditionally drawn from 2 Maccabees 12 (prayers for the dead), 1 Corinthians 3:15 ("saved, but only as through fire") and Matthew 12:32. The doctrine was articulated in the Latin West during the Middle Ages — the noun purgatorium appears in the 12th century — and affirmed at the Councils of Lyon II, Florence and Trent.

Other traditions

Most Protestant churches reject purgatory as unscriptural; its rejection was a central issue of the Reformation, particularly in disputes over indulgences. Eastern Orthodoxy affirms an intermediate state and prayers for the dead but generally does not teach the Latin doctrine of purgatory with its juridical framework of temporal punishment. Analogous concepts of post-mortem purification exist in some other religions, such as the twelve-month Gehenna of rabbinic Judaism and aspects of Islamic barzakh.

In literature and art

The most famous literary depiction is the second part of Dante Alighieri's Divine Comedy, the Purgatorio, which imagines purgatory as a seven-terraced mountain island in the Southern Hemisphere where souls purge the seven capital vices. The doctrine has inspired extensive art, music (requiem settings) and devotional literature.
