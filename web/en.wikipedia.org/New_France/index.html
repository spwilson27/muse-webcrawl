New France (French: Nouvelle-France) was the territory colonised by France in North America, beginning with Jacques Cartier's exploration of the Gulf of St. Lawrence in 1534 and ending with the cession of most of the territory to Britain and Spain after the Seven Years' War in 1763. At its height around 1712, New France extended from Newfoundland to the Rocky Mountains and from Hudson Bay to the Gulf of Mexico.

Colonies and settlement

New France comprised several colonies. Canada, along the St. Lawrence River, was the most populous; Samuel de Champlain founded Quebec City in 1608 and Montreal followed in 1642. Acadia lay in the Maritimes, Louisiana stretched up the Mississippi valley, and smaller colonies existed in Newfoundland and at Île Royale (Cape Breton Island). Settlement was driven by the fur trade, especially beaver pelts, carried on through alliances with Indigenous nations. The French population remained small — about 70,000 in Canada by 1760 — compared with over a million in the British colonies.

Government and society

From 1663 Canada was a royal province governed by a governor general, an intendant responsible for justice and finance, and a Sovereign Council, under the seigneurial system of landholding. The Catholic Church, through orders such as the Jesuits, ran missions, schools, and hospitals. Relations with Indigenous peoples mixed trade, alliance, mission work, and warfare, including the Beaver Wars with the Haudenosaunee.

Fall

The French and Indian War (1754–1763), the North American theatre of the Seven Years' War, ended with British victories at Quebec (1759) and Montreal (1760). By the Treaty of Paris (1763), France ceded Canada and its eastern Louisiana claims to Britain and western Louisiana to Spain, retaining only the small islands of Saint Pierre and Miquelon. French language, law, and Catholic institutions nevertheless survived in Quebec, shaping modern Canada.
