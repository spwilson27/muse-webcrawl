Reparations (transitional justice)

In transitional justice, reparations are measures provided to victims of gross human rights violations to recognise the harm suffered and, as far as possible, to repair its material and moral consequences. Reparations are one of the principal pillars of transitional justice, alongside truth-seeking, criminal prosecutions and institutional reform.

Forms

International standards, including the UN Basic Principles and Guidelines on the Right to a Remedy and Reparation (2005), recognise five forms: restitution (restoring the victim's original situation), compensation (money for economically assessable damage), rehabilitation (medical, psychological and social services), satisfaction (official acknowledgment, apologies, memorials and accurate historical records), and guarantees of non-repetition. Reparations may be individual or collective, material or symbolic.

Examples

Germany's post-war restitution and compensation programmes for Holocaust survivors (beginning with the 1952 Luxembourg Agreements) are often cited as the foundational modern scheme. Chile's Rettig Commission led to pensions for families of the executed and disappeared; South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission recommended reparations, though final payments were far smaller than proposed; Morocco, Peru, Colombia (under the 2011 Victims' Law) and Sierra Leone have operated large administrative programmes. The International Criminal Court may order reparations against convicted persons, supported by the Trust Fund for Victims.

Challenges

Programmes face dilemmas over identifying eligible victims, financing payments amid many competing needs, and avoiding the perception that money substitutes for justice. Scholars argue that reparations work best when linked with truth-telling, prosecutions and reform, and when victims participate meaningfully in their design.
