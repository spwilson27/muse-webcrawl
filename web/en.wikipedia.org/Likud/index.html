Likud (Hebrew: הַלִּכּוּד, HaLikud, "The Consolidation"), officially the Likud – National Liberal Movement, is a major right-wing political party in Israel. It was founded in 1973 by Menachem Begin and Ariel Sharon as an alliance of the Herut and Liberal parties and several smaller groups, and became a single unified party in 1988. Likud has been one of Israel's two dominant parties for most of the period since 1977 and has supplied several prime ministers, including Begin, Yitzhak Shamir, Benjamin Netanyahu, and Sharon (who later split to found Kadima).

History

Likud's roots lie in the Revisionist Zionist movement of Ze'ev Jabotinsky and in Begin's Herut party, which sat in opposition to the dominant Labor movement for Israel's first three decades. In the 1977 Knesset election — remembered as the Mahapakh ("upheaval") — Likud won a plurality and formed the first non-Labor government in Israel's history. Begin's government concluded the Camp David Accords and the 1979 peace treaty with Egypt while also accelerating settlement building in the occupied territories and launching the 1982 Lebanon War.

After periods in and out of power, Benjamin Netanyahu led Likud to victory in 1996, governing 1996–1999, and returned to the premiership in 2009, becoming Israel's longest-serving prime minister. Under Netanyahu, Likud has formed governments with religious and right-wing partners, and after the November 2022 election he formed a coalition widely described as the most right-wing in Israel's history. Likud governments have combined support for free-market reforms (including the liberalizations led by Netanyahu as finance minister in 2003–2005) with nationalist positions on territory and security, and opposition to a Palestinian state has hardened in party platforms over time.

Ideology and platform

Likud historically combined Revisionist Zionist territorial nationalism with the economic liberalism of the Liberal Party. Its 1977 platform asserted Jewish sovereignty between the Mediterranean Sea and the Jordan River. The party supports a strong military, Jerusalem as Israel's undivided capital, and — in its more recent platforms — application of Israeli law to Jewish settlements. At the same time, Likud leaders have sometimes taken pragmatic steps Begin's Sinai withdrawal and Netanyahu's acceptance in principle of a demilitarized Palestinian state in his 2009 Bar-Ilan speech (later qualified). Critics accuse the party of populism and of weakening judicial checks on government, particularly through the 2023 judicial reform programme; supporters frame such measures as restoring the balance between elected officials and the courts.
