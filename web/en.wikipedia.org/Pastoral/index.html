Pastoral

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The pastoral landscape: an idealised countryside of shepherds and flocks

The pastoral is a mode of literature, art and music that depicts the life of shepherds and rural existence in an idealised form, usually for an urban audience. Derived from the Latin pastor ("shepherd"), the pastoral tradition contrasts the innocence and simplicity of country life with the corruption and complexity of the court or city.

Origins in poetry

Pastoral poetry begins with the Idylls of the Greek poet Theocritus in the third century BC, whose shepherds sing, compete in song and lament lost loves in a stylised Sicilian countryside. Virgil's Eclogues brought the mode to Rome and gave it its enduring fictional landscape, Arcadia, while also allowing the shepherds' songs to glance at contemporary politics and patronage. The pastoral romance in prose, such as Jacopo Sannazaro's Arcadia (1504) and Philip Sidney's Arcadia, spread the fashion through Renaissance Europe.

Drama, verse and later forms

Pastoral drama flourished in Italy with Tasso's Aminta and Guarini's Il pastor fido, and in England the mode runs from Spenser's Shepheardes Calender through Milton's Lycidas to the shepherd scenes of Shakespeare's As You Like It. In the eighteenth century the pastoral was both cultivated and parodied — Pope wrote pastorals and also mocked their conventions — and critics such as William Empson, in Some Versions of Pastoral (1935), read the mode as a way of putting complex social truths into simple rural dress. Musical pastorals range from the shepherd scenes of Baroque opera to the "Pastoral" Symphony of Beethoven.

Themes

Recurring pastoral themes include the lost golden age, nostalgia for a simpler world, the singing contest, the lament for a dead shepherd (the pastoral elegy), and the retreat from court to country. Modern criticism also reads the pastoral against the real labour and dispossession of rural life, which its idealisations tend to conceal.
