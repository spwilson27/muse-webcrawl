Irish republicanism

Core idea: A united, independent Irish republic comprising the whole island of Ireland

Irish republicanism (Irish: Poblachtánachas Éireannach) is the political ideology that the island of Ireland should be unified as a single independent republic, free of British rule. It sits within the broader tradition of Irish nationalism, but is distinguished by its republican (rather than monarchical or home-rule) goal and, historically, by the willingness of many adherents to use physical force to achieve it.

Origins

Republican ideas arrived in Ireland in the 1790s through the Society of United Irishmen, inspired by the American and French Revolutions. Led by Theobald Wolfe Tone, the United Irishmen sought an independent republic uniting Catholic, Protestant and Dissenter; their 1798 rebellion was crushed. Nineteenth-century republicanism was carried on by Young Ireland and by the Fenians of the Irish Republican Brotherhood, who influenced later generations.

Twentieth century

The Easter Rising of 1916 and the declaration of an Irish Republic made republicanism the dominant strand of nationalism; Sinn Féin's 1918 election victory and the War of Independence led to the Irish Free State in 1922. Republicans who rejected the Treaty fought the Civil War, and abstentionist republicanism persisted in the IRA and Sinn Féin. During the Troubles, the Provisional IRA and Provisional Sinn Féin were the movement's main expressions. The Good Friday Agreement (1998) committed mainstream republicanism to achieving unity by exclusively peaceful and democratic means, with the principle of consent; Sinn Féin subsequently became the largest nationalist party in both jurisdictions. Small "dissident republican" groups reject this settlement.
