Tail

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The tail is the section at the rear end of certain kinds of animals' bodies; in general, the term refers to a distinct, flexible appendage to the torso. It is the part of the body that corresponds roughly to the sacrum and coccyx in mammals, reptiles, and birds. While tails are primarily a feature of vertebrates, some invertebrates including scorpions and springtails, as well as snails and slugs, have tail-like appendages that are sometimes referred to as tails.

Tailed objects are sometimes referred to as "caudate" and the part of the body associated with or proximal to the tail are given the adjective "caudal".

Function

Animal tails have evolved a wide variety of uses. They may be used for balance and thermoregulation, defence against predators, locomotion, communication, and prehension (grasping). The tails of many lizards can be shed when caught by a predator, a process known as autotomy; the detached tail continues to wriggle, distracting the predator while the lizard escapes.

In many monkeys, the tail is prehensile and can be used to grasp branches and manipulate objects. Birds use their tail feathers for steering and stability in flight. Fish and other marine animals use the tail as a fin for propulsion. Insectivores such as cats use their tails expressively to communicate mood, while dogs wag their tails as a social signal.

Human tails

Humans have a "tail bone" (the coccyx) attached to the pelvis; a structure on which the tails of other mammals develop. The coccyx is the remnant of a vestigial tail. During embryonic development, the human embryo briefly develops a tail-like structure, which is usually reabsorbed before birth. Occasionally, a child is born with a "soft tail", which contains no vertebrae, only blood vessels, muscles, and nerves; this is regarded as an atavism.
