Change blindness

Change blindness
FieldCognitive psychology
DefinitionFailure to notice large changes in a visual scene
RelatedInattentional blindness, visual attention

Change blindness is a perceptual phenomenon in which an observer fails to notice a large change in a visual stimulus, particularly when the change coincides with a brief visual disruption such as a flicker, a cut in a film, or an eye movement. It demonstrates that people retain far less of a scene than they intuitively believe.

Research

In the "flicker paradigm" developed by Ronald Rensink and colleagues, two versions of a scene alternate with a brief blank screen; observers may take many cycles to spot even a prominent change. In a famous real-world study by Simons and Levin, about half of pedestrians failed to notice that the person they were giving directions to had been swapped for someone else behind a passing door.

Implications

Change blindness challenges the idea that we build a complete internal picture of the world, instead suggesting that attention is required to perceive change. It has practical implications for eyewitness testimony, driving safety, and interface design, and is exploited by magicians and film editors, whose cuts rely on continuity errors going unnoticed.
