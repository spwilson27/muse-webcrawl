Denial

Denial, in ordinary English usage, is asserting that something said is untrue. In psychology, it is a defence mechanism proposed by Anna Freud which involves a refusal to accept reality, thus blocking external events from awareness. A person in denial avoids a reality that would otherwise cause psychological pain or anxiety.

Denial shields a person from an unbearable reality — at a cost

In psychoanalysis

Sigmund Freud used the concept of Verleugnung (disavowal) for the refusal to acknowledge a painful perception, and his daughter Anna Freud catalogued denial among the ego's defence mechanisms in her 1936 work The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence. Denial is considered one of the most primitive defences because it operates by simply rejecting aspects of reality.

In grief and addiction

Elisabeth Kübler-Ross placed denial as the first of her five stages of grief: denial, anger, bargaining, depression, and acceptance. In addiction medicine, "denial" describes the characteristic refusal of a person with substance dependence to acknowledge the severity of their problem, a pattern that clinicians in the tradition of Alcoholics Anonymous regard as a central obstacle to recovery.

Healthy and pathological denial

Short-lived denial can be adaptive, buffering shock after traumatic news. Persistent denial, however, can delay treatment for illness, sustain addiction, and distort relationships; psychotherapy often works by gently confronting denied realities at a pace the patient can tolerate.
