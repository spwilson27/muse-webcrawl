Mythologies of the indigenous peoples of the Americas

The Aztec sun stone, depicting the myth of the Five Suns

The mythologies of the indigenous peoples of the Americas comprise the body of traditional narratives associated with the many peoples of North, Central and South America prior to European colonization. There is no single commonly shared mythology, although certain common motifs — creation from chaos or emergence from a lower world, world floods, trickster figures, and culture heroes — recur across many cultures. These traditions were largely oral, though civilizations such as the Maya and Aztec also preserved myths in codices, inscriptions, and monumental art.

North America

Arctic and Subarctic

Inuit mythology centres on figures such as Sedna, mistress of the sea animals, whose favour hunters must retain. Shamanic practice mediates between human communities and the spirits of animals and weather. Among Subarctic peoples, tales of the trickster-transformer are widespread.

Plains and Great Lakes

Plains peoples tell of creation through earth-diver myths, in which an animal dives into primordial waters to bring up mud from which the earth is made. Trickster figures such as Coyote and the Lakota Iktomi teach as well as deceive. The Great Spirit (Wakan Tanka among the Lakota) is a central concept, alongside origin stories linked to sacred places such as the Black Hills.

Southwest

Pueblo peoples such as the Hopi and Zuni recount emergence narratives in which humanity climbs from underworlds through a reed or hole (sipapu) into the present world. Navajo (Diné) mythology describes the deeds of Changing Woman and the Holy People, and is enacted in healing ceremonies (sings).

Mesoamerica

Maya mythology, recorded in works such as the Popol Vuh of the K'iche' Maya, narrates the creation of humanity from maize after failed attempts with mud and wood, and the adventures of the Hero Twins Hunahpu and Xbalanque in the underworld of Xibalba. Aztec (Mexica) mythology describes the legend of the Five Suns — successive worlds destroyed by catastrophe — and the self-sacrifice of the gods at Teotihuacan to set the sun in motion, as well as the story of Quetzalcoatl, the feathered serpent.

South America

Inca mythology features the creator god Viracocha, the sun god Inti, and the founding myth of Manco Cápac and Mama Ocllo emerging from Lake Titicaca or the cave of Pacaritambo. Amazonian peoples maintain rich traditions of myths explaining the origins of cultivated plants, animals, and shamanic knowledge, while Andean communities venerate mountain spirits (apu) and Pachamama, the earth mother.

Common themes

  • Creation and emergence narratives
  • Tricksters such as Coyote, Raven, and Anansi-like figures
  • Culture heroes who bring fire, maize, or arts to humanity
  • World renewal ceremonies and cyclical time
  • Close relationships between humans, animals, and the land
