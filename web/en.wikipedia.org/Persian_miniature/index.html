A Persian miniature is a small, richly detailed painting on paper, developed within the tradition of Persian book illustration. Created to illuminate manuscripts — epics such as Ferdowsi's Shahnameh, romances like the Khamsa of Nizami, histories and scientific works — the Persian miniature is one of the most celebrated achievements of Islamic art.

Characteristics

Persian miniatures typically combine a high horizon, flat or tilted perspective, dense patterning, jewel-like colour and extensive use of gold. Scenes unfold across multiple registers of architecture and landscape; figures are stylised rather than naturalistic, and the image is often enclosed in elaborate borders with calligraphy integrated into the page design. Workshops (kitabkhana) divided labour among masters who composed the scene and assistants who coloured and finished it.

Development

The tradition crystallised under the Mongol Ilkhanids in the late 13th and 14th centuries, absorbing Chinese influences such as cloud bands and landscape motifs. Under the Timurids, the Herat school reached a classical refinement epitomised by Kamal ud-Din Behzad (c. 1450–1535). Safavid patronage in Tabriz and later Isfahan produced both sumptuous manuscripts and, increasingly, single-page paintings for albums (muraqqa). Persian painters carried the style to Mughal India, where it fused with local traditions.
