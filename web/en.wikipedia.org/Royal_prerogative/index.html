Royal prerogative

The royal prerogative is a body of customary authority, privilege and immunity recognised in common law as belonging to the sovereign alone. It is the means by which some of the executive powers of government are possessed by and vested in a monarch. In most modern constitutional monarchies, including the United Kingdom, prerogative powers are exercised in practice by ministers, usually on the advice of the prime minister or cabinet, rather than by the monarch personally.

Scope

Historically the prerogative covered a wide range of powers: making war and peace, concluding treaties, summoning and dissolving Parliament, appointing ministers and judges, granting honours and pardons, issuing passports, and directing the armed forces. The constitutional settlement following the Glorious Revolution of 1688, embodied in the Bill of Rights 1689, subordinated the prerogative to statute: the Crown could not suspend or dispense with laws, levy taxation, or maintain a standing army in peacetime without parliamentary consent.

Prerogative in the United Kingdom

In the UK, surviving prerogative powers include the appointment of the prime minister, the granting of royal assent, the declaration of war and deployment of the armed forces, the conduct of foreign affairs and treaty-making, the prorogation and dissolution of Parliament (subject to statutory change), the royal pardon, and the issue of passports. Many have been placed on a statutory footing or constrained by convention and judicial review. Landmark cases such as the GCHQ case (1985) confirmed that prerogative powers are, in principle, reviewable by the courts; in R (Miller) v The Prime Minister (2019) the Supreme Court held that an unlawful prorogation advice rendered the prorogation itself void.

Other Commonwealth realms

Similar prerogative powers exist in other Commonwealth realms, exercised by governors-general as the sovereign's representatives. In Canada, for example, reserve powers of the Crown were at issue in the King–Byng affair of 1926; in Australia, Governor-General Sir John Kerr's use of reserve powers to dismiss the Whitlam government in 1975 remains controversial.

Reform

Because prerogative powers bypass Parliament, they have long attracted criticism and periodic reform proposals. The Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 temporarily abolished the prerogative of dissolution before its repeal by the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022, which revived the prerogative power. Conventions now require, in practice, a parliamentary vote before major military deployments.
