Post-quantum cryptography

Post-quantum cryptography (PQC), sometimes referred to as quantum-proof, quantum-safe or quantum-resistant cryptography, is the development of cryptographic algorithms that are thought to be secure against cryptanalysis by a quantum computer. The problem with currently popular algorithms is that their security relies on one of three hard mathematical problems: the integer factorisation problem, the discrete logarithm problem, or the elliptic-curve discrete logarithm problem. All of these problems could be easily solved on a sufficiently powerful quantum computer running Shor's algorithm.

Even though present-day quantum computers lack processing power to break modern cryptographic algorithms, cryptographers are designing new algorithms now in preparation for the day when a cryptographically relevant quantum computer exists — a point in time known as "Y2Q" or "Q-Day". The concern is sharpened by "harvest now, decrypt later" attacks, in which adversaries record encrypted traffic today for future decryption.

Families of algorithms

Post-quantum schemes are based on mathematical problems for which no efficient quantum algorithm is known:

  • Lattice-based cryptography — security from problems such as Learning With Errors (LWE) and NTRU; includes the ML-KEM key encapsulation mechanism and ML-DSA signatures standardised by NIST.
  • Hash-based signatures — security from the properties of cryptographic hash functions alone; includes SPHINCS+ (SLH-DSA) and stateful schemes LMS and XMSS.
  • Code-based cryptography — based on decoding random linear codes, exemplified by the McEliece cryptosystem proposed in 1978.
  • Multivariate cryptography — based on solving systems of multivariate polynomial equations.
  • Isogeny-based cryptography — based on maps between elliptic curves; the prominent SIKE scheme was broken in 2022 by a classical attack.

Standardisation

The US National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) ran a public competition beginning in 2016 and in August 2024 published the first three final standards: FIPS 203 (ML-KEM, derived from CRYSTALS-Kyber), FIPS 204 (ML-DSA, from CRYSTALS-Dilithium) and FIPS 205 (SLH-DSA, from SPHINCS+), with a FALCON-based signature standard to follow. Governments and industry have begun migration programmes, and hybrid deployments combining classical and post-quantum key exchange are already used in protocols such as TLS.
