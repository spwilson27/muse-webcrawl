Functionalism (philosophy of mind)

Functionalism is a theory in the philosophy of mind, developed in the 1960s by Hilary Putnam and others, according to which mental states are individuated not by their internal material constitution but by their functional role — their causal relations to sensory inputs, behavioural outputs, and other mental states. Pain, for example, is whatever state is typically caused by tissue damage and typically causes withdrawal, distress, and the desire that it stop.

Within the mind–body problem

Functionalism was proposed as a solution to the mind–body problem that avoids both Cartesian dualism and the type-identity theory's implausible claim that pain must be one specific brain state in every species. Because functional roles can be realised in many different materials — neurons, silicon circuits, perhaps alien biology — functionalism entails multiple realizability and treats the mind as analogous to software running on the hardware of the brain. It thereby underpins the computational theory of mind and much of cognitive science.

Objections

Critics argue that functional roles omit the qualitative feel of experience: a system could instantiate the functional organisation of pain without anything hurting (the problem of absent qualia, illustrated by Ned Block's "China brain" thought experiment), or with inverted qualia (see the inverted spectrum). John Searle's Chinese room similarly targets the computational version of functionalism. Defenders respond that richer functional specifications — including relations among experiences themselves — can capture what the objections leave out.
