Hard problem of consciousness

The hard problem of consciousness is widely regarded as the deepest form of the mind–body problem: even if we knew exactly which neural states realise which mental functions, it seems unexplained why any of those physical processes should be accompanied by subjective experience — why pain feels like anything at all. The label was introduced by David Chalmers in 1995, distinguishing the hard problem from the "easy problems" of explaining reportability, attention, and behavioural control.

Relation to the mind–body problem

Classical positions on the mind–body problem distribute differently over the hard problem. Dualists take it at face value as evidence that experience is a fundamental, non-physical feature of the world. Reductive physicalists deny the gap is genuine: on their view, once the functional facts are in, nothing remains to explain. Functionalists hold that a state with the right causal role simply is a conscious state. Chalmers' own framing relies on the conceivability of philosophical zombies — molecular duplicates of conscious beings that lack experience — to argue that physical facts do not entail phenomenal facts.

Contemporary debate

Proposed responses include the phenomenal concept strategy, Russellian monism and panpsychism (on which experience is the intrinsic nature of physical processes), and illusionism, which denies that phenomenal properties exist in the form introspection suggests. Empirical research pursues the neural correlates of consciousness while philosophers dispute whether correlation could ever close the explanatory gap that defines the hard problem.
