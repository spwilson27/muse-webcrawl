René Descartes

René Descartes
Born: 31 March 1596, La Haye, France
Died: 11 February 1650, Stockholm, Sweden
Notable work: Meditations on First Philosophy (1641)
Notable idea: Cogito ergo sum

René Descartes (1596–1650) was a French philosopher, mathematician, and scientist whose work set the terms of the modern mind–body problem. In the Meditations on First Philosophy (1641), reasoning from radical doubt to the indubitable cogito ergo sum ("I think, therefore I am"), Descartes argued that the mind can be conceived clearly and distinctly apart from the body, and concluded that they are really distinct substances.

Dualism and the interaction problem

Cartesian dualism divides reality into res cogitans, thinking substance whose essence is thought, and res extensa, extended substance whose essence is spatial extension. Because the two substances must nonetheless interact — volitions move limbs, sensations arise from bodily impacts — Descartes proposed that they interface in the pineal gland, the brain's only unpaired structure. Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia pressed the obvious difficulty in correspondence of 1643: by what mechanism can an immaterial, unextended thing push on matter? Descartes' replies are generally judged inconclusive, and the "interaction problem" has shadowed dualism ever since.

Legacy in philosophy of mind

Nearly every subsequent theory of mind defines itself against Descartes: physicalism by rejecting his second substance, behaviourism by rejecting his private inner theatre, and Gilbert Ryle by caricaturing dualism as the doctrine of the "ghost in the machine". Yet Descartes' framing — consciousness as the transparent, self-present datum from which inquiry must start — still structures debates over the hard problem. Outside philosophy he founded analytic geometry and made foundational contributions to optics and mechanics.
