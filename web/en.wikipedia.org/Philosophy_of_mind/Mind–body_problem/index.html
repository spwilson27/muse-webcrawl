Mind–body problem

Mind–body problem
FieldPhilosophy of mind, metaphysics
Central figureRené Descartes
PositionsDualism, physicalism, idealism, neutral monism, panpsychism
Related problemHard problem of consciousness

The mind–body problem concerns the relationship between thought and consciousness in the human mind and the matter of the physical body. It asks how mental phenomena—beliefs, desires, sensations, and experiences—relate to physical states of the brain and body: are they identical, distinct but interacting, or aspects of a single reality?

Descartes and substance dualism

The problem took its classic modern form in René Descartes, who argued in the Meditations (1641) that mind (res cogitans) and body (res extensa) are distinct substances. Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia pressed the decisive objection: how can an immaterial substance causally interact with a material one? Descartes's suggestion that interaction occurred in the pineal gland satisfied few. Later dualists adopted occasionalism, parallelism, or epiphenomenalism to avoid the interaction problem.

Monist alternatives

Monists deny the two-substance picture. Physicalists identify mental states with brain states (identity theory) or with functional roles (functionalism); behaviorists and eliminative materialists reinterpret or reject folk-psychological states. Idealists take mind as fundamental; neutral monists (Spinoza, Russell) treat both mind and matter as aspects of a deeper reality. Property dualism, defended by David Chalmers, holds that physical facts may necessitate distinct phenomenal properties.

Contemporary status

The problem persists largely through the hard problem of consciousness: physical explanations seem to leave subjective experience unexplained. Neuroscience has mapped increasingly fine correlations between brain activity and mental states, but the metaphysical interpretation of those correlations remains contested.
