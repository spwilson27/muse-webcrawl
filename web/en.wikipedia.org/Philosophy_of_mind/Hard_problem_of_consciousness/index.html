Hard problem of consciousness

Hard problem of consciousness
Coined byDavid Chalmers (1995)
FieldPhilosophy of mind, cognitive science
QuestionWhy and how physical processes give rise to subjective experience
Related conceptsExplanatory gap, qualia, philosophical zombie

The hard problem of consciousness is the problem of explaining why and how sentient organisms have qualia—subjective, phenomenal experiences. The term was coined by philosopher David Chalmers in his 1995 paper "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness" and developed in his book The Conscious Mind (1996). Chalmers contrasted it with the "easy problems" of explaining cognitive functions such as discrimination, integration of information, and verbal report, which seem tractable by standard neuroscientific methods even if difficult in practice.

Formulation

Even a complete account of brain function, Chalmers argues, would leave unexplained why that functioning is accompanied by experience—why seeing red feels like something rather than proceeding "in the dark." The problem is linked to Joseph Levine's notion of the explanatory gap and to Thomas Nagel's question of what it is like to be a bat. Supporting thought experiments include Frank Jackson's Mary the colour scientist, who knows all physical facts about colour but learns something new on first seeing red, and the conceivability of philosophical zombies—beings physically identical to us but lacking experience.

Responses

Responses divide broadly into those that accept the problem and those that dissolve it. Chalmers himself defends a form of naturalistic dualism and later panpsychism. Dual-aspect theory, mysterianism (Colin McGinn), and Russellian monism also treat experience as fundamental. By contrast, Daniel Dennett, Patricia Churchland, and illusionists such as Keith Frankish argue the hard problem rests on mistaken intuitions about introspection. Integrated information theory and global workspace theory attempt, in different ways, to give it empirical content.
