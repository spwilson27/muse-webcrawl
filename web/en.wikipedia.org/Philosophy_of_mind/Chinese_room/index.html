Chinese room

The Chinese room argument is a thought experiment proposed by the philosopher John Searle in his 1980 paper "Minds, Brains, and Programs". It aims to show that a computer executing a program cannot thereby have a mind, understanding, or consciousness, no matter how intelligently it may behave — a direct challenge to "strong AI" and to the computational theory of mind.

The argument

Searle asks us to imagine a person who understands no Chinese locked in a room with a set of Chinese symbols and a rule book (the program) written in English. People outside pass in Chinese characters (questions); following the rule book, the person manipulates symbols and passes back other characters (answers) indistinguishable from those of a native speaker. To outside observers the room passes the Turing test for understanding Chinese — yet the person inside understands nothing. Syntax, Searle concludes, is not sufficient for semantics: symbol manipulation alone does not produce intentionality or understanding.

Replies and significance

The best-known responses include the systems reply (the whole system, not the person, understands), the robot reply (embodiment in a perceiving robot would ground the symbols), and the brain simulator reply. The argument remains a standard reference point in debates over machine consciousness, standing alongside the knowledge argument and the hard problem of consciousness as a challenge to physicalism about the mind.
