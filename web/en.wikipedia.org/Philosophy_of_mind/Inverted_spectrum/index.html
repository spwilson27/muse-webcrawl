Inverted spectrum

The inverted spectrum is a thought experiment in the philosophy of mind, traceable to a passage in John Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1689). It imagines two people whose subjective colour experiences are systematically inverted — what one experiences on seeing red, the other experiences on seeing green, and vice versa — while their behaviour, language, and discriminatory capacities remain identical.

Philosophical significance

If such inversion is genuinely possible without any functional or behavioural difference, then the qualitative character of experience (qualia) cannot be identical to any functional or physical state, since every such state is held fixed in the scenario. The argument thus pressures functionalism and behaviourism, and it complements the knowledge argument and the zombie argument in the case against reductive physicalism.

Responses

Functionalists reply that a true spectrum inversion would in fact involve subtle differences — in colour similarity spaces, memory comparisons, and emotional associations (ripe tomatoes and fire engines are "warm" colours) — so a behaviourally undetectable inversion may be impossible. Others, such as Sydney Shoemaker, argue that if qualia are individuated by their relations to other experiences, the scenario is incoherent. The thought experiment remains a staple test case for theories of consciousness and its hard problem.
