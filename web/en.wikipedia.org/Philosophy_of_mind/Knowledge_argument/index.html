Knowledge argument

The knowledge argument is a philosophical argument against physicalism — the view that everything, including consciousness, is physical. First formulated in its modern form by Frank Jackson in 1982 ("Epiphenomenal Qualia"), it centres on the thought experiment of Mary the colour scientist.

Mary's room

Mary is a brilliant scientist who knows everything there is to know about the physical processes of colour vision — every fact of physics, neurophysiology, and functional organisation — but has lived her whole life in a black-and-white room and has never seen a colour. When she leaves the room and sees a red tomato for the first time, she seems to learn something new: what it is like to experience red. But if she already knew all the physical facts, then what she learns cannot be a physical fact; so physicalism, which claims all facts are physical, must be false. Jackson concluded that qualia are real but epiphenomenal — though he later renounced the argument.

Responses

Physicalists offer three main replies. The ability hypothesis (Lewis, Nemirow) says Mary gains no new propositional knowledge but only know-how — abilities to recognise, remember, and imagine red. The acquaintance hypothesis says she gains knowledge by acquaintance of an old fact under a new mode of presentation. The phenomenal concept strategy holds that Mary acquires a new concept for a physical fact she already knew. The argument remains, with the hard problem and the zombie argument, one of the central challenges to a purely physical account of mind.
