Qualia

In philosophy of mind, qualia (singular: quale) are defined as instances of subjective, conscious experience. Examples of qualia include the perceived sensation of pain of a headache, the taste of wine, and the redness of an evening sky. As qualitative characters of sensation, qualia stand in contrast to propositional attitudes, where the focus is on beliefs about experience rather than what it feels like to undergo it.

The term "qualia" derives from the Latin word for "what sort" or "what kind". The importance of qualia in philosophy of mind comes largely from the fact that they are often seen as posing a fundamental challenge to physicalist accounts of the mind. Thought experiments such as Frank Jackson's "Mary's room" and Thomas Nagel's "What is it like to be a bat?" are intended to show that qualia cannot be fully captured by physical descriptions.

Definitions

Daniel Dennett identifies four properties commonly ascribed to qualia: they are ineffable (cannot be communicated or apprehended by other means than direct experience), intrinsic (non-relational properties), private (interpersonal comparisons are impossible), and directly or immediately apprehensible in consciousness. Dennett argues that no coherent notion satisfying all four properties exists.

History

Precursors of the concept appear in Charles Sanders Peirce and in C. I. Lewis's Mind and the World Order (1929), which used "qualia" for the recognizable qualitative characters of experience. The notion was sharpened by later analytic philosophers and became central to debates about consciousness.

Arguments concerning qualia

The knowledge argument

Frank Jackson's thought experiment concerns Mary, a scientist who knows everything physical about color vision but has lived in a black-and-white room. Jackson argued that when Mary leaves the room and sees color for the first time, she learns something new — what it is like to see red — showing that physical knowledge is incomplete.

Inverted spectrum

The inverted spectrum scenario imagines two people whose color experiences are systematically swapped while their behavior and functional states remain identical. If possible, it suggests qualia are not fixed by functional or physical facts.

Philosophical zombies

David Chalmers's zombie argument imagines beings physically identical to conscious humans but lacking any subjective experience. Their conceivability is taken to support the conclusion that consciousness does not logically supervene on the physical.

Criticism

Physicalists and eliminativists respond in various ways: some deny that qualia as traditionally defined exist (eliminativism), others argue that qualia are identical to brain states (identity theory), and functionalists maintain that mental states are defined by their causal roles. Dennett's "intuition pump" critiques and Paul Churchland's arguments are prominent critiques of the traditional concept.
