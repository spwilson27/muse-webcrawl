Hard problem of consciousness

The hard problem of consciousness, formulated by the philosopher David Chalmers in 1995, is the problem of explaining why and how physical processes in the brain give rise to subjective experience — why there is "something it is like" to see red, taste coffee, or feel pain. It is to be contrasted with the "easy problems" of consciousness, which concern explaining cognitive functions such as discrimination, integration of information, attention, and verbal report.

The explanatory gap

Chalmers argues that even a complete account of the brain's functional and physical processes would leave unanswered why those processes are accompanied by experience at all. The problem builds on Thomas Nagel's 1974 essay "What Is It Like to Be a Bat?", Joseph Levine's notion of an explanatory gap, and Frank Jackson's knowledge argument about Mary the colour scientist. Chalmers also deploys the conceivability of philosophical zombies — beings physically identical to us but lacking experience — to argue that consciousness does not logically supervene on the physical, challenging physicalism.

Responses

Physicalists respond in several ways: some, like Daniel Dennett, deny that the hard problem is well posed and accuse it of trading on failed intuitions; illusionists such as Keith Frankish hold that phenomenal consciousness as traditionally conceived is an introspective illusion; others propose new theories — integrated information theory, global workspace theory, higher-order theories — that attempt to bridge the gap. Chalmers himself has defended a form of naturalistic dualism and found panpsychism increasingly attractive. The hard problem remains the central organising puzzle of the study of consciousness, motivating empirical work on its neural correlates.
