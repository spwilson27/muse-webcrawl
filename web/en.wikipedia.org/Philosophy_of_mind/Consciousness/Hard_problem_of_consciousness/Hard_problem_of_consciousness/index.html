Coined by: David Chalmers, 1995
Field: Philosophy of mind

Hard problem of consciousness

The hard problem of consciousness is the problem of explaining why and how sentient organisms have qualia, or phenomenal experiences — why physical processes in the brain are accompanied by subjective experience at all. The term was coined by philosopher David Chalmers in 1995, contrasting it with the "easy problems" of explaining cognitive functions such as discrimination, integration of information, and reportability.

The explanatory gap

Even a complete account of the brain's functional processes, Chalmers argues, would leave unanswered why those processes are accompanied by experience. This is related to Joseph Levine's notion of the explanatory gap and to Thomas Nagel's question of what it is like to be a conscious organism.

Responses

Responses vary widely. Dualists and panpsychists take consciousness to be a fundamental feature of reality not reducible to physical processes. Physicalists respond that the problem rests on a conceptual confusion, or that phenomenal concepts mislead us into seeing a gap where none exists. Illusionists, such as Daniel Dennett and Keith Frankish, hold that introspection misrepresents experience, and that explaining why we believe we have qualia dissolves the hard problem.
