Singular: quale
Field: Philosophy of mind

Qualia

In philosophy of mind, qualia (singular: quale) are defined as instances of subjective, conscious experience. Examples of qualia include the perceived sensation of pain in a headache, the taste of wine, and the redness of an evening sky. As qualitative characters of sensation, qualia stand in contrast to propositional attitudes, where the focus is on beliefs about experience rather than what it is like to undergo it.

Definitions

Daniel Dennett identifies four properties commonly ascribed to qualia: they are ineffable (cannot be communicated in full), intrinsic (non-relational), private (not interpersonally comparable), and directly apprehensible in consciousness. Whether qualia exist, and what their existence would imply for physicalism, remains contested.

Arguments

Famous thought experiments about qualia include Frank Jackson's knowledge argument, involving Mary, a scientist who knows all the physical facts about colour vision but has never seen colour, and the inverted spectrum, in which two people functionally alike might experience colours differently. Critics such as Dennett argue that qualia, as traditionally conceived, do not exist and that the intuitions behind these arguments are confused.
