Hard problem of consciousness

The hard problem of consciousness is a philosophical problem concerning why and how humans and other organisms have qualia — subjective, first-person experiences. It was formulated by the philosopher David Chalmers in 1995. The hard problem contrasts with the so-called "easy problems" of explaining the physical systems that give us the ability to discriminate, integrate information, report mental states, and focus attention — problems that are hard in practice but tractable by the standard methods of neuroscience.

The hard problem asks why physical processes in the brain should be accompanied by experience at all: why, when the brain processes information in certain ways, there is something it is like to be the subject of that processing.

Formulation

Chalmers writes that even when all the relevant perceptual and cognitive functions have been explained, "there may still remain a further unanswered question: Why is the performance of these functions accompanied by experience?" The problem builds on older observations, including Thomas Nagel's question "What is it like to be a bat?" and Joseph Levine's notion of an "explanatory gap" between physical descriptions and experience.

The explanatory gap

The explanatory gap refers to the difficulty of accounting for subjective experience in purely physical terms. Even a complete neuroscience of the brain's information processing seems, to many philosophers, to leave out the qualities of experience themselves.

Responses

Eliminativism and illusionism

Daniel Dennett and others deny that the hard problem is well-posed, arguing that consciousness can be explained in terms of cognitive functions — Dennett's "multiple drafts model" and "fame in the brain". Keith Frankish defends illusionism: introspection represents experiences as having phenomenal properties, but those properties are introspective illusions.

Physicalist responses

Some physicalists argue that, with enough empirical knowledge, the apparent mystery will dissolve, comparing the situation to past cases in which phenomena such as life once seemed inexplicable. Others propose identity theories or representationalist accounts holding that phenomenal character is a kind of representation.

Non-physicalist responses

Property dualists such as Chalmers hold that experience is a fundamental feature of the world, perhaps calling for new fundamental laws (a "naturalistic dualism"). Panpsychists, such as Galen Strawson and Philip Goff, propose that experience is fundamental and ubiquitous. Mysterians such as Colin McGinn argue the problem is real but cognitively closed to human minds.

Scientific approaches

Neuroscientific theories of consciousness — including global workspace theory, integrated information theory, and higher-order theories — attempt to identify the neural correlates of consciousness. Critics debate whether such theories address the hard problem or only the easy problems of function and access.
