Qualia

Qualia (singular quale) are the individual instances of subjective, conscious experience: the redness of a sunset, the taste of wine, the pain of a headache. The term, from the Latin for "what sort" or "what kind", was given its modern philosophical use by C. I. Lewis in 1929 and became central to late twentieth-century debates about whether consciousness can be fully explained in physical terms.

Properties and arguments

Qualia are standardly characterised as ineffable (their character cannot be fully communicated), intrinsic, private, and directly apprehensible in consciousness. Frank Jackson's knowledge argument uses qualia to challenge physicalism: Mary, who knows every physical fact about colour vision but has never seen colour, seems to learn something new — what red looks like — when she first sees it. The inverted spectrum scenario raises a related puzzle: two people might have systematically different colour qualia while making identical discriminations.

Criticism

Daniel Dennett has argued that qualia, as defined by their defenders, do not exist, offering "intuition pumps" such as the case of the wine tasters and the neurosurgeon to show that our pre-theoretic notions are confused. Functionalists and representationalists instead identify qualia with representational or functional properties of mental states, while illusionists treat phenomenal properties as introspective misrepresentations. Whether qualia are irreducible remains the crux of the hard problem of consciousness.
