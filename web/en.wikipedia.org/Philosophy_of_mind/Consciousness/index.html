Consciousness

Consciousness

Consciousness, at its simplest, is sentience or awareness of internal and external existence. Despite millennia of inquiry by philosophers, theologians, and scientists, it remains one of the most familiar and most mysterious aspects of human life: the fact that mental states such as seeing red, feeling pain, or hearing music are accompanied by subjective experience—there is "something it is like" to have them, in Thomas Nagel's phrase.

Aspects and distinctions

Philosophers distinguish phenomenal consciousness (raw experience) from access consciousness (information available for reasoning and report, in Ned Block's terminology), and creature consciousness from state consciousness. Self-consciousness involves awareness of oneself as a subject. Levels of consciousness vary from deep sleep and coma through dreaming and drowsiness to full wakefulness; disorders such as the vegetative and minimally conscious states are assessed clinically with scales like the Glasgow Coma Scale.

The hard problem

David Chalmers divided the study of consciousness into "easy problems"—explaining discrimination, integration, and reportability of information—and the hard problem: why physical processing is accompanied by experience at all. Proposed scientific theories include global workspace theory, integrated information theory, higher-order theories, and predictive processing accounts. Philosophical positions range from dualism and panpsychism to illusionism and various forms of physicalism.

History of the concept

René Descartes located consciousness in an immaterial mind interacting with the body; John Locke made it central to personal identity. William James described consciousness as a "stream" in his Principles of Psychology (1890). Behaviorism largely excluded it from science in the early twentieth century, but the cognitive revolution and advances in neuroimaging restored it as a central research topic from the 1990s onward.
