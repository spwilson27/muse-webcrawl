Philosophy of mind

Philosophy of mind is the branch of philosophy that studies the nature of the mind, mental events, mental functions, mental properties, and consciousness, and their relationship to the physical body — the cluster of questions known as the mind–body problem. It is closely connected to metaphysics, epistemology, cognitive science, neuroscience, and artificial intelligence.

Central problems

The field's founding modern text is René Descartes' Meditations (1641), which argued for dualism: mind and body as distinct substances. The dominant contemporary alternative is physicalism, in its reductive forms (the identity theory, functionalism) and non-reductive forms. Beyond the mind–body problem, philosophers of mind study intentionality — how thoughts can be about things — personal identity, free will, perception, emotion, and the nature of consciousness itself, whose subjective character generates the hard problem and motivates research into qualia and the neural correlates of consciousness.

Key debates

Signature thought experiments organise the field: the Chinese room against the computational theory of mind; the knowledge argument and the philosophical zombie against physicalism; the inverted spectrum against functionalism. Positions in play include eliminative materialism, anomalous monism, emergentism, property dualism, and panpsychism. The discipline increasingly engages empirical work in psychology and neuroscience while insisting that conceptual questions about experience and intentionality cannot be dissolved by data alone.
