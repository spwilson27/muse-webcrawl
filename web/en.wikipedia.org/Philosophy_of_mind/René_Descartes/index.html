René Descartes (1596–1650) was a French philosopher, scientist, and mathematician, widely considered the father of modern Western philosophy and of analytic geometry. Writing in an age when Aristotelian scholasticism still dominated the universities, he sought to rebuild knowledge on self-evident foundations and to explain nature mechanistically.

Method and metaphysics

In the Discourse on the Method (1637) Descartes proposed resolving problems into simple parts and doubting everything that could possibly be doubted. The Meditations on First Philosophy (1641) dramatize this search: even if an evil demon deceives him, the fact that he thinks proves he exists—cogito ergo sum ("I think, therefore I am"). From this foundation he argued for the existence of God and for a real distinction between the thinking mind and extended matter, the substance dualism at the core of the mind–body problem.

Science and mathematics

The Discourse carried appendices on optics, meteorology, and geometry; La Géométrie introduced the coordinate system that bears his name (Cartesian coordinates) and fused algebra with geometry. His Principles of Philosophy (1644) presented a vortex theory of planetary motion and laws of nature that influenced physics until Newton. He also made early contributions to the theory of reflexes and to the conservation of motion.

Death and influence

Descartes spent most of his productive life in the Dutch Republic. In 1649 he accepted Queen Christina of Sweden's invitation to Stockholm, where the cold climate and early-morning tutorials contributed to the pneumonia that killed him in February 1650. His works frame problems—dualism, skepticism, the foundations of knowledge—that philosophy still addresses.
