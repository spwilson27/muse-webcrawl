Physicalism

Physicalism is the philosophical position that everything is physical — that there are no entities, properties, or facts beyond those of the physical world as described by the physical sciences. In the philosophy of mind, physicalism (closely related to materialism) holds that mental states, including consciousness, are physical states of the brain or of the organism, and that a complete physical description of the world would in principle be a complete description of everything.

Forms

Reductive physicalism includes the type-identity theory (mental states are identical to specific brain states) and functionalism, which identifies mental states by causal role while allowing multiple physical realisations. Non-reductive physicalism accepts that mental properties are not straightforwardly reducible yet insists they supervene on the physical: no mental difference without a physical difference. Eliminative materialism, defended by Paul and Patricia Churchland, predicts that folk-psychological categories such as belief and desire will be discarded as neuroscience matures.

Challenges

Physicalism faces a battery of arguments from the character of experience: the knowledge argument (Mary learns something new on seeing red), the conceivability of philosophical zombies, the inverted spectrum, and the hard problem of consciousness generally. Hempel's dilemma presses from another direction: define "physical" by current physics and the thesis is probably false (current physics is incomplete); define it by some ideal future physics and the thesis becomes vacuous. Defenders respond with the phenomenal concept strategy and a posteriori identities modelled on water = H₂O, maintaining that the success of neuroscience makes physicalism the default position against dualism.
