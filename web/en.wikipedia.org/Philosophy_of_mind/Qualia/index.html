Qualia (singular quale) are the individual instances of subjective, conscious experience: the redness of red, the painfulness of pain, the taste of coffee. The term, from the Latin for "what sort" or "what kind," was used in its modern philosophical sense by C. I. Lewis in Mind and the World Order (1929) and became central to debates about whether the physical sciences can fully explain the mind.

Characteristics

Daniel Dennett summarized the properties traditionally ascribed to qualia as fourfold: they are ineffable (cannot be fully communicated), intrinsic (non-relational properties of experience), private (accessible only to their subject), and directly apprehensible (known immediately, without inference). Disagreement over whether anything satisfies this description drives much of the debate.

Arguments involving qualia

Frank Jackson's knowledge argument asks us to imagine Mary, a scientist who knows every physical fact about colour vision but has never seen colour; when she first sees red she seems to learn something new, suggesting qualia outrun physical facts. Thomas Nagel argued that the subjective character of experience—what it is like to be a bat—escapes objective physical description. The possibility of inverted spectra (your red being my green while all behavior matches) and of philosophical zombies is likewise used to probe whether qualia are reducible.

Criticism

Physicalists respond in various ways: the "ability hypothesis" holds Mary gains know-how, not new facts; representationalists identify qualia with representational content; illusionists such as Dennett and Keith Frankish deny that qualia, as traditionally defined, exist at all. Qualia remain at the heart of the hard problem of consciousness.
