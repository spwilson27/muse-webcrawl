Computational theory of mind

The computational theory of mind (CTM), also known as computationalism, is a family of views in the philosophy of mind holding that the human mind is, or is best understood as, an information processing system, and that thought is a form of computation. Developed by Hilary Putnam in the 1960s and elaborated by Jerry Fodor, the theory treats mental states as computational relations to mental representations — sentences in a "language of thought" — and mental processes as operations on those representations.

Core ideas

On the classical version, thinking consists of the rule-governed manipulation of symbols whose syntactic structure mirrors their semantic content, much as a Turing machine or digital computer transforms inputs into outputs. Because the same computation can be implemented in different physical media, computationalism underwrites functionalism: what makes a state a belief or desire is its causal-computational role, not its material substrate. The theory provided the theoretical foundation for classical cognitive science and symbolic artificial intelligence.

Criticisms

John Searle's Chinese room argues that computation, defined syntactically, can never by itself yield semantic understanding. Others object that CTM neglects emotion, embodiment, and the qualitative character of experience (see the hard problem of consciousness). Connectionism — modelling cognition with artificial neural networks rather than explicit symbols — arose partly as a rival computational framework, and contemporary debates ask whether large-scale connectionist systems vindicate, revise, or refute the classical theory.
