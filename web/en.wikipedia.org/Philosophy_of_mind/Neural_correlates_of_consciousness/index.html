Neural correlates of consciousness

The neural correlates of consciousness (NCC) are the minimal set of neuronal events and mechanisms jointly sufficient for a specific conscious percept or experience. The research programme, promoted by Francis Crick and Christof Koch from the 1990s, seeks to find which patterns of brain activity correspond to seeing a face, feeling a pain, or hearing a tone — and which activity, by contrast, proceeds unconsciously.

Approach and findings

The standard method contrasts matched conditions that differ only in conscious content, as in binocular rivalry, where a constant retinal stimulus alternates in perception between two images; neural activity that tracks the percept rather than the stimulus is a candidate NCC. Studies using EEG, fMRI, and intracranial recording implicate widespread cortical activity, particularly in posterior sensory areas and in frontoparietal networks, together with late, broadly distributed responses (such as the P3b wave) rather than early feed-forward activity confined to primary sensory cortex.

Theoretical stakes

Rival theories interpret the correlates differently. Global workspace theory takes consciousness to consist in information broadcast across a frontoparietal "workspace"; integrated information theory (IIT) locates it in posterior cortex and quantifies it with the measure Φ; higher-order theories tie it to metarepresentation. Adversarial collaborations have begun testing these theories against one another experimentally. Philosophers note, however, that correlation alone cannot settle the hard problem: identifying the NCC describes where and when experience occurs, not why those processes are experienced at all — a gap that divides physicalists from dualists.
