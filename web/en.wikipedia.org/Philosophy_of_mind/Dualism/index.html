Dualism (philosophy of mind)

In the philosophy of mind, dualism is a family of views holding that mental phenomena are, in some respects, non-physical — that mind and body are distinct kinds of thing or bear distinct kinds of properties. Its best-known form is the substance dualism of René Descartes, who argued that the mind (a thinking substance, res cogitans) is essentially distinct from the body (an extended substance, res extensa).

Varieties

Substance dualism posits two kinds of substance, mental and physical. Property dualism, defended in modern form by David Chalmers, holds that there is only physical substance but that conscious experience involves non-physical properties not reducible to physical ones. Epiphenomenalism grants mental properties reality but denies them causal power over the physical world, while interactionism — Descartes' own view — asserts two-way causation between mind and body, which Descartes tentatively localised in the pineal gland.

Arguments and objections

Dualists appeal to the conceivability of philosophical zombies, the knowledge argument, and the apparent privacy and indivisibility of consciousness. The classic objection, pressed by Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia on Descartes himself, is the interaction problem: how can an immaterial mind causally affect a material body without violating the causal closure of physics? Most contemporary philosophers reject substance dualism in favour of physicalism, though property dualism and panpsychism have seen renewed interest driven by the hard problem of consciousness.
