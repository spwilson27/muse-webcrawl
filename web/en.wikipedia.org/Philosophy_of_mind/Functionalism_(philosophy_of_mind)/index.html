Functionalism (philosophy of mind)

Functionalism
FieldPhilosophy of mind
Developed1960s–1970s
Key figuresHilary Putnam, Jerry Fodor, David Lewis
Core claimMental states are defined by their causal/functional roles

In the philosophy of mind, functionalism is the thesis that mental states are constituted by their functional role—that is, by their causal relations to sensory inputs, other mental states, and behavioral outputs—rather than by their internal physical composition. Just as a mousetrap is defined by what it does rather than what it is made of, a mental state such as pain is defined by its typical causes (tissue damage) and effects (wincing, avoidance, the desire that it cease).

Origins and multiple realizability

Functionalism was introduced by Hilary Putnam in the 1960s, drawing on Alan Turing's machine model of computation. A central motivation is multiple realizability: creatures with very different physical make-ups—humans, octopuses, conceivably machines or aliens—could share the same mental states, which the earlier mind–brain identity theory could not easily accommodate. Variants include machine-state functionalism, psycho-functionalism (roles taken from empirical psychology), and analytic functionalism (roles from common-sense psychology, developed by David Lewis and David Armstrong).

Objections

The best-known objections hold that functional organization omits phenomenal experience. Ned Block's "China brain" thought experiment imagines the population of China simulating a brain's functional organization, and asks whether the whole system would be conscious; John Searle's Chinese room argument similarly attacks the inference from functional simulation to understanding. Other objections include the problem of inverted and absent qualia, and the difficulty of specifying functional roles without circularity.

Influence

Functionalism became the default framework of cognitive science and classical artificial intelligence, treating the mind as software realized in the brain's hardware. Although many philosophers now regard it as incomplete as an account of consciousness, it remains one of the most influential positions in modern philosophy of mind.
