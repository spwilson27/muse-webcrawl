Philosophical zombie

A philosophical zombie (or p-zombie) is a hypothetical being that is physically and behaviourally identical to a conscious human being but lacks conscious experience entirely: it winces, complains of pain, and writes philosophy papers about consciousness, yet there is nothing it is like to be it — inside, all is dark. The concept, used by Robert Kirk in the 1970s and made central by David Chalmers in The Conscious Mind (1996), is a tool in arguments against physicalism.

The zombie argument

The argument runs: (1) philosophical zombies are conceivable — no contradiction arises in imagining a molecule-for-molecule duplicate of our world minus experience; (2) if zombies are conceivable, they are metaphysically possible; (3) if they are possible, then conscious experience is something over and above the physical facts, since all physical facts are held fixed while experience varies; therefore (4) physicalism is false. The zombie argument is the conceivability engine behind the hard problem of consciousness, related to the knowledge argument and the inverted spectrum.

Responses

Physicalists attack both premises. Type-B materialists (such as David Papineau and Joseph Levine) grant conceivability but deny it implies metaphysical possibility, drawing an analogy with water and H₂O: identities can be necessary yet not knowable a priori. Others deny zombies are genuinely conceivable in the strong sense required, arguing the intuition rests on an incomplete grasp of what a full physical duplicate entails. Dualists and panpsychists take the argument to show that experience is a fundamental feature of the world.
