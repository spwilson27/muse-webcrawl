Philosophy of mind

The relationship between mind and brain is the central question of the field.

Philosophy of mind is a branch of philosophy that deals with the nature of the mind and its relation to the body and the external world.

The mind–body problem is a central issue: how can mental phenomena — thoughts, sensations, consciousness — be explained in relation to the physical brain? René Descartes defended substance dualism, holding that mind and body are distinct substances. Most contemporary philosophers instead adopt some form of physicalism, but they disagree about how mental states relate to physical states.

Major positions

  • Dualism — mind and matter are fundamentally distinct (Descartes; property dualism in some modern forms).
  • Identity theory — mental states are identical to brain states (U. T. Place, J. J. C. Smart).
  • Functionalism — mental states are defined by their causal roles, and can be realized in different physical systems (Hilary Putnam, Jerry Fodor).
  • Eliminative materialism — folk-psychological concepts like "belief" will be displaced by mature neuroscience (Paul and Patricia Churchland).
  • Behaviorism — mental states are dispositions to behave (Gilbert Ryle; logical behaviorism in analytic philosophy).
  • Panpsychism and dual-aspect theories — consciousness is a fundamental or ubiquitous feature of reality.

Key problems

Consciousness and qualia. Thomas Nagel argued that subjective experience — "what it is like" to be a creature — resists physical explanation; Frank Jackson's "Mary's room" thought experiment and David Chalmers's formulation of the hard problem of consciousness pose similar challenges. Chalmers distinguishes the "easy problems" of explaining cognitive functions from the hard problem of explaining experience itself.

Intentionality. Following Franz Brentano, many philosophers take "aboutness" — the directedness of thoughts toward objects — as the mark of the mental, raising the question of how physical systems can represent the world.

Other topics include personal identity, free will, mental causation, artificial intelligence and machine consciousness, and the philosophy of perception and emotion.
