Social facilitation

Social facilitation is the tendency for people to perform better on simple or well-learned tasks, and worse on complex or novel tasks, when in the presence of others. It is considered one of the oldest topics in social psychology, originating with Norman Triplett's 1898 observation that cyclists set faster times when racing against others than against the clock alone, and his follow-up experiments showing children wound fishing reels faster in competition.

History and the puzzle

Early experiments by Floyd Allport (1920s), who coined the term, found improvements in many co-action tasks. But subsequent studies also found the opposite — social inhibition — as in animals learning mazes more slowly with an audience. The field stalled until Robert Zajonc's 1965 drive theory unified the results: the presence of others increases physiological arousal (drive), which strengthens dominant responses. For easy or well-learned tasks the dominant response is correct, so performance improves; for difficult or new tasks the dominant response is often wrong, so performance deteriorates.

Explanations

Later theories refined Zajonc's account. Evaluation apprehension theory (Cottrell) holds that arousal comes not from mere presence but from learned concern about being judged. Distraction-conflict theory (Baron) attributes the effect to conflict between attending to the task and to the audience, which narrows attention — helpful for simple tasks, harmful for complex ones. Robert Zajonc's mere-presence account was supported by studies in animals, including cockroaches running mazes, which are presumably free of evaluation concerns.

Applications

Social facilitation effects appear in sports (home advantage, audience effects), workplaces and classrooms, and online settings, influencing how work is organized — for example, performing routine work in company but learning new skills in private. It contrasts with social loafing, the reduction of individual effort in groups where contributions are not identifiable.
