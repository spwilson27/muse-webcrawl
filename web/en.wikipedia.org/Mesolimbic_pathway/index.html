Mesolimbic pathway

The mesolimbic pathway, sometimes referred to as the reward pathway, is a dopaminergic pathway in the brain. The pathway connects the ventral tegmental area in the midbrain to the ventral striatum, principally the nucleus accumbens, of the basal ganglia in the forebrain. It is one of the four major dopamine pathways in the brain and is central to the brain's reward system, mediating the perception of pleasure and the reinforcement of rewarding behaviour.

Anatomy and function

Dopamine released from neurons of the ventral tegmental area (VTA) projects to the nucleus accumbens, amygdala, hippocampus and parts of the prefrontal cortex. Release of dopamine in the nucleus accumbens signals the salience and predicted reward value of stimuli; the pathway is activated by primary rewards such as food and sex, by social rewards, and by cues predicting them.

Clinical significance

Most addictive drugs, including cocaine, amphetamine, nicotine, opioids and alcohol, increase dopamine signalling in the mesolimbic pathway, and changes in this circuit are considered core to the development of addiction and craving. Hyperactivity of mesolimbic dopamine transmission is implicated in the positive symptoms of schizophrenia (the dopamine hypothesis), and almost all antipsychotic drugs act by blocking dopamine D2 receptors. Dysregulation in the opposite direction is associated with anhedonia in depression.
