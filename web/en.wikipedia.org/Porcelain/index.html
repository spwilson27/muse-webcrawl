Porcelain is a ceramic material made by firing refined clays — principally kaolin — at very high temperatures (about 1,200–1,400 °C) until the body vitrifies into a hard, white, translucent and resonant material. Long produced only in China, it was exported worldwide and imitated for centuries in Europe before being successfully replicated at Meissen in 1708.

History

Proto-porcelain appeared in China by the Shang dynasty, and true porcelain matured during the Tang and Song dynasties at centres such as Jingdezhen, the "porcelain capital". Blue-and-white ware of the Yuan and Ming dynasties became a global luxury. European makers long produced only soft-paste imitations; the alchemist Johann Friedrich Böttger, working for Augustus the Strong of Saxony, achieved true hard-paste porcelain at Meissen in 1708, after which factories spread across Europe (Sèvres, Wedgwood and others).

Types

  • Hard-paste: the original Chinese type, fired highest, made from kaolin and petuntse.
  • Soft-paste: European early imitation using glassy frits, fired lower.
  • Bone china: English development incorporating bone ash, prized for whiteness and translucency.

Uses

Beyond tableware and art objects, porcelain serves in electrical insulators, dental crowns, laboratory ware and sanitary ware, thanks to its strength, chemical inertness and low porosity.
