Orangutans are great apes native to the rainforests of Indonesia and Malaysia, now found only in parts of Borneo and Sumatra. They are the only surviving members of the subfamily Ponginae and the genus Pongo, which comprises three species: the Bornean orangutan (P. pygmaeus), the Sumatran orangutan (P. abelii), and the Tapanuli orangutan (P. tapanuliensis), described in 2017 from a small population south of Lake Toba. Their name derives from the Malay words orang ("person") and hutan ("forest") — "person of the forest". Orangutans are the most arboreal of the great apes and among the most solitary, spend nearly all their time in the trees, and have the longest interbirth interval of any land mammal (about 7–9 years).

Anatomy and behaviour

Orangutans have long reddish-brown hair, exceptionally long arms (a span of up to about 2 m in adult males), and grasping hands and feet adapted for climbing. Fully mature ("flanged") males develop prominent cheek pads and a throat sac used for long calls that carry across the forest; younger "unflanged" males resemble females. They build sleeping nests in trees each night, are highly intelligent, and have been observed using tools — sticks to extract insects or seeds, leaves as gloves, umbrellas and napkins — with distinct local traditions interpreted as culture. Their diet centres on ripe fruit, supplemented by leaves, bark, flowers, insects and mineral soil.

Ecology and threats

As large frugivores, orangutans are key seed dispersers for many rainforest trees. All three species are Critically Endangered. The chief threats are habitat loss and fragmentation from logging and conversion to oil-palm plantations, fires, and hunting and the illegal pet trade. Bornean populations fell by more than half in recent decades (one 2018 study estimated losses of over 100,000 animals between 1999 and 2015); the Tapanuli orangutan numbers fewer than 800 individuals and is considered the most endangered great ape. Conservation efforts focus on protecting remaining forest (e.g. Gunung Leuser and Tanjung Puting national parks), wildlife corridors, rehabilitation and reintroduction centres, and sustainable palm-oil sourcing.
