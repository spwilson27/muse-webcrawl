De Officiis

De Officiis (On Duties or On Obligations) is a 44 BC treatise by Marcus Tullius Cicero divided into three books, in which Cicero expounds his conception of the best way of living, behaving, and observing moral obligations. Written in the form of a letter to his son Marcus, then studying philosophy in Athens, it was composed in the last months of Cicero's life, after the assassination of Julius Caesar.

Content

Book I treats the morally honourable (honestum) through the four cardinal virtues — wisdom, justice, courage, and temperance. Book II discusses the expedient or useful (utile) and how honourable conduct secures advantage, influence, and goodwill. Book III addresses apparent conflicts between the honourable and the expedient, arguing that nothing truly useful can be dishonourable. Cicero drew heavily on the Stoic philosopher Panaetius of Rhodes, supplementing him with Roman historical examples such as Regulus, who returned to Carthaginian captivity rather than break his oath.

Legacy

De Officiis was enormously influential: it was one of the first classical books printed in Europe (Mainz, 1465), shaped Renaissance humanist ethics, and was praised by figures from Erasmus and Voltaire. Its discussion of just war, good faith in contracts, and the duties of rulers made it a cornerstone of Western moral and political thought.
