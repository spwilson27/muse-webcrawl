Battle of Crécy

The Battle of Crécy, 26 August 1346 — English longbowmen hold the ridge against repeated French charges.
Date26 August 1346
LocationCrécy-en-Ponthieu, Picardy, France
ResultEnglish victory
BelligerentsKingdom of England; Kingdom of France
CommandersEdward III, Edward the Black Prince; Philip VI, John II of Bohemia

The Battle of Crécy took place on 26 August 1346 in northern France and was one of the most important engagements of the Hundred Years' War. An English army under King Edward III, marching through Normandy on a great chevauchée, was pursued by a much larger French army under King Philip VI and brought to battle on a ridge near the village of Crécy-en-Ponthieu. Despite being heavily outnumbered and exhausted from the march, the English won a decisive victory that shattered French prestige and left thousands of French nobles dead on the field.

Background

Edward III landed in Normandy in July 1346 with roughly 15,000 men, sacked Caen, and marched towards Paris before turning north, crossing the Seine and the Somme. Philip VI gathered a large host, including a contingent of Genoese crossbowmen, and overtook the English near Crécy. Edward chose a strong defensive position on a ridge, dismounted his men-at-arms, and placed divisions of longbowmen on the flanks, with a baggage park behind.

The battle

Late in the afternoon, against Philip's intention to wait until the next day, the French army advanced. The Genoese crossbowmen opened the attack but were outshot by the English longbowmen, whose rate of shooting far exceeded that of the crossbow, and were hampered by wet bowstrings and a lack of pavises. As the Genoese recoiled, French cavalry charged through and over them. Through the evening the French mounted division after division — perhaps fifteen separate charges — but each was broken by massed archery, the stakes and obstacles before the English position, and dismounted English men-at-arms fighting on foot. Among the French dead was John, the blind King of Bohemia, who famously had himself led into the battle. Philip VI was wounded and eventually led from the field.

Aftermath

French casualties were enormous by medieval standards — contemporary estimates ran to many thousands, including a great part of the flower of French chivalry — while English losses were comparatively light. The victory allowed Edward to march unopposed to Calais, which he besieged and captured in 1347, giving England a bridgehead in France that it held for more than two centuries. Crécy, together with Poitiers (1356) and Agincourt (1415), demonstrated the tactical dominance of the dismounted defence combined with the longbow over the mounted charge that had defined warfare in earlier centuries.

Legacy

The battle is often cited as a milestone in the decline of the armoured knight and the rise of infantry and missile weapons. The motto and three feathers of the Prince of Wales are popularly linked to Edward the Black Prince's role at Crécy and to the arms of John of Bohemia, though historians debate the details of that tradition.
