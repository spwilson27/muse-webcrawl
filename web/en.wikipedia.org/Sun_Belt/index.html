Sun Belt

The Sun Belt is a region of the United States generally considered to stretch across the Southeast and Southwest, roughly below the 36th parallel. The region has seen substantial population and economic growth since the Second World War, driven by a warm climate, the spread of air conditioning, lower costs, and employment growth in services, technology, defence and energy.

Definition

There is no official boundary. The term was popularised by political analyst Kevin Phillips in his 1969 book The Emerging Republican Majority. Common definitions run from Florida and the Carolinas across the Gulf Coast and Texas to Arizona, Nevada and Southern California. The region is frequently contrasted with the industrial Rust Belt of the Northeast and Midwest.

Growth

Several forces drove Sun Belt growth after 1945: military bases and defence contracts sited in the South and West during and after the Second World War; the Interstate Highway System; cheap land and energy; right-to-work laws that attracted employers; and residential air conditioning, which made hot summers tolerable for year-round work and living. Retirees migrated to Florida and Arizona, while later decades brought large-scale immigration and booms in technology (Silicon Valley, Austin), finance, logistics and health care.

Political significance

Population growth translated directly into political power: Sun Belt states gained seats in the House of Representatives and votes in the Electoral College at the expense of the Northeast and Midwest in every reapportionment from 1950 onward. The region underpinned the rise of modern American conservatism — Barry Goldwater of Arizona, and Ronald Reagan of California — while recent decades have seen some of its large metropolitan areas become politically competitive or Democratic-leaning.

Challenges

Rapid growth has strained water supplies, especially in the arid Southwest dependent on the Colorado River; exposed cities such as Phoenix and Las Vegas to extreme heat; and subjected Florida and the Gulf Coast to hurricane risk and rising seas. Sprawling, car-dependent development patterns are also widely criticised.
