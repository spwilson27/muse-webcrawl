Oxidation

Oxidation is the loss of electrons or an increase in the oxidation state of a chemical species. When oxidation occurs, the opposite process — reduction, the gain of electrons — happens simultaneously; reactions involving both are called redox (reduction–oxidation) reactions. The substance that causes oxidation in another is an oxidizing agent (oxidant), which is itself reduced.

History of the concept

The term originally described reactions with oxygen: Lavoisier used it for processes in which substances combine with oxygen, as when iron rusts (forming iron oxide) or fuel burns. With the development of electronic theories of chemistry in the early 20th century, the definition was generalized to the loss of electrons, regardless of whether oxygen is involved — for example, sodium losing an electron to chlorine when forming sodium chloride.

Examples

Common oxidations include the rusting of iron (4 Fe + 3 O2 → 2 Fe2O3), the combustion of hydrocarbons, the tarnishing of silver, the browning of cut fruit, and cellular respiration, in which glucose is oxidized to carbon dioxide and water. In metallurgy and industry, oxidation is used in processes such as anodizing, bleaching, and the production of chemicals like nitric acid (the Ostwald process). Antioxidants are substances that inhibit oxidation, important in food preservation, materials science, and biology, where uncontrolled oxidation by free radicals contributes to ageing and disease (oxidative stress).

Oxidation state

The oxidation state (oxidation number) of an atom is a bookkeeping measure of its degree of oxidation, assigning electrons in bonds to the more electronegative partner. Elemental atoms have oxidation state 0; in compounds, values range widely, e.g. +7 for manganese in permanganate.
