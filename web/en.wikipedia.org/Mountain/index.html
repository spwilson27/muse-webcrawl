Mountain

A mountain is an elevated portion of the Earth's crust, generally with steep sides that show significant exposed bedrock. Mountains are formed through tectonic forces or volcanism, which act to raise the surface slowly over geological time, while erosion by water, ice and wind shapes and eventually lowers them. Although there is no universally accepted definition, a mountain typically differs from a plateau in having a limited summit area and is usually higher and steeper than a hill.

Formation

Mountain ranges form chiefly where tectonic plates collide. Compressional folding and faulting at convergent boundaries created great ranges such as the Himalayas (from the collision of India with Asia), the Alps and the Andes. Other mountains arise from volcanic activity, from rifting and block faulting, or as erosional remnants. Mount Everest, at 8,849 m (29,032 ft), is the highest mountain above sea level; Mauna Kea in Hawaii is taller measured from its base on the ocean floor.

Climate and ecology

Temperature falls with altitude (about 6.5 °C per kilometre), so mountains host stacked climatic zones, from forest through alpine tundra to permanent snow and ice. Mountains capture precipitation, feed most of the world's great rivers, and act as refuges and barriers that drive biological diversity and endemism. Roughly a quarter of the world's land area is mountainous, and mountains supply freshwater to more than half of humanity.

Mountains and humans

Mountains have long held religious significance — Olympus, Sinai, Fuji and Kailash among many sacred peaks — and they shape settlement, agriculture, mining and tourism. Mountaineering developed as a sport in the 19th century. Mountain environments are highly sensitive to climate change: glaciers are retreating worldwide and species are shifting upslope.
