Drug discovery is the process by which new candidate medications are discovered. Historically, drugs were found by identifying active ingredients in traditional remedies or by serendipity — famously penicillin — but modern discovery systematically screens libraries of chemical compounds and biological molecules against validated biological targets, then optimises the most promising "hits" into drug candidates suitable for clinical testing.

The pipeline

Discovery begins with target identification and validation: establishing that a molecule, usually a protein such as an enzyme, receptor, or ion channel, plays a causal role in a disease and can be modulated. Hit discovery then screens compound collections — sometimes millions of molecules — by high-throughput screening, fragment-based screening, virtual screening, or DNA-encoded libraries. Confirmed hits are refined through hit-to-lead and lead optimisation, in which medicinal chemists iterate the chemical structure to improve potency, selectivity, solubility, metabolic stability, and safety while reducing toxicity, governed by the balance of properties known as ADMET.

Challenges

Attrition is severe: of thousands of compounds entering discovery, only a handful reach human trials, and most of those fail, chiefly for lack of efficacy or unexpected toxicity. Costs have risen steadily — a trend dubbed "Eroom's law" — prompting interest in phenotypic screening, drug repurposing, computer-aided and AI-assisted design, and new modalities such as biologics, PROTACs, and RNA-based medicines that expand the range of druggable targets.
