The Hartford Convention was a series of meetings from December 15, 1814, to January 5, 1815, in Hartford, Connecticut, in which the New England Federalist Party met to discuss their grievances concerning the ongoing War of 1812 and the political problems arising from the federal government's increasing power.

Background

New England's maritime economy had been devastated by Jefferson's Embargo of 1807 and Madison's war measures, and Federalists resented the political dominance of Virginia and the southern states, reinforced by the three-fifths clause. Radical Federalists, the so-called Essex Junto, had flirted with secession since 1804, but moderates led by Harrison Gray Otis controlled the convention and rejected disunion.

Proceedings and proposals

Meeting behind closed doors, the delegates produced a report proposing seven amendments to the U.S. Constitution: abolishing the three-fifths representation rule, requiring a two-thirds vote of Congress for embargoes, admission of new states and declarations of war, limiting embargoes to 60 days, barring naturalized citizens from federal office, and limiting presidents to one term and forbidding successive presidents from the same state.

Consequences

Commissioners carrying the proposals to Washington arrived just as news broke of the Treaty of Ghent ending the war and of Andrew Jackson's victory at New Orleans. The convention was branded near-treasonous; the Federalist Party never recovered and collapsed as a national force within a few years — an episode remembered as a cautionary tale about sectional dissent in wartime.
