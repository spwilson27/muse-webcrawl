Cavitation

FieldFluid mechanics
DefinitionFormation and collapse of vapour cavities in a liquid
Key parameterCavitation number
EffectsNoise, vibration, erosion of propellers and pumps

Cavitation is a phenomenon in which the static pressure of a liquid reduces to below the liquid's vapour pressure, leading to the formation of small vapour-filled cavities in the liquid. When subjected to higher pressure, these cavities — "bubbles" or "voids" — collapse, generating shock waves that are strong close to the bubble. Cavitation is usually divided into inertial (transient) cavitation, driven by rapid pressure changes, and non-inertial cavitation, such as that induced by acoustic fields.

In engineering

Cavitation is a significant cause of wear in pumps, turbines, and ship propellers. Where moving blades accelerate liquid, the local pressure can fall below vapour pressure; the subsequent collapse of bubbles erodes metal surfaces, produces noise and vibration, and reduces efficiency. The problem was famously encountered on early high-speed steamships in the 1890s, including HMS Daring, and was studied by Lord Rayleigh, who in 1917 published an analysis of bubble collapse. Designers quantify the risk with the cavitation number and mitigate it through blade shape, operating depth, and net positive suction head.

Exploitation

Supercavitation — a large, stable gas cavity enveloping a moving body — drastically reduces skin friction and is used in some torpedoes and fast underwater projectiles. Cavitation is also harnessed in ultrasonic cleaning, homogenisation, water treatment, and medical techniques such as lithotripsy and histotripsy.

In nature

Some animals exploit cavitation: the snapping shrimp closes its claw so rapidly that the resulting cavity collapse stuns prey and produces a flash of light (sonoluminescence). Conversely, cavitation damages plant xylem under drought stress when water columns break, and it may injure tissue in marine mammals exposed to intense sound.
