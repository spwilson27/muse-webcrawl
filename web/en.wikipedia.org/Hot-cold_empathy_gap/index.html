Hot-cold empathy gap

People in a "cold" state underestimate the power of "hot" visceral states

The hot-cold empathy gap is a cognitive bias in which people underestimate the influence that visceral drives — hunger, thirst, pain, sexual arousal, cravings or strong emotions — have on their attitudes, preferences and behaviours. The concept was developed by economist and psychologist George Loewenstein in the 1990s.

Description

When in a "cold" (calm) state, people find it hard to appreciate how differently they will act when "hot," and so make poor predictions about their future behaviour — for example, underestimating how hunger will drive snack purchases, or how craving will undermine a resolution to quit smoking. The gap also operates in reverse: people currently in a hot state overestimate how long the state will persist and how much it will dominate their future choices. Because the same failure applies to imagining other people's states, it is described as an empathy gap.

Implications

The bias helps explain failures of self-control, impulsive decisions, and why advance commitment devices — deciding in a cold state how one will act when hot — can be effective. It has been applied in research on addiction, dieting, consumer behaviour, medical decision-making and negotiations.
