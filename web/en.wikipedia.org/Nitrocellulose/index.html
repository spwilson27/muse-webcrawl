Nitrocellulose (also known as cellulose nitrate, flash paper, flash cotton, guncotton, pyroxylin, and flash string) is a highly flammable compound formed by nitrating cellulose through exposure to a mixture of nitric acid and sulfuric acid. It was one of the earliest synthetic explosives and propellants, and remains in wide use in smokeless powders, lacquers, coatings, and photographic and motion-picture film base (historically).

History

Henri Braconnot discovered in 1832 that nitric acid acted on starch or wood fibres to produce a flammable material. Christian Friedrich Schönbein is generally credited with the invention of guncotton in 1846, preparing it by treating cotton with mixed acids; independently, Rudolf Böttger arrived at a similar process. Early guncotton factories suffered devastating explosions until Frederick Abel developed a stabilising process of pulping and thorough washing in the 1860s. In 1884 Paul Vieille gelatinised nitrocellulose with ether and alcohol to create Poudre B, the first practical smokeless powder, and in 1887 Alfred Nobel patented ballistite.

Properties and production

The degree of nitration determines the product. Highly nitrated material (about 12.5–13.5% nitrogen) is guncotton, used in propellants and explosives; lower-nitrogen pyroxylin dissolves in ether–alcohol to form collodion and is used in lacquers. Nitrocellulose burns extremely rapidly with little smoke or residue — hence its use in stage magic as flash paper. Dry or deteriorating material is a serious fire and explosion hazard, and aged nitrate film stock has caused catastrophic archive fires.

Uses

  • Propellants: the base of single-, double- (with nitroglycerin), and triple-base smokeless powders for small arms and artillery.
  • Film base: celluloid film stock used by the motion-picture industry until the early 1950s, when it was replaced by safety film.
  • Coatings and lacquers: wood finishes, nail polish, printing inks, and leather dressings.
  • Other: collodion in medicine and wet-plate photography; membranes in molecular biology (nitrocellulose blotting); table-tennis balls and other celluloid goods.
