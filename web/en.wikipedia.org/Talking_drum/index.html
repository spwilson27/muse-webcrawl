Talking drum

Talking drum — an hourglass-shaped drum from West Africa whose pitch can be regulated to imitate the tone and prosody of human speech. Known under many names: dundun, tamma, gangan, moon drum.

The talking drum is an hourglass-shaped drum from West Africa, whose pitch can be regulated so that it is used as a speech surrogate. Different parts of West Africa have unique names for this drum, such as gangan or dundun among the Yoruba, tamma among the Wolof, and luna or moon drum among the Dagomba.

Construction and playing

The drum's two heads are joined by leather tension cords, which allow the player to modulate the pitch of the drum by squeezing the cords between arm and body while striking the head with a curved stick and the free hand. A skilled player can mimic the contours of a tonal language such as Yoruba, Igbo, or Hausa, reproducing proverbs, names, histories, and announcements.

History and communication

Talking drums have been used for centuries by peoples including the Yoruba, Akan, and Hausa to relay messages between villages, recite oriki praise poetry, and lead ceremonies and dances. European explorers in the 18th and 19th centuries remarked on the speed and distance of drum communication. In modern music the instrument is a staple of West African popular genres and appears in Western popular and film music, often played solo as a virtuoso instrument.
