Scotoma

A scotoma is an area of partial or complete loss of vision within an otherwise normal visual field. The term comes from the Greek skotos, meaning "darkness". Scotomas may affect one or both eyes and can arise from damage anywhere along the visual pathway, from the retina and optic nerve to the visual cortex of the brain.

Types

Scotomas are classified by their location and shape. A central scotoma lies at the point of fixation and severely impairs reading and detailed vision, as in age-related macular degeneration. An arcuate scotoma follows the arcuate nerve fibre bundle of the retina and is characteristic of glaucoma. A paracentral scotoma lies near, but not at, fixation. Scintillating scotomas are shimmering, zigzag-bordered blind spots that expand across the visual field, most often as the aura of migraine.

The natural blind spot of the eye, where the optic nerve leaves the retina, is a physiological scotoma of which people are normally unaware.

Causes

Common causes include glaucoma, macular degeneration, diabetic retinopathy, multiple sclerosis (via optic neuritis), stroke affecting the visual pathways, retinal detachment, and certain medications or toxins. Detection is by visual field testing (perimetry), in which the patient reports when presented targets disappear.
