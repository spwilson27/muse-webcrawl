Lymph

Lymph forms from interstitial fluid entering lymph capillaries.

Lymph is the fluid that flows through the lymphatic system, a network of vessels, tissues and organs including lymph nodes, the spleen and the thymus. Lymph is essentially interstitial fluid — the fluid bathing the body's cells — after it has entered lymphatic capillaries. It is usually clear and slightly yellow, and its composition resembles that of blood plasma, though with less protein.

Formation and circulation

Each day, several litres of fluid leak from blood capillaries into the tissues. Most is reabsorbed, but the remainder — about 3 litres daily — enters blind-ended lymph capillaries, becoming lymph. Unlike blood, lymph has no central pump; it is moved by skeletal muscle contractions, breathing movements and valves in the collecting vessels. The lymph passes through one or more lymph nodes, where immune cells monitor it for pathogens, before draining back into the venous blood via the thoracic duct and right lymphatic duct near the subclavian veins.

Composition and functions

Lymph contains water, proteins, salts, glucose, fats and white blood cells, especially lymphocytes. After meals, lymph from the intestines (called chyle) is rich in absorbed fats transported as chylomicrons. The lymphatic system returns excess fluid and leaked plasma proteins to the blood, preventing tissue swelling (oedema); transports dietary lipids; and provides a route for immune surveillance, carrying antigens and antigen-presenting cells to lymph nodes.

Clinical aspects

Blockage or damage to lymphatic drainage — for example from surgery, radiotherapy, infection or the parasitic disease lymphatic filariasis — causes lymph to accumulate in tissues as lymphedema. Cancer cells can spread (metastasise) via lymph vessels to regional nodes, which is why nodes are sampled in cancer staging.
