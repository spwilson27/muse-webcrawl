The Old Man of the Sea (Arabic: شيخ البحر, Shaykh al-Bahr) is a character in the story of Sinbad the Sailor in the One Thousand and One Nights. He appears in Sinbad's fifth voyage.

In the tale

Shipwrecked on an island, Sinbad meets a frail old man who begs to be carried across a stream. Once on Sinbad's shoulders, the old man wraps his legs around Sinbad's neck and refuses to dismount, riding him day and night and forcing him to gather food. Sinbad finally escapes by fermenting grape juice into wine, which he leaves where the old man will drink it; intoxicated, the creature loosens his grip, and Sinbad kills him with a stone.

Interpretations and influence

The figure has been compared to similar "rider" monsters in world folklore and to the Greek sea god Proteus, sometimes called the Old Man of the Sea. The story has inspired later works, including James Joyce's references in Ulysses, where the Old Man of the Sea becomes a metaphor for an oppressive burden that cannot be shaken off.
