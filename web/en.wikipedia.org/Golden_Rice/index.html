Golden Rice

Golden Rice is a variety of rice (Oryza sativa) produced through genetic engineering to biosynthesize beta-carotene, a precursor of vitamin A, in the edible parts of rice. It is intended to be a fortified food for areas with a dietary deficiency of vitamin A, which causes blindness and death in millions of children each year, particularly in Africa and Southeast Asia.

Development

Golden Rice was developed by Ingo Potrykus of the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology and Peter Beyer of the University of Freiburg, working through the 1990s; their results were published in Science in 2000. Ordinary rice plants produce beta-carotene in their leaves but not in the polished grain. The researchers introduced genes — originally from daffodil and a soil bacterium — that switch on the carotenoid pathway in the endosperm, giving the grain its characteristic golden-yellow colour.

A second version, Golden Rice 2, announced in 2005, replaced the daffodil gene with one from maize and produces up to 23 times more beta-carotene than the original.

Deployment and controversy

Golden Rice has been at the centre of the debate over genetically modified crops. Supporters regard it as a humanitarian technology for preventing vitamin A deficiency; critics, including Greenpeace, have questioned its effectiveness, safety assessment and necessity compared with dietary diversification. The technology was made available royalty-free for subsistence farmers through humanitarian licensing.

Regulatory approvals for food use were granted in several countries from 2017 onward, including Australia, Canada, the United States and, in 2019, the Philippines, which in 2021 became the first country to approve commercial propagation. In 2024 a Philippine court revoked the biosafety permit, halting planting pending further proceedings.
