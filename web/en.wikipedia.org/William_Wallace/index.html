Sir William Wallace (c. 1270 – 23 August 1305) was a Scottish knight who became one of the main leaders during the First War of Scottish Independence. He is remembered as a national hero of Scotland for resisting the English occupation of Edward I.

Rise to prominence

Little is known of Wallace's early life. He emerged in 1297, killing the English Sheriff of Lanark, and soon joined forces with Andrew Moray. On 11 September 1297, Wallace and Moray defeated a much larger English army at the Battle of Stirling Bridge, exploiting the narrow bridge over the River Forth. Wallace was appointed Guardian of Scotland and commander of its armies.

Defeat and capture

In July 1298 Edward I personally led an army north and defeated Wallace at the Battle of Falkirk. Wallace resigned the guardianship and spent time abroad, possibly seeking support from France and the Papacy. He was captured near Glasgow in August 1305 and taken to London, where he was tried for treason. He replied that he could not be a traitor to Edward, never having sworn him allegiance.

Death and legacy

Wallace was executed at Smithfield on 23 August 1305 by hanging, drawing and quartering. His resistance helped pave the way for Robert the Bruce, who secured Scottish independence at Bannockburn in 1314. The 15th-century poem The Wallace by Blind Harry and the 1995 film Braveheart popularised his legend worldwide.
