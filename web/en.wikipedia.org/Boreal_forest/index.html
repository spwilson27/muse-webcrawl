Boreal forest

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The boreal forest, also known as taiga (Russian: тайга) or the snow forest, is a biome characterized by coniferous forests consisting mostly of pines, spruces, and larches. The taiga is the world's largest land biome, forming a circumpolar band across North America, Europe, and Asia between roughly 50°N and 70°N latitude, lying between the temperate forests and grasslands to the south and the tundra to the north.

Climate and soils

The taiga has a subarctic climate with very large temperature range between seasons. Winters are long, dark, and cold, with average temperatures well below freezing for six months or more; summers are short and cool to warm. Precipitation is modest, mostly falling as summer rain and winter snow. Soils are typically thin, nutrient-poor, and acidic (podzols), and permafrost underlies large areas, impeding drainage and creating vast wetlands.

Flora and fauna

Conifers dominate: spruce, fir, pine, and larch, the last being unusual in shedding its needles in winter. Broadleaf trees such as birch, poplar, willow, and alder occur, particularly in warmer and disturbed areas. Fire is a major natural disturbance that renews the forest. Animal life includes moose, bears, wolves, lynx, wolverines, beavers, snowshoe hares, and vast numbers of breeding migratory birds; insects such as the spruce budworm periodically defoliate enormous areas.

Carbon storage and threats

The boreal forest stores more carbon than any other terrestrial biome, much of it locked in soils, peatlands, and permafrost — estimates run to over a trillion tonnes. Logging, mining, oil and gas development, hydroelectric projects, and climate warming — which proceeds fastest at high northern latitudes — threaten the biome; thawing permafrost and intensifying wildfires could release stored carbon and amplify warming.
