The North Sea is a marginal sea of the Atlantic Ocean between Great Britain, Jutland, Norway, Germany, the Netherlands, Belgium, and France. It connects to the Atlantic through the English Channel in the south and the Norwegian Sea in the north, and to the Baltic Sea via the Skagerrak and Kattegat. It is more than 960 kilometres (600 mi) long and 580 kilometres (360 mi) wide, covering roughly 750,000 square kilometres.

Geography

The North Sea is mostly on the European continental shelf, with a mean depth of about 95 metres. The shallow southern part includes the Dogger Bank, once dry land (Doggerland) linking Britain to mainland Europe during the last ice age. The Norwegian Trench, running along Norway's coast, reaches depths of about 700 metres. Major rivers flowing into the sea include the Rhine, Elbe, Thames, and Forth.

History

The sea has been a major trade route since Roman times, central to the Hanseatic League, Dutch maritime power in the 17th century, and the naval rivalries of the First and Second World Wars, including the Battle of Jutland in 1916. The catastrophic North Sea flood of 1953 inundated coasts in the Netherlands, Belgium, and England, killing more than 2,500 people and prompting major flood defences such as the Dutch Delta Works and the Thames Barrier.

Economy

The North Sea hosts important fisheries, busy shipping lanes serving ports such as Rotterdam, Antwerp, and Hamburg, and—since the 1960s—major offshore oil and gas fields whose output underpinned the economies of the United Kingdom and Norway. In the 21st century it has become a leading region for offshore wind power.

Ecology

The sea supports rich but heavily exploited fish stocks, including cod, haddock, herring, and mackerel, as well as seabird colonies and marine mammals. Pollution, eutrophication, and overfishing are persistent environmental concerns managed under international agreements such as the OSPAR Convention.
