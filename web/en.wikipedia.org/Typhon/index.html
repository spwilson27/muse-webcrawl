Typhon (Greek: Τυφῶν) is a monstrous serpentine giant of Greek mythology, the deadliest creature of the Greek tradition and the final great challenger to the rule of Zeus.

Mythology

According to Hesiod's Theogony, after Zeus drove the Titans into Tartarus, Earth (Gaia) bore Typhon to Tartarus in revenge. He is described with a hundred snake heads growing from his shoulders, fire flashing from his eyes, and a cacophony of voices — bellowing of bulls, roaring of lions, hissing of serpents — issuing from his mouths. With his mate Echidna, "mother of monsters", he fathered many of the famous beasts of Greek myth.

In the ensuing battle (the Typhonomachy), Typhon nearly overcame Zeus, in some versions tearing out the sinews of his hands and feet and hiding them in a cave guarded by the dragoness Delphyne, until Hermes and Aegipan restored them. Zeus finally struck Typhon down with his thunderbolts and hurled him into Tartarus; later tradition placed him imprisoned beneath Mount Etna in Sicily, whose eruptions were attributed to his struggles. The myth is thought to reflect Near Eastern combat myths and possibly memories of volcanic activity.

Legacy

Typhon's name is connected with the word "typhoon" (via folk etymology through Persian and Arabic storm terms), and he is the namesake of the proposed planet Tyche analogues in popular culture; in Egyptian syncretism he was identified with the god Set.
