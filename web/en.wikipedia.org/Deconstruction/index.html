Deconstruction

Deconstruction
OriginatorJacques Derrida (1930–2004)
Key workOf Grammatology (1967)
FieldsPhilosophy, literary criticism, theory
Key conceptsDifférance, logocentrism, binary opposition, aporia

Deconstruction is a form of philosophical and literary analysis, developed by the French philosopher Jacques Derrida, that questions the fundamental conceptual distinctions — or "binary oppositions" — in Western philosophy through close examination of texts. Introduced in Derrida's works of 1967, including Of Grammatology and Writing and Difference, it became a major influence on literary theory, law, architecture, and the humanities generally from the 1970s onward.

Concepts

Derrida argued that Western thought is structured by hierarchical oppositions such as speech/writing, presence/absence, nature/culture, and reason/madness, in which the first term is privileged. Deconstructive reading shows how a text undermines its own hierarchy — how the subordinate term proves essential to the privileged one — producing moments of undecidability (aporia). His neologism différance (combining "difference" and "deferral") names the way meaning arises from differences between signs and is endlessly deferred along chains of signifiers. Derrida also critiqued logocentrism, the privileging of speech and presence as sources of stable meaning.

Method

Rather than a method or a critique in the ordinary sense, Derrida described deconstruction as something that "takes place" within texts. A deconstructive reading typically identifies a text's governing oppositions, locates passages where the text contradicts its own stated hierarchy, and shows the resulting instability of meaning. Derrida resisted definitions, remarking in his "Letter to a Japanese Friend" that "deconstruction is not a method and cannot be transformed into one".

Influence and reception

Deconstruction shaped the "Yale School" of literary critics (Paul de Man, J. Hillis Miller, Harold Bloom, Geoffrey Hartman) and influenced fields from legal studies to architecture, where "deconstructivism" took its name. It was also fiercely contested: analytic philosophers criticised its obscurity, and the 1992 controversy over Cambridge University's proposed honorary doctorate for Derrida made the dispute public. The Sokal affair of 1996 further fuelled the "science wars" debates about postmodern theory.
