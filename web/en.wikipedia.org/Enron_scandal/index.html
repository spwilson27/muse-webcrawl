Enron scandal

Date: Revealed October 2001

Bankruptcy: December 2, 2001

Key figures: Kenneth Lay, Jeffrey Skilling, Andrew Fastow

The Enron scandal was an accounting scandal involving Enron Corporation, an American energy company based in Houston, Texas, and its auditor Arthur Andersen. Upon revelation in October 2001, the company filed for bankruptcy on December 2, 2001 — at the time the largest corporate bankruptcy in United States history — and Arthur Andersen was effectively dissolved after being found guilty of obstruction of justice (a verdict later overturned, too late to save the firm).

Background

Enron grew rapidly in the 1990s into one of America's most admired companies, reporting revenues of nearly $111 billion in 2000 and ranking seventh on the Fortune 500. Behind the growth, executives used mark-to-market accounting and a web of off-balance-sheet special purpose entities to hide debt and inflate profits, while selling their own shares before the collapse.

Collapse and investigations

Journalists and analysts questioned Enron's opaque financial statements through 2001; whistleblower Sherron Watkins warned chairman Kenneth Lay in August. In October the company reported a $618 million loss and a $1.2 billion write-down, and in November it restated years of earnings. Its share price fell from over $90 in 2000 to pennies, wiping out employees' retirement savings.

Trials and reform

Chief executive Jeffrey Skilling and Lay were convicted of fraud and conspiracy in 2006; Lay died before sentencing, and Skilling served over a decade in prison. CFO Andrew Fastow pleaded guilty and cooperated with prosecutors. The scandal prompted the Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002, tightening corporate governance and accounting oversight in the United States.
