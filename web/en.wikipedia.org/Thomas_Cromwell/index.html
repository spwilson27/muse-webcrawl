Thomas Cromwell (c. 1485 – 28 July 1540) was an English statesman and lawyer who served as chief minister to King Henry VIII from 1534 to 1540, when he was beheaded on charges of treason and heresy. Born to a working-class family in Putney, he rose through service to Cardinal Wolsey to become the architect of the English Reformation's administrative revolution.

Rise and reforms

Cromwell engineered the legal break with Rome: the Acts in Restraint of Appeals (1533) and of Supremacy (1534) declared Henry head of the Church of England, enabling the annulment of the king's marriage to Catherine of Aragon. As vicegerent in spirituals he directed the dissolution of the monasteries (1536–1541), transferring vast church wealth to the Crown. He also promoted statute law, parliamentary sovereignty, closer union with Wales, and social legislation, and patronised evangelical reformers and English Bible translation.

Fall

His arrangement of Henry's marriage to Anne of Cleves (1540), which the king detested, gave Cromwell's conservative enemies their opening. Despite being created Earl of Essex in April 1540, he was arrested in June, condemned by act of attainder without trial, and executed on Tower Hill on 28 July 1540 — the same day Henry married Catherine Howard. Historians such as Geoffrey Elton credited Cromwell with a "revolution in government"; modern biographers, notably Diarmaid MacCulloch, have reassessed him as a principled and exceptionally able reformer.
