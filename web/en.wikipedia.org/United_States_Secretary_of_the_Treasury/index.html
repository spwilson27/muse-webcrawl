The United States secretary of the treasury is the head of the United States Department of the Treasury, and is the chief financial officer of the federal government of the United States. The secretary of the treasury serves as the principal advisor to the president of the United States on all matters pertaining to economic and fiscal policy. The secretary is, by custom, a member of the president's cabinet and, by law, a member of the National Security Council, and fifth in the U.S. presidential line of succession.

Duties

The secretary oversees the Treasury Department's functions, including collecting federal taxes through the Internal Revenue Service, managing government debt and accounts, producing currency and coinage, supervising national banks, and enforcing financial sanctions and anti-money-laundering laws. The secretary's signature appears on Federal Reserve notes. The secretary also represents the United States at the International Monetary Fund and the World Bank.

History

The office was created by the Act of September 2, 1789, establishing the Treasury Department. Alexander Hamilton, the first secretary (1789–1795), established much of the young nation's financial system, including a national bank, assumption of state debts, and a system of tariffs and excises. The longest-serving secretary was Andrew Mellon (1921–1932), while Albert Gallatin (1801–1814) served nearly as long.

Line of succession and protocol

Under the Presidential Succession Act, the secretary of the treasury follows the secretary of state in the line of succession. The position is generally regarded as equivalent to a finance minister in other countries.
