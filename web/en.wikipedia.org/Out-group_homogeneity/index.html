Out-group homogeneity

Out-group homogeneity is the phenomenon in social psychology whereby members of an out-group are perceived as more similar to one another ("they are all alike") than members of one's own in-group are perceived to be ("we are diverse"). It is one of the most robust findings in the study of intergroup perception.

Explanations

Several mechanisms have been proposed. People typically have more contact with, and more differentiated knowledge of, in-group members, so they encode them as individuals; out-group members are more often processed through category-level information and stereotypes. Motivational accounts suggest that perceiving the out-group as homogeneous can justify discrimination and maintain positive social identity. The effect applies to age, gender, nationality, political, and even minimal laboratory groups.

Related effects

Out-group homogeneity is related to the cross-race effect in face recognition (own-race faces being recognized better), to stereotyping, and to the tendency to attribute out-group members' behavior to their group membership. Greater intergroup contact and individuating information can reduce the effect, though it is rarely eliminated entirely.
