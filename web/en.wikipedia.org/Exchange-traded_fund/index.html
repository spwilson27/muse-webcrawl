An exchange-traded fund (ETF) is a type of investment fund that holds a basket of assets — such as stocks, bonds, or commodities — and whose shares trade on a stock exchange throughout the day, like an ordinary share. Most ETFs are index funds, designed to track the performance of a benchmark such as the S&P 500, though actively managed ETFs have grown since regulatory changes in 2019. ETFs combine the diversification of a mutual fund with the liquidity and pricing transparency of a listed stock.

How ETFs work

ETF shares are created and redeemed in large blocks ("creation units") by institutional investors called authorised participants. To create shares, an authorised participant delivers the underlying basket of securities to the fund and receives ETF shares in exchange; redemption reverses the process. This in-kind mechanism, together with arbitrage whenever the market price strays from net asset value, keeps the trading price close to the value of the holdings. Because shares change hands between investors on the exchange rather than being sold back to the fund, ETFs are typically more tax-efficient than conventional mutual funds in the United States.

History and growth

The first successful ETF, the SPDR S&P 500 Trust (ticker SPY), launched on the American Stock Exchange in January 1993; an earlier Canadian fund, the Toronto Index Participation Fund, dated from 1990. Assets grew explosively in the 2000s and 2010s as funds appeared tracking bond indices, international equities, commodities (such as gold), and eventually leveraged, inverse, thematic, and factor strategies. Global ETF assets passed ten trillion US dollars in the mid-2020s, and in 2024 the United States approved spot bitcoin ETFs, which rapidly became among the most successful launches in the industry's history.

Costs and risks

Index ETFs are known for low expense ratios, sometimes below 0.05% per year, though investors also pay bid–ask spreads and possibly brokerage commissions. Risks include tracking error, market risk of the underlying assets, liquidity issues in stressed markets, and, for leveraged and inverse products, daily rebalancing effects that can erode returns over longer horizons.
