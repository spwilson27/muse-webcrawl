Hugh Bamford Cott (6 July 1900 – 18 April 1987) was a British zoologist, an authority on natural and military camouflage, and a wildlife photographer and scientific illustrator. His book Adaptive Coloration in Animals (1940) became the standard work on camouflage in nature and was widely used by the military during the Second World War.

Life and career

Cott studied at Cambridge, graduating in 1925, and undertook expeditions to the Amazon, the Zambezi, and East Africa, studying the adaptive coloration of amphibians, reptiles, and birds. From 1938 he was a lecturer in zoology at Cambridge and later Curator of the University Museum of Zoology. He was an accomplished photographer and artist whose drawings illustrated his own and others' works.

Adaptive Coloration in Animals

Published in 1940, Cott's 550-page synthesis documented concealing coloration, countershading, disruptive patterning, and masquerade across the animal kingdom, and persuasively argued these patterns were products of natural selection. The book drew extensively on his fieldwork and illustration.

Military camouflage

During the Second World War Cott served as a camouflage expert with the Royal Engineers, applying zoological principles — countershading of gun barrels, disruptive painting of vehicles and structures — to military problems. He famously contrasted a well-camouflaged gun emplacement with the ineffective approaches of officials who lacked biological insight, and campaigned for camouflage to be treated as a scientific discipline within the armed services.
