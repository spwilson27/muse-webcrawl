Natural frequency

Natural frequency, measured in hertz, is the rate at which an oscillatory system tends to oscillate in the absence of disturbance. A foundational example is a playing child on a swing: if pushed at the swing's natural frequency, the motion grows; pushes at other rates work against the natural motion. Most systems have more than one natural frequency, and the frequencies at which they occur are called resonant frequencies.

Overview

When an external force is applied at a system's natural frequency, the system is said to resonate: its oscillation amplitude can become very large, limited only by damping. This is why soldiers break step when crossing bridges — marching in unison could excite a bridge at its natural frequency.

Mass–spring system

For an ideal mass–spring system with mass m and spring stiffness k, the natural frequency is:

fn = (1 / 2π) · √(k / m)

Increasing stiffness raises the natural frequency, while increasing mass lowers it. In structural engineering, natural frequencies of buildings, bridges and machinery are calculated to avoid coincidence with expected excitation sources such as wind, earthquakes or rotating equipment.

Electrical circuits

In an LC circuit, the natural (resonant) frequency is determined by the inductance L and capacitance C:

fn = 1 / (2π√(LC))

This principle underlies the tuning of radio receivers, where adjusting the capacitance selects the station whose carrier frequency matches the circuit's natural frequency.
