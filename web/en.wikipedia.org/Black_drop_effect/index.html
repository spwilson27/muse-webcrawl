Black drop effect

The black drop effect as Venus crosses the limb of the Sun.

The black drop effect is an optical phenomenon observed during transits of Venus and, to a lesser degree, transits of Mercury across the Sun. Just after the planet's disk has fully entered the solar disk (second contact), and again just before it begins to leave (third contact), the planet appears connected to the edge (limb) of the Sun by a thin dark neck or "drop," distorting its silhouette from a clean circle.

Cause

The effect was once attributed to the atmosphere of Venus. Modern observations — notably from the TRACE spacecraft during the 1999 transit of Mercury, a planet with essentially no atmosphere — demonstrated that the black drop arises from a combination of the point spread function of the telescope optics and solar limb darkening: the Sun's disk is darker near its edge, and instrumental blurring smears the planet's image so it appears to cling to the limb. Imperfect optics and atmospheric seeing on Earth intensify the effect.

Historical significance

The black drop plagued 18th- and 19th-century expeditions that timed the contacts of Venus transits (1761, 1769, 1874, 1882) to measure the astronomical unit by parallax, limiting the precision of their results. The 1761 observations also led Mikhail Lomonosov to infer that Venus has an atmosphere, based on a luminous arc seen at ingress and egress.
