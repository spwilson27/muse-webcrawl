Acheulean (sometimes spelled Acheulian) is an archaeological industry of stone tool manufacture characterised by distinctive oval and pear-shaped "handaxes" associated with Homo erectus and derived species such as Homo heidelbergensis. Acheulean tools were produced during the Lower Paleolithic period across the Old World and have been dated from roughly 1.76 million years ago to about 130,000 years ago.

Name and discovery

The industry is named after the type site of Saint-Acheul, a suburb of Amiens in northern France, where handaxes were recognised in river gravels in the 19th century. John Frere had earlier described similar tools at Hoxne, Suffolk, in 1797, though their great antiquity was not appreciated until decades later.

The handaxe

The type fossil of the Acheulean is the biface, worked on both faces to produce a symmetrical tool with a continuous cutting edge, most commonly in teardrop, oval or triangular form. Handaxes were made from flint, quartzite, basalt and other fine-grained rocks, likely used as multipurpose tools for butchery, woodworking and digging. Their symmetry and persistence over more than a million years have prompted debate about whether form was purely functional or also reflected cognitive or social signalling.

Distribution and chronology

The earliest Acheulean assemblages, from sites such as Kokiselei in Kenya, date to about 1.76 million years ago. The tradition spread throughout Africa, western Asia and Europe, but is notably absent from most of East and Southeast Asia — a contrast known as the Movius Line, though bifaces have since been found in China and Korea. Late Acheulean industries show increasing use of the Levallois prepared-core technique and grade into Middle Paleolithic industries.
