Canidae (/ˈkænɪdiː/) is a biological family of dog-like carnivorans, colloquially referred to as dogs or canids. A member of this family is called a canid. The family includes three subfamilies: the extinct Borophaginae and Hesperocyoninae, and the extant Caninae. Living canids include domestic dogs, wolves, coyotes, foxes, jackals, dingoes, and many others.

Evolution

The Canidae first appeared in North America during the late Eocene, about 37 million years ago, with early genera such as Hesperocyon. The family radiated on that continent for most of its history, reaching South America during the Great American Interchange and the Old World via the Bering land bridge. Extinct subfamilies included the bone-crushing borophagines, which dominated North American carnivore guilds for millions of years.

Characteristics

Canids are generally long-legged, digitigrade runners with non-retractile claws, long muzzles, and bushy tails. Most species have a dental formula adapted for both meat shearing and crushing, with prominent carnassial teeth. They have keen senses of smell and hearing. Body size ranges from the fennec fox, weighing about 1 kg, to the grey wolf, which may exceed 50 kg.

Behaviour and ecology

Many canids are social, living in family groups or packs with cooperative hunting and pup-rearing; others, such as most foxes, are more solitary. Communication relies on vocalizations, scent marking, and body postures. Wild canids occur on every continent except Antarctica, occupying habitats from Arctic tundra to deserts. The domestic dog, descended from Pleistocene wolves, was the first species domesticated by humans and is now found worldwide.
