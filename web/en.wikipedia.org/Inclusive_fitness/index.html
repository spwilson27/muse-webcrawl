Inclusive fitness

Inclusive fitness is a concept in evolutionary biology, introduced by W. D. Hamilton in 1964, that measures an organism's evolutionary success not only by the offspring it produces itself but also by the effects of its actions on the reproductive success of its genetic relatives. It resolved the long-standing puzzle of how apparently altruistic behavior — such as sterile worker castes in social insects — could evolve by natural selection.

Hamilton's rule

Hamilton formalized the idea in the inequality rB > C, known as Hamilton's rule: a gene for helping behavior will spread when the benefit to the recipient (B), discounted by the coefficient of relatedness (r) between actor and recipient, exceeds the cost to the actor (C). Relatedness is the probability that a gene in one individual is identical by descent to a gene in the other — 0.5 for full siblings or parent and offspring, 0.25 for half-siblings, and 0.125 for first cousins.

Kin selection

Inclusive fitness theory underlies kin selection, explaining alarm calls in ground squirrels, cooperative breeding in birds, and the extreme altruism of eusocial insects. The haplodiploid sex-determination system of ants, bees, and wasps makes sisters related by 0.75, which Hamilton suggested helps explain why eusociality evolved repeatedly in that group, though the importance of haplodiploidy remains debated.

Criticism and development

The theory has been enormously influential, shaping sociobiology and behavioral ecology. Some researchers, including E. O. Wilson in later work, argued that multilevel (group) selection offers a better account of eusociality, sparking renewed controversy in 2010. Most evolutionary biologists regard inclusive fitness and multilevel selection as mathematically related frameworks, with inclusive fitness remaining the standard accounting method.
