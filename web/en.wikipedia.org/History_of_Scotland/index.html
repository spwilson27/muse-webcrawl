History of Scotland

The history of Scotland begins with the arrival of humans after the last Ice Age and proceeds through the formation of the Kingdom of Scotland in the ninth century, the Wars of Independence, the Reformation, union with England in 1707, the Scottish Enlightenment and industrialisation, and the modern movement for devolution and independence.

Prehistory and Roman era

Mesolithic hunters reached Scotland around 8500 BCE; Neolithic farmers built monuments such as Skara Brae and the Callanish Stones. Roman armies invaded from 79 CE under Agricola, winning at Mons Graupius (83/84), but never conquered the Highlands; Hadrian's Wall and the shorter-lived Antonine Wall marked the frontier. Beyond it lived the peoples the Romans called Caledonians and later the Picts.

Formation of the kingdom

Gaelic Scots from Ireland settled Argyll as the kingdom of Dál Riata, while Angles held the south-east and Britons the kingdom of Strathclyde. Kenneth MacAlpin (Cináed mac Ailpín) is traditionally credited with uniting Picts and Scots in 843. Under Malcolm III (Canmore) and his successors, feudal institutions and burghs spread, and David I (r. 1124–1153) transformed government and the Church. Most of the Hebrides were won from Norway by the Treaty of Perth (1266).

Wars of Independence

When the succession failed in 1290, Edward I of England claimed overlordship, provoking the Wars of Independence. William Wallace won at Stirling Bridge (1297) but was executed in 1305. Robert the Bruce, crowned in 1306, defeated Edward II at Bannockburn in 1314; the Declaration of Arbroath (1320) asserted Scottish sovereignty, and England recognised independence in 1328. Renewed wars followed in the 1330s–50s.

Late medieval and Reformation Scotland

The Stewart dynasty ruled from 1371. James IV fell at Flodden (1513) against England. His granddaughter Mary, Queen of Scots, returned from France in 1561 to a country transformed by the Protestant Reformation of 1560, led by John Knox; deposed in 1567, she was executed by Elizabeth I in 1587. Her son James VI inherited the English crown in 1603 (Union of the Crowns).

Union and Enlightenment

The seventeenth century saw the Covenanters' wars against Charles I's religious policies, Cromwellian occupation, and after 1688 the Presbyterian settlement and Jacobite risings in 1715 and 1745 — the latter crushed at Culloden in 1746, after which Highland society was forcibly changed and later devastated by the Clearances. Financial disaster in the Darien scheme helped drive the Acts of Union 1707, merging Scotland and England into Great Britain while preserving Scots law, the Kirk and the universities. The eighteenth-century Scottish Enlightenment — David Hume, Adam Smith, James Hutton and others — made Edinburgh a centre of world thought, and Glasgow and the Clyde led in industry and shipbuilding.

Modern Scotland

Industrial Scotland exported ships, steel and engineers worldwide; the twentieth century brought decline of heavy industry, North Sea oil from the 1970s, and growing nationalism. A referendum in 1997 approved a devolved Scottish Parliament, which sat from 1999. In the 2014 independence referendum, voters chose to remain in the United Kingdom by 55% to 45%.
