Sunspot

Sunspots are temporary dark spots on the Sun's surface (the photosphere) caused by intense magnetic activity that inhibits convection, creating regions of reduced surface temperature. Although the surrounding photosphere is about 5,500 °C, sunspot umbrae are around 3,000–4,500 °C and thus appear dark by contrast. Individual sunspots can be larger than Earth and may last from days to months.

Structure

A mature sunspot has a dark central umbra, where the magnetic field is strongest and roughly vertical, surrounded by a lighter filamented penumbra where the field is more inclined. Sunspots usually appear in pairs or groups of opposite magnetic polarity, connected by loops of field lines rising into the corona.

The solar cycle

Sunspot numbers rise and fall over an approximately 11-year cycle, first systematically recorded by Heinrich Schwabe in the 1840s and charted by Rudolf Wolf, whose relative sunspot number remains in use. At cycle maximum, spots appear at mid-latitudes and migrate toward the equator as the cycle progresses, a pattern shown in the butterfly diagram. Prolonged minima, such as the Maunder Minimum (1645–1715), coincided with the Little Ice Age in Europe.

History of observation

Sunspots were recorded by Chinese astronomers over two thousand years ago. With the telescope, Galileo, Thomas Harriot and others observed them around 1610–1611; their motion across the disc demonstrated the Sun's rotation, challenging the doctrine of celestial perfection. George Ellery Hale's discovery in 1908 that sunspots are strongly magnetic revealed their true nature.

Effects on Earth

Sunspots are associated with solar flares and coronal mass ejections that can disturb Earth's magnetosphere, affecting radio communications, satellites and power grids, and producing aurorae. Total solar irradiance actually rises slightly at sunspot maximum because bright faculae surrounding the spots more than compensate for their darkness.
