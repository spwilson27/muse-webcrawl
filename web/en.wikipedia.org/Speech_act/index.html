Speech act

In the philosophy of language and linguistics, a speech act is an utterance that a speaker makes that performs an action in addition to conveying meaning. Examples include apologising, promising, ordering, answering, requesting, complaining, warning, inviting, refusing and congratulating.

History

The concept was introduced by the Oxford philosopher J. L. Austin in his 1955 William James Lectures at Harvard, published posthumously as How to Do Things with Words (1962). Austin distinguished utterances that merely state facts, which he called constatives, from performatives, which accomplish an act by being uttered, such as "I now pronounce you husband and wife". His student John Searle systematised the theory in Speech Acts (1969).

Levels of action

Following Austin, analysts commonly distinguish three levels: the locutionary act (the utterance of words with a certain sense and reference), the illocutionary act (the act performed in saying something, such as promising or asserting), and the perlocutionary act (the effects achieved by saying something, such as persuading or frightening the hearer).

Searle's classification

Searle proposed five classes of illocutionary acts: assertives, directives, commissives, expressives and declarations. For a speech act to succeed, certain "felicity conditions" must be met; otherwise the act is infelicitous — for example, a promise made insincerely or a marriage performed by an unauthorised person.
