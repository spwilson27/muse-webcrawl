Sahel

The Sahel is the semi-arid transition zone of West and north-central Africa between the Sahara desert to the north and the more humid savannas to the south. Its name derives from the Arabic sāḥil, "coast" or "shore" — the shore of the sea of sand that is the Sahara. Stretching some 5,900 km from the Atlantic coast of Senegal and Mauritania to the Red Sea coast of Sudan and Eritrea, the belt crosses parts of Senegal, Mauritania, Mali, Burkina Faso, Niger, Nigeria, Chad, Sudan, South Sudan, Ethiopia and Eritrea.

Climate and ecology

The Sahel receives between about 100 and 600 mm of rain a year, almost all of it in a short summer rainy season, and rainfall is highly variable between years. Vegetation is steppe-like: scattered thorny acacia trees, shrubs and annual grasses that green briefly after the rains. Livelihoods combine rain-fed farming of millet, sorghum and groundnuts with transhumant pastoralism, herders moving livestock seasonally between dry-season and wet-season pastures.

History

Historically the Sahel was the meeting ground of trans-Saharan trade and the West African savanna kingdoms: the Ghana, Mali and Songhai empires, the Hausa city-states and Kanem-Bornu grew wealthy controlling trade in gold, salt and kola nuts, and cities such as Timbuktu and Djenné became centres of Islamic scholarship. The region was colonised chiefly by France and Britain in the late nineteenth century and became independent around 1960.

Drought and environment

Prolonged droughts in the 1970s and 1980s caused famine and large-scale mortality and drove fears of desertification. Later satellite studies found substantial "re-greening" in some areas, aided by farmer-managed natural regeneration of trees, notably in Niger. The African Union's Great Green Wall initiative, originally conceived as a belt of trees across the continent, has evolved into a mosaic of land-restoration projects across the Sahel.

Security and politics

Since the 2010s the central Sahel — Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger — has suffered jihadist insurgencies linked to al-Qaeda and Islamic State affiliates, communal violence between farmers and herders intensified by climate pressure, and a series of military coups (Mali 2020–21, Burkina Faso 2022, Niger 2023). The French-led Operation Barkhane ended in 2022, regional bloc ECOWAS has fractured over the coups, and the three junta-led states formed the Alliance of Sahel States. Hundreds of thousands have been displaced, and the region faces one of the world's fastest-growing humanitarian crises.
