Igneous intrusion

Igneous intrusion
FieldGeology — igneous petrology
ProcessMagma cools and solidifies within the Earth's crust
Main formsPluton, batholith, stock, dyke, sill, laccolith, lopolith

An igneous intrusion (or intrusive body) is a body of intrusive igneous rock that forms by the crystallisation of magma slowly cooling below the surface of the Earth. Intrusions have a wide variety of forms and compositions, illustrated by such examples as the Palisades Sill of New York and New Jersey, the Henry Mountains of Utah, and the Bushveld Igneous Complex of South Africa. Because the host ("country") rock insulates the magma, intrusions cool slowly and develop coarse-grained (phaneritic) textures of visible mineral crystals.

Classification

Intrusions are classified by their shape and their relationship to the layering of the surrounding rock:

  • Dyke (dike) — a tabular, discordant sheet that cuts across bedding or foliation; dykes often occur in radiating or parallel swarms.
  • Sill — a tabular, concordant sheet injected parallel to bedding planes.
  • Laccolith — a concordant, lens-shaped intrusion that arches the overlying strata into a dome.
  • Lopolith — a large, saucer-shaped concordant intrusion, typically mafic and layered (e.g. the Bushveld Complex).
  • Pluton — a general term for a large intrusive body; bodies over ~100 km² in outcrop area are batholiths (often composite, e.g. the Sierra Nevada Batholith), smaller ones are stocks.
  • Volcanic neck/pipe — the solidified conduit of an eroded volcano, e.g. Shiprock, New Mexico.

Formation

Magma rising from the mantle or lower crust stalls where its buoyancy is neutralised or where it meets mechanical barriers, inflating magma chambers. Emplacement mechanisms include stoping (engulfing blocks of roof rock), assimilation, ballooning and the propagation of dyke fractures. Intrusive suites cluster above subduction zones, where they form the roots of volcanic arcs, and in rifts and large igneous provinces.

Significance

Intrusions host major ore deposits — the layered Bushveld Complex holds most of the world's platinum-group elements and chromium — and their radiometric dating (e.g. U–Pb on zircon) anchors the geological timescale. Contact metamorphism of the surrounding rock produces aureoles of hornfels and skarn.
