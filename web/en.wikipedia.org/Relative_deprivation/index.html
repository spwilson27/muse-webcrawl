Relative deprivation

People judge their situation against reference groups, not absolute measures.

Relative deprivation is the experience of being deprived of something to which one believes oneself entitled, judged in comparison with others rather than by an absolute standard. The concept originates in Samuel Stouffer's wartime studies of American soldiers (The American Soldier, 1949), which found, paradoxically, that satisfaction with promotion was lowest in units where promotion was most common — because expectations rose with the reference group.

Development

Sociologist Robert K. Merton elaborated the idea within reference-group theory, and W. G. Runciman's Relative Deprivation and Social Justice (1966) specified conditions for the feeling: a person lacks X, sees others possessing X, wants X, and feels entitled to X. Ted Robert Gurr's Why Men Rebel (1970) made the gap between value expectations and value capabilities a central explanation of political violence and rebellion.

Researchers distinguish egoistic deprivation (an individual comparing themselves with other individuals) from fraternalistic deprivation (a group comparing itself with other groups); the latter is more consistently linked to collective action and protest. The concept has been applied to riots, revolutions, voting behaviour, health inequalities and consumer dissatisfaction, and is related to the J-curve hypothesis, in which unrest follows when a period of rising expectations meets a sudden reversal.

Criticism

Critics note that deprivation is hard to measure independently of the discontent it is meant to explain, and that perceived injustice, organisation and opportunity also matter for whether grievance becomes action.
