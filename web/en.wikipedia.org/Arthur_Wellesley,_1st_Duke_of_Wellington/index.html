Arthur Wellesley, 1st Duke of Wellington (1 May 1769 – 14 September 1852), was an Anglo-Irish statesman and soldier who is one of the leading military and political figures in British history. As commander of the Anglo-Allied army he defeated Napoleon Bonaparte at the Battle of Waterloo in 1815, and he twice served as Prime Minister of the United Kingdom.

Military career

Born in Dublin to the Earl and Countess of Mornington, Wellesley was commissioned as an ensign in 1787. He made his reputation in India (1796–1805), winning the Battle of Assaye (1803) during the Second Anglo-Maratha War and serving as governor of Seringapatam and Mysore. In the Peninsular War (1808–1814) he commanded British and Portuguese forces against Napoleonic France, winning at Talavera, Salamanca, and Vitoria, and invading southern France. Created Duke of Wellington in 1814, he commanded the Allied army during the Hundred Days, defeating Napoleon at Waterloo on 18 June 1815 alongside Blücher's Prussians. He never lost a major battle, though his style was famously defensive and attritional, and he was a master of logistics and terrain.

Political career

Wellington led the Tory government as Prime Minister from 1828 to 1830, during which, despite his own misgivings, he pushed through Catholic emancipation (1829) to avert civil unrest in Ireland. His opposition to parliamentary reform made him unpopular — his London home, Apsley House, had its windows smashed, prompting the iron shutters behind his nickname "Iron Duke". He later served as Foreign Secretary and as Commander-in-Chief of the Forces, and remained an elder statesman until his death at Walmer Castle in 1852, when he received a state funeral and burial in St Paul's Cathedral.
