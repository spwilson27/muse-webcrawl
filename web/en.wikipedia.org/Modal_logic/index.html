Modal logic

Modal logic is a branch of logic for representing and reasoning about modalities — statements qualified by notions such as necessity, possibility, knowledge, obligation, time or belief. Classical modal logic adds to propositional logic the operators □ ("necessarily") and ◇ ("possibly"), from which a wide family of systems — alethic, deontic, epistemic, temporal and doxastic logics — is built.

History

Aristotle and the Stoics discussed modal syllogisms, and medieval logicians developed them extensively. The modern subject began with C. I. Lewis's systems of strict implication (from 1912–1918), designed as alternatives to the material implication of classical logic. Ruth Barcan Marcus added quantifiers, and Saul Kripke's possible-worlds semantics (1959–1963) gave the field its now-standard model theory.

Semantics

In Kripke semantics a model consists of a set of possible worlds and an accessibility relation between them. □p is true at a world if p is true at every accessible world; ◇p if p is true at some accessible world. Properties of the relation correspond to axioms: reflexivity validates T (□p → p), transitivity validates 4, and symmetry validates B. The systems K, T, S4 and S5 form the classical hierarchy; S5, in which every world accesses every other, is widely used for logical necessity.

Applications

Modal logics model obligation and permission in ethics and law (deontic logic), knowledge and belief in philosophy and artificial intelligence (epistemic logic), program behaviour in computer science (dynamic logic and the modal μ-calculus, used in verification), and time (temporal logic).
