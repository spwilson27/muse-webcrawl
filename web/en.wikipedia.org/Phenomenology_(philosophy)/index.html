Phenomenology (philosophy)

Phenomenology is the philosophical study of the structures of experience and consciousness. Founded in the first decades of the 20th century by Edmund Husserl and later developed by his student Martin Heidegger and others, phenomenology seeks to describe phenomena as they are given to consciousness, setting aside ("bracketing", in Husserl's epoché) presuppositions about the external world's independent existence.

Core concepts

Central to phenomenology is intentionality — the directedness of consciousness toward objects: every act of perceiving, remembering, or judging is consciousness of something. Husserl distinguished the act of consciousness (noesis) from its object as meant (noema), and developed methods of eidetic variation to disclose the essential structures of experience. Heidegger recast phenomenology as fundamental ontology in Being and Time (1927), analysing human existence (Dasein), care, temporality, and being-toward-death.

Later developments

In France, phenomenology was transformed by Jean-Paul Sartre, Maurice Merleau-Ponty — whose Phenomenology of Perception (1945) centred the lived body — Simone de Beauvoir, and Emmanuel Levinas. The movement deeply influenced existentialism, hermeneutics, deconstruction, and contemporary philosophy of mind, where it informs debates about consciousness and embodiment, as well as qualitative research methods in psychology and the social sciences. It is distinct from the phenomenology of religion, though the two share roots in Husserl's method.
