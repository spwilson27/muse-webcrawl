Space Shuttle thermal protection system

The Space Shuttle thermal protection system (TPS) is the barrier that protected the Space Shuttle Orbiter during the searing 1,650 °C (3,000 °F) heat of atmospheric reentry. A secondary goal was to protect from the heat and cold of space during orbit. The TPS covered essentially the entire orbiter surface and consisted of seven different materials in varying locations based on the amount of heating protection required.

Materials

  • Reinforced carbon-carbon (RCC), a grey carbon composite material that withstood temperatures up to about 1,510 °C (2,750 °F), protected the wing leading edges and nose cap, the regions of greatest heating.
  • High-temperature reusable surface insulation (HRSI) tiles, made of coated LI-900 silica, protected temperatures up to 1,260 °C (2,300 °F). The black tiles covered the underside of the orbiter.
  • Fibrous refractory composite insulation (FRCI) tiles, stronger and more durable successors to some HRSI tiles.
  • Low-temperature reusable surface insulation (LRSI) tiles, white silica tiles used in areas experiencing temperatures below 650 °C (1,200 °F).
  • Advanced flexible reusable surface insulation (AFRSI) blankets, quilted composites that replaced many LRSI tiles.
  • Felt reusable surface insulation (FRSI), white Nomex felt blankets used where temperatures stayed below about 370 °C (700 °F).
  • Thermal panes of borosilicate glass protected the windows.

Design considerations

Unlike earlier spacecraft such as Apollo, which used ablative heat shields that charred and eroded away, the Shuttle required a reusable system because the orbiter was intended to fly many missions. Each tile was individually fitted and bonded to the airframe with room-temperature-vulcanizing silicone adhesive, often with a strain isolation pad. The tiles were extremely fragile and excellent insulators; a heated tile could be held by its edges bare-handed while glowing red-hot in the middle.

Problems and the Columbia accident

The TPS was a persistent maintenance concern: tiles were frequently damaged or lost, and tile inspection and repair consumed a large share of orbiter turnaround time. On 16 January 2003, during the launch of STS-107, a piece of foam insulation from the external tank struck the RCC panels on the left wing of Columbia. The damage went undetected, and on 1 February 2003 superheated gases entered the wing during reentry, causing the orbiter to break apart and killing all seven crew members. After the accident, NASA instituted on-orbit inspection of the TPS using the Orbiter Boom Sensor System and developed tile and RCC repair techniques.
