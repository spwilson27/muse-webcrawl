Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs

Signed30 March 1961
LocationNew York City
Effective13 December 1964
Parties186

The Single Convention on Narcotic Drugs, 1961 is an international treaty that controls activities relating to a specific set of narcotic drugs and lays down a system of regulations for their medical and scientific uses. It consolidated and replaced a series of earlier drug-control treaties dating back to the International Opium Convention of 1912, and it forms, together with the 1971 Convention on Psychotropic Substances and the 1988 Convention Against Illicit Traffic, the core of the international drug control system.

Provisions

The convention classifies controlled substances into four schedules according to their dependence potential and therapeutic value, and requires parties to limit production, manufacture, trade and possession of scheduled drugs exclusively to medical and scientific purposes. It established the International Narcotics Control Board (INCB) to monitor compliance and administer estimates of national drug requirements. Penal provisions require parties to criminalize unlicensed production and trafficking.

Amendments and debate

A 1972 protocol amended the convention to emphasize treatment and rehabilitation of drug users. In 2020, acting on a World Health Organization recommendation, the UN Commission on Narcotic Drugs removed cannabis and cannabis resin from Schedule IV, the most restrictive category, while keeping them in Schedule I. Critics argue the treaty restricts medical research and traditional uses, while supporters credit it with a unified global framework against illicit markets.
