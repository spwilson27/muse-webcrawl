The Škocjan Caves (Slovene: Škocjanske jame) are a cave system in south-western Slovenia, in the heart of the classical Karst region that gave its name to karst topography worldwide. They are considered one of the most significant underground phenomena on Earth, both for their enormous subterranean canyon and for their scientific importance.

Description

The caves were carved by the Reka River, which flows across the Karst surface, sinks underground near the village of Škocjan, and travels some 35 kilometres below ground before re-emerging near the Adriatic coast as the Timavo springs. Inside the system, the river runs through the Murmuring Cave (Tiha jama) and then through one of the largest known underground canyons in the world — roughly 3.5 kilometres long, up to 120 metres wide and 146 metres high — spanned high above the torrent by the Cerkvenik Bridge on the visitor trail.

Two great collapsed dolines, Mala dolina and Velika dolina, open the canyon to daylight at its end, their walls draped in vegetation where Mediterranean and alpine species meet. The chambers support a rich fauna of bats and endemic cave animals, and the cave microclimate harbours rare plants.

History and protection

The caves have been known since antiquity and were explored systematically in the nineteenth century; tourism began as early as 1819, when steps were cut into the doline. Inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 1986, protected as a Ramsar wetland of international importance in 1999, and included in UNESCO's Karst Biosphere Reserve, the system is managed by the Škocjan Caves Regional Park. Archaeological finds in nearby Tominc Cave show human presence since the Mesolithic.
