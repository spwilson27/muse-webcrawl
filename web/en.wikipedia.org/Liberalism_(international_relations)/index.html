Liberalism (international relations)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Liberalism is a school of thought within international relations theory which holds that state preferences, rather than state capabilities, are the primary determinant of state behaviour. Unlike realism, which treats states largely as unitary actors driven by the security imperative of an anarchic system, liberalism examines the plural identities, interests, and domestic arrangements of states, and it allows for the possibility of progress and durable cooperation in international politics.

Origins

Liberal international thought draws on Enlightenment thinkers such as Immanuel Kant, whose 1795 essay Perpetual Peace argued that republican constitutions, a federation of free states, and cosmopolitan right could produce lasting peace, and on liberal political economy from Adam Smith through 19th-century advocates of free trade such as Richard Cobden. In the 20th century, Woodrow Wilson's programme for a League of Nations embodied liberal hopes for collective security and self-determination. After the Second World War, liberal ideas were developed in functionalism (David Mitrany), integration theory (Ernst Haas), and the study of interdependence (Robert Keohane and Joseph Nye).

Main strands

Democratic peace

Democratic peace theory proposes that mature democracies rarely or never fight wars against one another. Drawing on Kant and revived by researchers such as Michael Doyle and Bruce Russett, the claim is usually stated dyadically: pairs of democracies are peaceful toward each other, though democracies remain war-prone against non-democracies.

Commercial liberalism

Commercial liberalism holds that trade and economic interdependence raise the cost of war and create domestic constituencies for peace — an idea sometimes summarized in the observation that countries integrated into global supply chains have strong incentives to avoid disrupting them.

Institutional liberalism

Liberal institutionalism (or neoliberal institutionalism) argues that international institutions and regimes reduce uncertainty, lengthen the shadow of the future, and facilitate cooperation even under anarchy, without requiring a world government. Keohane's After Hegemony (1984) is a canonical statement.

Preference-based liberalism

Andrew Moravcsik's liberal theory of international politics focuses on how domestic social actors form preferences, how governments represent those preferences, and how the configuration of interdependent state preferences shapes outcomes — including bargaining, institutional choice, and war.

Criticism

Realists criticize liberalism as utopian, pointing to the failure of the League of Nations and arguing that institutions merely reflect the underlying distribution of power. Critical theorists and Marxists argue that liberal peace may rest on hegemony and unequal exchange. Empirically, the democratic peace remains debated, including questions about its definition, its applicability to democratizing states, and possible alternative explanations such as common interests and alliances.
