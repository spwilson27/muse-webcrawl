The Principality of Antioch was one of the Crusader states established during the First Crusade. Founded by the Norman leader Bohemond I after the gruelling Siege of Antioch in 1098, it endured for 170 years in what is now southern Turkey and northwestern Syria, longer than the neighbouring County of Edessa, and was the second most powerful of the Crusader states after the Kingdom of Jerusalem.

History

After capturing Antioch, Bohemond refused to return it to the Byzantine emperor despite his earlier oath, establishing an independent Norman principality. The state survived disasters such as the Battle of Ager Sanguinis (1119), in which Prince Roger of Salerno was killed, and gradually lost territory to Muslim rulers including Zengi and Nur al-Din. Prince Raynald of Châtillon's raids provoked Saladin, but Antioch escaped the catastrophe of Hattin (1187) largely because Saladin turned south first.

In the 13th century the principality became entangled in disputes with Cilician Armenia and in the War of the Antiochene Succession. It revived briefly under Bohemond VI, who allied with the Mongols; Antioch received a Mongol garrison in 1260. The end came in 1268, when the Mamluk sultan Baibars stormed and sacked Antioch, massacring or enslaving its population — a blow from which the once-great city never recovered.

Government and society

The prince ruled with the advice of a court and barons, on the model of Norman Italy and France. Society was layered: a Latin Frankish aristocracy, Greek, Syrian and Armenian Christian populations, and Muslim subjects, with Italian merchants from Genoa and Venice enjoying trading quarters. The Assizes of Antioch, surviving in Armenian translation, record the principality's feudal law.
