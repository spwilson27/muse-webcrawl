Mehmed II (Ottoman Turkish: محمد ثانى, Meḥmed-i sānī; Turkish: II. Mehmet; 30 March 1432 – 3 May 1481), commonly known as Mehmed the Conqueror (Turkish: Fatih Sultan Mehmed), was an Ottoman sultan who ruled from August 1444 to September 1446, and then later from February 1451 to May 1481. In Mehmed II's first reign, he defeated the crusade led by John Hunyadi after the Hungarian incursions into his country broke the conditions of the truce Peace of Szeged. When Mehmed II ascended the throne again in 1451, he strengthened the Ottoman navy and made preparations to attack Constantinople. At the age of 21, he conquered Constantinople and brought an end to the Byzantine Empire.

After the conquest, Mehmed claimed the title Caesar of the Romans (Kayser-i Rûm), based on the assertion that Constantinople had been the seat and capital of the Roman Empire. He also carried out extensive administrative and legal reforms, repopulated Constantinople, and made it the Ottoman capital. Mehmed continued his conquests in Anatolia, reuniting it, and in Southeast Europe as far west as Bosnia. He is considered a hero in modern-day Turkey and parts of the wider Muslim world. Among other things, Istanbul's Fatih district, Fatih Sultan Mehmet Bridge and Fatih Mosque are named after him.

Early life

Mehmed II was born on 30 March 1432, in Edirne, then the capital city of the Ottoman state. His father was Sultan Murad II (1404–1451) and his mother Hüma Hatun, a slave girl of uncertain origin. When Mehmed was eleven years old, he was sent to Amasya with his two advisers to govern and obtain experience, in accordance with the custom of Ottoman rulers prior to his time. In May 1444, Murad II abdicated and retired to Manisa, leaving the twelve-year-old Mehmed on the throne — a controversial interlude that ended when Murad returned two years later amid Janissary unrest and the threat of a new crusade.

Conquest of Constantinople

When Mehmed ascended the throne for the second time on 18 February 1451, he devoted himself to strengthening the Ottoman navy and to preparing an assault on Constantinople. He built the fortress of Rumeli Hisarı on the European shore of the Bosphorus, opposite the earlier Anadolu Hisarı, to control passage into the Black Sea. On 29 May 1453, after a 53-day siege during which Ottoman cannon battered down the city's famed Theodosian Walls and a boom across the Golden Horn was circumvented by hauling ships overland, Constantinople fell. The last Byzantine emperor, Constantine XI Palaiologos, died in the fighting. The conquest ended the Byzantine Empire and marked a turning point in Ottoman and European history. See also the article on the Fall of Constantinople.

Later campaigns and reforms

Mehmed campaigned almost every year of his reign. He annexed Serbia (1459), conquered the Morea (1460), took Trebizond (1461) — ending the last Byzantine successor state — subdued Wallachia's Vlad the Impaler, fought a long war with Venice (1463–1479), annexed the Crimea (1475), and in 1480 landed troops at Otranto in Italy. At home he promulgated a law code (the Kanunname), regulated taxation, patronised scholars and artists such as the Venetian painter Gentile Bellini, endowed the complex around the Fatih Mosque, and founded the Grand Bazaar. His legal codification controversially permitted royal fratricide "for the welfare of the state."

Death and legacy

Mehmed died on 3 May 1481 near Gebze while marching at the head of his army on a campaign whose destination remains disputed (possibly Rhodes or Mamluk Egypt). He was buried in the tomb by the Fatih Mosque in Istanbul. His conquest of Constantinople is remembered as one of the pivotal events of the second millennium, and he is regarded in Turkey as the founder of Istanbul as an imperial capital.
