Artificial general intelligence

Artificial general intelligence (AGI) is a type of artificial intelligence that would match or surpass human capabilities across virtually all cognitive tasks. This is in contrast to narrow AI, which is designed for specific tasks. AGI is considered one of the definitions of strong AI.

Characteristics

Definitions of AGI vary, but generally require an ability to learn, reason, plan, communicate in natural language, and transfer knowledge between domains. A commonly cited criterion is the ability to perform any intellectual task that a human can, sometimes benchmarked against the Turing test or against tests such as the coffee test proposed by Apple co-founder Steve Wozniak.

History

The pursuit of machine intelligence with human-like generality dates to the Dartmouth workshop of 1956 and the early optimism of researchers such as Herbert Simon and Marvin Minsky. After periods of reduced funding known as AI winters, interest revived in the 2000s and 2010s with advances in deep learning and large language models, which some researchers argue show early, limited forms of generality.

Timelines and debate

Surveys of AI researchers show wide disagreement over when AGI might arrive, with median estimates shifting earlier following the release of capable large language models in the early 2020s. Critics argue that current systems lack genuine understanding, embodiment, and robustness, while others contend that scaling existing approaches may suffice.

Risks

AGI is a central concern of AI safety research. Commentators including Nick Bostrom and Eliezer Yudkowsky have argued that a sufficiently advanced AGI could pose existential risks if its goals are misaligned with human values, motivating work on AI alignment and governance.
