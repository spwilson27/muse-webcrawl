Fish sauce

Fish sauce is a liquid condiment made from fish or krill that have been coated in salt and fermented for up to two years. It is a staple ingredient in many Southeast and East Asian cuisines — nước mắm in Vietnam, nam pla in Thailand, patis in the Philippines — valued for its pure savoury (umami) flavour. Similar fermented fish sauces, such as garum, were made in the ancient Mediterranean world.

Production

Small oily fish, most commonly anchovies, are mixed with sea salt — typically around three parts fish to one part salt — and packed into large wooden barrels or earthenware jars. Over months of fermentation, enzymes from the fish and salt-tolerant bacteria break the proteins down into amino acids, producing a clear amber liquid rich in glutamates. The first pressing yields the most prized, highest-protein sauce; lower grades are made by re-extracting the residue with brine. Quality is often graded by nitrogen content, expressed in degrees (°N).

Culinary use

Fish sauce is used both as a cooking ingredient and, mixed with lime juice, sugar, water and chilli, as a dipping sauce. In Vietnam the diluted mixture nước chấm accompanies spring rolls and vermicelli dishes; in Thailand it seasons stir-fries, soups and salads such as som tam. Despite its pungent aroma raw, fish sauce mellows when cooked, contributing depth rather than fishiness.

History

Fermented fish products have ancient origins: the Roman condiment garum and its Greek predecessor were traded throughout the Mediterranean. In Asia, fish fermentation likely developed independently in the coastal regions of what is now China and Southeast Asia, where fish sauce gradually displaced fermented fish pastes as the dominant savoury seasoning.
