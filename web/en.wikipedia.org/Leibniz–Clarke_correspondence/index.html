Leibniz–Clarke correspondence

The Leibniz–Clarke correspondence is a famous exchange of letters from 1715–1716 between the German philosopher Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz and the English philosopher and theologian Samuel Clarke, who wrote with the knowledge and probable assistance of Isaac Newton. Mediated by Caroline, Princess of Wales, the debate ranged over the nature of space and time, God's relation to the world, miracles, free will and the principle of sufficient reason. It ended only with Leibniz's death in November 1716 and was published in 1717.

Background

Leibniz opened the exchange with a letter to Princess Caroline warning that natural religion was decaying in England, attacking Newton's view that space is the sensorium of God and the Newtonian idea that God must periodically intervene to correct the clockwork of the solar system — which Leibniz mocked as implying an imperfect craftsman. Clarke, Newton's friend and translator of his Opticks into Latin, replied on the Newtonian side.

Space and time

The central philosophical dispute opposed Newton's absolute space and time — a fixed container existing independently of objects — to Leibniz's relational theory, in which space is merely the order of coexistences among things. Leibniz deployed his principle of sufficient reason and the identity of indiscernibles: if space were absolute, God could have placed the whole universe three feet to the left with no reason for choosing one placement over another, and the two states would be indiscernible yet supposedly different. Clarke answered that God's mere will is a sufficient reason. The debate anticipated arguments later revived in the substantivalism–relationalism controversy and, through Einstein, in general relativity.

Other topics

The letters also treat the soul and its location, the vacuum and atoms, whether the world could have existed sooner, miracles, and gravity — which Leibniz criticised as an "occult quality" unless mechanically explained, while Clarke defended it as a well-established phenomenon whose cause could be left to God.
