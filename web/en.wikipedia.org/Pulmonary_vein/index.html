The pulmonary veins are the veins that transfer oxygenated blood from the lungs to the heart. The largest pulmonary veins are the four main pulmonary veins, two from each lung, that empty into the left atrium of the heart. They are among the few veins in the body that carry oxygen-rich blood — the usual convention that veins carry deoxygenated blood does not apply here (nor in the umbilical vein of the fetus).

Structure

Each lung is drained by a superior and an inferior pulmonary vein: the right and left superior pulmonary veins drain the upper (and on the right, also middle) lobes, while the inferior veins drain the lower lobes. The veins emerge from the hilum of each lung and pass to the posterior aspect of the left atrium. Their ostia (openings) in the atrium are frequent sites of the ectopic electrical activity that triggers atrial fibrillation.

Development and variation

During development the common pulmonary vein is absorbed into the expanding left atrium, producing the usual four separate openings; variations in number and arrangement are common and of interest in cardiac imaging and surgery. In the congenital anomaly total anomalous pulmonary venous return, the pulmonary veins drain instead into the systemic venous circulation, requiring surgical correction.

Clinical significance

Catheter ablation for atrial fibrillation typically isolates the pulmonary vein ostia electrically ("pulmonary vein isolation"). Rarely, pulmonary vein stenosis can follow ablation or occur congenitally.
