In anatomy, the atlas (C1) is the most superior (first) cervical vertebra of the spine. It is named for Atlas of Greek mythology because, just as Atlas supported the globe, the atlas supports the entire weight of the head.

The atlas differs significantly from other vertebrae. It has no vertebral body and no spinous process; instead it is ring-shaped, consisting of an anterior arch, a posterior arch, and two lateral masses. Together with the axis, the vertebra immediately below it, the atlas forms the joint that connects the skull to the spine.

Structure

The anterior arch forms about one-fifth of the ring and bears the anterior tubercle on its front surface, giving attachment to the longus colli muscles. On its posterior surface is a smooth oval facet, the fovea dentis, which articulates with the dens (odontoid process) of the axis. The posterior arch forms about two-fifths of the ring and ends in a small posterior tubercle, a rudimentary spinous process. The lateral masses are the bulkiest parts of the bone; each bears a superior articular facet that cups the corresponding occipital condyle and an inferior facet that articulates with the axis. Each transverse process is pierced by a transverse foramen transmitting the vertebral artery.

Function

The atlanto-occipital joint between the atlas and the occipital bone permits the head to nod, as in signifying "yes". The atlanto-axial joint between atlas and axis allows the head to rotate from side to side, as in signifying "no"; in this movement the atlas, carrying the skull, rotates around the dens of the axis.

Clinical significance

A fracture of the atlas, classically described by Sir Geoffrey Jefferson in 1919, is the Jefferson fracture, typically a burst fracture of the anterior and posterior arches caused by an axial load on the top of the head, such as diving into shallow water. Congenital anomalies include posterior arch defects and assimilation of the atlas to the occipital bone.
