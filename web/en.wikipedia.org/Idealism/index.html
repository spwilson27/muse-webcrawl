Idealism

Idealism in philosophy is the set of metaphysical perspectives asserting that, most fundamentally, reality is equivalent to mind, spirit, or consciousness; that reality is entirely a mental construct; or that ideas are the highest form of reality and that material objects are only real insofar as they exist in the mind. Often idealism is contrasted with materialism, which holds that matter is primary. The term derives from "idea", and its modern philosophical usage dates from the early 18th century.

George Berkeley (1685–1753), whose motto esse est percipi — "to be is to be perceived" — epitomises subjective idealism.

Varieties

Metaphysical idealism claims reality itself is immaterial or mind-dependent. Subjective idealism, associated with George Berkeley, holds that material objects are merely collections of ideas in perceiving minds, sustained by God's perception. Transcendental idealism, developed by Immanuel Kant, distinguishes phenomena (things as they appear, structured by the mind's categories of space, time and causation) from noumena (things-in-themselves), holding we can know only the former. Absolute idealism, associated with G. W. F. Hegel and British idealists such as F. H. Bradley, sees reality as a single all-encompassing Absolute Mind or Spirit realising itself through history. Objective idealism, found in Plato's theory of Forms and in Schelling, posits ideas as fundamental but independent of any individual mind.

History

Antecedents appear in Plato, in Vedantic and Yogācāra Buddhist thought (which holds that phenomena are "mind-only"), and in Neo-Platonism. Modern idealism began with Descartes' method of doubt and Berkeley's immaterialism, reached its zenith in German idealism (Kant, Fichte, Schelling, Hegel) between the 1780s and 1840s, and dominated British and American philosophy in the later 19th century (T. H. Green, Josiah Royce). It declined with the rise of analytic philosophy under G. E. Moore and Bertrand Russell — Moore's "Refutation of Idealism" (1903) is a landmark — and under Marxist materialism, though idealist themes persist in phenomenology, philosophy of mind, and some interpretations of quantum mechanics.

Criticism and legacy

Critics such as Samuel Johnson — who kicked a stone saying "I refute it thus" — charged idealism with denying obvious features of the world; idealists reply that they deny only mind-independent matter, not the stable, law-governed world of experience. Idealist arguments about perception anticipate modern discussions of consciousness and virtual reality, and its epistemological strand continues as anti-realism in the philosophy of science.
