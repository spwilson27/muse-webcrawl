Essence

In philosophy, essence is the property or set of properties that make an entity or substance what it fundamentally is, and which it has by necessity, as opposed to its accidental properties—properties it happens to have but could lack. The notion goes back to Aristotle, who treated the essence of a thing (its to ti ên einai, "what it was to be" that thing) as the object of definition.

History

Plato's theory of Forms treated essences as separate, eternal realities of which sensible things are copies. Aristotle relocated essences within things themselves as their form. Medieval scholastics, notably Thomas Aquinas, distinguished essence from existence: in all created things the two are really distinct, and only in God do they coincide.

John Locke distinguished "real essence" (the inner constitution of a thing) from "nominal essence" (the abstract idea by which we classify it). In the 20th century, existentialists such as Jean-Paul Sartre inverted the scholastic slogan, arguing that for human beings "existence precedes essence".

Essentialism

Essentialism is the view that entities have essences—that there are necessary, defining attributes without which an entity would not be of its kind. Contemporary defenders such as Saul Kripke have argued for essentialism about origins and natural kinds, while anti-essentialists question whether such definitions can be drawn non-arbitrarily.
