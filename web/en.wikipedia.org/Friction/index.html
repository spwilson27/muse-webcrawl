Friction

Friction is the force resisting the relative motion of solid surfaces, fluid layers, and material elements sliding against each other. There are several types of friction: dry friction between two solid surfaces, subdivided into static friction between non-moving surfaces and kinetic friction between moving surfaces; fluid friction between layers of a viscous fluid; lubricated friction, where a lubricant separates the surfaces; skin friction, a component of drag; and internal friction within a deforming body.

Laws of dry friction

The classical empirical laws, associated with Leonardo da Vinci, Guillaume Amontons, and Charles-Augustin de Coulomb, state that the friction force is approximately proportional to the normal load, approximately independent of the apparent area of contact, and (for kinetic friction) roughly independent of sliding speed. The ratio of friction force to normal force is the coefficient of friction, usually denoted by the Greek letter μ. Static coefficients are generally higher than kinetic ones, which is why a stuck object suddenly lurches once it starts to move.

Causes

At the microscopic level, friction arises from adhesion between surface asperities, deformation of the contacting material, and ploughing of softer material by harder asperities. The true area of contact between apparently smooth surfaces is a small fraction of the apparent area, and it grows in proportion to load, which underlies the proportionality of the friction force to load.

Applications and consequences

Friction is essential for walking, gripping, braking, and traction, but it also causes wear and wastes energy in machinery; roughly a fifth of the world's energy consumption is estimated to be spent overcoming friction. Tribology is the science and engineering of interacting surfaces in relative motion, including friction, wear, and lubrication.
