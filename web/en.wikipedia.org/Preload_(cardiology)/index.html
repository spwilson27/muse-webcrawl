In cardiac physiology, preload is the degree to which the ventricles are stretched before contracting, conventionally expressed as the end-diastolic volume (or the corresponding end-diastolic pressure and wall stress) at the moment just before systole begins.

Definition and measurement

Preload is determined principally by venous return and ventricular compliance. Because it cannot be measured directly in patients, it is estimated clinically using end-diastolic volume, end-diastolic pressure, or surrogate measures such as pulmonary capillary wedge pressure for the left ventricle and central venous pressure for the right ventricle.

Frank–Starling relationship

According to the Frank–Starling law of the heart, within physiological limits the stroke volume increases as preload increases: a greater end-diastolic stretch places the cardiac muscle fibres at a more favourable length and sensitises the contractile apparatus to calcium, producing a stronger contraction. This mechanism matches ventricular output to venous return beat by beat.

Clinical significance

Preload is increased in volume-overload states such as heart failure, valvular regurgitation and fluid retention, and decreased in hypovolemia, dehydration and venodilation. Many therapies—including diuretics and vasodilators—work partly by reducing preload, while intravenous fluids increase it.
