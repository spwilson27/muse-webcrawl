Conflict thesis

The conflict thesis is a historiographical position holding that science and religion have been in inevitable, perpetual conflict throughout history. It was popularised in the 19th century by John William Draper's History of the Conflict between Religion and Science (1874) and Andrew Dickson White's A History of the Warfare of Science with Theology in Christendom (1896).

Criticism

Most modern historians of science reject the conflict thesis as an oversimplification. They note that religious institutions funded and preserved science for centuries, that figures such as Galileo had supporters within the Church, and that supposed emblematic conflicts — such as the medieval Church supposedly teaching a flat earth — are myths (the "myth of the flat Earth"). Historians including John Hedley Brooke have advanced a "complexity thesis" instead, emphasising that relations between science and religion have varied from conflict to harmony to mutual indifference depending on context.

Nevertheless, the conflict narrative remains influential in popular culture and in discussions of specific modern controversies such as evolution and creationism.
