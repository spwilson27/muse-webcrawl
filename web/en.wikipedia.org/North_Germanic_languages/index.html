North Germanic languages

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The North Germanic languages, also called the Scandinavian languages or Nordic languages, are a branch of the Germanic language family spoken natively by about 20 million people, principally in Scandinavia and Iceland. They descend from Old Norse, itself a descendant of Proto-Norse, and today comprise Danish, Norwegian (Bokmål and Nynorsk), Swedish, Icelandic, and Faroese, together with the nearly extinct Norn of Orkney and Shetland and the Elfdalian dialect of Sweden sometimes regarded as a separate language.

History

Proto-Norse, attested in Elder Futhark runic inscriptions from about the 2nd century CE, developed into Old Norse by the Viking Age. Old Norse later split into an eastern branch (Old Danish and Old Swedish) and a western branch (Old Norwegian, Old Icelandic, and Old Faroese). The insular languages — Icelandic and Faroese — remained grammatically conservative, preserving the Old Norse system of four cases and three genders, while the mainland Scandinavian languages simplified greatly under Low German influence during the Hanseatic period, losing most inflection and developing fixed word order and (in Danish) the glottal stød.

Mutual intelligibility and features

The mainland languages form a dialect continuum: Danish, Norwegian, and Swedish are largely mutually intelligible, especially in writing, though spoken Danish is notoriously difficult for other Scandinavians because of its extensive consonant weakening and vowel reduction. Shared features include the postposed definite article suffixed to the noun (e.g. Swedish huset, "the house"), the vocabulary of common Germanic origin enriched by Low German loans, and a pitch-accent distinction in Norwegian and Swedish. Icelandic pursues linguistic purism, coining neologisms from native roots, making modern Icelandic remarkably close to the language of the sagas.

Status

Danish, Norwegian, Swedish, Icelandic, and Faroese are official languages of their respective states, and Scandinavian languages enjoy recognition in the Nordic Council's language convention, which guarantees Nordic citizens the right to use their own language in dealings with authorities across the region. English proficiency is high throughout Scandinavia, prompting ongoing debate about "domain loss" for the national languages in science and business.
