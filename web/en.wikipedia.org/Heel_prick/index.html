Heel prick

Other namesGuthrie test (originally), newborn blood spot test
PurposeNewborn screening for treatable metabolic and genetic disorders
TimingTypically 24–72 hours after birth
Named afterRobert Guthrie (bacterial inhibition assay for PKU)

A heel prick is a blood sampling procedure performed on newborn babies, in which the heel is punctured with a small lancet and drops of blood are collected onto filter paper. It is the standard method used in newborn screening programmes worldwide to detect serious but treatable disorders before symptoms appear.

Procedure

The baby's heel is warmed and cleaned, then pricked on the medial or lateral plantar surface with an automated incision device. Blood spots are applied to a special filter-paper card (the "blood spot card"), dried, and sent to a screening laboratory. The test is usually done within the first few days of life; in the United Kingdom it is offered on day 5.

Conditions screened

The first disorder screened this way was phenylketonuria (PKU), using the bacterial inhibition assay developed by Robert Guthrie in the early 1960s. Modern panels, greatly expanded by tandem mass spectrometry, commonly include congenital hypothyroidism, cystic fibrosis, sickle cell disease, medium-chain acyl-CoA dehydrogenase deficiency (MCADD) and a range of other inherited metabolic disorders; the exact panel varies by country.

Considerations

The procedure causes brief pain, and comfort measures such as breastfeeding or oral sucrose are recommended. Screening programmes raise policy questions about consent, storage of blood spot cards, and their later use in research.
