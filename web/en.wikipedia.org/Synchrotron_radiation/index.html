TypeElectromagnetic radiation
SourceRelativistic charged particles in magnetic fields

Synchrotron radiation (also known as synchrotron light) is the electromagnetic radiation emitted when relativistic charged particles are subject to an acceleration perpendicular to their velocity, as when electrons are forced to move in curved paths by magnetic fields. It is produced naturally by astronomical objects and artificially in synchrotron light sources, where it serves as an exceptionally bright, tunable source of X-rays and other radiation.

Properties

Synchrotron radiation forms a continuous spectrum extending from the infrared to hard X-rays, with a characteristic energy that rises steeply with particle energy. It is emitted in a narrow cone tangent to the particle's path, is highly collimated and polarized, and comes in intense pulses reflecting the bunch structure of the circulating beam. Its brightness exceeds that of conventional X-ray tubes by many orders of magnitude.

History

The theory was developed in the 1940s by Dmitri Ivanenko, Isaak Pomeranchuk, Julian Schwinger, and others. The radiation was first observed in 1947 at the General Electric Research Laboratory in Schenectady, New York, in a 70 MeV synchrotron — initially as a nuisance that drained energy from the beam. From the 1960s onward, dedicated synchrotron light sources were built, and facilities such as ESRF, the Advanced Photon Source, and SPring-8 now serve thousands of researchers.

Applications

Synchrotron light is used for X-ray crystallography of proteins and materials, spectroscopy, imaging and tomography, lithography, and studies of chemical reactions in real time. In astronomy, synchrotron emission reveals energetic processes in supernova remnants, pulsars, and the jets of active galactic nuclei; the Crab Nebula is a classic synchrotron source.
