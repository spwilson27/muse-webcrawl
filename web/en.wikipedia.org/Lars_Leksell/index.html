Lars Gustaf Fritiof Leksell (1907–1986) was a Swedish neurosurgeon and professor of neurosurgery at the Karolinska Institute in Stockholm. He is widely regarded as the father of radiosurgery, having pioneered the use of precisely focused radiation to treat intracranial disorders without opening the skull, and he invented the Gamma Knife as well as the stereotactic frame that bears his name.

Early life and career

Leksell studied medicine at the Karolinska Institute, receiving his medical degree in the 1930s, and trained in neurosurgery under Herbert Olivecrona. He completed his doctorate in 1945 with work on the muscular afferents of motor nerves. Appointed professor of neurosurgery at Lund in 1958, he returned to the Karolinska Institute in 1960 as professor and head of neurosurgery, a post he held until his retirement in 1974.

Stereotaxy and radiosurgery

In 1949 Leksell introduced a new stereotactic frame based on a polar coordinate ("arc-centered") principle, allowing a probe to reach any intracranial target along a reproducible arc. The Leksell Stereotactic System became a standard tool for functional neurosurgery. Seeking a less invasive alternative to open surgery, Leksell proposed in 1951 the concept of radiosurgery: using cross-fired beams of ionizing radiation, targeted stereotactically, to destroy a small intracranial volume in a single session.

Working with physicist Börje Larsson, he first used proton beams and then, in 1967, treated the first patient with the prototype Gamma Knife — a device using many cobalt-60 sources whose beams converge on a single point — at the Sophiahemmet Hospital in Stockholm. The first clinical series treated trigeminal neuralgia, acoustic neuromas, and arteriovenous malformations. To commercialize the technology, Leksell founded the company Elekta in 1972. Radiosurgery has since become a standard treatment for brain tumors, vascular malformations, and functional disorders worldwide.
