Essence–energies distinction

The essence–energies distinction is a theological doctrine of Eastern Orthodox Christianity, systematised by St Gregory Palamas in the 14th century, which holds that God is unknowable in his essence (ousia) but knowable in his energies (energeiai)—his actions and self-manifestations in the world, such as the uncreated light of the Transfiguration.

Background

The distinction has roots in the Cappadocian Fathers, who taught that "we know our God from His operations, but do not undertake to approach near to His essence". Basil of Caesarea argued that the divine essence remains inaccessible, while the energies descend to creatures.

Hesychast controversy

In the 1330s–1340s the monk Barlaam of Calabria attacked the Hesychast monks of Mount Athos, who claimed to experience the uncreated light of God in prayer. Gregory Palamas defended them by arguing that this light is a divine energy, truly God yet distinct from the imparticipable essence. Councils at Constantinople in 1341, 1347, and 1351 vindicated Palamas, and the doctrine became dogma in the Orthodox Church.

Relation to Western theology

Roman Catholic theologians have often been critical, regarding the distinction as compromising divine simplicity; Orthodox theologians reply that the distinction is not a division in God but preserves both his transcendence and his real presence to creation.
