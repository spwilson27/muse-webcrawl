The optic chiasm (or optic chiasma) is the X-shaped structure at the base of the brain where the two optic nerves meet and the nerve fibres originating in the nasal (inner) half of each retina cross to the opposite side. It lies just in front of and above the pituitary gland, beneath the hypothalamus. The word "chiasm" comes from the Greek letter chi (Χ), whose shape the crossing resembles.

Anatomy and function

Each optic nerve carries about a million axons from the retinal ganglion cells of one eye. At the chiasm, fibres from the nasal retina—which receives light from the temporal (outer) visual field—decussate, while fibres from the temporal retina continue uncrossed. Beyond the chiasm, the resulting optic tracts each carry a complete map of the opposite half of the visual world from both eyes to the lateral geniculate nucleus of the thalamus and onward to the visual cortex. This arrangement lets each cerebral hemisphere process one half of visual space with input from both eyes, a prerequisite for binocular depth perception.

The proportion of crossing fibres varies among species: in animals with laterally placed eyes and panoramic vision, such as rodents, nearly all fibres cross; in primates with forward-facing eyes, roughly half cross. Developmental guidance molecules, including ephrins at the chiasm midline, sort the axons.

Clinical significance

Because of its position directly above the pituitary gland, the chiasm is classically compressed by pituitary adenomas growing upward. Damage to the crossing nasal fibres produces the characteristic visual field defect of bitemporal hemianopia—loss of the outer half of the visual field in both eyes, sometimes described as tunnel vision. Other causes of chiasmal injury include craniopharyngiomas, meningiomas, aneurysms, and demyelinating disease.
