Wiedergutmachung

Wiedergutmachung (German for "making good again") refers to the reparations that the German government has paid since 1952 to victims of Nazi persecution during the Holocaust, as well as to the broader German policy of material and moral compensation for Nazi-era crimes. The policy began with the Luxembourg Agreements of 1952, negotiated between the Federal Republic of Germany, Israel, and the Conference on Jewish Material Claims Against Germany.

The Luxembourg Agreements

Signed on 10 September 1952, the agreements committed West Germany to pay Israel three billion Deutschmarks in goods and services and 450 million marks to the Claims Conference for victims outside Israel. Chancellor Konrad Adenauer pushed the agreements through the Bundestag despite significant domestic opposition, arguing that Germany had a moral obligation to compensate for crimes committed in its name.

Individual compensation

The Federal Compensation Laws (Bundesentschädigungsgesetze, 1953 and 1956) established payments to individual survivors for loss of life, health, liberty, and property. Over subsequent decades the programmes expanded: pensions for survivors, payments to former forced labourers through the Foundation "Remembrance, Responsibility and Future" established in 2000, and hardship funds for victims previously excluded, including many in Eastern Europe. In total, Germany has paid well over 80 billion dollars in compensation since 1952.

Debate

Many survivors and critics have argued that no sum can "make good" the crimes of the Holocaust and that bureaucratic procedures often retraumatised applicants; the term itself has been criticised as euphemistic. Nonetheless, the programme is frequently cited as a landmark in the development of reparations and transitional justice.
