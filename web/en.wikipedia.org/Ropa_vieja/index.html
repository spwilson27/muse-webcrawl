Ropa vieja

Ropa vieja served with rice, black beans and plantains

Ropa vieja (Spanish for "old clothes") is a dish of shredded, stewed beef in a tomato-based sauce, and is regarded as one of the national dishes of Cuba. It is also popular throughout the Caribbean and in the Canary Islands of Spain, where a version incorporating chickpeas is traditional. The name refers to the appearance of the shredded meat, said to resemble tattered rags.

Origins

The dish is generally traced to the Sephardic Jewish communities of medieval Spain, where a slow-cooked stew of leftover meat—analogous to the Sabbath stew prepared to avoid cooking on the day of rest—was shredded and recooked. Spanish colonists carried the recipe to the Canary Islands and onward to the Caribbean, where it took root in Cuba and evolved with local ingredients and tastes.

A popular Cuban folk tale explains the name: a poor man, having nothing to feed his family, shredded and cooked his own clothes, and his prayers miraculously transformed them into a rich beef stew.

Preparation

Traditionally the dish uses flank steak or skirt steak, which is first boiled or braised until tender, then shredded along the grain. The meat is sautéed with a sofrito of onions, bell peppers and garlic, then simmered in a sauce of tomato, cumin, oregano and dry wine or cooking wine; olives, capers or pimientos may be added. In Cuba it is typically served with white rice, black beans (moros y cristianos when cooked together), and fried plantains (maduros or tostones).

Regional variants

In the Canary Islands, ropa vieja often combines beef or chicken with chickpeas and potatoes and may include other leftovers from the puchero stew. In Puerto Rico and Venezuela—where a similar shredded beef dish, carne mechada, forms part of the national dish pabellón criollo—the seasoning profile varies. Cuban-American restaurants in Florida have made ropa vieja a signature dish of the diaspora.
