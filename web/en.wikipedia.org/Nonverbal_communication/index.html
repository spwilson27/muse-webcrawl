Nonverbal communication

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nonverbal communication (NVC) is the transmission of messages or signals through a nonverbal platform such as eye contact (oculesics), body language (kinesics), social distance (proxemics), touch (haptics), voice (paralanguage), physical environments and appearance, and the use of objects and time (chronemics).

Functions

Nonverbal signals can repeat, complement, substitute for, regulate, or contradict verbal messages. Facial expressions of emotion — extensively studied by Paul Ekman — are among the most powerful channels, though their interpretation varies across cultures. Gestures include emblems with conventional meanings, illustrators that accompany speech, and adaptors such as fidgeting.

Study

The scientific study of nonverbal behaviour is often dated to Charles Darwin's The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals (1872). Twentieth-century researchers such as Ray Birdwhistell (kinesics) and Edward T. Hall (proxemics) systematised the field. Popular claims that a fixed percentage — often quoted as 93% — of communication is nonverbal derive from a misreading of Albert Mehrabian's narrow experiments on feelings and attitudes.
