Pyrethroid

Pyrethroids are synthetic insecticides modelled on the natural pyrethrins from chrysanthemum flowers. Examples: permethrin, cypermethrin, deltamethrin, lambda-cyhalothrin.

A pyrethroid is an organic compound similar to the natural pyrethrins, which are extracted from the flowers of pyrethrum plants (Chrysanthemum cinerariifolium and C. coccineum). Pyrethroids constitute the majority of the commercial household insecticide market and are widely used in agriculture, mosquito control, and veterinary products.

Mode of action

Pyrethroids are axonic excitotoxins: they bind to voltage-gated sodium channels in insect nerve membranes and prevent them from closing, causing repetitive firing, paralysis ("knockdown"), and death. They are generally much less toxic to mammals, whose sodium channels are less sensitive and whose body temperature and detoxifying enzymes reduce their effect, though they are highly toxic to fish and aquatic invertebrates and to cats for some compounds (notably permethrin).

Development and types

Early pyrethroids such as allethrin (1949) were photolabile like the natural compounds. Work in the 1960s and 1970s, notably by Michael Elliott at Rothamsted Research, produced photostable compounds such as permethrin, cypermethrin, and deltamethrin, greatly expanding agricultural use. Pyrethroids are often divided into Type I (without an alpha-cyano group) and Type II (with one), which produce somewhat different poisoning syndromes.

Resistance and environment

Target-site mutations in sodium channels (knockdown resistance, or kdr) and enhanced metabolic detoxification have produced widespread resistance in mosquitoes, bed bugs, and agricultural pests, complicating malaria control programmes that rely on pyrethroid-treated bed nets.
