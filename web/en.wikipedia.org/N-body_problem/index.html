In physics, the n-body problem is the problem of predicting the individual motions of a group of celestial objects interacting with each other gravitationally. For two bodies, the problem has a complete analytic solution (elliptical Kepler orbits). For three or more bodies, no general closed-form solution exists, and most systems are chaotic: small differences in initial conditions grow exponentially.

History

Newton solved the two-body case in the Principia (1687). The three-body problem occupied Euler, Lagrange (who found special periodic solutions, including the Lagrange points), and Poincaré, whose work on it in the 1880s–1890s founded the qualitative theory of dynamical systems and revealed chaos. Karl Sundman found a convergent series solution for the three-body problem in 1912, but it converges far too slowly for practical use.

Special solutions

Particular configurations — Lagrange's equilateral-triangle and collinear solutions, the figure-eight orbit (found numerically in 1993), and many periodic "choreographies" — are exactly solvable or long-lived, though typically unstable.

Numerical approaches

Practical work uses numerical integration, from simple direct summation (O(n²)) to tree codes such as the Barnes–Hut algorithm and fast multipole methods in astrophysics, where N-body simulations model star clusters, galaxy formation and cosmology with millions to billions of particles.
