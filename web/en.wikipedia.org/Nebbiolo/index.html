Nebbiolo (Italian: [nebˈbjɔːlo]; Piedmontese: nebieul) is an Italian red wine grape variety predominantly associated with the Piedmont region, where it makes the Denominazione di Origine Controllata e Garantita (DOCG) wines of Barolo, Barbaresco, Gattinara, Roero and Ghemme. The name is thought to derive from nebbia, Italian for "fog", which settles over the Langhe hills during the late harvest season — though it may instead refer to the powdery bloom on the ripe berries.

Characteristics

Nebbiolo is one of the first varieties to bud and the last to ripen, with harvest often in late October. The wines are famously deceptive: pale garnet in colour, yet intensely flavoured, highly tannic and acidic, requiring years of ageing to soften. Their signature aroma profile is memorably described as "tar and roses", developing notes of dried fruit, leather, truffle and herbs with bottle age.

History and regions

Nebbiolo was already esteemed in the 13th century and is deeply rooted in the Langhe, where Barolo and Barbaresco — separated by just a few kilometres — express subtly different styles. Outside Piedmont it is grown in Lombardy's Valtellina (where it is called Chiavennasca), in Sardinia, and in small plantings in California, Australia, Mexico and elsewhere, though it rarely matches its Piedmontese quality elsewhere, being notably sensitive to site (terroir).

Synonyms and clones

Regional clones and synonyms include Lampia, Michet and Rosé in the Langhe, Picotendro in the Aosta Valley and Chiavennasca in Valtellina. DNA profiling has identified Nebbiolo as a parent or grandparent of several Piedmontese varieties including Freisa and Vespolina.
