Wright–Fisher model

The Wright–Fisher model is a foundational model in population genetics describing how allele frequencies change over time due to genetic drift. Named after Sewall Wright and Ronald Fisher, who developed it in the early 1930s, the model considers a population of constant size with non-overlapping generations.

Assumptions and formulation

In the model, a population of N diploid individuals (2N gene copies) reproduces by random sampling: each gene copy in the next generation is drawn independently, with replacement, from the gene copies of the current generation. If an allele has frequency p in the current generation, the number of copies in the next generation follows a binomial distribution with parameters 2N and p. The model assumes no selection, mutation, or migration, so change is purely stochastic.

Consequences

Under the Wright–Fisher model, allele frequencies perform a random walk with absorbing boundaries: eventually every allele is either fixed (frequency 1) or lost (frequency 0), and the probability of fixation equals its current frequency. The variance in allele frequency change per generation is p(1 − p)/(2N), so drift is stronger in small populations. The model underlies the coalescent theory of Kingman, which traces gene copies back to common ancestors.

Related models

The Moran model is a continuous-time alternative with overlapping generations; in the diffusion limit both models agree up to a scaling of time.
