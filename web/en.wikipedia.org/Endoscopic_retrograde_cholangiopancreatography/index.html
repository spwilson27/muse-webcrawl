Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography

Endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography (ERCP) is a technique that combines the use of endoscopy and fluoroscopy to diagnose and treat certain problems of the biliary or pancreatic ductal systems. It is primarily carried out by highly skilled and specialty-trained gastroenterologists. Through the endoscope the physician can see the inside of the stomach and duodenum, and inject a contrast medium into the ducts in the biliary tree and the pancreas so that they can be seen under fluoroscopy.

Procedure

The patient is sedated and a side-viewing duodenoscope is passed through the mouth to the duodenum. The ampulla of Vater — the opening of the common bile duct and pancreatic duct — is cannulated, contrast is injected, and X-ray images are taken. Interventions can include sphincterotomy (cutting the sphincter), extraction of gallstones with baskets or balloons, dilation of strictures, placement of plastic or metal stents to drain obstructed ducts, and tissue sampling.

Indications

ERCP is used mainly therapeutically: for obstructive jaundice due to bile-duct stones or tumours, acute cholangitis, bile leaks after surgery, pancreatic duct stones and strictures in chronic pancreatitis, and palliation of malignant biliary obstruction. Purely diagnostic use has declined since magnetic resonance cholangiopancreatography (MRCP) and endoscopic ultrasound can image the ducts non-invasively with less risk.

Complications

The most common serious complication is post-ERCP pancreatitis, occurring in roughly 3–10% of cases; others include bleeding after sphincterotomy, duodenal perforation, cholangitis and cardiopulmonary events from sedation. Prevention strategies include rectal indomethacin, guidewire cannulation technique and prophylactic pancreatic stents in high-risk patients.
