Apulia (Italian: Puglia) is a region of southern Italy forming the "heel" of the Italian peninsula, bordered by the Adriatic Sea to the east, the Ionian Sea to the southeast, and the regions of Molise, Campania, and Basilicata. Its capital is Bari. With about 800 km of coastline, it is one of Italy's flattest and most agricultural regions.

Geography and history

The region includes the Gargano promontory (the "spur"), the fertile Tavoliere plain, the limestone Murge plateau, and the Salento peninsula. In antiquity it was settled by the Iapygians and Greek colonists (Magna Graecia—Tarentum, modern Taranto, was founded by Sparta), then ruled by Rome, Byzantines, Lombards, Normans, and the Kingdom of Naples. The Holy Roman Emperor Frederick II built notable castles here, including the octagonal Castel del Monte, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

Economy and culture

Apulia is Italy's leading producer of olive oil and a major producer of wine, durum wheat, and tomatoes. Landmarks include the trulli of Alberobello (UNESCO), the baroque city of Lecce ("the Florence of the South"), the pilgrimage sites of San Giovanni Rotondo (Padre Pio) and Monte Sant'Angelo, and the cliffside town of Polignano a Mare. Regional cuisine features orecchiette pasta, burrata cheese, focaccia barese, and Primitivo and Negroamaro wines. Tourism has grown rapidly in the 21st century, making the region one of Italy's most visited.
