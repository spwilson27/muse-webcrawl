Cook Islands Māori

Cook Islands Māori (also called Te reo Māori Kūki 'Āirani or Rarotongan) is an Eastern Polynesian language and, together with English, an official language of the Cook Islands. It is closely related to Tahitian and to New Zealand Māori, and its dialects across the archipelago — Rarotongan proper and the varieties of Aitutaki, Mangaia, Atiu and the other islands — are largely mutually intelligible, though the dialects of the northern group, such as Penrhyn (Tongareva), diverge more strongly.

Classification and dialects

Cook Islands Māori belongs to the Tahitic branch of the Polynesian languages. The speech of each island traditionally forms its own dialect; the dialect of Rarotonga, seat of government and commerce, functions as a de facto standard. Some northern varieties, including those of Manihiki and Rakahanga and of Pukapuka (which belongs to the Samoic branch and is sometimes treated as a separate language), differ considerably.

Status and use

The Te Reo Maori Act 2003 declared Cook Islands Māori an official language of the Cook Islands. In practice, English dominates government, commerce and secondary education, and the language is considered vulnerable: many Cook Islanders live in New Zealand and Australia, where intergenerational transmission is weaker. Revitalisation efforts include Māori-language broadcasting, dictionaries, school curricula and an annual language week.

Grammar and phonology

Like other Polynesian languages, Cook Islands Māori has a small consonant inventory (including a glottal stop written with an apostrophe or macron-marked long vowels), open syllables, and a verb–subject–object basic word order. It distinguishes inclusive and exclusive first-person plural pronouns and singular, dual and plural number.
