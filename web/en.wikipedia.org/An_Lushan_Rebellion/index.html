An Lushan Rebellion

Date16 December 755 – 17 February 763
LocationNorthern China
ResultTang victory; dynasty severely weakened
BelligerentsTang dynasty vs. Yan dynasty
CommandersEmperor Xuanzong, Suzong, Daizong; Guo Ziyi, Li Guangbi vs. An Lushan, An Qingxu, Shi Siming, Shi Chaoyi

The An Lushan Rebellion (also known as the An–Shi Rebellion) was a devastating rebellion against the Tang dynasty of China that lasted from 755 to 763. It began when the general An Lushan, military governor of three northeastern frontier commands, declared himself emperor of a new Yan dynasty. The rebellion was among the deadliest wars in history and marked the turning point of the Tang dynasty from its golden age into long decline.

Background

An Lushan, of Sogdian and Turkic background, rose through the frontier armies to command roughly 160,000 troops. At court he enjoyed the favour of Emperor Xuanzong and the consort Yang Guifei, but clashed with the chancellor Yang Guozhong. Fearing arrest, he rebelled in December 755.

Course of the war

Rebel forces rapidly captured Luoyang and then, after the disastrous Tang defeat at Tong Pass, the capital Chang'an in 756. Xuanzong fled toward Sichuan; on the way his soldiers mutinied at Mawei station and forced the execution of Yang Guifei and Yang Guozhong. The crown prince succeeded as Emperor Suzong and, with Uyghur help, recaptured both capitals in 757. An Lushan was murdered by his own son An Qingxu in 757; the rebellion continued under Shi Siming and then his son Shi Chaoyi, whose suicide in 763 ended the war.

Consequences

Registered population in the census fell from about 53 million in 754 to about 17 million in 764 — partly from administrative collapse rather than deaths alone, but reflecting enormous loss of life and displacement. The Tang court survived but lost effective control of the northeast, where former rebel generals ruled as semi-independent military governors (jiedushi), a structural weakness that plagued the dynasty until its fall in 907.
