Queen ant

A queen ant (formally known as a gyne) is an adult, reproducing female ant in an ant colony; generally she is the mother of all the other ants in that colony. In most species the queen is distinctly larger than the workers, with a bulky thorax that housed the flight muscles of her winged youth and an enlarged abdomen (gaster) for egg production. Some ant species have more than one queen per colony (polygyny), while in others a single queen monopolises reproduction (monogyny).

Life cycle

Queens develop from fertilised eggs that receive abundant nourishment as larvae. As virgin queens (alates) they leave the nest on a nuptial flight, mate with males in the air, and store sperm in a specialised organ, the spermatheca, sufficient to fertilise eggs for the rest of their lives. A newly mated queen lands, sheds her wings, and digs a founding chamber, raising her first brood of workers on reserves from her own body, metabolising her now-useless flight muscles.

Once the first workers emerge, the queen's sole task becomes egg laying. Ant queens are among the longest-lived insects known: queens of the black garden ant Lasius niger have lived nearly 29 years in captivity, and queens are fed and groomed by workers and protected deep in the nest. If a colony's queen dies, the colony typically declines and dies with her, though some species can rear replacement queens or adopt new ones.
