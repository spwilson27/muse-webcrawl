Sephardi Jews

Sephardi Jews (also Sephardic Jews, Sephardim) are a Jewish diaspora population associated with the Iberian Peninsula. The term derives from Sepharad, a biblical name applied in the Middle Ages to Spain. In the strict sense, Sephardim are the descendants of Jews who lived in Spain and Portugal before their expulsion in 1492 and 1497; in broader modern usage, especially in Israel, the term may also cover Jews of Middle Eastern and North African background who follow Sephardic religious customs.

History

Jewish communities existed in Iberia from Roman times and flourished under Muslim rule in al-Andalus, producing a golden age of philosophy, poetry, and science associated with figures such as Maimonides (who later lived in Egypt), Judah Halevi, and Solomon ibn Gabirol. Conditions deteriorated under Christian kingdoms: the massacres and forced conversions of 1391 created a large class of conversos, and in 1492 the Catholic Monarchs issued the Alhambra Decree expelling practising Jews from Spain; Portugal followed in 1497.

Diaspora

The exiles scattered across North Africa, Italy, the Netherlands, and especially the Ottoman Empire, where Sultan Bayezid II welcomed them; Salonica (Thessaloniki), Istanbul, Safed, and Smyrna became great Sephardic centres. In Safed, Joseph Karo compiled the Shulchan Aruch, the authoritative code of Jewish law. Western Sephardim — often former conversos who returned to Judaism — founded communities in Amsterdam, Hamburg, London, Bordeaux, and the Americas, including the first Jewish congregation in North America, Shearith Israel in New Amsterdam (1654).

Culture and language

The traditional language of most Sephardim is Judeo-Spanish, called Ladino or Judaeo-Spanish, a Romance language derived from Old Spanish with Hebrew, Turkish, Greek, and other admixtures; in northwestern Morocco the variant Haketia developed. Sephardic religious practice has its own liturgical rite, pronunciation traditions, melodies, and customs, and Sephardic cuisine reflects Iberian, Ottoman, and Mediterranean influences. The Holocaust destroyed the great Greek communities, notably Salonica; after 1948 most Sephardim of the Middle East and North Africa migrated to Israel, France, and the Americas.
