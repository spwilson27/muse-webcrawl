The history of Liberia is distinctive in Africa: the region was settled from 1822 by freed African Americans and Afro-Caribbeans under the auspices of the American Colonization Society. These Americo-Liberian settlers declared the Republic of Liberia independent on 26 July 1847, modelling its constitution and flag on those of the United States; its capital, Monrovia, was named after U.S. President James Monroe.

Americo-Liberian rule

For more than a century the settler minority, organised in the True Whig Party, dominated the Indigenous peoples of the interior. William Tubman's presidency (1944–1971) brought economic growth under an "Open Door" policy and gradual political unification. Resentment at inequality culminated in the 1980 coup led by Samuel Doe, which ended Americo-Liberian rule and began a decade of increasingly authoritarian government.

Civil wars and recovery

In 1989 Charles Taylor's NPFL launched a rebellion that plunged Liberia into civil war (1989–1997), followed by a second war (1999–2003). The conflicts killed an estimated 250,000 people and devastated the economy. A peace agreement in 2003 led to UN peacekeeping and the 2005 election of Ellen Johnson Sirleaf, Africa's first elected female head of state. Liberia later faced the Ebola epidemic of 2014–2016 and continues its post-war reconstruction.
