United States district court

The United States district courts are the trial courts of the U.S. federal judiciary. There is one district court for each federal judicial district, which each covers one U.S. state or, in some cases, a portion of a state. Each district includes a U.S. bankruptcy court as a unit. There are 94 federal judicial districts, including at least one district in each state, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico.

District courts are the general trial courts of the federal system.

Jurisdiction

The district courts have original jurisdiction over most federal civil and criminal cases, including cases arising under the Constitution and federal statutes (federal question jurisdiction) and suits between citizens of different states where the amount in controversy exceeds $75,000 (diversity jurisdiction). In criminal cases, a grand jury issues indictments, and trials are conducted with a jury drawn from the district. Judges are appointed by the president, confirmed by the Senate, and serve for life under Article III. Magistrate judges and bankruptcy judges assist the district judges with pretrial matters and specialized proceedings.

Structure

Every state has at least one district; populous states such as California, New York, and Texas have four. Districts may be divided into divisions for administrative convenience. The District Court for the District of Columbia hears many cases involving federal agencies, while the Southern District of New York is notable for major financial and terrorism prosecutions. Annual filings across all districts run to several hundred thousand civil and criminal cases.
