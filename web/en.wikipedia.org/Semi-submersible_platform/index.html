Semi-submersible platform

A semi-submersible platform is a specialised marine vessel used in a number of specific offshore roles including as offshore drilling rigs, safety vessels, oil production platforms, and heavy lift cranes. They are designed with good stability and seakeeping characteristics. The structure has pontoons and columns that are partially submerged when ballasted to operating draft, so that wave action on the structure is greatly reduced compared with a surface-piercing hull of similar displacement.

Design

A semi-submersible consists of a deck structure supported by columns on two or more large horizontal pontoons. In transit, the unit floats on the pontoons with a shallow draft and can be towed or, if self-propelled, move under its own power. On location, ballast water is pumped into the pontoons so the unit sinks until the pontoons are well below the surface and only the columns pierce the waterplane. Because the waterplane area is small, wave forces are modest and motions are gentle, making the design well suited to drilling in rough seas.

History

The semi-submersible concept emerged in the early 1960s; the first purpose-built drilling semi-submersible, Ocean Driller, was launched in 1963. The design proved itself in the harsh North Sea environment in the 1970s, and generations of rigs have grown progressively larger and capable of working in ultra-deep water. Well-known units include the Deepwater Horizon, whose loss in the 2010 Gulf of Mexico oil spill was a major industrial disaster.

Applications

Beyond drilling, semi-submersibles serve as floating production systems (with topside processing plant and storage or export via pipeline or shuttle tanker), as heavy-lift crane vessels such as Saipem 7000 and Thialf, as floatels (flotels) providing offshore accommodation, and as heavy transport vessels like the Blue Marlin, which can submerge its deck to float on cargo such as other ships and rigs.

Stability and station-keeping

Stability is governed by the separation of the columns and the ballasting sequence; semi-submersibles are notably stable but must be ballasted carefully during the transition between drafts. On station, rigs are held in place either by a spread of anchors and mooring lines or by dynamic positioning systems using computer-controlled thrusters.
