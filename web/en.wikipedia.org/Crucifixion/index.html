Crucifixion

Crucifixion is a method of capital punishment in which the victim is tied or nailed to a large wooden cross, or cross-shaped structure, and left to hang until eventual death, usually from exhaustion and asphyxiation. It was used by the Persians, Carthaginians and Macedonians and most notoriously by the Roman Republic and Empire, which reserved it principally for slaves, pirates and enemies of the state.

Practice in Rome

The condemned typically carried the horizontal beam (the patibulum) to the place of execution, where it was fixed to an upright stake. Death could take hours or days; guards sometimes hastened it by breaking the victim's legs. Because it was considered the most shameful of deaths, Roman citizens were normally exempt. Mass crucifixions punctuated Rome's wars: after the slave revolt led by Spartacus in 71 BC, some 6,000 captured slaves were crucified along the Appian Way.

Crucifixion of Jesus

The crucifixion of Jesus of Nazareth under Pontius Pilate in Judaea (c. AD 30/33) is central to Christian theology, in which the cross became the principal symbol of the faith. Constantine the Great abolished crucifixion in the Roman Empire in the fourth century out of veneration for it.

Later use

Forms of crucifixion were used sporadically in later centuries, including in Japan during the persecution of Christians, and have been reported in a few modern conflicts and judicial systems, though capital crucifixion survives in law in only a handful of states.
