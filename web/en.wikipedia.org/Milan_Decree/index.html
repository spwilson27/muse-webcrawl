Milan Decree

Date17 December 1807
Issued byNapoleon Bonaparte
ContextContinental System, Napoleonic Wars

The Milan Decree was a decree issued by Napoleon I on 17 December 1807 from Milan, in the Kingdom of Italy, as part of his Continental System of economic warfare against the United Kingdom. It extended and sharpened the earlier Berlin Decree of 1806 and was issued in response to Britain's Orders in Council.

Provisions

The decree declared that any vessel that had submitted to British search, paid British duties, or sailed to or from a British port was to be treated as British property and liable to seizure — in effect "denationalised". It also provided that no ship coming from England or English colonies was to be received in any port under French control. Together with the Berlin Decree, it aimed to close all continental ports to British trade and any neutral trade that passed through Britain.

Consequences

The decree fell heavily on neutral shipping, particularly that of the United States, whose vessels could be confiscated by France for trading with Britain or by Britain under its Orders in Council for trading with France. The resulting commercial distress contributed to American grievances in the run-up to the War of 1812. The Continental System also distorted European economies, encouraged smuggling, and pushed Russia to defect from the system — one of the causes of Napoleon's invasion of Russia in 1812. (The Milan Decree should not be confused with the Edict of Milan of 313 AD, which legalised Christianity in the Roman Empire.)
