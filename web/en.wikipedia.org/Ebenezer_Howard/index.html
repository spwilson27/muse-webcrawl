Sir Ebenezer Howard (29 January 1850 – 1 May 1928) was an English urban planner and founder of the garden city movement. His book To-morrow: A Peaceful Path to Real Reform (1898), reissued as Garden Cities of To-morrow (1902), proposed self-contained satellite towns combining the advantages of town and country — an idea illustrated by his famous "Three Magnets" diagram.

Life

Howard was born in the City of London and went to work as a clerk. In 1871 he emigrated to America, farming briefly in Nebraska and then working in Chicago as a shorthand reporter — the trade, parliamentary and court reporting, that supported him for most of his life. He returned to London in 1876 and read widely in social reform literature, including Edward Bellamy's Looking Backward. He was also an Esperantist and a stenographer who invented a shorthand typewriter.

The garden city idea

Howard proposed planned towns of about 32,000 people on roughly 6,000 acres, the land held in trust for the community so that rising land values funded public services. Each garden city would be surrounded by a permanent agricultural green belt; when it reached its limit, a new city would be founded beyond the belt, the cluster linked by rail as a "social city". Industry, housing, gardens and civic institutions would be balanced within walking distance.

Implementation

The Garden City Association (1899) promoted the idea, and development began at Letchworth Garden City in Hertfordshire in 1903, designed by Barry Parker and Raymond Unwin. After the First World War Howard bought land for a second town, Welwyn Garden City (1919), where he lived until his death. He was knighted in 1927.

Legacy

Howard's ideas influenced town planning worldwide — from the British New Towns programme after 1946 to garden suburbs, Canberra, and modern debates about green belts and sustainable urbanism.
