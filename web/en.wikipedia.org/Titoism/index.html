Titoism is a socialist political philosophy most closely associated with Josip Broz Tito and the policies of the Socialist Federal Republic of Yugoslavia. It is characterised by policies of workers' self-management, a foreign policy of non-alignment in the Cold War, a comparatively decentralised federal structure, and a degree of market socialism distinct from the Soviet model of central planning. The term is typically used to describe Yugoslav socialism, which developed after the Tito–Stalin split of 1948 placed Yugoslavia outside the Soviet sphere of influence.

Origins

During the Second World War, the Yugoslav Partisans under Tito liberated much of the country with comparatively little direct Soviet assistance, giving the Yugoslav leadership an independent power base. After 1945, Yugoslavia initially modelled itself on the Soviet Union, but tensions over economic policy, foreign policy in the Balkans and Greece, and Tito's ambitions for regional leadership culminated in the Cominform resolution of June 1948, which expelled the Communist Party of Yugoslavia and accused its leadership of nationalist deviation. Yugoslavia refused to submit, and the ensuing split forced the Yugoslav leadership to chart an independent course.

Workers' self-management

The centrepiece of Titoism was the system of workers' self-management (samoupravljanje), introduced by law in 1950 with the slogan "factories to the workers". State enterprises were formally handed over to their workforces, who elected workers' councils with authority over production plans, pricing, investment and the distribution of income. In practice, the League of Communists retained political control, but self-management became the ideological cornerstone distinguishing Yugoslav socialism from Soviet state bureaucracy. Edvard Kardelj was the system's principal theorist.

Market socialism and decentralisation

Yugoslavia progressively introduced market mechanisms, allowing enterprises to trade with one another and, increasingly, with the West. Economic reforms in 1965 expanded the role of prices and profitability, and Yugoslavs enjoyed greater freedom of travel and consumer choice than citizens of the Warsaw Pact states. The federation itself was decentralised through successive constitutions, culminating in the 1974 constitution, which granted extensive autonomy to the republics and to the autonomous provinces of Kosovo and Vojvodina.

Non-alignment

Abroad, Titoism meant refusal to join either Cold War bloc. Tito was a co-founder of the Non-Aligned Movement, formalised at the Belgrade Conference of 1961 alongside Jawaharlal Nehru of India, Gamal Abdel Nasser of Egypt, Kwame Nkrumah of Ghana and Sukarno of Indonesia. Yugoslavia maintained relations with both East and West, receiving Western economic and military aid after 1948 while remaining a one-party socialist state.

National question

Domestically, Titoism promoted "brotherhood and unity" (bratstvo i jedinstvo) among the Yugoslav peoples, suppressing nationalist movements such as the Croatian Spring of 1971 while formally recognising the equality of the federation's nations. Political dissent was policed by the UDBA security service, and dissidents such as Milovan Djilas — once Tito's close associate, later a critic of the party bureaucracy — were imprisoned or marginalised.

Legacy

After Tito's death in 1980, the system he built came under mounting strain from foreign debt, inflation and republic-level nationalism, and Yugoslavia disintegrated in the wars of the 1990s. Titoism is remembered both for Yugoslavia's relative openness and prosperity and for its dependence on Tito's personal authority and on the League of Communists' monopoly of power.
