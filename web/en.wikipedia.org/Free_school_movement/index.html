Free school movement

The free school movement is an American and British educational movement of the 1960s and 1970s in which parents, teachers, and students founded independent alternative schools outside the public system, emphasizing student freedom, democratic participation, and learning driven by interest rather than compulsory curricula. It drew on earlier experiments such as A. S. Neill's Summerhill School (founded 1921) and the ideas of writers including Paul Goodman, John Holt, and Jonathan Kozol, whose books criticized conventional schooling.

Characteristics

Free schools typically rejected grades, compulsory classes, and hierarchical authority; students often had an equal voice with staff in school meetings, and attendance at lessons was voluntary. Hundreds of such schools were founded in the United States, many within the counterculture of the era, and a smaller number in Britain, including the Liverpool Free School and Scotland's and London's community schools. In the United States the movement overlapped with the desegregation-era "freedom schools" of the civil rights movement, though those had a distinct history and purpose.

Legacy

Most free schools were short-lived, undermined by funding difficulties, internal conflict, and accreditation problems, but the movement influenced alternative education broadly — including democratic schools such as Sudbury Valley School (founded 1968), home schooling advocacy, and later free-school movements in other countries, including the state-funded "free schools" programme introduced in England in 2010, which borrowed the name but not the philosophy.
