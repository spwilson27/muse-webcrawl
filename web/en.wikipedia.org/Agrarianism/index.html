Agrarianism

Agrarianism is a political and social philosophy that regards the rural, agricultural way of life as superior to urban industrial life, valuing the independent family farmer as the ideal citizen and the moral backbone of society. It has appeared in forms ranging from the ideas of the ancient world to Thomas Jefferson's vision of a republic of yeoman farmers, and to 20th-century movements such as the Southern Agrarians and distributism.

Ideas

Agrarian thought typically holds that farming cultivates virtues — independence, self-reliance, attachment to place, piety, and community — that wage labor and finance erode. It advocates wide distribution of land ownership, skepticism of industrial capitalism and banking, and local self-sufficiency. Jefferson wrote in Notes on the State of Virginia (1785) that "those who labour in the earth are the chosen people of God." In the 1930s, the Southern Agrarians' manifesto I'll Take My Stand (1930) defended the agrarian South against industrialism; G. K. Chesterton and Hilaire Belloc's distributism made similar arguments in Britain.

Agrarian parties

Agrarian parties were powerful in interwar Europe — in Scandinavia (Centre Parties), Poland, Bulgaria under Aleksandar Stamboliyski, and elsewhere — representing peasant interests against both capital and organized labor. Populist movements such as the American People's Party of the 1890s also drew heavily on agrarian grievances.
