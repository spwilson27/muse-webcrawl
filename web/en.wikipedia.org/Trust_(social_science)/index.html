Trust (social science)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Trust is a central concept in the social sciences: it is the belief that another person or institution will act as expected, even when one cannot monitor or control them, and a willingness to accept vulnerability on that basis. Trust enables cooperation, exchange, and collective action, and is treated by many scholars as a core element of social capital.

Dimensions and study

Researchers distinguish interpersonal trust in specific others, generalised trust in strangers, and institutional trust in organisations such as courts, banks, or governments. Surveys such as the World Values Survey ask the classic question of whether "most people can be trusted," and cross-country comparisons link high generalised trust with lower corruption, stronger economies, and better health. Robert Putnam's work popularised the association between trust, civic engagement, and institutional performance.

Trust and cooperation

In game theory, trust is studied through experiments such as the trust game, in which a sender risks money hoping a receiver will reciprocate. Trust develops through repeated interaction, reputation, and shared norms, and can be destroyed by betrayal, sometimes with lasting effects. In organisations and economics, trust reduces transaction costs by making contracts and monitoring less necessary; in digital systems, mechanisms such as ratings and escrow attempt to engineer trust between strangers.
