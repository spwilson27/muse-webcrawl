In philosophy, identity is the relation each thing bears only to itself. The notion of identity gives rise to many philosophical problems, including the identity of indiscernibles (if x and y share all their properties, are they one and the same thing?), the problem of change (how can a thing change yet remain the same thing?), and the problem of personal identity over time. Identity contrasts with qualitative similarity: two twins may be exactly alike yet numerically two.

Principles of identity

Two principles associated with Leibniz govern much discussion. The indiscernibility of identicals states that if a is identical to b, then every property of a is a property of b and vice versa; it is widely accepted and underlies substitution of identicals in logic. The converse, the identity of indiscernibles, states that if a and b share absolutely every property, then they are identical; this is controversial — Max Black's example of a universe containing exactly two perfectly symmetric spheres is a famous proposed counterexample.

Identity over time

Diachronic identity asks what makes something at one time the same thing as something at another time. The ancient example is the Ship of Theseus: if every plank is gradually replaced, is it still the same ship? Theorists divide between endurantists (objects are wholly present at each time) and perdurantists (objects are four-dimensional "worms" extended through time). For persons, candidates for persistence include bodily continuity (animalism) and psychological continuity of memory and character (Locke, Parfit); Derek Parfit argued that identity is "not what matters" in survival, defending instead Relation R.

Relative and contingent identity

Peter Geach and others defended relative identity — "the same F as" without absolute identity — though most logicians treat identity as absolute. Saul Kripke argued that true identity statements between rigid designators (such as "Hesperus is Phosphorus") are necessarily true though knowable only a posteriori. Puzzles like the statue and the lump of clay motivate theories of contingent identity and of constitution as distinct from identity.
