Quercus suber

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Quercus suber, commonly called the cork oak, is a medium-sized evergreen oak of the family Fagaceae, native to southwest Europe and northwest Africa. It is the primary source of cork for wine bottle stoppers and many other products. The tree is distinctive for its very thick, rugged bark, which is rich in suberin and forms the cork layer.

Cork harvesting

Cork is stripped from the trunk by hand, a skilled process that does not kill the tree: the bark regenerates, and a tree is harvested roughly every nine to twelve years, beginning at about 25 years of age, and can live and produce for 150–250 years. Portugal produces about half of the world's cork, largely from the managed montado landscapes of the Alentejo, which combine cork oak stands with grazing and support notable biodiversity.

Ecology

The cork oak is adapted to the Mediterranean climate: evergreen leathery leaves limit water loss, and the insulating bark protects the tree against wildfire, allowing rapid resprouting. Its acorns feed free-ranging Iberian pigs, whose acorn-finished ham (jamón ibérico de bellota) is a celebrated product of the dehesa/montado system. Threats include the root pathogen Phytophthora cinnamomi and shifting wine-industry demand for alternative closures.
