Marine protected area

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

No-take marine reserves protect ecosystems and allow fish populations to recover.

A marine protected area (MPA) is a protected area of a sea, ocean, estuary, or (in the United States) the Great Lakes. These areas restrict human activity for conservation purposes, typically to protect natural or cultural resources. The IUCN defines an MPA as a clearly defined geographical space, recognised, dedicated and managed, through legal or other effective means, to achieve the long-term conservation of nature. MPAs range from multiple-use areas that allow regulated fishing to strictly protected no-take zones where all extraction is banned.

Benefits

Well-enforced no-take reserves consistently show higher biomass, density, and average size of exploited species than surrounding fished areas, and can export larvae and adult "spillover" to adjacent fisheries. MPAs also serve as scientific reference sites, protect spawning aggregations and nursery habitat, support tourism, and contribute to ecosystem resilience against climate change.

Coverage and targets

As of the mid-2020s, roughly 8% of the world's ocean falls within designated MPAs, but only about 3% is in fully or highly protected no-take areas. Under the Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework (2022) and the "30 by 30" campaign, countries pledged to protect 30% of the ocean by 2030. The 2023 UN High Seas Treaty (BBNJ Agreement) created a mechanism to establish MPAs in international waters for the first time.

Notable MPAs

  • Papahānaumokuākea Marine National Monument (Hawaii, USA) — about 1.5 million km², among the largest protected areas on Earth
  • Ross Sea region MPA (Antarctica) — established 2016 by CCAMLR, about 1.55 million km²
  • Great Barrier Reef Marine Park (Australia) — about 344,000 km², zoned for multiple uses with no-take zones
  • Galápagos Marine Reserve (Ecuador) — about 133,000 km², later expanded
  • Phoenix Islands Protected Area (Kiribati) — about 408,000 km²

Challenges

Many MPAs are criticised as "paper parks" with inadequate funding, staffing, and enforcement. Effectiveness depends on size, age, isolation, enforcement, and no-take status. Managing MPAs also involves balancing conservation with the livelihoods of fishing communities.
