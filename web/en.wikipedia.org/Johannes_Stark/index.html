Johannes Stark

Johannes Stark (15 April 1874 – 21 June 1957) was a German physicist who won the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1919 "for his discovery of the Doppler effect in canal rays and the splitting of spectral lines in electric fields" — the latter phenomenon being known as the Stark effect.


Stark, 1919

Scientific career

Stark held chairs at Hanover, Aachen, Greifswald and Würzburg. In 1913 he demonstrated that a strong electric field splits the spectral lines of hydrogen (the Stark effect), an important verification in the development of quantum theory, and he had earlier observed the Doppler effect in canal rays (1905). He was a prolific editor and author on atomic physics.

Deutsche Physik

Under the Nazi regime, Stark became a leading figure, with Philipp Lenard, of the antisemitic "Deutsche Physik" ("German Physics") movement, which denounced relativity and quantum mechanics as "Jewish physics" and attacked Werner Heisenberg and others. Stark presided over the Physikalisch-Technische Reichsanstalt (1933–1939) and sought control of German science funding, though he gradually lost influence in internal party struggles.

Later life

After the war, a denazification court in 1947 classified Stark as a "major offender" and sentenced him to four years in a labour camp, though the sentence was later suspended. He died in Bavaria in 1957.
