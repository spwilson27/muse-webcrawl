An entail, or fee tail, is an estate in land in English common law that restricts the inheritance of the property to a particular line of descendants, typically the heirs of the body of the original grantee. The holder of an entailed estate (the tenant in tail) could enjoy the land for life but could not sell or devise the fee simple, so that great estates could be kept intact within a family across generations.

History

The entail was created by the statute De Donis Conditionalibus (1285), part of the Statute of Westminster II, which gave effect to a donor's intention that land descend to the grantee's issue and revert if the line failed. Landowners and lawyers soon devised ways to "bar" entails — notably the fictitious lawsuit known as the common recovery, sanctioned in Taltarum's Case (1472) — and the strict settlement of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries re-created the practical effect for successive generations through conveyancing.

Abolition and survival

Entails were widely criticised for removing land from commerce and concentrating wealth. Most American states abolished the fee tail in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, often converting it into a fee simple or a life estate followed by a remainder in fee. In England and Wales, no new entails could be created after the Law of Property Act 1925 restricted them to equitable interests, and the Trusts of Land and Appointment of Trustees Act 1996 ended their creation altogether. Analogous institutions include the Scottish tailzie and the Spanish mayorazgo.

In culture

The entail is a famous plot device in English literature: in Jane Austen's Pride and Prejudice, the Bennet estate is entailed away from the daughters to a male cousin, and in the television drama Downton Abbey the entail drives the opening storyline.
