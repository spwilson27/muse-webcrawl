Hydrocracking

Hydrocracking is a catalytic chemical process used in petroleum refineries to convert high-boiling, heavy hydrocarbon fractions — such as vacuum gas oil — into more valuable lower-boiling products such as gasoline, kerosene (jet fuel), diesel, and LPG. It combines catalytic cracking with hydrogenation: feedstock reacts with hydrogen at high pressure (typically 100–200 bar) and moderate-to-high temperature over a bifunctional catalyst, breaking large molecules while saturating them with hydrogen.

Process

Hydrocracking catalysts combine an acidic support (often amorphous silica-alumina or zeolite Y) that promotes carbon–carbon bond scission with metal sites (nickel–molybdenum, nickel–tungsten, cobalt–molybdenum, or noble metals such as palladium and platinum) that promote hydrogenation and help remove sulfur and nitrogen as hydrogen sulfide and ammonia. Units may be single-stage or two-stage, with interstage separation; the process is strongly exothermic and highly flexible — refiners can swing output between jet fuel, diesel, and naphtha by adjusting severity and catalyst choice.

Advantages

Compared with fluid catalytic cracking, hydrocracking produces low-sulfur, high-quality middle distillates and feeds low in contaminants, and can process heavy, sour, or aromatic feeds that would poison other units. Its principal drawbacks are high capital cost and large hydrogen consumption, usually supplied by steam reforming of natural gas.
