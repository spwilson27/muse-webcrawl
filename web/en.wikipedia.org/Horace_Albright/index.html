Horace Marden Albright (January 6, 1890 – September 28, 1987) was an American conservationist and administrator who, together with Stephen Mather, helped found the National Park Service and served as its second director from 1929 to 1933.

Early life and career

Albright was born in Bishop, California, and graduated from the University of California, Berkeley, in 1912. He went to Washington, D.C., to study law and became an assistant to Secretary of the Interior Franklin K. Lane. In 1915 he began working with Stephen Mather on the campaign to create a separate parks bureau, realized with the National Park Service Organic Act of 1916.

National Park Service

Albright served as assistant director under Mather and as superintendent of Yellowstone National Park from 1919 to 1929, effectively running the Service during Mather's illnesses. When Mather retired in 1929, Albright became director. His tenure emphasized historical preservation: in 1933 he engineered the transfer of military parks, monuments and the parks of Washington, D.C., to the National Park Service, greatly broadening its scope.

Later life

Albright resigned in 1933 to work for the United States Potash Company, but remained an influential voice in conservation for the rest of his long life. He received the Presidential Medal of Freedom in 1980, and the Albright Training Center at Grand Canyon National Park is named in his honor.
