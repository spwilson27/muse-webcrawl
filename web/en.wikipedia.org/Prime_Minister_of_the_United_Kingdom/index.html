Prime Minister of the United Kingdom

The prime minister of the United Kingdom is the head of government of the United Kingdom. The prime minister advises the sovereign on the exercise of much of the royal prerogative, chairs the Cabinet, and selects its ministers. As modern prime ministers hold office by virtue of their ability to command the confidence of the House of Commons, they sit as members of Parliament.

History

The office of prime minister is not established by any statute or constitutional document but exists only through long-established convention. Robert Walpole, First Lord of the Treasury from 1721 to 1742, is generally regarded as the first prime minister, although the title was not formally used at the time. Over the 18th and 19th centuries the office gradually acquired its modern form as the Cabinet system and party politics developed.

Authority and role

The prime minister is head of government: they chair the Cabinet, appoint and dismiss ministers, coordinate the work of government departments, and are responsible for civil service organisation. Many executive powers are legally vested in the monarch or ministers, exercised by convention on the prime minister's advice. The prime minister also appoints senior judges, senior civil servants, and members of many public bodies, and recommends honours.

Selection

The monarch appoints as prime minister the person most likely to command the confidence of the House of Commons — in practice, the leader of the party or coalition with a majority of seats. If no party holds a majority, or a prime minister resigns mid-term, the governing party typically selects a new leader who then becomes prime minister without a general election.

Residence

The prime minister's official residence is 10 Downing Street in London, with Chequers in Buckinghamshire serving as the country residence.
