Rialto Bridge

The Rialto Bridge (Italian: Ponte di Rialto; Venetian: Ponte de Rialto) is the oldest of the four bridges spanning the Grand Canal in Venice, Italy. Connecting the sestieri of San Marco and San Polo, it has been rebuilt several times since its first construction as a pontoon bridge in 1173, and is one of the architectural icons of Venice.

History

The first crossing at the Rialto — Venice's commercial heart — was a pontoon bridge built in 1173. It was replaced by a wooden bridge with a movable central section for tall ships; this structure, lined with shops, appears in Carpaccio's painting of 1494. The wooden bridge partially collapsed under the weight of a crowd in 1444 and burned in the revolt of 1514, and was rebuilt in wood again.

The stone bridge

Plans for a stone bridge were debated for decades; designs were submitted by famous architects including Michelangelo, Palladio and Vignola. The contract was finally awarded in 1588 to Antonio da Ponte, whose daring single-arch design — resembling the rejected Palladio scheme — was completed in 1591. The bridge's engineering was considered so audacious that critics predicted its collapse; it has stood for over four centuries, though its arch bears the heavy load of two rows of shops along a central walkway divided by arcades.

Present day

The Rialto Bridge remains one of Venice's top tourist attractions, surrounded by the Rialto markets. It underwent an extensive restoration in 2015–2017, its first comprehensive renovation in over 400 years.
