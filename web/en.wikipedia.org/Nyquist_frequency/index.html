Nyquist frequency

The Nyquist frequency is one-half of the sampling rate of a discrete signal processing system. Named after the Swedish-American engineer Harry Nyquist, it is the highest frequency that can be unambiguously represented by samples taken at that rate. Signals containing energy above the Nyquist frequency appear at lower frequencies in the sampled data, an artefact called aliasing.

Sampling theorem

The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem states that a band-limited signal can be perfectly reconstructed from its samples if the sampling rate exceeds twice its highest frequency component. That minimum rate — twice the signal's own bandwidth — is called the Nyquist rate, and must not be confused with the Nyquist frequency, which is defined relative to a given sampling rate. To prevent aliasing, real systems filter out frequencies above the Nyquist frequency before sampling, using an anti-aliasing filter.

Example

Compact discs sample audio at 44.1 kHz, giving a Nyquist frequency of 22.05 kHz, just above the roughly 20 kHz limit of human hearing. Telephone systems sample at 8 kHz, so their Nyquist frequency of 4 kHz limits voiceband audio accordingly.

Related concepts

In multidimensional sampling — for images, for instance — the same idea applies spatially: insufficient spatial sampling produces moiré patterns, which are the two-dimensional analogue of aliasing.
