Media effects

Media effects refers to a field of study within communication science and media studies examining how media (particularly mass media such as television, film, radio, newspapers and digital media) influence or reinforce the behaviour, attitudes, beliefs and values of individuals and audiences. Research questions include the effects of violent content on aggression, the role of media in political campaigns and vote choice, media influence on body image and health behaviours, and the persuasive impact of advertising.

History

Early research swung between models of powerful, direct effects (the "hypodermic needle" model) and limited-effects findings that emphasised selective exposure and interpersonal influence (the two-step flow). From the 1970s, theories such as cultivation theory, agenda-setting, priming, framing and social learning theory restored attention to long-term and conditional effects.

Key theories

  • Agenda-setting: media prominence influences what issues audiences consider important.
  • Cultivation: heavy exposure shapes perceptions of social reality.
  • Social cognitive theory: audiences learn behaviours by observing models, including mediated ones.
  • Third-person effect: people believe others are more influenced by media than themselves.

Criticism

The field has been criticised for small effect sizes, laboratory artificiality, replication problems, and moral panics about new media; contemporary research increasingly studies interactive and algorithmic media environments.
