Cadherin

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cadherins (named for "calcium-dependent adhesion") are a class of type-1 transmembrane proteins that play important roles in cell adhesion, forming adherens junctions that bind cells within tissues together. Their adhesion is calcium-dependent: the extracellular portion of a cadherin molecule contains repeated cadherin domains that bind Ca2+ ions, rigidifying the structure and allowing it to bind a cadherin on a neighbouring cell, usually of the same type (homophilic binding).

Types

The classical cadherins include E-cadherin (epithelial), N-cadherin (neural) and P-cadherin (placental). Their cytoplasmic tails bind catenins (β-catenin and α-catenin), which link the junction to the actin cytoskeleton. The wider cadherin superfamily also includes desmosomal cadherins (desmogleins and desmocollins), protocadherins, and atypical members such as Fat and Dachsous. More than 100 cadherin-family members are encoded in the human genome.

Functions and disease

Cadherins are essential for embryonic development, tissue morphogenesis and the maintenance of epithelial integrity. Loss of E-cadherin function is a hallmark of the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and promotes cancer invasion and metastasis; inherited mutations in the E-cadherin gene CDH1 cause hereditary diffuse gastric cancer. Mutations in other cadherins cause conditions including Usher syndrome (CDH23) and various neurodevelopmental disorders.

Discovery

Cadherins were identified in the 1970s–80s through the work of Masatoshi Takeichi and colleagues, who showed that cells' calcium-dependent adhesion was mediated by specific surface glycoproteins, naming E-cadherin (initially called uvomorulin in parallel work) as the key epithelial adhesion molecule.
