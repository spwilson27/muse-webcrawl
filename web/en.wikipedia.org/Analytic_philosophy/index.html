Analytic philosophy

Analytic philosophy is a broad movement within Western philosophy, dominant in English-speaking countries since the 20th century, that emphasizes clarity of language, logical analysis, and argumentative rigor. Its roots lie in the work of Gottlob Frege, Bertrand Russell, G. E. Moore, and Ludwig Wittgenstein, who turned philosophy toward the analysis of language and concepts and away from the system-building of 19th-century idealism.

History

Frege's revolution in logic and Russell's theory of descriptions supplied the tools and the model of analysis. Wittgenstein's Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus (1921) inspired the logical positivists of the Vienna Circle, who held that meaningful statements are either analytic or empirically verifiable. After the Second World War the movement diversified: W. V. O. Quine challenged the analytic–synthetic distinction; ordinary language philosophy flourished at Oxford with J. L. Austin and Gilbert Ryle; and later figures such as Saul Kripke and David Lewis revived metaphysics within an analytic idiom.

Characteristics and topics

Analytic philosophers typically prize precision, explicit argument, and attention to language, and they work across philosophy of language, mind, science, logic, ethics, and metaphysics, often engaging closely with the natural sciences. The term is conventionally contrasted with continental philosophy, though the boundary is contested and both traditions have cross-fertilized.
