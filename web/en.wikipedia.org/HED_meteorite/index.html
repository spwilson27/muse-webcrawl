HED meteorite

HED meteorites are a clan of achondrite meteorites named for their three member groups: howardites, eucrites, and diogenites. Together they make up roughly five to six percent of all meteorite falls observed on Earth. They are igneous rocks — fragments of the crust and upper mantle of a differentiated asteroid — and are widely believed to originate from the asteroid 4 Vesta, one of the largest bodies in the asteroid belt.

Classification

Eucrites are basaltic lava rocks from the surface or shallow crust of the parent body. Diogenites are coarse-grained orthopyroxene-rich rocks that crystallized in deeper magma chambers. Howardites are breccias — mixtures of eucrite and diogenite fragments cemented together by impact processes, resembling the regolith of the parent asteroid.

Origin on Vesta

The link to Vesta rests on spectral reflectance: the surfaces of HED meteorites match telescopic observations of Vesta's basaltic surface, and a dynamical family of smaller "Vestoid" asteroids provides the delivery route to Earth-crossing orbits. NASA's Dawn spacecraft, which orbited Vesta in 2011–2012, confirmed the compositional match and identified the giant Rheasilvia impact basin near the south pole as the likely excavation site of most HED material.

Significance

Because they sample a differentiated planetesimal that formed within the first few million years of Solar System history, HED meteorites provide key evidence about early planetary melting, crust formation, and the chronology of the inner Solar System. Some eucrites have crystallization ages near 4.56 billion years, among the oldest dated igneous rocks known.
