Sweat gland

Image placeholder: anatomy of an eccrine sweat gland in the skin.

Sweat glands, also known as sudoriferous or sudoriparous glands, are small tubular structures of the skin that produce sweat. Sweat glands are a type of exocrine gland, which produce and secrete substances onto an epithelial surface by way of a duct.

Types

There are two main types of sweat gland. Eccrine glands are distributed over most of the body and open directly onto the surface of the skin; they are the major glands responsible for thermoregulatory sweating, producing a clear, mostly watery secretion containing salts. Apocrine glands are larger, develop during puberty, occur mainly in the armpits, groin and around the nipples, and secrete into hair follicles. Their secretion is milky and initially odourless; body odour arises when skin bacteria metabolise it.

Structure and function

A sweat gland consists of a coiled secretory tubule deep in the dermis and a duct that carries sweat to the skin surface. Humans have roughly two to four million eccrine glands. Evaporation of sweat from the skin surface is the body's principal means of dissipating heat, a function essential to endurance activity and survival in hot climates.

Clinical significance

Disorders of the sweat glands include hyperhidrosis (excessive sweating), anhidrosis (absent sweating), miliaria (heat rash, from blocked ducts) and bromhidrosis (foul-smelling sweat). See also sweat lodge for the ceremonial use of induced sweating.
