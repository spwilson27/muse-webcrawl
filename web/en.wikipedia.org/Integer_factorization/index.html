Integer factorization

In number theory, integer factorization is the decomposition of a positive integer into a product of smaller integers. If these factors are further restricted to be prime numbers, the process is called prime factorization. The fundamental theorem of arithmetic states that every positive integer greater than 1 has a unique prime factorization.

While multiplying large integers is computationally easy, factoring them appears to be hard: no efficient classical algorithm is known for factoring numbers with hundreds of digits. This asymmetry underlies the security of the widely used RSA cryptosystem.

A factor tree: every composite number decomposes uniquely into primes.

Complexity

Integer factorization is not known to be in the complexity class P, nor is it known to be NP-complete; it lies in both NP and co-NP. The fastest known classical method, the general number field sieve (GNFS), runs in sub-exponential time. Shor's algorithm, devised by Peter Shor in 1994, factors integers in polynomial time on a quantum computer, which would break RSA and related systems if a large, fault-tolerant quantum computer were built.

Algorithms

  • Trial division — testing divisibility by successive primes; feasible only for small factors.
  • Pollard's rho algorithm — a probabilistic method effective for factors of moderate size.
  • Quadratic sieve — long the fastest for numbers up to about 100 digits.
  • Elliptic curve method — its speed depends on the size of the smallest factor.
  • General number field sieve — the best known method for large numbers, used for all recent records.

Factoring records

The RSA Factoring Challenge (1991–2007) published semiprime challenge numbers. RSA-768 was factored in 2009, and in 2020 a team led by researchers including Fabrice Boudot and Nadia Heninger factored RSA-250 — a 250-digit number (829 bits) — using the number field sieve at a cost of roughly 2,700 core-years. Practical RSA keys of 2,048 bits remain far beyond classical reach.
