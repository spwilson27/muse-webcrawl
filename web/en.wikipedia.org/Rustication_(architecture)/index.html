Rustication (architecture)

Rusticated masonry on the Palazzo Medici in Florence.

Rustication is a masonry treatment in which the faces of stone blocks are deliberately left rough and protruding, with deeply cut, recessed joints, giving a wall a rugged, monumental appearance. The technique contrasts the rough block faces with the smooth, finished joints, and conveys an impression of strength and antiquity.

History

Rough-faced masonry was used in Roman construction, but rustication became a signature device of Italian Renaissance architecture. Fifteenth-century Florentine palaces such as the Palazzo Medici and Palazzo Strozzi stacked graduated forms — roughest at the ground floor, progressively smoother above — to suggest both fortification and refinement. Sebastiano Serlio's treatise codified several types, and the manner spread through Europe, appearing in country houses, civic buildings, and garden structures such as grottos.

Forms and use

Varieties include banded rustication, in which only the horizontal joints are emphasized, and vermiculated rustication, whose surfaces are carved with worm-like patterns. The technique was revived in Palladian and Baroque architecture and in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries in stone, brick, and even concrete; in wood-frame buildings, "rustic" siding imitates the effect. Rustication is most often applied to ground floors and basements to anchor a building visually to the earth.
