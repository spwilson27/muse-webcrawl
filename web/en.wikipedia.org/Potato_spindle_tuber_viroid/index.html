Potato spindle tuber viroid

Potato spindle tuber viroid (PSTVd)
FamilyPospiviroidae
GenusPospiviroid
GenomeCircular single-stranded RNA, ~359 nucleotides
HostsPotato, tomato, and other Solanaceae

The potato spindle tuber viroid (PSTVd) is a plant pathogen and the first viroid to be identified. Viroids are the smallest known infectious agents: PSTVd consists of a short, circular, single-stranded RNA molecule of about 359 nucleotides that encodes no proteins and replicates using the host plant's own enzymes.

Discovery

The "spindle tuber" disease of potatoes was recognised in the 1920s, but its cause long resisted identification because no virus particles could be found. In 1971 Theodor O. Diener demonstrated that the infectious agent was a free, low-molecular-weight RNA — a "viroid" — a discovery that opened a new field of plant pathology and molecular biology.

Symptoms and impact

Infected potato plants grow upright and stunted, with smaller, elongated, spindle-shaped tubers that may crack; yields can fall by 20–40% or more. In tomato, PSTVd causes the "bunchy top" disease, with stunting, leaf curling, and fruit distortion. The viroid spreads mainly through infected seed potatoes and tubers, contaminated cutting tools, and mechanical contact; it can also be transmitted by aphids in association with potato leafroll virus. Because it can be symptomless in some hosts and ornamentals, PSTVd is a quarantine pest in many countries, and control relies on certified viroid-free seed stock, hygiene, and sensitive molecular testing (RT-PCR and hybridisation assays).

Structure and replication

The RNA folds into a rod-like secondary structure with conserved regions involved in replication by rolling-circle amplification in the nucleus, mediated by host DNA-dependent RNA polymerase II redirected to copy the RNA template.
