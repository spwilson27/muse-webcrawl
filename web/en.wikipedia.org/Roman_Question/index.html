Roman Question

The Roman Question (Italian: Questione romana) was a political dispute in Italy concerning the temporal power of the popes and the status of Rome following Italian unification. It arose when Rome was annexed by the Kingdom of Italy in 1870, ending the Papal States, and was not resolved until the Lateran Treaty of 1929.

Background

During the Risorgimento, Italian nationalists sought to make Rome the capital of a united Italy, but the city was ruled by the Pope and protected by French troops. After France withdrew its garrison during the Franco-Prussian War, Italian troops breached Porta Pia on 20 September 1870 and occupied Rome, which became the Italian capital in 1871.

The dispute

Pope Pius IX refused to recognize the annexation and considered himself a "prisoner in the Vatican," rejecting the Italian Law of Guarantees (1871), which unilaterally offered the pope sovereignty over the Vatican and Leonine City and an annual subsidy. Successive popes maintained this protest, and the Holy See discouraged Italian Catholics from participating in national elections (the non expedit policy). The dispute left open the legal status of the pope and the Vatican for nearly six decades.

Resolution

The Roman Question was settled on 11 February 1929 by the Lateran Treaty between the Holy See and the Kingdom of Italy under Benito Mussolini. The treaty created the independent Vatican City State, recognized the sovereignty of the Holy See, and provided financial compensation for the loss of the Papal States, establishing the framework that still governs relations between Italy and the Vatican.
