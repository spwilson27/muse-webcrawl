Behavioural targeting

Behavioural targeting (also called online behavioural advertising) is a range of technologies and techniques used by online website publishers and advertisers to increase the effectiveness of their campaigns by capturing data generated by website visitors and landing page users. When a consumer visits a website, the sites they visit, the searches they make, and other activity help create a "profile" that links to that visitor's web browser, so site publishers can use the data to create defined audience segments on which to target advertisements.

How it works

Behavioural targeting uses previously collected information about a user's web-browsing behaviour, such as the pages they have visited or the searches they have made, to select the advertisements to display. Advertisers place cookies or similar tracking technologies on users' devices, build interest segments (such as "sports enthusiast" or "prospective car buyer"), and bid in real-time auctions to show relevant ads through ad networks and exchanges.

Types

Onsite behavioural targeting is carried out by a single website personalising content or ads from behaviour on that site alone. Network behavioural targeting combines data across many partner websites. Retargeting shows ads to users who previously visited an advertiser's own site — for example, displaying a product that was viewed but not purchased.

Privacy concerns

The practice has been widely criticised for opaque tracking and profiling without meaningful consent. Regulators have responded with transparency and consent requirements, including the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) and ePrivacy rules, and US state laws such as the California Consumer Privacy Act. The deprecation of third-party cookies by major browsers and the growth of privacy-preserving advertising techniques have reshaped the industry.

Effectiveness

Studies find behavioural targeting generally outperforms untargeted advertising in click-through and conversion, though the size of the advantage and its economic value to publishers are debated, and concerns persist about filter bubbles, discrimination in ad delivery, and "surveillance advertising".
