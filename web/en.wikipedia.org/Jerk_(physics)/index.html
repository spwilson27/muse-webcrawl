Jerk (physics)

In physics, jerk or jolt is the rate at which an object's acceleration changes with respect to time. It is a vector quantity, being the third derivative of position with respect to time — or equivalently the second derivative of velocity and the first derivative of acceleration. Jerk is commonly denoted j and expressed in metres per second cubed (m/s³) in SI units. Unlike velocity and acceleration, jerk is not specified by any universal physical law; it is instead an engineering quantity of central importance in designing motion profiles for lifts, vehicles, roller coasters, and machinery, because abrupt changes in acceleration are what passengers and mechanisms experience as uncomfortable — or damaging — jolts.

Definition and expressions

Jerk is defined mathematically as

j = da/dt = d²v/dt² = d³x/dt³

where a is acceleration, v velocity, and x position, all functions of time t. Where acceleration is caused by a force acting on a mass, the yank — the rate of change of force, equal to mass times jerk when mass is constant — describes how quickly the applied force ramps up. Higher derivatives also have informal names: the fourth derivative of position is called snap or jounce, the fifth crackle, and the sixth pop.

Physical significance

The human body senses jerk rather than steady acceleration: a gradually applied acceleration can be tolerated to high levels, whereas a sudden onset produces the sensation of being thrown or slammed. Excessive jerk contributes to motion discomfort in trains and cars, causes loads to shift, and in machinery produces vibration, noise, and wear — for example in gear teeth, cam followers, and railway track transitions. In manufacturing, jerk-limited motion profiles (such as S-curve acceleration profiles) are standard in CNC machines, robotics, and elevator control.

Applications and design values

Transportation engineers design vertical and horizontal transition curves so that jerk stays within comfort bounds; typical design targets are around 0.3–0.5 m/s³ for passenger comfort in railways and lifts, with higher values tolerated in amusement rides for brief intervals. Roller-coaster designers manipulate jerk to produce thrills while avoiding injury. In vehicle dynamics, the jerk experienced when a driver suddenly applies or releases the throttle or brakes is sometimes called "surge" or "lurch." Jerk also matters in seismology (the abrupt onset of ground acceleration), in the design of crash-test and impact systems, and — in a celebrated theoretical curiosity — in the Abraham–Lorentz theory of radiation reaction, where the equation of motion of a radiating charged particle involves the jerk of the electron.

Jerk in rotational motion

The rotational analogue, angular jerk, is the time derivative of angular acceleration and is important in the design of rotating machinery and in analysing vibrations of shafts and turbines. Similarly, in deformable-body mechanics and vibration theory, controlling jerk helps limit excitation of structural resonances.
