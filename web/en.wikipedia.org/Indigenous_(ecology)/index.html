Indigenous (ecology)

In biogeography, a native species in a given region or ecosystem is also said to be indigenous if its presence in that region is the result of only local natural evolution (with no human intervention) during history. The term is equivalent to the concept of indigenous or autochthonous species. A native species in a location is not necessarily endemic to that location; endemic species are exclusively found in a particular location, whereas indigenous species may be found in a range of places.

Definition and criteria

Every wild organism (as opposed to a domesticated organism) is known as an introduced species within the regions it was introduced to through human activities, deliberately or accidentally. The notion of nativeness is often a blurred concept, in part because it is a function of time and political boundaries. Over long periods of time, local conditions and migratory patterns constantly change as tectonic plates move, the climate changes, and species disperse naturally. A native species may therefore be defined by a timeframe — for example, in Britain, species that colonised following the last ice age without human assistance.

Conservation significance

Native species form co-evolved communities and support specialist herbivores, pollinators and other dependent organisms. Conservation and ecological restoration therefore typically prioritise native species over introduced ones, some of which may become invasive and displace the indigenous biota.
