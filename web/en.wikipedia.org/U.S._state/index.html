U.S. state

In the United States, a state is a constituent political entity, of which there are currently fifty. The states share sovereignty with the federal government under the United States Constitution. Because of this shared sovereignty, an American is a citizen both of the federal republic and of the state of domicile. The fifty states are geographically spread across North America and the Pacific: forty-eight contiguous states lie in central North America, Alaska is in the northwest, and Hawaii is an archipelago in the Pacific Ocean.

Government

Each state has its own constitution, an elected governor, a legislature (bicameral in every state except Nebraska, which is unicameral), and a court system. Under the Tenth Amendment, all powers not granted to the federal government nor prohibited to the states are reserved to the states or the people, giving states broad authority over areas such as education, policing, and property law. In Congress, each state is represented by two senators, while representation in the House of Representatives is apportioned by population.

Admission to the Union

Article IV of the Constitution empowers Congress to admit new states. Delaware was the first to ratify the Constitution, on December 7, 1787; Hawaii was the fiftieth and most recent, admitted on August 21, 1959. States range enormously in size and population: California is the most populous with about 39 million residents, Wyoming the least populous with fewer than 600,000, while Alaska is by far the largest in area and Rhode Island the smallest.

Besides the fifty states, the federal district of Washington, D.C., and the inhabited territories — including Puerto Rico, Guam, the U.S. Virgin Islands, American Samoa, and the Northern Mariana Islands — are under U.S. sovereignty but are not states.
