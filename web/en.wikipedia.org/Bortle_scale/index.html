Bortle scale

The Bortle scale is a nine-level numeric scale that measures the night sky's brightness and the quality of astronomical observing conditions at a particular location. It was created by amateur astronomer John E. Bortle, who published it in Sky & Telescope magazine in February 2001 to help observers compare the darkness of observing sites.

The classes

ClassDescription
1Excellent dark-sky site — zodiacal light, gegenschein, and the Milky Way cast shadows; M33 visible to the naked eye
2Truly dark site — highly structured Milky Way; clouds visible only as dark holes
3Rural sky — some light pollution on the horizon; Milky Way still complex
4Rural/suburban transition — light domes over towns; Milky Way impressive but loses detail
5Suburban sky — Milky Way washed out near the horizon, faint overhead
6Bright suburban sky — sky greyish-white up to about 35°; only bright Messier objects visible
7Suburban/urban transition — strong light pollution; Milky Way nearly invisible
8City sky — sky orange or white; only the brightest stars and planets seen
9Inner-city sky — sky brightly lit; only the Moon, planets, and a few bright clusters visible

Use

The scale is qualitative, based on observable features such as the visibility of the zodiacal light, airglow, and faint celestial objects, rather than instrument measurements. It complements the naked-eye limiting magnitude and sky-quality meter readings and is widely used by dark-sky advocates and site-certification programmes.
