Italian horror films are horror movies produced in Italy that became an influential strand of European genre cinema, especially from the late 1950s through the 1980s. Working with modest budgets for an international market, Italian filmmakers developed distinctive styles — Gothic period horror, the stylish murder-mystery of the giallo, and graphic zombie and cannibal films — that attracted a durable global cult following and influenced directors from Tim Burton to Quentin Tarantino.

Origins and the Gothic cycle

Under Fascism, horror was effectively discouraged, and Italy's first true sound-era horror film is generally considered Riccardo Freda's I Vampiri (1957), completed with cinematographer Mario Bava. Bava's directorial debut, Black Sunday (1960), starring Barbara Steele, launched a cycle of black-and-white Gothic horrors — castles, crypts, and vengeful witches — made by Bava, Freda, and Antonio Margheriti. Bava moved into colour with Black Sabbath (1963) and Blood and Black Lace (1964), the latter a foundational giallo.

Giallo

The giallo — named for the yellow covers of Italian pulp thrillers — combined murder mystery with stylized violence, eroticism, and striking visual design. Dario Argento's debut, The Bird with the Crystal Plumage (1970), popularized the form internationally, followed by his Deep Red (1975) and supernatural turns in Suspiria (1977) and Inferno (1980), famed for saturated colour and Goblin's scores. Directors such as Sergio Martino, Aldo Lado, and Lucio Fulci also worked in the genre, which strongly influenced the American slasher film.

Zombies, cannibals and the video-nasty era

The success of George A. Romero's Dawn of the Dead (1978) — released in Italy as Zombi in an Argento-supervised cut — triggered an Italian zombie boom led by Lucio Fulci's Zombi 2 (1979) and his "Gates of Hell" trilogy (1980–81), noted for surreal plots and extreme gore effects. A parallel cannibal cycle, including Ruggero Deodato's Cannibal Holocaust (1980), pushed graphic violence so far that many titles were prosecuted or banned abroad — several appeared on Britain's "video nasties" list. By the late 1980s the collapse of Italy's theatrical genre industry and the rise of home video ended the classic era, though figures such as Argento and Michele Soavi (Cemetery Man, 1994) carried the tradition forward, and the films' reputation has grown steadily through restorations and scholarship.
