Prehistory of Southeast Asia

Sulawesi's cave art, dated to at least 45,000 years ago, is among the world's earliest figurative painting.

The prehistory of Southeast Asia spans from the first hominin occupation of the region over a million years ago to the emergence of literate states in the first millennium AD. Homo erectus fossils from Java — the famous "Java Man" found by Eugène Dubois at Trinil in 1891 — date to as early as ~1.8 million years ago, and archaic humans later shared the region with the diminutive Homo floresiensis of Flores and Homo luzonensis of Luzon.

Modern humans and the Hoabinhian

Anatomically modern humans reached Southeast Asia by at least 70,000–60,000 years ago, likely moving along a southern coastal route. Rock art in Sulawesi has been dated to at least 45,500 years ago. During the late Pleistocene and early Holocene, hunter-gatherers of the Hoabinhian technocomplex, characterised by cobble tools, occupied much of the mainland.

Neolithic and Bronze Age

Agriculture arrived with expanding Austroasiatic- and Austronesian-speaking populations: rice and millet farming spread from southern China into Vietnam and Thailand by about 2500–2000 BC, while Austronesian voyagers carried crops, animals and languages through island Southeast Asia and eventually across the Pacific and to Madagascar. Bronze metallurgy appeared by ~1000 BC, exemplified by the Đông Sơn culture of Vietnam with its great bronze drums, followed by ironworking in the centuries around 500 BC. Rising sea levels, trade in prestige goods, and growing social complexity set the stage for the early kingdoms of the historic era.
