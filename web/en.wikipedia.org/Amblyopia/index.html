Amblyopia

Amblyopia
Other name: Lazy eye
Specialty: Ophthalmology
Onset: Early childhood

Amblyopia, commonly known as lazy eye, is a disorder of sight in which the brain fails to process inputs from one eye, favouring the other eye, so that vision in the affected eye does not develop normally during early childhood. The eye itself is usually structurally normal. Amblyopia affects roughly 1–5% of children and is the most common cause of decreased vision in childhood.

Causes

The condition arises when the visual pathways do not mature properly because the two eyes send mismatched or degraded images to the brain. Causes include strabismus (misalignment of the eyes), refractive errors that differ between the eyes (anisometropia) or are severe in both eyes, and deprivation from obstructions such as congenital cataracts or a drooping eyelid. The brain suppresses the poorer image, and without treatment the deficit can become permanent.

Treatment

Treatment is most effective before about age seven or eight, while the visual system is still developing. It includes correcting refractive error with glasses, and forcing use of the weaker eye by patching the stronger eye or blurring it with atropine drops. Early detection through childhood vision screening is important, because amblyopia causes no obvious symptoms in many children.
