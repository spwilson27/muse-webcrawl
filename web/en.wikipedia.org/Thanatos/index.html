Thanatos

In Greek mythology, Thanatos (Greek: Θάνατος, "Death") is the personification of death. He was a minor figure, seldom central to myths, and rarely individually worshipped. According to Hesiod's Theogony, Thanatos was the son of Nyx (Night) and Erebos (Darkness), and the twin brother of Hypnos (Sleep). Homer's Iliad likewise pairs the brothers, who carry the body of the slain hero Sarpedon from the field of Troy to Lycia.

Character and myths

Thanatos was depicted as a winged, bearded youth, often carrying an inverted torch or a sword with which he cut a lock of hair from the dying, dedicating them to the underworld. Though relentless, he could occasionally be outwitted or resisted: Heracles wrestled Thanatos and defeated him to restore Alcestis to her husband Admetus — the plot of Euripides' Alcestis — and the trickster Sisyphus famously bound Thanatos with chains, so that for a time no one on earth could die.

Psychoanalytic usage

In psychoanalysis, Thanatos became the name for the death drive, the counterpart of Eros (the life drive). The concept was developed by Sabina Spielrein and adopted by Sigmund Freud in Beyond the Pleasure Principle (1920), which proposed an instinctual drive towards death and dissolution operating in tension with the life-preserving drives. Although "Thanatos" itself was not Freud's usual term, it became standard in later psychoanalytic and popular writing.

In culture

Thanatos appears in art, literature, and popular culture as a figure for death's inevitability, and the word has given English terms such as thanatology, the study of death and dying.
