Effects of climate change

The effects of climate change span the impacts of human-caused warming on the environment, human societies, and ecosystems. The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) has concluded that climate change has caused widespread adverse impacts and related losses and damages to nature and people, beyond natural climate variability. Global surface temperature has risen by about 1.2 °C since the late nineteenth century, and every increment of additional warming increases the severity of impacts.

Physical changes

Observed changes include rising global temperatures, ocean warming and acidification, retreating glaciers, decreased snow cover and Arctic sea ice, and global mean sea level rise of more than 20 cm since 1900, driven by thermal expansion and melting land ice. The frequency and intensity of some extreme events have increased: heatwaves on land and in the ocean, heavy precipitation in many regions, and droughts in others. Tropical cyclones have likely become more intense on average, and wildfire weather has become more frequent in fire-prone regions.

Impacts on ecosystems

Species are shifting their ranges poleward and to higher elevations, phenology is changing, and coral reefs have suffered mass bleaching from marine heatwaves; the IPCC assesses that many species face elevated extinction risk, particularly under higher warming. Ocean acidification affects shell-forming organisms, and thawing permafrost releases stored carbon.

Impacts on people

Climate change affects food and water security: crop yields for staples such as maize and wheat have been reduced in many regions, fisheries are shifting, and hundreds of millions of people face increased water scarcity. Heat-related mortality has risen, and the ranges of some infectious diseases are expanding. Sea level rise threatens low-lying coasts and small island states, and weather-related disasters displace millions of people each year. Impacts fall disproportionately on vulnerable populations that have contributed least to emissions.

Future risks and adaptation

Projected risks escalate sharply with warming level; at 2 °C and above, the IPCC finds widespread risks to food systems, health, cities, and ecosystems, including the possibility of passing tipping points such as long-term loss of the Greenland and West Antarctic ice sheets. Adaptation — flood defenses, heat action plans, drought-resistant crops, ecosystem restoration — can reduce many risks but faces hard limits, especially for coastal and water-dependent systems, making mitigation to limit warming itself essential.
