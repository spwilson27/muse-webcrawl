Battle of Lewes

The Battle of Lewes was fought on 14 May 1264 in Sussex, England, during the Second Barons' War. The baronial army of Simon de Montfort, Earl of Leicester, defeated the royal army of King Henry III, capturing both the king and his son, the Lord Edward (the future Edward I).

Background

Conflict between Henry III and his barons over the Provisions of Oxford erupted into war in 1264. Henry occupied Lewes with a strong army, including a formidable cavalry force under Edward and Richard of Cornwall. De Montfort, with a smaller army, advanced overnight and occupied the high ground of the South Downs above the town.

The battle

Edward charged and routed the baronial left wing — largely Londoners — but pursued them far from the field. In his absence de Montfort's main body attacked downhill and overwhelmed the royal centre and right. Richard of Cornwall was captured hiding in a windmill, Henry III took refuge in Lewes Priory, and by evening the king had agreed to the Mise of Lewes, accepting the Provisions and surrendering Edward and his cousin Henry of Almain as hostages.

Aftermath

The victory made de Montfort the effective ruler of England. He summoned the famous parliament of 1265, which included burgesses from the towns alongside knights of the shire — a milestone in the development of Parliament. His ascendancy ended at the Battle of Evesham in August 1265, where Edward escaped custody and de Montfort was killed.
