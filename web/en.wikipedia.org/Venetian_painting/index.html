Venetian painting

Venetian painting is famed for its emphasis on colour — colorito — over line.

Venetian painting was a major force in Italian Renaissance painting and beyond. Beginning with the Bellini family and Antonello da Messina, who is credited with bringing oil painting to Venice, and continuing with Giorgione, Titian, Tintoretto, Veronese, and Tiepolo, the tradition is distinguished by its emphasis on colour and atmosphere — colorito — as against the Florentine emphasis on line and design (disegno). Venetian painters worked in a city whose shimmering light on water informed their luminous palettes, and their large canvases adorned the palaces, churches, and confraternities (scuole) of the Venetian Republic.

Origins

Early Venetian painting long preserved Byzantine conventions — gold grounds and stylised figures — through Paolo Veneziano and his workshop in the 14th century. Gentile da Fabriano and later the Bellini family — Jacopo, Gentile, and Giovanni — moved the school toward naturalism. Giovanni Bellini, teacher of Giorgione and Titian, transformed Venetian painting with his mastery of oil paint, serene Madonnas, and glowing landscapes.

The High Renaissance

Giorgione introduced the small, poetic cabinet picture and a dreamy pastoral mood in works such as The Tempest. Titian, the school's most famous painter, developed a virtuoso brushwork and colour across portraits, mythologies, and altarpieces for patrons including Emperor Charles V and Philip II of Spain. Tintoretto filled the Scuola Grande di San Rocco with vast dramatic canvases, and Veronese painted opulent feasts and pageants celebrating Venetian splendour. Lorenzo Lotto and Palma Vecchio were other leading figures of the period.

Later centuries

Venetian painting declined in the 17th century but enjoyed a last great flowering in the 18th century with Giovanni Battista Tiepolo's luminous ceiling frescoes, Canaletto's precise vedute (city views), Francesco Guardi's freer landscapes, and Pietro Longhi's scenes of everyday life. The tradition's attention to colour and painterly surface profoundly influenced later European art, from Rubens, who studied Titian in Madrid, to the Impressionists.

Characteristics

Long damp winters made fresco unreliable in Venice, so painters used canvas early and extensively, developing oil-on-canvas techniques suited to rich, layered colour. The scuole and state commissions demanded enormous narrative canvases; Venetian painters responded with dynamic compositions, deep atmospheric perspective, and sensuous rendering of fabrics, flesh, and light on water.
