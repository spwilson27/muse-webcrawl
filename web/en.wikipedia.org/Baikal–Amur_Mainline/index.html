The Baikal–Amur Mainline (Russian: Байкало-Амурская магистраль, Baykalo-Amurskaya magistral), abbreviated BAM, is a railway line in Russia traversing Eastern Siberia and the Russian Far East for about 4,324 km (2,687 mi). Running roughly 610–770 km north of, and parallel to, the Trans-Siberian Railway, the BAM stretches from Tayshet on the Trans-Siberian, north of Lake Baikal, eastward through Bratsk, Severobaikalsk, Tynda and Komsomolsk-on-Amur to the Pacific port of Sovetskaya Gavan.

History

A northern route bypassing the Trans-Siberian was first surveyed in the Tsarist era. Construction began in the 1930s and 1940s using Gulag prisoner labour (the Bamlag camps) under Stalin; sections near Tayshet and in the Far East were built, but most work was abandoned after 1953. The project was revived in 1974 as a prestige "Project of the Century" of the Brezhnev era, built largely by Komsomol volunteers and military railway troops through permafrost, swamps and mountain ranges, crossing eleven major rivers and seven mountain ranges. The line was declared complete with a ceremonial "golden spike" at Kuanda in 1984, although regular through operation only became possible later; the 15.3 km Severomuysky Tunnel, the longest in Russia, was not finished until 2003.

Route and operation

Most of the BAM is single-track with diesel traction, with electrification on western sections. Traffic has long been below design capacity, though the line carries growing volumes of coal, timber and container freight from Far Eastern ports, and expansion works (the so-called BAM-2 programme) to double capacity have been under way in the 2010s–2020s as Russia's trade pivots eastward.

Branch lines

The Amur–Yakutsk Mainline branches north from the BAM at Tynda/Berkakit toward Yakutsk, providing the rail link into the Sakha Republic.
