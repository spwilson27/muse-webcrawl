Disability in the United States

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, more than one in four adults in the United States — about 28 percent, or over 70 million people — report having some type of disability, including difficulties with mobility, cognition, hearing, vision, independent living, or self-care. Disability has been a major subject of American social policy, civil-rights law, and public-health research.

History

For much of the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, disabled Americans were commonly institutionalised, and many cities passed "ugly laws" prohibiting people with visible disabilities from appearing in public. Veterans of the Civil War, the World Wars, and later conflicts drove the creation of pension and rehabilitation systems. The independent living movement, emerging in Berkeley, California in the 1970s, reframed disability as a matter of social barriers rather than individual defect.

Law

Key federal laws include the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, whose Section 504 banned disability discrimination by federal agencies and recipients of federal funds; the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), guaranteeing a free appropriate public education; and the Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 (ADA), a landmark civil-rights law prohibiting discrimination in employment, public services, and public accommodations, and requiring accessible design. Social Security Disability Insurance (SSDI) and Supplemental Security Income (SSI) provide income support.

Activism

The disability rights movement achieved major victories through direct action: the 1977 Section 504 sit-ins — the longest occupation of a federal building in US history — and the 1990 "Capitol Crawl", in which activists left their wheelchairs to climb the Capitol steps, helped secure passage of the ADA. Poverty rates among disabled Americans remain roughly double those of non-disabled people, and employment gaps persist, keeping economic inclusion a central policy concern.
