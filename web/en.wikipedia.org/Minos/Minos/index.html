Minos

In Greek mythology, Minos was a king of Crete, son of Zeus and Europa. He ruled from his palace at Knossos, where every nine years he made King Aegeus of Athens pick seven young boys and seven young girls to be sent to the Labyrinth to be eaten by the Minotaur. After his death, Minos became a judge of the dead in Hades.

Mythology

Minos ascended the Cretan throne with the help of Poseidon, who sent a magnificent white bull from the sea as a sign of his kingship. When Minos kept the bull instead of sacrificing it, Poseidon caused his wife Pasiphaë to fall in love with the animal; their offspring was the Minotaur, hidden in the Labyrinth built by Daedalus. Minos was famed as a lawgiver: he was said to receive laws directly from Zeus in a cave on Mount Ida, retiring there every nine years, and Plato praised the antiquity of Cretan law.

Sea power

Thucydides credited Minos as the first person to establish a navy, master of the Aegean Sea who colonised the Cyclades and suppressed piracy. The 19th-century archaeologist Arthur Evans adopted his name for the Bronze Age civilisation of Crete, the Minoan civilisation.

Death and afterlife

Pursuing Daedalus to Sicily, Minos was killed by the daughters of King Cocalus, who poured boiling water over him in his bath. In the underworld he became, with Aeacus and Rhadamanthus, one of the three judges of the dead; Dante placed him at the entrance of the second circle of Hell, assigning sinners to their punishments by wrapping his tail around himself.
