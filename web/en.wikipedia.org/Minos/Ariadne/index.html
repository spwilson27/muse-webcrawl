Ariadne

Ariadne
Parents: Minos and Pasiphaë of Crete
Consort: Dionysus (in most traditions)
Known for: the thread that guided Theseus through the Labyrinth
Symbols: ball of thread, crown (Corona Borealis)

In Greek mythology, Ariadne was a Cretan princess, the daughter of King Minos of Crete and his queen Pasiphaë. She is best known for helping the Athenian hero Theseus escape the Labyrinth after he slew the Minotaur, providing him with a ball of thread ("Ariadne's thread") by which he could retrace his steps.

Myth

According to the myth, Athens was obliged to send seven youths and seven maidens to Crete as tribute, where they were devoured by the Minotaur in the Labyrinth built by Daedalus. Theseus volunteered for the mission. Ariadne fell in love with him and, on Daedalus's advice, gave him a sword and a ball of yarn, which he unwound as he entered; having killed the Minotaur, he followed the thread out. The pair fled Crete with the Athenians, but Theseus abandoned Ariadne on the island of Naxos—accounts vary as to whether he did so willingly, under divine command, or because of a storm.

Ariadne and Dionysus

On Naxos, the god Dionysus found Ariadne and married her; in many versions she became immortal and bore him children. Her wedding crown was placed among the stars as the constellation Corona Borealis. In some darker variants, Ariadne died on Naxos—killed by Artemis or in childbirth—or hanged herself. Homer mentions that Artemis slew her on Dia "on the testimony of Dionysus."

Worship and legacy

Ariadne was worshipped on Naxos, Cyprus, and Crete, and her figure may preserve echoes of a Minoan goddess—her name has been read as "most holy" (from Cretan Greek ari- and adnos). She has been a favorite subject of art and opera, including Richard Strauss's Ariadne auf Naxos (1912). "Ariadne's thread" survives as a metaphor, including in logic and computer science, for solving a problem by exhaustive exploration with a record of the path taken.
