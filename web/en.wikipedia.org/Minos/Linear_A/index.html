Linear A

Linear A is a writing system used by the Minoan civilization of Bronze Age Crete and the Aegean islands from about 1800 BC to 1450 BC. It was the primary script of the Minoan palatial culture, used for palace administration, accounting, and possibly religious inscriptions. Linear A remains undeciphered, although its signs largely correspond to the later Linear B, which was adapted to write Mycenaean Greek.

Corpus and use

Roughly 1,400 inscriptions survive, comprising some 7,400 signs, mostly short texts on clay tablets, nodules, and sealings recording commodities and quantities. Longer religious texts appear on stone libation tables and votive objects, including a recurring formula often called the "libation formula." Finds come from Hagia Triada, Khania, Phaistos, Zakros, Knossos, and other sites across Crete, with examples also from the Aegean islands, mainland Greece, and even Israel.

The script

Linear A contains around 70 syllabic signs with open syllables (consonant–vowel), a set of ideograms or logograms representing commodities (often accompanied by numerals), and a decimal number system. Comparison with Linear B, which shares most sign forms, allows probable sound values to be assigned to many Linear A signs, so inscriptions can be partially "read" phonetically—yet the underlying language, conventionally called Minoan, is unknown and apparently not Greek, which is why the texts cannot be understood.

Decipherment attempts

Scholars have proposed links between the Minoan language and various language families—Anatolian, Semitic, and others—but none has won acceptance. Linear A is regarded as a descendant of the earlier Cretan hieroglyphic script, and in turn influenced Linear B and possibly the Cypro-Minoan script of Cyprus. Its undeciphered status makes it one of the most famous unsolved problems in the study of ancient writing.
