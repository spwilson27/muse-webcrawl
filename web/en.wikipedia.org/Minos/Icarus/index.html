Icarus

In Greek mythology, Icarus (Ancient Greek: Ἰκαρος) was the son of the master craftsman Daedalus, the architect of the labyrinth of Crete. After Theseus, king of Athens and enemy of Minos, escaped from the labyrinth, King Minos suspected that Icarus and Daedalus had revealed the labyrinth's secrets and imprisoned them — in a tower or in the labyrinth itself, according to different versions. Daedalus fashioned wings of feathers held together with beeswax for himself and his son so they could fly to freedom.

Daedalus warned Icarus not to fly too low, lest the sea's dampness clog his wings, nor too high, lest the sun's heat melt the wax. Icarus ignored his father's instructions not to fly too close to the sun; when the wax melted, Icarus fell into the sea, in an area that bears his name, the Icarian Sea near Icaria, and drowned. His father flew safely to Sicily.

The myth

The fullest surviving account is in Ovid's Metamorphoses (Book 8). Daedalus, wearied of exile in Crete and longing for home, constructed wings for their escape since Minos controlled the land and sea. He collected feathers, arranged them in layered rows, and bound them with thread and wax. He warned Icarus to follow him and keep to the middle course. They passed the islands of Samos, Delos, and Lebinthos; Icarus, exhilarated by flight, soared upward. The sun softened the wax, the feathers came loose, and Icarus plunged into the sea calling his father's name. Daedalus found the body, buried it on the island since called Icaria, and eventually reached Sicily, where he built a temple to Apollo.

Interpretation and legacy

The story is traditionally read as a warning against hubris — disobedience, overambition, and youthful recklessness — balanced by the opposite danger of flying too low; it is thus an allegory of the "golden mean". Renaissance artists took up the subject, notably Pieter Bruegel the Elder's Landscape with the Fall of Icarus (c. 1560), in which the drowning is nearly invisible amid everyday labour — the subject of W. H. Auden's poem "Musée des Beaux Arts" (1938). The phrase "flew too close to the sun" survives as a modern idiom for ruinous ambition, and psychologists have named the "Icarus complex" after the figure.
