Athens

Athens is the capital and largest city of Greece. A major coastal urban area in the Mediterranean, Athens dominates its surrounding region of Attica and is one of the world's oldest cities, with its recorded history spanning over 3,400 years; it is widely considered the cradle of democracy and Western civilisation. Classical Athens was a powerful city-state that emerged in the archaic period, and in the 5th century BC it achieved cultural and intellectual monuments — above all the Parthenon on the Acropolis — that remain world-famous.

History

Athens has been continuously inhabited for over 5,000 years. The reforms of Solon (594 BC) and Cleisthenes (508/507 BC) laid the foundations of Athenian democracy, which reached its height under Pericles in the 5th century BC. Athens led the Delian League against Persia, fought Sparta in the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC), and produced the philosophers Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle, the tragedians Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides, and the historians Herodotus and Thucydides. Under Roman, Byzantine, and Ottoman rule the city shrank to a small town; it became capital of the modern Greek state in 1834, when its population numbered only a few thousand.

Geography and climate

The city occupies a plain in Attica ringed by mountains — Aigaleo to the west, Parnitha to the north, Penteli to the northeast, and Hymettus to the east — and extends to the Saronic Gulf at Piraeus. Athens enjoys a hot-summer Mediterranean climate with long, hot, dry summers and mild winters.

Culture and landmarks

The Acropolis, crowned by the Parthenon, Propylaea, Erechtheion, and Temple of Athena Nike, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Other major sites include the Ancient Agora, the Theatre of Dionysus, the Panathenaic Stadium (site of the first modern Olympic Games in 1896), and the Acropolis Museum (opened 2009). Athens hosted the 2004 Summer Olympics, which spurred major infrastructure investment including a new metro and airport.
