Mycenaean Greece

Mycenaean Greece (or the Mycenaean civilization) was the last phase of the Bronze Age in ancient Greece, spanning roughly 1750 to 1050 BC. It represents the first advanced Greek-speaking civilization in mainland Greece, characterized by palatial states, urban organization, distinctive art and architecture, and the first attested writing of the Greek language in the Linear B script. The civilization is named after Mycenae, its most prominent site in the northeastern Peloponnese.

Society and palaces

Mycenaean society was organized around palace centers such as Mycenae, Tiryns, Pylos, Thebes, Athens, and—after about 1450 BC—Knossos on Crete. Each palace was ruled by a king, the wanax, supported by a bureaucracy that kept detailed records of landholding, livestock, rations, and craft production on clay tablets. The palaces were fortified with massive "Cyclopean" walls and are famous for the Lion Gate at Mycenae and tholos (beehive) tombs such as the Treasury of Atreus.

Trade and warfare

The Mycenaeans traded widely across the eastern Mediterranean, exporting pottery, olive oil, and wine and importing metals and luxury goods from Cyprus, Egypt, the Levant, and the Hittite world. Their warrior culture is vividly reflected in weapon burials, chariot frescoes, and later Greek epic tradition: Homer's Iliad preserves a legendary memory of this world, including the catalogue of Achaean forces and the figure of Agamemnon, "king of men," at Mycenae.

Collapse

Around 1200 BC the palace system began to break down amid the wider Late Bronze Age collapse, and by about 1050 BC the Mycenaean world had fragmented, literacy was lost, and Greece entered the Greek Dark Ages. Causes proposed for the collapse include invasion or internal revolt, drought and climate change, earthquakes, and disruption of trade networks.
