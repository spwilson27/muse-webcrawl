Theseus

Theseus was a mythical king and founder-hero of Athens. Like Hercules/Heracles, he faced a series of labours and adversaries on his journey, but his greatest deed was the slaying of the Minotaur in the Labyrinth of King Minos on Crete, which freed Athens from its tribute of youths and maidens.

Youth and the road to Athens

The son of Aethra and — in different versions — King Aegeus of Athens or the god Poseidon, Theseus was raised at Troezen. Reaching manhood he lifted the rock under which his father had hidden a sword and sandals, and travelled to Athens by the dangerous land route, defeating the brigands Sinis, Sciron and Procrustes, who each received from Theseus the deaths they had inflicted on others.

Crete and the Minotaur

At Athens Theseus volunteered to join the tribute sent to Crete. Ariadne, daughter of Minos, fell in love with him and gave him a clew of thread so he could find his way back through the Labyrinth. Theseus killed the Minotaur, freed the Athenian captives and sailed away with Ariadne, abandoning her on Naxos. Approaching Athens he forgot to hoist the white sail signalling success; Aegeus, seeing black sails, threw himself into the sea that bears his name.

King of Athens

As king, Theseus was credited with the synoecism — the political unification of Attica under Athens — and with founding institutions of Athenian democracy. His later adventures included the abduction of Helen, a descent to the underworld with his friend Pirithous, and marriage to Phaedra, whose false accusation destroyed his son Hippolytus. Theseus died on Skyros; the Athenians later claimed to have recovered his bones and enshrined them in the Theseion.
