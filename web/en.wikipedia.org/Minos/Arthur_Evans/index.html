Arthur Evans

Sir Arthur John Evans (8 July 1851 – 11 July 1941) was a British archaeologist and pioneer in the study of Aegean civilisation in the Bronze Age. He is famous for unearthing the palace of Knossos on the Greek island of Crete. Based on the structures and artefacts found there and throughout the eastern Mediterranean, Evans found that he needed to distinguish the civilisations he was discovering from Greece proper. Evans was the first to define the Cretan scripts he called Linear A and Linear B, as well as an earlier pictographic writing.

Early life

Evans was the son of John Evans, a noted antiquarian and geologist. Educated at Harrow, Oxford (Brasenose College), and Göttingen, he travelled widely in the Balkans, working as a journalist and becoming involved in the politics of the region; he was imprisoned by the Austrian authorities in Ragusa (Dubrovnik) on suspicion of espionage before being expelled in 1882. In 1884 he became keeper of the Ashmolean Museum at Oxford.

Knossos

Drawn to Crete by his interest in ancient seal-stones bearing unknown scripts, Evans purchased the site of Knossos and began large-scale excavation in 1900. Within weeks his team uncovered a vast multi-storey palace complex with storerooms, workshops, a throne room, and sophisticated drainage. Evans named the civilisation "Minoan" after the legendary King Minos and spent the rest of his life excavating, restoring, and publishing the site, notably in his multi-volume work The Palace of Minos (1921–1935).

Restoration and legacy

Evans's use of reinforced concrete to reconstruct parts of the palace in their supposed original form was pioneering and remains controversial: the vivid reconstructions shape visitors' experience of Knossos but embody Evans's own interpretations, including his vision of a peaceful, goddess-worshipping, matriarchal society. His classification of Minoan chronology and his recognition of the Linear scripts — Linear B being deciphered after his death by Michael Ventris as an early form of Greek — founded Aegean prehistory as a discipline.
