Labyrinth


Image placeholder: the seven-circuit Cretan labyrinth

In Greek mythology, the Labyrinth was an elaborate, confusing structure designed and built by the legendary craftsman Daedalus for King Minos of Crete at Knossos. Its purpose was to hold the Minotaur, the monstrous creature with the head of a bull and the body of a man, who was eventually killed by the Athenian hero Theseus. Daedalus had so cunningly designed the structure that he himself could barely escape it after building it.

The myth

According to the story, Athens was required to send a tribute of seven youths and seven maidens every year (or every nine years) to Crete, where they were placed in the Labyrinth to be devoured by the Minotaur. Theseus volunteered to go as one of the youths; with the help of Minos's daughter Ariadne, who gave him a ball of thread ("Ariadne's thread") to unwind as he entered, he slew the Minotaur and retraced his steps to freedom, escaping with the other Athenians.

Historical associations

The word "labyrinth" is probably pre-Greek; it has been connected with the Lydian word labrys ("double axe"), a symbol prominent at Knossos, suggesting the Labyrinth was originally "the house of the double axe" — the great palace complex itself, whose sprawling, multi-roomed plan may have inspired the legend. Classical writers also described an Egyptian labyrinth at Hawara, which Herodotus claimed surpassed the pyramids, and later authors listed several other famous labyrinths. The seven-circuit "Cretan" labyrinth pattern appears on ancient coins of Knossos and survives today in turf and pavement labyrinths used for walking meditation.

Labyrinth and maze

In modern usage, a labyrinth is often distinguished from a maze: a unicursal labyrinth has a single, unambiguous path to the centre, whereas a maze offers choices and dead ends. The labyrinth motif has become a widespread symbol of the journey, pilgrimage, and the human mind, from cathedral pavement labyrinths such as Chartres to contemporary art and gardens.
