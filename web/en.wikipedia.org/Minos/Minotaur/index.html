Minotaur

In Greek mythology, the Minotaur is a mythical creature portrayed during classical antiquity with the head and tail of a bull and the body of a man. He dwelt at the centre of the Labyrinth, an elaborate maze-like construction designed by the architect Daedalus and ordered built by King Minos of Crete. The Minotaur was eventually killed by the Athenian hero Theseus.

Origin

The Minotaur was the offspring of Pasiphaë, wife of Minos, and the Cretan Bull sent by Poseidon. His proper name in some sources is Asterion, "the starry one". Unable to nourish the creature normally, and warned by an oracle, Minos had Daedalus construct the Labyrinth to confine him.

The Athenian tribute

After Minos's son Androgeus was killed in Athens, Minos waged war and demanded a tribute: every nine years (in some versions every year) Athens sent seven youths and seven maidens to be devoured by the Minotaur. Theseus volunteered to go as one of the victims, and with the help of Minos's daughter Ariadne — who gave him a ball of thread to retrace his path — he entered the Labyrinth, slew the Minotaur, and escaped with the Athenians.

Interpretation

The myth has been read as reflecting the historical dominance of Minoan Crete over the Aegean and the tribute it exacted, the bull cults and bull-leaping rituals depicted in Cretan art, and the bewildering plan of the palace at Knossos itself. The Minotaur remains a potent artistic subject, from Greek vase painting to Picasso's Minotauromachy.
