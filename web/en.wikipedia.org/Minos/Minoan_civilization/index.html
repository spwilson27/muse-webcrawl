Minoan civilization

Minoan civilization
Region: Crete and the Aegean, Greece
Period: c. 3100–1100 BCE (Bronze Age)
Major sites: Knossos, Phaistos, Malia, Zakros
Scripts: Cretan hieroglyphs, Linear A (undeciphered)

The Minoan civilization was a Bronze Age culture centered on the island of Crete and other Aegean islands, flourishing from about 3100 to 1100 BCE. It was rediscovered at the turn of the 20th century through the work of British archaeologist Arthur Evans, who named it after the mythical King Minos. The Minoans built elaborate palace complexes, created vivid art, traded across the eastern Mediterranean, and developed writing systems that remain partly undeciphered.


The Palace of Knossos, partly reconstructed by Evans

Chronology and palaces

Evans divided Minoan history into Early, Middle, and Late periods. Around 2000 BCE large palace complexes arose at Knossos, Phaistos, Malia, and Zakros; destroyed by earthquakes around 1700 BCE, they were rebuilt grander, with multi-storey buildings, light wells, advanced drainage, elaborate frescoes (bull-leaping, processions, marine life), and vast storage magazines for oil, wine, and grain. Knossos, the largest Bronze Age site on Crete, may have housed a population in the tens of thousands in its surrounding town. Remarkably, the palaces lacked heavy fortifications, suggesting a thalassocracy secured by sea power, as Thucydides later described of Minos.

Society, trade, and religion

The Minoans exported wine, olive oil, timber, and fine pottery and metalwork to Egypt, the Levant, and mainland Greece, importing copper, tin, ivory, and gold. Their religion featured peak sanctuaries, sacred caves, double axes (labrys), and prominent female figurines often interpreted as goddesses or priestesses; bulls held a central ritual role. Administration used Cretan hieroglyphs and then Linear A, neither yet deciphered; the later Linear B, adapted for Mycenaean Greek, was deciphered by Michael Ventris in 1952 and records a Greek-speaking administration at Knossos in its final phase.

Decline

Around 1628 BCE the massive eruption of Thera (Santorini) buried the Minoan town of Akrotiri and sent tsunamis and ash across the Aegean, though Crete's palaces continued for a time. In the mid-15th century BCE most palace sites were destroyed by fire, after which Mycenaean Greeks appear to have taken over Knossos. Minoan culture faded into the Greek Dark Ages, leaving legends of Minos, the Labyrinth, and Daedalus in Greek myth.
