Minos

In Greek mythology, Minos (Greek: Μίνως) was a king of Crete, son of Zeus and Europa. Every nine years, he made King Aegeus of Athens pick seven young men and seven young women to be sent to the Labyrinth to be eaten by the Minotaur. After his death, Minos became a judge of the dead in Hades. The civilization of Bronze Age Crete, the Minoan civilization, is named after him.

Mythology

Minos was raised on Crete with his brothers Rhadamanthus and Sarpedon by King Asterion. On Asterion's death, Minos claimed the throne, proving his divine favour when Poseidon sent a magnificent bull from the sea. Minos promised to sacrifice the bull but kept it instead; in punishment, Poseidon caused Minos's wife Pasiphaë to lust after the bull, and she bore the Minotaur. Minos commissioned Daedalus to build the Labyrinth to house the creature.

Minos waged war on Athens after his son Androgeos was killed there, and imposed the tribute of youths. He pursued Daedalus to Sicily, where he was killed in the bath by the daughters of King Cocalus. In the afterlife he sat with a golden sceptre as one of the three judges of the dead, alongside Aeacus and Rhadamanthus — a role depicted by Homer, Virgil and Dante, who portrays Minos in the Inferno assigning sinners to their circles by wrapping his tail.

Historical interpretations

Ancient historians such as Thucydides treated Minos as a historical sea-king, the first to build a navy and suppress piracy in the Aegean. Sir Arthur Evans named the Bronze Age Cretan civilization "Minoan" after excavating the palace at Knossos from 1900.
