Knossos

Knossos is a Bronze Age archaeological site in Crete and Europe's oldest city. It was the ceremonial and political centre of the Minoan civilisation and culture, and the legendary seat of King Minos, whose palace was said to contain the Labyrinth of the Minotaur. The site lies near the modern city of Heraklion.

The palace

The great palace, built around 2000 BC and rebuilt after an earthquake around 1700 BC, spread over some 20,000 square metres with hundreds of rooms arranged around a central court: royal apartments, workshops, shrines, storerooms with giant pithoi, and the so-called Throne Room with its gypsum throne and griffin frescoes. Advanced features included light wells, flushing toilets and an elaborate drainage system.

Excavation

The site was excavated by Arthur Evans beginning in 1900. Evans named the civilisation "Minoan" after Minos and controversially restored parts of the palace in reinforced concrete, repainting frescoes — interventions that remain debated. Tablets found at Knossos in the Linear B script were deciphered in 1952 by Michael Ventris, proving the language of the palace's final phase was an early form of Greek.

Decline

The palace was destroyed by fire around 1350 BC, and Knossos declined in importance, though the town continued into the Roman era.
