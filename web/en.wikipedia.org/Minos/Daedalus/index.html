Daedalus

In Greek mythology, Daedalus was a skillful architect and craftsman, seen as a symbol of wisdom, knowledge and power. At the court of King Minos of Crete he created the Labyrinth that held the Minotaur, and later fashioned wings of feathers and wax so that he and his son Icarus could escape the island by air.

In Crete

Daedalus came to Crete in the service of Minos. When the queen Pasiphaë was cursed with desire for a bull, Daedalus built her a hollow wooden cow in which she could conceal herself. From that union came the Minotaur, for whom Daedalus designed the Labyrinth — a maze so cunning that even its maker could scarcely find his way out. Later Daedalus gave Ariadne the clew of thread by which Theseus escaped the maze after killing the monster.

The flight

Angered, Minos imprisoned Daedalus and Icarus, in some versions within the Labyrinth itself. Daedalus crafted wings from feathers bound with wax and warned his son neither to fly too low, lest the sea dampen the feathers, nor too high, lest the sun melt the wax. Icarus, exulting in flight, climbed too near the sun; the wax melted, and he fell into the sea that still bears his name. Daedalus flew on to Sicily, where he found refuge at the court of King Cocalus.

Legacy

Daedalus became the archetype of the ingenious inventor; in Athens his name was attached to a legendary line of craftsmen, and the Greeks credited him with inventions including the saw, the axe and the drill. James Joyce adopted him as the emblem of the artist through the character Stephen Dedalus.
