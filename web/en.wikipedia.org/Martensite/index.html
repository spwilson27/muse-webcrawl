Martensite

Martensite is a very hard form of steel crystalline structure. It is named after German metallurgist Adolf Martens, who described the microstructure in 1890. Martensite is formed in carbon steels by the rapid cooling (quenching) of the austenite form of iron at such a high rate that carbon atoms do not have time to diffuse out of the crystal structure in large enough quantities to form cementite (Fe3C).

Formation

The transformation from austenite (face-centred cubic) to martensite is diffusionless and displacive: the lattice shears cooperatively into a body-centred tetragonal structure, with carbon atoms trapped in interstitial sites, distorting the lattice and producing very high hardness and strength but low ductility. The transformation begins at the martensite-start temperature (Ms) and proceeds athermally as temperature falls. As-quenched martensite is usually brittle and is almost always tempered — reheated to a moderate temperature — to trade some hardness for toughness, as in quenched and tempered steels.

Morphology and properties

Depending on carbon content, martensite appears as lath martensite (low carbon, packets of parallel laths) or plate/lens martensite (high carbon, twinned plates). Hardness increases strongly with carbon content up to about 0.6%. Retained austenite left untransformed can affect dimensional stability. The same class of diffusionless martensitic transformation occurs in other alloys and underlies shape-memory alloys such as nitinol.
