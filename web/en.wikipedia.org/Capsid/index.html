Capsid

A capsid is the protein shell of a virus (latent for 'viral particle'). It consists of several oligomeric subunits made of protein, called protomers, which assemble into units known as capsomeres. The capsid encloses the viral genetic material — DNA or RNA — sometimes together with enzymes, forming the nucleocapsid.

Functions

The capsid protects the viral genome from physical, chemical, and enzymatic damage, mediates attachment to host cells, and in many viruses controls the release of the genome during infection.

Structures

Capsids generally take one of a few forms. Icosahedral capsids are roughly spherical twenty-faced shells, as in adenoviruses and poliovirus. Helical capsids consist of protein subunits arranged in a spiral around the genome, forming a rod or filament, as in tobacco mosaic virus. Complex capsids, such as those of poxviruses and many bacteriophages, combine elements — a bacteriophage typically has an icosahedral head and a helical tail.

Envelope

Some viruses surround the capsid with a lipid envelope derived from host membranes; viruses without one are called naked viruses. Enveloped viruses are generally more sensitive to detergents and desiccation.

Assembly

Capsids self-assemble from their protein subunits, often with the help of scaffolding proteins or a portal complex through which the genome is packaged. Capsid proteins are frequent targets of antiviral drugs and vaccines.
