Pharmacognosy

Pharmacognosy is the study of drugs derived from natural sources, principally plants, but also animals, fungi, and microorganisms. The term derives from the Greek pharmakon (drug) and gnosis (knowledge). The American Society of Pharmacognosy defines it as "the study of the physical, chemical, biochemical, and biological properties of drugs, drug substances, or potential drugs or drug substances of natural origin, as well as the search for new drugs from natural sources."

Scope

Classical pharmacognosy concerned itself with crude drugs — dried plant materials such as roots, barks, leaves, and seeds — and their identification by macroscopic, microscopic, and chemical characters. Modern pharmacognosy extends to the isolation and structural elucidation of bioactive constituents, biosynthesis, biotechnology and cell culture of medicinal plants, and marine natural products.

History

The discipline grew out of materia medica, the ancient study of medicinal substances systematized by Dioscorides in De materia medica (1st century AD). The word "pharmacognosy" was introduced in the early 19th century (used by Johann Adam Schmidt in 1811 and in print by C. A. Seydler in 1815). Many landmark drugs emerged from this tradition: morphine from opium, quinine from cinchona bark, digoxin from foxglove, and later artemisinin from Artemisia annua, work for which Tu Youyou shared the 2015 Nobel Prize.

Relevance today

Natural products remain a major source of new medicines, particularly antibiotics and anticancer agents, and pharmacognostic methods underpin the quality control and standardization of herbal medicines.
