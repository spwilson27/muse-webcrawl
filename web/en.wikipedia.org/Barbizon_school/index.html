Barbizon school

The Barbizon school of painters were part of an art movement toward Realism in art, which arose in the context of the dominant Romantic movement of the time. It was active roughly from 1830 through 1870. It takes its name from the village of Barbizon, France, near the Forest of Fontainebleau, where many of the artists gathered and lived.

Characteristics and artists

The Barbizon painters rejected the idealised, classical landscape conventions of the French Academy in favour of painting nature directly and realistically, often working en plein air. Leading figures included Théodore Rousseau, Jean-Baptiste-Camille Corot, Jean-François Millet, Charles-François Daubigny, Narcisse Virgilio Díaz de la Peña, Jules Dupré, and Constant Troyon. Their subjects included forest interiors, marshes, and peasant life; Millet's The Gleaners (1857) is among the most famous works associated with the school.

Influence

The school's emphasis on direct observation of nature and tonal landscape painting influenced later movements, particularly Impressionism, as well as landscape painters in Britain, the Netherlands (Hague School), and the United States.
