Genetic fingerprinting

Genetic fingerprinting, also called DNA profiling or DNA fingerprinting, is a laboratory technique used to establish a link between biological evidence and a suspect in a criminal investigation, to test parentage, or to identify human remains. It exploits the fact that certain regions of DNA vary between individuals, so a sample of cells can be used to produce a profile that is (except for identical twins) unique to a person.

Technique

The method was developed in 1984 by Alec Jeffreys at the University of Leicester, who recognised that minisatellites — variable number tandem repeat (VNTR) sequences — could be detected simultaneously with a single probe to give an individual-specific pattern. Modern profiling instead uses polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of short tandem repeat (STR) loci, which are compared at a standard set of markers (for example the CODIS core loci in the United States). The probability that two unrelated people share a full profile is astronomically small.

Applications

Genetic fingerprinting was first used forensically in 1986, in the investigation of the murders of two girls in Leicestershire, England; it exonerated an innocent suspect and led to the conviction of Colin Pitchfork, the first person convicted on DNA evidence. It is now routine in criminal justice, disaster-victim identification, immigration and paternity disputes, and genealogy. National DNA databases store millions of profiles, raising continuing debate about privacy and the retention of samples from people never convicted of a crime. The technique has also been used to exonerate wrongfully convicted prisoners, notably through the work of the Innocence Project.
