William Tyndale

William Tyndale (c. 1494 – October 1536) was an English scholar and Protestant reformer who produced the first printed English translation of the New Testament from the original Greek. His translation profoundly shaped the English language and later versions, including the King James Bible. He was executed for heresy in the Low Countries.

Early life

Tyndale studied at Oxford and Cambridge, becoming fluent in Greek, Hebrew, and several other languages. Influenced by Erasmus and by Martin Luther, he resolved to make the Scriptures available to ordinary English readers — reportedly vowing that "a boy that driveth the plough" should know more of Scripture than his clerical opponents.

Translation work

Denied support in England, Tyndale sailed to Germany in 1524. His New Testament was printed at Worms in 1526 and smuggled into England, where it was denounced and burned. He went on to translate parts of the Old Testament from Hebrew and wrote works such as The Obedience of a Christian Man (1528), which Anne Boleyn brought to the attention of Henry VIII.

Arrest and death

Living in Antwerp, Tyndale was betrayed in 1535, arrested, and imprisoned at Vilvoorde Castle. In 1536 he was convicted of heresy, strangled, and burned at the stake. His reported last words were "Lord, open the King of England's eyes."

Legacy

Tyndale's phrasing passed wholesale into the King James Version of 1611; scholars estimate that the great majority of its New Testament derives from his rendering. Many common English phrases — "let there be light," "the salt of the earth," "a law unto themselves" — trace to Tyndale.
