Goldberg Variations

The Goldberg Variations, BWV 988, is a musical composition for keyboard by Johann Sebastian Bach, consisting of an aria and a set of 30 variations. First published in 1741, it is named after Johann Gottlieb Goldberg, who may have been the first performer of the work. It is considered one of the most important examples of the variation form and a pinnacle of Baroque keyboard music.

Origin

According to an anecdote told by Bach's biographer Johann Nikolaus Forkel, the variations were commissioned by Count Hermann Karl von Keyserlingk, the former Russian ambassador to Saxony, who suffered from insomnia. Goldberg, a young harpsichordist in the Count's service and a pupil of Bach, was to play the variations during the Count's sleepless nights. Bach was reportedly rewarded with a golden goblet filled with 100 louis d'or. The story's accuracy is debated, but the work was published in Nuremberg in 1741 as the fourth part of Bach's Clavier-Übung, under the title "Aria with diverse variations for a harpsichord with two manuals."

Structure

The variations are built not on the aria's melody but on its bass line and harmonic progression. Every third variation is a canon at a progressively widening interval, from the unison to the ninth. Variation 16 is a French overture marking the halfway point, and the cycle includes dances, arabesques, fughettas and the virtuosic hand-crossing variations suited to a two-manual harpsichord. The work concludes with a quodlibet (Variation 30) weaving together folk tunes, after which the aria is repeated da capo.

Recordings and reception

Long regarded as an esoteric technical exercise, the work was brought to wide attention in the 20th century, notably through Glenn Gould's celebrated recordings of 1955 and 1981, which frame strikingly different interpretations. It is now performed on both harpsichord and piano and is regarded as one of the supreme achievements of Western music.
