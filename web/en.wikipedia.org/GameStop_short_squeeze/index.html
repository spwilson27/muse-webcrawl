GameStop short squeeze

In January 2021, a short squeeze of the stock of the American video game retailer GameStop and other securities took place, causing major financial consequences for certain hedge funds and large losses for short sellers. Approximately 140 percent of GameStop's public float had been sold short, and the rush to buy shares to cover those positions as the price rose caused it to rise even further. The short squeeze was initially triggered by users of the subreddit r/wallstreetbets, an Internet forum on the social news website Reddit.

Background

GameStop, a brick-and-mortar retailer, was seen as struggling amid digital game distribution, and its stock was heavily shorted by institutional investors including Melvin Capital. Retail investors, among them Keith Gill (known online as Roaring Kitty / DeepFuckingValue), argued the company was undervalued and began buying shares and call options from 2019–2020 onward, coordinating discussion through social media.

The squeeze

Between January 11 and January 26, 2021, the stock price rose dramatically; at its pre-market high on January 28 it exceeded $500 per share (pre-split). On January 28, several brokerages, most notably Robinhood, halted purchases of GameStop and other "meme stocks", citing clearing-house deposit requirements — a move that drew widespread criticism, lawsuits, and congressional hearings. Melvin Capital lost about 53 percent of its value in January and later shut down in 2022. The volatility spread to other heavily shorted names including AMC Entertainment, BlackBerry, and Nokia.

Aftermath

The episode prompted investigations and reports by the U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission, debate over payment for order flow and gamified trading apps, and lasting cultural interest, including the 2023 film Dumb Money. GameStop used its elevated share price to raise capital and attempted a turnaround into e-commerce.
