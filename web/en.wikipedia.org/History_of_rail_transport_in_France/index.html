History of rail transport in France

The history of rail transport in France dates to 1823, when the first railway concession was granted for a line serving the coal mines of Saint-Étienne. France went on to build one of Europe's densest networks and, in the TGV era, became a world leader in high-speed rail.

Origins and the star network

The Saint-Étienne–Andrézieux line opened in 1827, initially horse-drawn. The 1842 railway law established the "Legrand star" — a radial network centered on Paris built through public-private partnership. Major companies (Nord, PLM, PO, Midi, Est, Ouest) each operated regional monopolies from Parisian termini.

Nationalization and decline

In 1938 the major companies were merged into the state-owned SNCF. Post-war reconstruction emphasized electrification; the 1955 world speed record of 331 km/h by French electric locomotives foreshadowed high-speed ambitions. Competition from cars and motorways from the 1950s caused widespread rural line closures.

The TGV era

The TGV Sud-Est opened between Paris and Lyon in 1981, revolutionizing French rail travel. Successive LGV lines reached the Atlantic (1989), the north and Lille (1993), the Mediterranean (2001), the east (2007), and the southwest and Brittany (2017). In 2007 a modified TGV set the world rail speed record at 574.8 km/h. EU-driven reforms separated infrastructure (now SNCF Réseau) from operations and opened passenger services to competition in the 2020s.
