Council estate

A council estate is a form of British public housing built by local authorities. A council estate consists of a building complex or neighbourhood of council houses (or flats) provided, typically at subsidised rents, to people who need housing and cannot afford the open market. In the decades after the Second World War, council estates housed a large share of the British population.

History

Large-scale council housing began with the Housing, Town Planning, &c. Act 1919 — the "Homes Fit for Heroes" act promoted by David Lloyd George and implemented by Christopher Addison — which funded local councils to clear slums and build new homes. Building peaked in the 1950s and 1960s, including tower blocks and vast estates such as Becontree in London, once the largest public housing development in the world.

Right to Buy and decline

The Housing Act 1980 introduced the Right to Buy, allowing tenants to purchase their homes at a discount; millions did so, and the stock of council housing shrank dramatically as replacements were not built at the same rate. Remaining stock was in many places transferred to housing associations.

Design and reputation

Early cottage estates followed garden-city principles, while post-war modernist estates used system building and high-rise blocks, some of which — like Park Hill in Sheffield — are now listed buildings, and others were demolished after falling into disrepair. Council estates remain a significant cultural reference point in Britain, carrying both stigma and strong community identity.
