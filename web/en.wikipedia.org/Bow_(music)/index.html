Bow (music)

[Image placeholder: violin bow]

In music, a bow is a tensioned stick with hair (usually horsehair) coated in rosin affixed to it, used to produce sound from string instruments such as the violin, viola, cello, and double bass, and from some other chordophones worldwide. Drawing the rosined hair across a string makes the string vibrate through a stick-slip action (Helmholtz motion), producing a sustained tone. The musician varies pitch with the fingers of one hand and controls dynamics, articulation, and timbre with the bow held in the other.

Construction

A modern bow consists of a slender stick — traditionally pernambuco wood, now also carbon fibre or brazilwood — with a ribbon of about 150–200 horsehairs stretched between the tip (point) and the adjustable frog. Turning a screw at the butt end changes hair tension. Bows differ in length, weight, and balance among instruments: a violin bow weighs about 60 grams, a cello bow about 80 grams, while double bass bows come in two patterns, French (held overhand) and German (held underhand).

History

Bowed instruments spread across Asia, the Middle East, and Europe by roughly the 10th century, likely originating in Central Asia. Early European bows were strongly convex, like an archery bow. Through the Baroque and Classical eras the bow lengthened and straightened, and around 1785 François Xavier Tourte standardized the modern design — concave camber, pernambuco stick, and a screw-adjustable frog — sometimes called the "Stradivari of the bow."

Bowing techniques

Players employ many articulations: détaché (separate strokes), legato, staccato, spiccato and sautillé (bounced strokes), martelé, tremolo, col legno (striking with the wood), and pizzicato (plucking instead). Bow direction is conventionally marked in scores: down-bow and up-bow symbols guide phrasing.
