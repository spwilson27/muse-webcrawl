Capital adequacy ratio

DefinitionBank capital ÷ risk-weighted assets
Basel III minimum8% total capital, plus buffers
Regulator frameworkBasel Accords (BCBS)

The capital adequacy ratio (CAR), also known as the capital-to-risk-weighted-assets ratio (CRAR), is a measure of a bank's available capital expressed as a percentage of its risk-weighted credit exposures. Regulators use it to ensure that banks hold enough loss-absorbing capital relative to the riskiness of their assets, protecting depositors and financial stability.

Tier 1 and Tier 2 capital components entering the ratio.

Calculation

CAR = (Tier 1 capital + Tier 2 capital) ÷ Risk-weighted assets. Tier 1 capital is the bank's core capital — common equity and disclosed reserves — able to absorb losses without the bank ceasing operations; it includes common equity Tier 1 (CET1) and additional Tier 1 instruments. Tier 2 capital is supplementary, including subordinated debt and certain reserves, and absorbs losses in liquidation. Assets are weighted by risk: cash and government securities may carry a 0% weight, while unsecured commercial loans carry 100% or more.

Minimum requirements

Under Basel III, banks must maintain total capital of at least 8% of risk-weighted assets, of which at least 4.5% must be CET1 and 6% Tier 1, plus a capital conservation buffer of 2.5% and possible countercyclical and systemic surcharges for large banks. Banks falling below thresholds face restrictions on dividends and bonuses and may be required to raise capital or reduce risk-weighted assets.

Significance

The ratio is a central tool of prudential supervision. Its weaknesses — including gaming of risk weights and the failure of banks judged adequately capitalized before 2008 — motivated the supplementary leverage ratio and stress testing regimes.
