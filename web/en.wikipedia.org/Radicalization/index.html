Radicalization

Radicalization (or radicalisation) is the process by which an individual or a group comes to adopt increasingly radical views in opposition to a political, social, or religious status quo. The term entered widespread use after the September 11 attacks and the bombings in Madrid (2004) and London (2005), as governments sought to understand how individuals come to support or engage in terrorism.

Several models describe radicalization as a staged process, though researchers stress it is neither linear nor inevitable.

Definitions and debates

There is no single agreed definition of radicalization. The Royal Canadian Mounted Police defines it as "the process by which individuals—usually young people—are introduced to an overtly ideological message and belief system that encourages movement from moderate, mainstream beliefs towards extreme views". Critics argue that the concept can conflate radical beliefs, which are legal and sometimes socially beneficial, with violent extremism.

Models

Researchers have proposed several models of the process. The NYPD's 2007 report described four stages: pre-radicalization, self-identification, indoctrination, and "jihadization". Fathali Moghaddam's "staircase to terrorism" model depicts narrowing options perceived by individuals who feel deprived or humiliated. Other frameworks emphasize social networks, group dynamics, and online propaganda rather than individual psychology alone.

Online radicalization

The growth of social media and video platforms has raised concerns about algorithmic amplification and echo chambers exposing users to extremist content. Governments and technology companies have developed counter-messaging and deplatforming strategies, while researchers debate the actual causal weight of online content relative to offline relationships.

Counter-radicalization

Programmes such as the United Kingdom's Prevent strategy and Denmark's Aarhus model aim to identify and support individuals considered at risk. Such programmes remain controversial, with critics warning of stigmatization of communities and infringements on civil liberties.
