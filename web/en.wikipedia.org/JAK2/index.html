Janus kinase 2 (JAK2) is a non-receptor tyrosine kinase and a member of the Janus kinase family, which also includes JAK1, JAK3, and TYK2. In humans it is encoded by the JAK2 gene on chromosome 9. JAK2 mediates signalling by many cytokine and hormone receptors — including those for erythropoietin, thrombopoietin, growth hormone, prolactin, and granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor — through the JAK-STAT pathway.

Function

JAK2 is permanently associated with the intracellular domains of its partner receptors. When a ligand binds, JAK2 molecules phosphorylate each other and the receptor, recruiting and activating STAT transcription factors such as STAT5, as well as the PI3K and MAPK pathways. Because erythropoietin and thrombopoietin signalling depend entirely on JAK2, the kinase is indispensable for the production of red blood cells and platelets; knockout mice die in embryonic life from failure of definitive erythropoiesis.

The V617F mutation

In 2005 several groups identified a recurrent somatic point mutation in JAK2, in which phenylalanine replaces valine at position 617 (V617F) in the pseudokinase domain. The mutation removes the pseudokinase domain's normal autoinhibitory restraint, making the kinase constitutively active. JAK2 V617F is found in about 95% of patients with polycythaemia vera and in roughly half of those with essential thrombocythaemia and primary myelofibrosis, and its discovery transformed the diagnosis and classification of the myeloproliferative neoplasms. Mutations in exon 12 of JAK2 account for most V617F-negative polycythaemia vera.

Clinical relevance

The ATP-competitive JAK1/JAK2 inhibitor ruxolitinib was approved in 2011 for myelofibrosis and later for polycythaemia vera and graft-versus-host disease; fedratinib, a more JAK2-selective inhibitor, followed. Chromosomal rearrangements fusing JAK2 to partners such as ETV6 occur in some leukaemias and lymphomas, and germline JAK2 variants are associated with hereditary thrombocytosis.
