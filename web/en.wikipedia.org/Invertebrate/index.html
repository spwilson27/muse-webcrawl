Invertebrates are animals that neither develop nor retain a vertebral column (commonly known as a backbone or spine), derived from the notochord. It is a paraphyletic grouping: it unites all animals other than the vertebrate subphylum of chordates. Familiar examples include arthropods (insects, arachnids, crustaceans), mollusks (snails, octopuses), annelids (earthworms), echinoderms (starfish) and cnidarians (jellyfish, corals).

Diversity

Invertebrates make up the overwhelming majority of animal diversity — around 97% of all described animal species, and well over a million named species, with millions more undescribed. They range in size from microscopic rotifers (under 0.1 mm) to the colossal squid, and they occupy virtually every habitat on Earth, from deep-sea vents to the upper atmosphere. Many phyla are entirely invertebrate; even within Chordata, tunicates and lancelets are invertebrates.

Characteristics

Lacking a unifying shared ancestry, invertebrates instead share the absence of a backbone and display enormous variety of body plans: radial or bilateral symmetry or none, exoskeletons of chitin or shell, hydrostatic skeletons, and nervous systems from nerve nets to the sophisticated brains of cephalopods. Support is provided by external skeletons, shells or fluid pressure rather than an internal vertebral axis.

Importance

Invertebrates underpin most food webs and ecosystem services: insects pollinate crops and wild plants, worms and marine invertebrates cycle nutrients and build soils and reefs, and crustaceans and mollusks support major fisheries. They are also vital model organisms in science — the fruit fly Drosophila melanogaster and the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans among them — and many, from bees to reef corals, are in decline and of conservation concern.
