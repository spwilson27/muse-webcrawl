Terrain awareness and warning system

A TAWS terrain display in the cockpit

A terrain awareness and warning system (TAWS) is an aircraft safety system designed to prevent controlled flight into terrain (CFIT), historically one of the leading causes of fatal air accidents. TAWS uses digital terrain and obstacle databases, together with the aircraft's position and flight path, to predict conflicts with the ground and alert the flight crew.

Background

TAWS is the generic term adopted by regulators for systems descended from the ground proximity warning system (GPWS), invented by Canadian engineer C. Donald Bateman in the 1960s and mandated for large airliners in the 1970s. GPWS relied on the radio altimeter and gave warnings such as "PULL UP" and "TERRAIN." Enhanced GPWS (EGPWS), marketed as TAWS by manufacturers, added GPS position and a worldwide terrain database, allowing look-ahead warnings and graphical terrain displays colour-coded by proximity.

Classes and mandates

The FAA classifies TAWS equipment as Class A (required for most turbine aircraft with six or more passenger seats), Class B (required for smaller turbine aircraft), and Class C (voluntary, for piston aircraft). Widespread carriage of TAWS is credited with a dramatic reduction in CFIT accidents.
