Saturated fat

Saturated fat is a type of fat in which the fatty acid chains have all single bonds between carbon atoms — they are "saturated" with hydrogen atoms and contain no carbon–carbon double bonds. Saturated fats are generally solid at room temperature. Common dietary sources include fatty meats, butter, lard, cheese and other full-fat dairy products, and the tropical oils coconut oil and palm oil. Examples of saturated fatty acids are stearic acid, palmitic acid, myristic acid and lauric acid.

Chemistry

Because their hydrocarbon chains are straight and un-kinked, saturated fatty acid molecules pack tightly together, which accounts for the relatively high melting points of saturated fats compared with unsaturated oils, whose cis double bonds bend the chain. Fats in food are mixtures: even mostly "saturated" foods such as butter contain substantial monounsaturated fat, and foods are properly described by their overall fatty-acid profile.

Health effects

The relationship between saturated fat and cardiovascular disease has been studied for decades and remains a subject of scientific debate. Consuming saturated fat raises blood levels of LDL cholesterol, an established causal risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. Major health authorities — including the World Health Organization, the American Heart Association and the UK Scientific Advisory Committee on Nutrition — recommend limiting saturated fat to roughly 5–10% of total energy intake and replacing it with unsaturated fats; large reviews find that replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat reduces cardiovascular events. Some meta-analyses have found weak or no independent association between saturated fat intake and mortality, and researchers increasingly emphasise the importance of what replaces saturated fat in the diet and of the whole food matrix rather than the nutrient in isolation.
