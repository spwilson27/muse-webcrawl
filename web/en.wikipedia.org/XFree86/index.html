XFree86 was an implementation of the X Window System that ran on many Unix-like operating systems, most notably Linux and the BSD family. It was the dominant free X server from the early 1990s until the mid-2000s, when most of the community migrated to its fork, the X.Org Server.

History

The project began as X386, an X server for IBM PC compatibles written by Thomas Roell and distributed with X11R4 in 1991. A group of developers maintained it as the free software project XFree86, the "86" referring to the Intel 80386 architecture and the "Free" punning on the homophone "free X eighty-six". The freely available server made graphical Unix on commodity PC hardware practical and became a standard component of Linux distributions through the 1990s.

License controversy and the X.Org fork

In February 2004, the XFree86 Project adopted version 1.1 of the XFree86 License, which added an acknowledgement clause that many distributors and projects, including the GNU Project, considered incompatible with the GNU General Public License. Combined with dissatisfaction over the project's closed development model, the change prompted nearly the entire developer community to move to the X.Org Foundation's fork of XFree86 4.4 RC2. Within months, virtually all major Linux distributions replaced XFree86 with X.Org, and XFree86 itself became largely dormant; its final release, 4.8.0, appeared in December 2008.

Architecture

XFree86 provided both the X server and a large collection of client libraries, fonts, and utilities. Its server supported a wide range of graphics chipsets through built-in drivers, and its configuration was traditionally managed through the XF86Config file, later renamed xorg.conf in the X.Org lineage.
