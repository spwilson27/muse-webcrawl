MP3 (formally MPEG-1 Audio Layer III or MPEG-2 Audio Layer III) is a coding format for digital audio developed largely by the Fraunhofer Society in Germany under the leadership of Karlheinz Brandenburg, with support from other digital scientists in the United States and elsewhere. Originally defined as the third audio format of the MPEG-1 standard, it was retained and further extended — defining additional bit rates and support for more audio channels and sample rates — as the third audio format of the subsequent MPEG-2 standard.

MP3 uses lossy data compression to encode data using inexact approximations and the partial discarding of data, based on a psychoacoustic model of human hearing. This allows a considerable reduction in file size compared to uncompressed audio: a typical MP3 encoded at 128 kbit/s is roughly one-eleventh the size of the corresponding CD audio, with quality acceptable to most listeners.

History

The MPEG-1 standard was published in 1993. The ".mp3" filename extension was chosen in 1995 by the Fraunhofer team after an internal poll. The format's popular breakthrough came in the late 1990s with the spread of personal computers, file-sharing services such as Napster (launched 1999), and portable players such as the iPod (2001). MP3 became the dominant format for digital music distribution and played a central role in debates over copyright and file sharing. Fraunhofer's MP3 licensing programme ended in April 2017, when the last patents expired and the format was effectively released into the public domain of unencumbered standards.

Design

MP3 compression works by exploiting limitations of human hearing — particularly auditory masking, in which a louder sound hides quieter sounds near it in frequency or time. The encoder transforms the audio into frequency sub-bands using a hybrid filter bank, applies a psychoacoustic model to decide which components can be discarded or encoded coarsely, and uses Huffman coding for further lossless compaction. Bit rates commonly range from 96 to 320 kbit/s; variable bit rate (VBR) encoding adjusts the rate according to the complexity of the audio.

ID3 tags

MP3 files commonly carry metadata in ID3 tags, which store information such as title, artist, album, and track number. ID3v2 tags may also embed images such as album artwork.

Successors

Newer formats — principally AAC and later Opus — achieve better quality at the same bit rate and have largely superseded MP3 in streaming and broadcasting, though MP3 remains universally supported by hardware and software.
