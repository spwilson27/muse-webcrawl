Submarine canyon

A submarine canyon is a steep-sided valley cut into the seabed of the continental slope, in some cases extending well onto the continental shelf and rivalling the world's great land canyons in scale. Hundreds are known worldwide; the largest, such as the Bering Sea canyons Zhemchug and Bering Canyon, are among the biggest canyon systems on Earth.

Formation

Submarine canyons are carved and maintained mainly by turbidity currents — fast, sediment-laden flows that cascade down the slope and erode it — together with slumping and other mass wasting. During ice-age low sea levels, rivers flowed across the exposed shelf to the shelf edge, and their sediment fed the canyon heads; some canyons, like the Congo Canyon, still connect directly to a great river mouth today. The 1929 Grand Banks earthquake famously snapped telegraph cables in sequence, giving the first direct evidence of turbidity currents at work.

Examples

Monterey Canyon off California, comparable in depth to the Grand Canyon, begins just offshore and reaches depths of over 3,600 metres; its regular turbidity-current activity is monitored by seafloor observatories. Other notable canyons include Hudson Canyon off New York, the Nazaré Canyon off Portugal — which funnels the giant waves surfed at Nazaré — Kaikōura Canyon off New Zealand, and the Whittard Canyon off Ireland.

Ecology

Canyons concentrate nutrients and organic matter, support cold-water coral gardens and rich fisheries, and act as conduits carrying carbon and, increasingly, litter and microplastics from the shelf to the deep sea.
