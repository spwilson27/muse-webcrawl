History of Afghanistan

The history of Afghanistan spans several millennia. Situated at the crossroads of Central Asia, South Asia and the Middle East, the land that is now Afghanistan has been home to ancient civilisations and a succession of empires, and has repeatedly been an invasion route between them — earning it, in modern times, the label "graveyard of empires".

Prehistory and antiquity

Evidence of farming communities dates to around 7000 BCE, and the region later formed part of the Indus Valley sphere and of Bactria. Around 500 BCE the area was incorporated into the Achaemenid Persian Empire. Alexander the Great conquered it in the 320s BCE, after which Greek influence persisted under the Seleucids and the independent Greco-Bactrian kingdom, which later gave way to the Indo-Greek kingdoms and then the Kushan Empire, a great patron of Buddhism along the Silk Road.

Islamic era

Arab armies brought Islam in the seventh century, though conversion was gradual. The region was ruled in turn by the Saffarids, Samanids, Ghaznavids (whose empire was centred on Ghazni), and Ghurids, and produced major cultural figures such as Rumi, born in Balkh. Mongol invasions under Genghis Khan in the thirteenth century caused widespread devastation. Timur (Tamerlane) later ruled from Herat, which flourished as a centre of art and learning under the Timurid Renaissance.

Early modern period

From the sixteenth century the region was contested between Safavid Persia, the Mughal Empire and the Uzbeks. Pashtun power rose in the eighteenth century: Mirwais Hotak's revolt at Kandahar in 1709 began the Hotak dynasty, which briefly conquered Persia. After Nader Shah's death in 1747, Ahmad Shah Durrani founded the Durrani Empire, widely regarded as the origin of the modern Afghan state.

The Great Game and the kingdom

In the nineteenth century Afghanistan became a buffer between the British and Russian empires. Britain fought the First (1839–42) and Second (1878–80) Anglo-Afghan Wars, and the Durand Line of 1893 fixed the frontier with British India. Under Emir Abdur Rahman Khan (1880–1901) the state was centralised by force. Afghanistan gained full independence in foreign affairs after the Third Anglo-Afghan War of 1919, under King Amanullah Khan, whose rapid reforms provoked a backlash. King Zahir Shah reigned from 1933 to 1973, a long period of stability.

Republic, communism and civil war

Zahir Shah was overthrown by his cousin Mohammad Daoud Khan in 1973, who proclaimed a republic and was himself killed in the communist Saur Revolution of 1978. The Soviet Union invaded in 1979 to support the communist government against mujahideen resistance, withdrawing in 1989 after a costly war. Civil war followed; the Taliban, emerging from Kandahar, captured Kabul in 1996 and imposed strict rule.

2001–present

After the September 11 attacks, a US-led coalition overthrew the Taliban in late 2001 for sheltering al-Qaeda. An Islamic Republic was established under Hamid Karzai and later Ashraf Ghani, supported by international forces for two decades. Following the Doha agreement of 2020 and the withdrawal of US forces, the Taliban retook Kabul in August 2021 and restored the Islamic Emirate, which remains unrecognised internationally.
