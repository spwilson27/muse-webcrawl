Frame Relay

A Frame Relay network interconnecting branch offices through permanent virtual circuits.

Frame Relay is a standardised wide area network (WAN) technology that specifies the physical and data link layers of digital telecommunications channels. It was designed in the late 1980s as a streamlined successor to X.25, taking advantage of the much lower error rates of digital fibre-optic lines by omitting most of X.25's error correction and flow control, which are instead left to the endpoints.

Operation

Frame Relay transmits data in variable-length frames over virtual circuits identified by Data Link Connection Identifiers (DLCIs). Most services used permanent virtual circuits (PVCs), provisioned in advance by the carrier. Customers typically leased a physical access line at one speed and subscribed to a committed information rate (CIR) for each circuit, with the ability to burst above the CIR when capacity allowed; frames sent above the committed rate could be marked discard eligible (DE) and dropped first under congestion.

Congestion is signalled using the FECN and BECN bits (forward and backward explicit congestion notification) in the frame header. The protocol data unit uses a two-byte header containing the 10-bit DLCI, an HDLC-style frame check sequence for detecting (but not correcting) errors, and flags delimiting each frame.

History and legacy

Frame Relay became one of the most popular WAN services of the 1990s for connecting corporate local area networks, because a single physical line could carry many logical circuits to different sites at lower cost than leased lines. From the 2000s it was progressively displaced by IP-based services such as MPLS VPNs and broadband Internet connections, and most carriers have since retired their Frame Relay networks.
