Bézier curve

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia (recreated from memory)

A Bézier curve is a parametric curve used in computer graphics and related fields. A set of discrete "control points" defines a smooth, continuous curve by means of a formula, usually based on Bernstein polynomials. Bézier curves are widely used in vector graphics, animation, computer-aided design, and font technology.

History

The curves are named after Pierre Bézier, an engineer at Renault who publicized them in the 1960s for designing automobile bodies; similar work was done by Paul de Casteljau at Citroën from 1959, and the underlying mathematics traces to Sergei Bernstein's polynomials of 1912. De Casteljau's algorithm remains the standard numerically stable method for evaluating and subdividing the curves.

Definition

A Bézier curve of degree n is defined by n+1 control points P0 through Pn. The linear curve (degree 1) is a straight segment; the quadratic curve (degree 2) uses three points; and the cubic curve (degree 3), the most common in practice, is given by B(t) = (1−t)³P0 + 3(1−t)²tP1 + 3(1−t)t²P2 + t³P3, for 0 ≤ t ≤ 1. The curve always passes through the first and last control points, is tangent to the control polygon at its ends, and lies within the convex hull of its control points.

Applications

Cubic Bézier curves underpin PostScript, PDF, SVG paths, and TrueType/PostScript font outlines; they are the standard "pen tool" curves in illustration software. Chains of curves form Bézier splines and paths, and the concept generalizes to Bézier surfaces in 3D modeling.
