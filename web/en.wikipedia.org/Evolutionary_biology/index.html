Evolutionary biology is the subfield of biology that studies the evolutionary processes — natural selection, common descent, and speciation — that produced the diversity of life on Earth. It seeks to explain both the adaptation of organisms to their environments and the branching history of species over geological time.

History

The modern field began with Charles Darwin and Alfred Russel Wallace, who independently proposed natural selection as the mechanism of evolution in 1858; Darwin's On the Origin of Species (1859) marshalled the evidence. Darwin lacked a theory of inheritance, which was supplied by Gregor Mendel's genetics, rediscovered in 1900. The modern synthesis of the 1930s and 1940s unified selection, Mendelian genetics and palaeontology, and later work incorporated molecular evolution, genetic drift, endosymbiosis and developmental biology ("evo-devo").

Subfields and evidence

Evolutionary biologists draw on the fossil record, comparative anatomy, biogeography and, increasingly, genome sequencing, which allows phylogenies to be reconstructed from DNA. Subfields include population genetics, phylogenetics, evolutionary ecology and palaeontology. As Theodosius Dobzhansky wrote in 1973, "nothing in biology makes sense except in the light of evolution."
