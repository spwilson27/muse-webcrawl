The Treaty of Sèvres was a 1920 treaty signed between the Allies of World War I and the Ottoman Empire on 10 August 1920 in Sèvres, France. It partitioned the Ottoman Empire, reducing it to a rump state in Anatolia under heavy Allied control. The treaty was never ratified by the Ottoman parliament and was rendered void by the Turkish victory in the Turkish War of Independence; it was replaced by the much more favourable Treaty of Lausanne in 1923.

Terms

The treaty detached all of the empire's remaining Arab provinces: the Hejaz was recognised as independent, while Syria, Mesopotamia (Iraq) and Palestine became League of Nations mandates under French and British control, and the Arabian territories and Egypt's status were settled on Allied terms. Within Anatolia and Thrace, the treaty gave Eastern Thrace and the Smyrna (İzmir) zone to Greek administration, with a plebiscite to follow; envisaged an independent Armenia in the east (the "Wilson Armenia" drawn by President Woodrow Wilson's arbitral award); and provided for possible Kurdish autonomy or independence. The Straits were demilitarised under an international commission, the Ottoman army was limited to a small gendarmerie, the capitulations were restored, and Allied financial control over the Ottoman budget was imposed.

Rejection and war

For Turkish opinion the treaty was a national humiliation — in Turkish political discourse, "Sèvres syndrome" still denotes the fear that foreign powers plot Turkey's partition. The sultan's government in Istanbul, under Allied occupation after the formal occupation of the capital in March 1920, signed the treaty, but Mustafa Kemal's nationalist movement, based in Ankara, repudiated it. Greek forces advancing from Smyrna deep into Anatolia were defeated at the Sakarya (1921) and at Dumlupınar (1922), and the nationalists recovered all of Anatolia and Eastern Thrace. With Allied unity broken — France and Italy negotiated separately with Ankara, and the Chanak Crisis of 1922 nearly brought Britain to war — the Armistice of Mudanya replaced the wartime settlement in the straits zone.

Lausanne and legacy

The Treaty of Lausanne (24 July 1923) recognised the sovereignty of the Republic of Turkey within essentially its modern borders, abolished the capitulations, and abandoned the Sèvres projects for Armenia and Kurdistan — though the Kurdish and Armenian questions the treaty had raised continue to shape regional politics. Sèvres, together with Trianon and Saint-Germain, is one of the lapsed Paris peace settlements of 1919–1920, and its contrast with Lausanne is a staple of Turkish national historiography.
