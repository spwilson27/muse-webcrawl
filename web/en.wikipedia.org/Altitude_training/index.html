Altitude training

Endurance athletes train at altitude to stimulate red blood cell production.

Altitude training is the practice by endurance athletes of training for several weeks at high altitude, traditionally above 2,400 metres (8,000 ft), in the hope of gaining a competitive advantage at sea level. The reduced oxygen availability stimulates the body to produce more red blood cells, potentially improving oxygen delivery when the athlete returns to lower altitude.

Physiology

Hypoxia at altitude triggers release of erythropoietin (EPO) from the kidneys, increasing haemoglobin mass over several weeks. This is the same pathway exploited illegally by recombinant EPO doping and blood doping, which altitude training mimics legally. Other proposed adaptations include improved buffering capacity and changes in muscle metabolism.

Methods

Because training intensity suffers in thin air, the influential "live high, train low" model — sleeping at altitude (or in altitude tents and hypoxic rooms that simulate it) while training hard at low altitude — was developed by Benjamin Levine and James Stray-Gundersen in the 1990s to preserve both adaptations and workout quality. Altitude camps in places such as Iten (Kenya), Flagstaff (Arizona), Font-Romeu (France) and Boulder (Colorado) are popular with elite distance runners and cyclists.

Evidence and debate

Research supports modest benefits for elite endurance athletes when protocols are well managed, but responses vary widely between individuals, and poor management of iron status, illness and training load can erase gains. Interest grew after the 1968 Mexico City Olympics, held at 2,240 m, highlighted altitude's effects on endurance performance.
