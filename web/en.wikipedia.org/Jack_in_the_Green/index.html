Jack in the Green

Jack in the Green is a traditional English folk custom associated with May Day celebrations. The central figure, Jack, wears a large conical or pyramidal framework of wicker or wood completely covered in greenery, leaves and flowers, so that he appears as a walking bush, and processes through the streets accompanied by musicians, Morris dancers and attendants.

History

The custom developed in the late 18th century, growing out of the May Day garlands carried by milkmaids and other trades, and became particularly associated with chimney sweeps in London and other towns during the late 18th and 19th centuries. Competition between rival Jacks and their accompanying dancers was fierce. The custom declined in the early 20th century as sweep processions were suppressed and May Day customs fell out of favour.

Revival

Jack in the Green was revived from the late 20th century. The best-known modern celebrations take place in Hastings, where the Jack is ceremonially "slain" at the end of the festival to release the spirit of summer, and at Rochester in Kent. Similar Jacks appear at May Day events across England, including Bristol, Deptford and Whitstable.

Interpretation

Folklorists have debated the figure's origins; early antiquarians linked such greenery figures to medieval church carvings of foliate heads ("Green Men"), though historians now regard the Jack primarily as a product of early modern urban street culture.
