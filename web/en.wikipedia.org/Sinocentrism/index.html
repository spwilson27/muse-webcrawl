Sinocentrism

Sinocentrism refers to the ideologies that regard China as the cultural, political, or economic centre of the world. Historically, it was the political theory in East and Southeast Asia that China was the centre of the civilised world, surrounded by barbarian peoples, and that neighbouring states should acknowledge Chinese supremacy through the tributary system.

The name Zhongguo (中國), commonly translated as "Middle Kingdom", reflects this worldview: the emperor, holding the Mandate of Heaven, ruled tianxia ("all under heaven") from the centre. Foreign relations were conceptualised as tribute missions from lesser rulers, and the imperial court ritual hierarchy placed China at the apex.

Historical system

The Sinocentric order structured diplomacy in East Asia for centuries, involving Korea, Vietnam, the Ryukyu Kingdom, Japan (intermittently) and states of Central and Southeast Asia. The system began to break down in the 19th century as Western powers imposed treaty relations on the Qing dynasty during the "century of humiliation", beginning with the Opium Wars and the Treaty of Nanking (1842).

Modern usage

In contemporary usage, "Sinocentric" may describe historical scholarship that treats Chinese sources as authoritative, foreign policies centred on China, or cultural attitudes emphasising Chinese centrality. It is often compared with Eurocentrism.
