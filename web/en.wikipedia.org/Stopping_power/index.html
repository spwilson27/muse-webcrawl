Stopping power

Stopping power is the ability of a weapon — typically a ranged weapon such as a firearm — to incapacitate or cause a target to cease whatever they are doing. The term is used almost exclusively in the context of handguns and hunting rifles, and is not a measure of a projectile's power in the physical sense. It stands in contrast to lethality: a projectile may be lethal (cause death) without reliably stopping a determined target quickly.

Stopping power is related to the physical properties of the bullet and the effects it has on its target, but the issue is complicated and not easily studied. Although higher-calibre ammunitions are generally thought to have greater stopping power, various factors — shot placement, penetration, bullet expansion, and the psychological state of the target — all contribute. The debate over stopping power has run for more than a century and produced competing indices such as the Taylor KO Factor and the Hatcher relative stopping power scale.

History

The concept gained prominence during American colonial conflicts. The Thompson–LaGarde Tests of 1904 were commissioned after reports that the .38 Long Colt revolvers used in the Philippine–American War had failed to stop Moro juramentado fighters. The tests, and John Taliaferro Thompson's and Louis A. LaGarde's conclusions favouring larger calibres, contributed to the adoption of the .45 ACP cartridge and the M1911 pistol. Julian Hatcher's later work in Textbook of Pistols and Revolvers (1935) attempted to quantify relative stopping power from bullet momentum, cross-sectional area and bullet shape.

Factors

Penetration and expansion

A bullet must penetrate deeply enough to reach vital structures. Expanding bullets, such as jacketed hollow-points, increase in diameter as they pass through tissue, enlarging the permanent wound cavity and transferring energy more rapidly. The FBI's ammunition testing protocol, developed after the 1986 Miami shootout, emphasises adequate penetration (12–18 inches in calibrated ballistic gelatin) as the primary criterion, with expansion secondary.

Psychological effects

Many "stops" are psychological rather than physiological: a person who is shot may cease hostile action because of pain, fright, or the realisation that they have been hit, regardless of the physical severity of the wound. This makes stopping power statistics difficult to interpret statistically, and one-shot stop percentages compiled from shooting reports remain controversial.

Criticism

Modern wound-ballistics researchers generally argue that reliable incapacitation results from hitting the central nervous system or from rapid blood loss causing unconsciousness, and that small differences in handgun calibre are far less important than shot placement. Under this view, "stopping power" as an intrinsic property of a cartridge is largely a myth, though the term remains in common use.
