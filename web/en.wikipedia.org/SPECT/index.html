Single-photon emission computed tomography

Single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT, or less commonly SPET) is a nuclear medicine tomographic imaging technique using gamma rays. It is very similar to conventional nuclear medicine planar imaging using a gamma camera, but is able to provide true 3D information. The patient receives an injection of a radiopharmaceutical — commonly labelled with technetium-99m — whose distribution reflects tissue function such as blood flow or receptor binding, and one or more gamma camera heads rotate around the patient acquiring projections from which cross-sectional images are reconstructed.

A SPECT scanner and reconstructed tomographic slices.

Principles

Unlike positron emission tomography (PET), which detects paired annihilation photons, SPECT detects single gamma photons emitted by the tracer. Collimators in front of the detectors define the direction of accepted photons, trading sensitivity for spatial resolution. Reconstruction algorithms, increasingly iterative methods with attenuation and scatter correction, assemble the projections into volumetric images, often fused with CT (SPECT/CT) for anatomical localisation.

Applications

SPECT is widely used for myocardial perfusion imaging to assess coronary artery disease, for bone scans, brain perfusion and dopamine transporter imaging in movement disorders, parathyroid localisation, and tumour imaging with labelled antibodies and peptides. It is generally less expensive and more widely available than PET, though with lower spatial resolution and sensitivity.
