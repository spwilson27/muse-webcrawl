Iberian Union

The Iberian Union was the dynastic union of the Monarchy of Spain and the Crown of Portugal that lasted from 1580 to 1640, uniting the whole of the Iberian Peninsula — and the two largest colonial empires of the age — under the Spanish Habsburg monarchs. It began after the Portuguese succession crisis of 1580, when Philip II of Spain asserted his claim to the Portuguese throne, and it ended with the Portuguese Restoration War and the acclamation of John IV of Portugal.

Establishment

When the young King Sebastian of Portugal was killed at the Battle of Alcácer Quibir in Morocco in 1578, the throne passed to his aged great-uncle, Cardinal Henry, whose death in January 1580 left the succession disputed. Philip II of Spain, a grandson of Manuel I of Portugal through his mother Isabella, pressed his claim with diplomacy, bribery and, ultimately, an army under the Duke of Alba, which defeated the forces of the Prior of Crato, António, at Alcântara in August 1580. Philip was recognised as Philip I of Portugal at the Cortes of Tomar in 1581.

At Tomar, Philip promised to preserve Portugal as a formally separate kingdom: its laws, currency, language of government and institutions were to remain Portuguese, offices were reserved for Portuguese subjects, and the union was one of crowns rather than of states. A Council of Portugal advised the king in Madrid, and a viceroy or governor represented him in Lisbon.

The union under strain

Under Philip II the terms of Tomar were broadly honoured, but under his successors the burden of Spain's wars fell increasingly on Portugal. The Dutch, now enemies of the Spanish monarchy, attacked Portuguese possessions from Brazil to Angola, Malacca and the Moluccas, while Olivares's plans for fiscal and military integration (the Union of Arms) eroded Portuguese autonomy and provoked resentment among the nobility and populace.

Restoration

On 1 December 1640, taking advantage of Spain's distraction by the Catalan revolt and war with France, Portuguese nobles stormed the palace in Lisbon and acclaimed the Duke of Braganza as King John IV. The subsequent Restoration War lasted until Spain finally recognised Portuguese independence in the Treaty of Lisbon of 1668.
