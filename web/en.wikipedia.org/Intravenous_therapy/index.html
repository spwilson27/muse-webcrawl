Intravenous therapy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Intravenous therapy (abbreviated IV therapy) is a medical technique that administers fluids, medications and nutrients directly into a person's vein. The intravenous route is the fastest way to deliver substances throughout the body, and it is used for fluid replacement in dehydration and shock, for blood transfusions, for medicines that cannot be given orally, and for correcting electrolyte imbalances.

Access

IV access is most commonly obtained with a peripheral cannula inserted into a vein of the hand or forearm. When longer-term or specialised access is needed, central venous catheters are placed into large veins (such as the internal jugular, subclavian or femoral), including peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs) and implanted ports. In emergencies, intraosseous access into bone marrow can substitute when veins cannot be obtained.

Fluids and substances

Crystalloid solutions include normal saline (0.9% sodium chloride), lactated Ringer's solution, and dextrose solutions of varying strength. Colloid solutions and blood products — packed red cells, plasma, platelets and clotting factors — are given for specific indications. Medications administered intravenously range from antibiotics and chemotherapy agents to analgesics, sedatives and electrolytes such as potassium chloride; parenteral nutrition provides complete intravenous feeding.

Administration

Fluids run by gravity through a drip chamber or are controlled by infusion pumps that regulate the rate precisely. IV drugs may be given as a bolus (push), as intermittent infusions ("piggyback"), or as continuous infusions.

Complications

Complications include infection at the insertion site or in the bloodstream, phlebitis (inflammation of the vein), infiltration or extravasation of fluid into surrounding tissue, air embolism, fluid overload, and, for central lines, pneumothorax during insertion. Aseptic technique, line care bundles and timely removal reduce these risks.

History

Experiments with intravenous injection date to the seventeenth century (including Christopher Wren's injections into animals), and intravenous saline was used during the cholera epidemics of the 1830s. Practical IV therapy developed with sterile technique, flexible plastic cannulas in the mid-twentieth century, and modern infusion pumps.
