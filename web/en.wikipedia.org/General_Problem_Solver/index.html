General Problem Solver

The General Problem Solver (GPS) was an early computer program created in 1957 by Herbert A. Simon, J. C. Shaw, and Allen Newell at the RAND Corporation and Carnegie Institute of Technology. It was one of the first programs in the field of artificial intelligence, and was intended, as its name suggests, to work as a universal problem-solving machine that could in principle solve any well-defined problem given suitable input.

Means-ends analysis

GPS's central method was means-ends analysis. The program compared the current state of a problem with the goal state, identified the most important difference between them, and searched for an "operator" — an action known to reduce that kind of difference — whose preconditions it then attempted to satisfy as a subgoal. The process recursed until the goal was reached or no operator applied. Working in the IPL list-processing language, GPS solved problems in domains such as symbolic logic proofs, the Tower of Hanoi, and simple algebra word problems.

Significance and limitations

GPS grew out of the same group's Logic Theorist (1956) and helped establish the information-processing approach in cognitive psychology: Simon and Newell compared the program's step-by-step behaviour with human subjects' spoken "think-aloud" protocols for the same problems, and used the match to argue that both were searching heuristic problem spaces. In practice the program's generality was limited: search exploded combinatorially on hard problems, and success depended on how knowledgeably the problem was formulated for it. Newell and Simon later summarised and criticised the work in their 1972 book Human Problem Solving. The ideas behind GPS influenced later architectures such as Soar and the means-ends analysis used in planning systems like STRIPS.
