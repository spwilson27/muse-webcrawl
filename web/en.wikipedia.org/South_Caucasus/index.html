South Caucasus

South Caucasus (Transcaucasia)
Countries: Armenia, Azerbaijan, Georgia
Region: south of the Greater Caucasus watershed, between the Black Sea and Caspian Sea
Population: about 17 million

The South Caucasus, also known as Transcaucasia, is the region on the southern flank of the Greater Caucasus mountain range, bounded by the Black Sea to the west, the Caspian Sea to the east, Russia to the north, and Iran and Turkey to the south. It comprises the modern states of Armenia, Azerbaijan and Georgia.

History

The region is one of the oldest centres of civilisation, home to early wine-making, metalworking and some of the first Christian states — Armenia adopted Christianity in 301 AD and Georgia soon after. Successively contested by Roman, Persian, Arab, Mongol, Ottoman and Russian empires, the South Caucasus was incorporated into the Russian Empire in the 19th century and later formed three republics of the Soviet Union. Brief independence in 1918–1921, including a short-lived Transcaucasian federation, was followed by Sovietisation.

Post-Soviet period

All three states regained independence in 1991. The transition was marked by armed conflicts: the Nagorno-Karabakh conflict between Armenia and Azerbaijan, and wars in Georgia's Abkhazia and South Ossetia regions, culminating in the 2008 Russo-Georgian War. Azerbaijan's Caspian oil and gas, exported via the Baku–Tbilisi–Ceyhan pipeline, made the region an important energy corridor between Central Asia and Europe.

Geography and peoples

The Lesser Caucasus and Armenian Highlands dominate the interior; the Kura and Araxes rivers drain to the Caspian. The region is exceptionally diverse linguistically, with three indigenous language families (Kartvelian, Northeast and Northwest Caucasian) alongside Indo-European Armenian and Turkic Azerbaijani.
