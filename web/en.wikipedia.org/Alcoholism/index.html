Alcoholism

Alcoholism (alcohol use disorder)
SpecialtyPsychiatry, addiction medicine
SymptomsCraving, loss of control, tolerance, withdrawal, continued use despite harm
Risk factorsGenetics, family history, stress, early drinking, mental illness
ComplicationsCirrhosis, pancreatitis, dementia, cancers, accidents, suicide
TreatmentCounselling, mutual-aid groups, medication (naltrexone, acamprosate, disulfiram)

Alcoholism is the older and still widely used term for a chronic condition characterised by an inability to control alcohol drinking despite harmful consequences; in modern clinical usage it corresponds largely to alcohol use disorder (AUD) in the DSM-5 and to harmful use or dependence on alcohol in the ICD. The World Health Organization estimates that alcohol causes about 2.6 million deaths per year worldwide, and that hundreds of millions of people live with alcohol use disorders.

Signs, symptoms and diagnosis

Features include drinking more or longer than intended, persistent desire or unsuccessful efforts to cut down, craving, failure to fulfil major obligations, continued use despite social or health problems, tolerance, and withdrawal symptoms on stopping. Screening tools include the AUDIT questionnaire and the CAGE questions. Genetic factors account for roughly 40–60% of the risk, alongside environment, age of first use and co-occurring mental disorders.

Health effects

Long-term heavy drinking damages nearly every organ system: liver disease progressing from fatty liver to alcoholic hepatitis and cirrhosis; pancreatitis; cardiomyopathy; peripheral neuropathy; Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome from thiamine deficiency; increased risks of several cancers (including mouth, oesophagus, liver, colon and breast); and fetal alcohol spectrum disorders when drinking occurs in pregnancy. Sudden cessation after dependence can trigger the potentially fatal alcohol withdrawal syndrome.

Treatment

Treatment combines medically managed withdrawal where needed, psychological therapies (motivational interviewing, cognitive behavioural therapy, relapse prevention), mutual-aid organisations such as Alcoholics Anonymous and SMART Recovery, and medications including naltrexone, acamprosate and disulfiram. Public-health measures — taxation, availability limits and drink-driving laws — reduce population-level harm.

History and society

The concept of alcoholism as a disease was articulated by Magnus Huss (1849) and popularised in the 20th century by E. M. Jellinek and the temperance and recovery movements. Attitudes remain contested between disease, moral and social-learning models, and stigma continues to deter treatment.
