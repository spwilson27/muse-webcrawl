Social norm

Social norms are shared standards of acceptable behaviour by groups. Social norms can be both formal, such as laws, and informal, governing casual interactions. They create expectations about how people will behave and provide order and predictability in social life. Norms are distinct from mere regularities: they carry a sense of obligation and are typically enforced by social sanctions, from mild disapproval to ostracism.

Types of norms

The sociologist William Graham Sumner distinguished three types of norms in his 1906 work Folkways:

  • Folkways are customs and conventions of everyday life, such as dress codes and table manners; violating them attracts mild disapproval.
  • Mores are norms embodying the moral views of a group; violating them attracts stronger sanctions.
  • Laws are norms formally codified and enforced by political authority.

Psychologists also distinguish descriptive norms (perceptions of what most people actually do) from injunctive norms (perceptions of what people approve or disapprove of).

Emergence and change

Norms emerge from repeated interactions and shared expectations. They can spread through socialisation, imitation and institutional enforcement, and they can change — sometimes rapidly — when enough people revise their beliefs about what others do or approve of. Scholars such as Cristina Bicchieri have modelled norm change in terms of shifting empirical and normative expectations within a reference group.

Enforcement and deviance

People who violate norms are often labelled deviant, and groups apply informal sanctions ranging from gossip and ridicule to exclusion. Informal enforcement by third parties — punishing violators at a cost to oneself — is considered a key mechanism sustaining cooperation in large societies.
