Genomic imprinting

Genomic imprinting
FieldEpigenetics, genetics
MechanismParent-of-origin-specific DNA methylation

Genomic imprinting is an epigenetic phenomenon that causes genes to be expressed or not, depending on whether they are inherited from the female or male parent. Genes can also be partially imprinted. Partial imprinting happens when alleles from both parents are differently expressed rather than complete expression and complete suppression of one parent's allele.

In imprinting, only the allele inherited from one parent is expressed; the other is silenced by methylation.

Forms of genomic imprinting have been demonstrated in fungi, plants and animals. In 2014, there were about 150 imprinted genes known in mice and about half that number in humans; by 2019, the Geneimprint database listed 192 imprinted genes in mice and 141 in humans. Imprinting is established in the germline: methylation marks at imprinting control regions are erased in primordial germ cells and then reset according to the sex of the individual producing the gametes, and maintained through embryonic development by proteins such as DNMT1 and ZFP57.

Mechanism

The central mark is DNA methylation at CpG-rich imprinting control regions (ICRs). Methylation of an ICR on one parental chromosome silences (or, for some loci, activates) a cluster of nearby genes in cis. At the famous H19/IGF2 locus, methylation of the paternal ICR prevents binding of the insulator protein CTCF, allowing enhancers to activate IGF2; on the unmethylated maternal chromosome, CTCF binds and insulates IGF2, so H19 is expressed instead.

Disorders

Disruption of imprinting causes several human disorders. Loss of paternal gene expression at chromosome 15q11-13 causes Prader–Willi syndrome, while loss of the maternal UBE3A allele in the same region causes Angelman syndrome; uniparental disomy of chromosome 15 can produce either condition. Errors at 11p15 cause Beckwith–Wiedemann and Silver–Russell syndromes. Imprinting defects are also suspected to contribute to some cancers, including Wilms tumour.

Evolutionary theories

The leading explanation, David Haig's kinship (parental conflict) theory, holds that paternally expressed genes tend to promote fetal growth (extracting more resources from the mother), while maternally expressed genes tend to restrain it — a tug-of-war visible at the IGF2 (paternally expressed growth promoter) / IGF2R (maternally expressed) locus. Imprinting may also relate to the demands of placental development and to defence against parthenogenesis.
