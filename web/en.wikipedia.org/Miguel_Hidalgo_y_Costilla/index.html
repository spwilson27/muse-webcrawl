Miguel Gregorio Antonio Ignacio Hidalgo y Costilla y Gallaga Mandarte Villaseñor (8 May 1753 – 30 July 1811), commonly known as Miguel Hidalgo, was a Mexican Roman Catholic priest and a leader of the Mexican War of Independence. He is revered in Mexico as the Father of the Nation.

Early life

Hidalgo was born at the Corralejo hacienda near Pénjamo, Guanajuato, to criollo parents. He studied at the Colegio de San Nicolás in Valladolid (now Morelia), later becoming its rector. Ordained a priest in 1778, he eventually served in the parish of Dolores, where he promoted education, agriculture, and industry among his parishioners and read Enlightenment writers whose works were restricted by the Church and Crown.

Grito de Dolores

In 1810 Hidalgo joined a conspiracy of criollos in Querétaro seeking independence from Spain. When the plot was discovered, on the morning of 16 September 1810 he rang the church bell in Dolores and addressed his parishioners in the event known as the Grito de Dolores (Cry of Dolores), calling them to rise against Spanish rule. He raised an army of indigenous and mestizo followers, carrying a banner of the Virgin of Guadalupe.

Campaign and death

Hidalgo's forces took Celaya, Guanajuato, and Valladolid, and marched on Mexico City, defeating royalists at Monte de las Cruces in October 1810, though he controversially decided not to assault the capital. Defeated at the Battle of Calderón Bridge in January 1811, he fled north, was betrayed and captured at Acatita de Baján, defrocked, and executed by firing squad at Chihuahua on 30 July 1811. His head was displayed at the Alhóndiga de Granaditas in Guanajuato for a decade.

Legacy

Mexico celebrates 16 September as Independence Day, and each year the President reenacts the Grito from the National Palace balcony. The Mexican state of Hidalgo bears his name, and his remains rest in the Angel of Independence monument in Mexico City.
