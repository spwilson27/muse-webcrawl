Opposition to immigration

Opposition to immigration, also known as anti-immigrationism, is a political position that seeks to restrict or reduce immigration, usually into a particular country. Opposition has recurred across eras and societies, ranging from mainstream restrictionist policies to nativist and ethnic-nationalist movements, and it has shaped landmark legislation such as the US Chinese Exclusion Act of 1882 and the Immigration Act of 1924, Australia's White Australia policy, and modern debates in Europe and North America.

Arguments

Arguments advanced for restriction include claims about labour-market competition and wage suppression, pressure on welfare systems and public services, housing costs, national security and public order, environmental and population concerns, and the preservation of cultural identity or social cohesion. Critics of restriction respond with evidence on the economic contributions of immigrants, demographic needs of ageing societies, humanitarian and legal obligations to refugees, and argue that many concerns are empirically weak or reflect prejudice.

History

Nativist movements appeared in the nineteenth-century United States (the Know Nothings), while the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries saw racially framed exclusion laws across the settler democracies. After 1945, postwar labour migration and decolonisation brought new politics of immigration in Western Europe; the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries saw restrictionist parties gain ground in many democracies, particularly after the 2015 European migrant crisis. Public opinion research generally finds attitudes vary with economic conditions, the pace of change, and perceptions of control and fairness, rather than immigrant numbers alone.
