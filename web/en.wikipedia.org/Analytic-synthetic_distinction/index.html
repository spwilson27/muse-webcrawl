Analytic–synthetic distinction

Immanuel Kant introduced the analytic/synthetic distinction into philosophy.

The analytic–synthetic distinction is a semantic distinction, used primarily in philosophy to distinguish between propositions (in particular, statements that are affirmative subject–predicate judgments) that are analytic, whose truth or falsity depends solely on the meanings of their terms, and those that are synthetic, whose truth also depends on facts about the world. "All bachelors are unmarried" is the classic analytic example; "All bachelors are happy" is synthetic.

Kant and the empiricists

Immanuel Kant introduced the distinction in the Critique of Pure Reason (1781), combining it with the a priori/a posteriori distinction and arguing controversially that some knowledge — such as mathematics and the principles of natural science — is synthetic a priori. The logical positivists of the 20th century leaned heavily on the analytic category, treating mathematical and logical truths as analytic and framing metaphysical statements as meaningless.

Quine's critique

In his 1951 paper "Two Dogmas of Empiricism", W. V. O. Quine attacked the distinction, arguing that attempts to define "analytic" rely on circular notions such as synonymy and definition, and that no clear boundary can be drawn in practice. Quine's criticism had a large influence on subsequent philosophy, though later philosophers — including in response debates by H. P. Grice and P. F. Strawson — have attempted to defend versions of the distinction.
