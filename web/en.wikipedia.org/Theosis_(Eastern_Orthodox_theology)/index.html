Theosis (Eastern Orthodox theology)

In Eastern Orthodox theology, theosis (Greek: θέωσις, "deification") is the process by which a Christian becomes united with God, sharing in his life by grace and becoming "all that Christ is by nature, save only identity of essence." It is presented as the very purpose of the Christian life and the meaning of salvation: not merely a legal pardon of sins, but the healing and transformation of the human person, body and soul, culminating in resurrection and the vision of God.

Biblical and patristic foundations

Orthodox theologians ground theosis in Scripture — especially 2 Peter 1:4, "partakers of the divine nature" — and in the Fathers: Irenaeus of Lyons, who taught that Christ "became what we are, that He might bring us to be even what He is Himself"; Athanasius, with his formula "He was made man, that we men might become gods"; the Cappadocians; Maximus the Confessor; and Symeon the New Theologian, who described conscious experience of the Holy Spirit as light. In the fourteenth century Gregory Palamas defended Hesychast monks who claimed to see the uncreated light of the Transfiguration, distinguishing God's essence, forever unknowable, from his energies, which are truly God and truly shareable; councils at Constantinople (1341–1351) affirmed his teaching as Orthodox doctrine.

Practice

Theosis is not an individual achievement but life in the Church: begun in baptism, nourished by the Eucharist and the other sacraments, and pursued ascetically through repentance, prayer of the heart (the Jesus Prayer), fasting, vigils, and works of mercy. The way is traditionally divided into purification, illumination, and union. The goal is likened to iron in the fire: the iron becomes glowing and fiery while remaining iron. Saints are held to manifest theosis in this life — in incorrupt relics, clairvoyance, and above all humility and love — and its fullness is eschatological, realized in the resurrection of the dead and the transfigured cosmos.

Modern exposition

Twentieth-century Orthodox theologians such as Vladimir Lossky (The Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church), John Romanides, Metropolitan Hierotheos (Vlachos), and Kallistos Ware made theosis central to Orthodox presentations to the West, and the doctrine has become a major topic in ecumenical dialogue with Roman Catholic, Lutheran, and Wesleyan theologians.
