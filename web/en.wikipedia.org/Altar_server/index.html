Altar server

Role: Lay assistant to clergy during liturgy
Traditions: Roman Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran, Eastern Orthodox
Vestments: Alb or cassock and surplice
Historical term: Acolyte

An altar server is a lay assistant to a member of the clergy during a religious service. Servers help at the altar by carrying the cross, candles, thurible, and processional items, holding liturgical books, presenting the water and wine at the offertory, ringing the altar bell, and assisting the priest or bishop as needed. Altar serving is found in the Roman Catholic Church, Eastern Orthodox and Eastern Catholic churches, and Anglican, Lutheran, and some other traditions.

History

The role derives from the minor order of acolyte in the early Church. For centuries serving was restricted to boys and men, and it was often seen as a seedbed for priestly vocations. In the Roman Catholic Church, girls and women were widely admitted after the Congregation for Divine Worship confirmed in 1994 that bishops could permit female altar servers, a practice later reaffirmed by Pope Francis in 2021, who also opened the instituted ministry of acolyte to women.

Duties and dress

Servers usually wear an alb, or a cassock (often red) with a white surplice. Senior servers may act as master of ceremonies. Training typically covers the order of Mass, the names of vessels and vestments, and processional order, sometimes through guilds such as the Guild of St Stephen.
