Bascule bridge


A bascule bridge in the raised position

A bascule bridge (also referred to as a drawbridge or a lifting bridge) is a moveable bridge with a counterweight that continuously balances a span, or leaf, throughout its upward swing to provide clearance for boat traffic. It may be single- or double-leafed.

The name comes from the French term for balance scale, after the seesaw-like action of the span. Bascule bridges have been in use since ancient times and were redeveloped with powered mechanisms in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. Joseph Strauss, designer of the Golden Gate Bridge, began his career designing bascule bridges.

Types

The single-leaf bridge has a hinge at one end of the span. In a double-leaf bridge, two leaves meet at the centre of the channel; the Tower Bridge in London is a famous double-leaf example, combining bascule leaves with suspension side spans. Counterweights may be located above the deck, below the deck, or within the pier. Designs are classified by the position of the trunnion (pivot) and the arrangement of the counterweight, including the heel trunnion and rolling lift types developed in Chicago, where dozens of bascule bridges cross the Chicago River.

Advantages and disadvantages

Bascule bridges open quickly, require relatively little power, and impose no height restriction on passing vessels. Their piers, housing machinery and counterweights, are massive, and the mechanism demands regular maintenance.
