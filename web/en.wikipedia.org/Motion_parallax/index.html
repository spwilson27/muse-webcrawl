Motion parallax

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Motion parallax is a depth cue arising from the relative motion of objects across the visual field when an observer moves. When the viewpoint translates — for example, when a passenger looks out of a train window — nearer objects sweep across the retina faster than distant ones, and objects nearer than the point of fixation move in the opposite direction to more distant objects. The visual system uses this pattern of optic flow to recover the relative distances of objects in the scene.

As a depth cue

Motion parallax is a monocular depth cue, available to a single eye, and is considered one of the strongest cues to depth from motion. It is closely related to the kinetic depth effect and to binocular parallax (retinal disparity), with which it shares a similar geometry: translation of the viewpoint functions like a baseline between views. Small head movements made unconsciously by stationary observers provide continuous parallax information that helps disambiguate shapes.

Uses and analogues

Many animals exploit motion parallax for distance judgement — locusts, for example, perform side-to-side "peering" movements before jumping. In technology, parallax effects are simulated in video games and interface design by moving layered backgrounds at different speeds (parallax scrolling), and motion-parallax cues are an important consideration in virtual-reality rendering, where correct viewpoint-dependent rendering reinforces the sense of presence.
