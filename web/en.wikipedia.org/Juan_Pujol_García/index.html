Juan Pujol García MBE (14 February 1912 – 10 October 1988), also known by the British codename Garbo and the German codename Arabel, was a Spanish double agent during the Second World War who provided false information to Nazi Germany on behalf of British intelligence. Operating from Lisbon and later London, he built an entirely fictitious network of sub-agents and played a crucial part in the success of Operation Fortitude, the deception that persuaded Germany that the 1944 Normandy landings were a diversion from the "real" invasion at the Pas-de-Calais.

Background and self-recruitment

Pujol was a Barcelona chicken farmer and hotelier who served briefly and unwillingly on both sides in the Spanish Civil War, emerging with a hatred of both fascism and communism. Determined to work for Britain, he was rebuffed by the British, so he instead offered his services to Germany in 1941 as a supposed Spanish government agent based in Britain. In reality he moved to Lisbon, Portugal, where — using guidebooks, maps, a railway timetable and reference books — he fabricated reports of his travels around Britain and invented an expanding network of imaginary agents.

Agent Garbo

The British, having intercepted his reports, identified and recruited him in 1942, bringing him to London under the handler Tomás Harris. As "Garbo" (named because an MI5 officer considered him "the best actor in the world"), Pujol and Harris ran a network that grew to some 27 fictitious sub-agents, whose reports the Abwehr trusted completely. During Operation Fortitude in 1944, Garbo's traffic — including a carefully timed warning that arrived just too late to be useful — convinced German intelligence that the Normandy invasion was a feint, helping pin the German Fifteenth Army at Calais for weeks after D-Day.

Later life

Uniquely, Pujol was decorated by both sides: he received the Iron Cross from Germany in July 1944 and was appointed MBE by George VI in November 1944. After the war, fearing reprisals, he faked his death in Angola in 1949 and settled in Venezuela, running a bookshop and gift shop in Lagunillas. He remained obscure until researcher Nigel West traced him in 1984; Pujol returned to London to be received by the Duke of Edinburgh shortly before the 40th anniversary of D-Day. He died in Caracas in 1988 and is buried in Choroní, Venezuela.
