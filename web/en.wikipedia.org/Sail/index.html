Sail

TypePropulsion surface
UsesSailing vessels, land yachts, iceboats
MaterialsCanvas, Dacron, nylon, laminates, carbon fibre

A sail is a tensile structure — usually a shaped sheet of fabric or other material — that uses wind power to propel a craft across water, land or ice. Sails convert the energy of the wind into forward motion through a combination of lift and drag, much like an aircraft wing.

How sails work

When wind flows around a properly trimmed sail, the pressure difference between its two sides generates a force roughly perpendicular to the sail's surface. The keel or centreboard of the vessel converts this into forward motion, allowing boats to sail at angles toward the wind as well as away from it.

Types of sails

Common sails include the mainsail, jib and genoa, spinnaker, and storm sails. Historically, square sails powered large ocean-going ships, while fore-and-aft rigs such as the lateen, lug and gaff sails gave greater manoeuvrability. Modern sails are cut by computer and made from synthetic fibres and laminated films for strength and shape retention.
