Aral Sea
LocationCentral Asia: Kazakhstan and Uzbekistan
TypeEndorheic lake
Area (1960)~68,000 km² (fourth-largest lake in the world)

The Aral Sea was an endorheic lake lying between Kazakhstan to its north and Uzbekistan to its south in Central Asia. Once the fourth-largest lake in the world, with an area of about 68,000 square kilometres, the Aral Sea began shrinking in the 1960s after the rivers that fed it — the Amu Darya and Syr Darya — were diverted by Soviet irrigation projects. Its desiccation is widely regarded as one of the planet's worst environmental disasters.

Shrinkage and consequences

To expand cotton production, the Soviet Union diverted the two rivers into irrigation canals, including the enormous Karakum Canal in Turkmenistan. By the 1980s the sea's inflow had fallen nearly to zero in some years; the shoreline receded tens of kilometres, salinity rose from about 10 grams per litre to over 100, and the commercial fishery — which had supported tens of thousands of jobs — collapsed. The sea split into the smaller North Aral Sea in Kazakhstan and the larger, saltier South Aral Sea in Uzbekistan; the exposed seabed became the Aralkum Desert, a source of salt- and pesticide-laden dust storms blamed for serious respiratory disease in surrounding populations. Iconic images of ships stranded in sand at the former port of Moynaq became emblems of the catastrophe.

Partial recovery

With World Bank support, Kazakhstan completed the Kok-Aral Dam in 2005, raising the level of the North Aral Sea, reducing its salinity and allowing fish stocks and fishing communities around Aralsk to revive. The South Aral in Uzbekistan continued to shrink; its eastern basin dried completely for the first time in modern history in 2014, though it has since partially refilled seasonally. Uzbekistan has planted saxaul shrubs across parts of the dry bed to stabilise it.

Geology and history

The Aral basin is an ancient depression whose lake level has fluctuated over millennia with shifts in the course of the Amu Darya, which at times flowed west to the Caspian Sea. Before the twentieth century the lake supported rich fisheries and served ports such as Aralsk and Moynaq; its name derives from the Kyrgyz and Kazakh for "sea of islands," referring to the more than 1,500 islands that dotted its waters.
