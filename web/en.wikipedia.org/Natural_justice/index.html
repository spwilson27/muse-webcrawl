Natural justice is a legal philosophy used in some jurisdictions, especially those of the common law tradition, that describes the fundamental procedural safeguards owed to a person affected by a decision. The term is derived from the Roman concepts of jus naturale and historically overlaps with the ideas of procedural fairness and, in the United States, procedural due process.

The two rules

Natural justice is traditionally expressed in two Latin maxims. Nemo iudex in causa sua — no one should be a judge in their own cause — is the rule against bias, covering actual bias, pecuniary interest (as in Dimes v Grand Junction Canal, 1852), and apparent bias, since "justice should not only be done, but should manifestly and undoubtedly be seen to be done" (R v Sussex Justices, ex parte McCarthy, 1924). Audi alteram partem — hear the other side — guarantees adequate notice of the case, an opportunity to respond, and a fair hearing before an impartial tribunal.

Modern development

Although once confined largely to judicial and quasi-judicial bodies, the doctrine was famously revived and extended to administrative decisions in Ridge v Baldwin [1964], where the House of Lords held that a chief constable dismissed without a hearing had been denied natural justice. Courts in England and Wales now often speak of a general duty of procedural fairness, whose content varies with context — from full oral hearings to a mere opportunity to make written representations. Breaches of natural justice render decisions voidable and are a standard ground of judicial review.

Application

The principles apply in administrative law, employment discipline, professional regulation, immigration, and prison discipline across Commonwealth jurisdictions including the United Kingdom, India, Australia, Canada, and Singapore. They do not, as a rule, dictate the substance of decisions, only the fairness of the process by which they are reached.
