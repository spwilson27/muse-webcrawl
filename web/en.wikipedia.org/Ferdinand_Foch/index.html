Ferdinand Foch

Ferdinand Foch (2 October 1851 – 20 March 1929) was a French general and military theorist who served as the Supreme Allied Commander during the final months of the First World War. A Marshal of France, he coordinated the Allied offensives of 1918 that led to the Armistice of 11 November 1918, which he accepted from the German delegation in a railway carriage in the Forest of Compiègne.

Early life and career

Foch was born in Tarbes, in southwestern France, the son of a civil servant. He enlisted during the Franco-Prussian War of 1870–71 and afterwards studied at the École Polytechnique, being commissioned in the artillery. He gained a reputation as a professor of strategy at the École Supérieure de Guerre, where he emphasised the moral will to win and the offensive spirit.

First World War

In August 1914, Foch commanded XX Corps at the Battle of the Frontiers and then the Ninth Army at the First Battle of the Marne, where his counterattacks helped stop the German advance on Paris. He later directed French forces in Flanders and at the Somme in 1916, before falling from favour and being sent to Italy after the disastrous Nivelle Offensive. In March 1918, with the German Spring Offensive threatening to split the British and French armies, the Allies appointed Foch to coordinate their efforts; his authority was soon expanded to that of commander-in-chief of the Allied forces on the Western Front.

Victory and later life

Foch directed the Allied counteroffensives beginning with the Second Battle of the Marne in July 1918 and the rolling Hundred Days Offensive that broke the German army. On 11 November 1918 he presented the armistice terms to the German envoys. He regarded the Treaty of Versailles as too lenient, famously remarking, "This is not a peace. It is an armistice for twenty years." Foch died in Paris in 1929 and was buried at Les Invalides.
