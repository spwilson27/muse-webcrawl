Cochin Jews

The Paradesi Synagogue (built 1568) in Kochi, Kerala

Cochin Jews (also known as Malabar Jews or Kochinim) are the oldest group of Jews in India, with roots that are claimed to date back to the time of King Solomon. Their community grew along the Malabar Coast in present-day Kerala, centred on the port of Kochi (Cochin), an ancient trading emporium involved in the spice trade — in commodities including the dyes and peppers of the early modern world trade.

History

Jewish copper plates granted by the Chera king Bhaskara Ravi Varma (dated by scholars to around 1000 CE) record privileges given to the Jewish merchant Joseph Rabban and are preserved in the Paradesi Synagogue. The community traditionally divides into Malabar Jews (the older group, sometimes called "Black Jews"), Paradesi Jews (descendants of Spanish and Portuguese Sephardic immigrants who arrived after the 1490s, called "White Jews"), and Meshuchrarim, manumitted slaves and their descendants.

The Paradesi Synagogue in Mattancherry, built in 1568, is the oldest active synagogue in the Commonwealth of Nations, famous for its hand-painted Chinese porcelain floor tiles and its copper plates. Visitors today reach the community's heritage sites via Cochin International Airport.

Emigration

After the founding of the State of Israel in 1948, most Cochin Jews emigrated (made aliyah) to Israel in the 1950s, where communities settled in moshavim such as Nevatim and Yuval. Only a few dozen Cochin Jews remain in Kerala, and the historic synagogues are maintained largely as heritage sites. Their dialect, Judeo-Malayalam, is the only known Jewish variety of a Dravidian language.
