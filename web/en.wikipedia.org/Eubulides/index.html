Eubulides of Miletus (Greek: Εὐβουλίδης; fl. 4th century BC) was a philosopher of the Megarian school, a pupil of Euclid of Megara, and a contemporary and rival of Aristotle. He is famous in the history of logic as the inventor, or at least the celebrated formulator, of several paradoxes that exercised philosophers for more than two thousand years and still bear on theories of truth and vagueness.

The paradoxes

Ancient sources attribute to Eubulides seven paradoxical arguments. The most famous is the Liar paradox: a man says "I am lying" — if he is telling the truth, he is lying; if lying, he is telling the truth. Others are the Sorites or Heap paradox (one grain does not make a heap; adding one grain never turns a non-heap into a heap; yet heaps exist), the Bald Man (the same reasoning applied to hair loss), the Veiled Man (you know your father, but you do not know the veiled man in front of you, who is your father), the Electra (Electra does and does not know that Orestes has come), and the Horned Man ("What you have not lost, you still have; you have not lost horns; therefore you have horns"). These puzzles probed the logic of identity, predication, and incremental change, and anticipated modern work on self-reference and vagueness.

Life and reputation

Little is known of his life. He was said to have taught the orator Demosthenes — legend has Demosthenes curing a defective pronunciation of the letter "r" under his instruction — and he attacked Aristotle in a lost work, allegedly with some slander. Diogenes Laertius preserves the list of his paradoxes, which he posed against opponents in dialectical contests typical of the Megarian school.
