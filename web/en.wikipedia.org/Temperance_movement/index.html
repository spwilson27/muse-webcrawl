Temperance movement

The temperance movement is the social movement against the consumption of alcoholic beverages, prominent in many countries during the 19th and early 20th centuries. Participants typically criticised alcohol intoxication or promoted complete abstinence from alcohol (teetotalism), citing its effects on health, families and society. The movement culminated in national Prohibition in the United States (1920–1933) and similar laws in Canada, Finland, Norway and elsewhere.

Origins

Temperance societies arose in the United States in the early 1800s, often linked to evangelical Protestant revivalism. The American Temperance Society, founded in 1826, claimed millions of members within a decade. The movement spread to Britain, Ireland — where Father Theobald Mathew administered the pledge to millions — and to the British colonies and Scandinavia. Campaigners founded teetotal societies, temperance hotels and drinking fountains, and promoted coffee and cocoa as alternatives.

Women and the WCTU

Women were central to the movement, as drunk husbands could squander wages and subject families to violence. The Woman's Christian Temperance Union, founded in 1874 and led from 1879 by Frances Willard, became one of the largest women's organisations of the era, connecting temperance to women's suffrage and other reforms. In 1900, Carrie Nation gained fame by attacking Kansas saloons with a hatchet.

Prohibition and decline

The Anti-Saloon League's political pressure led to the Eighteenth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution (1919), banning the manufacture and sale of alcohol nationwide. Prohibition reduced drinking but encouraged organised crime and illegal speakeasies, and it was repealed by the Twenty-first Amendment in 1933. Temperance organisations declined thereafter, though groups such as Alcoholics Anonymous (founded 1935) carried forward the goal of individual abstinence on a therapeutic rather than political basis.
