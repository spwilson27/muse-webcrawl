Animals are multicellular, eukaryotic organisms in the biological kingdom Animalia (also called Metazoa). With few exceptions, animals consume organic material (they are heterotrophs), breathe oxygen, are able to move (at least during part of their life cycle), reproduce sexually, and grow from a hollow sphere of cells called a blastula during embryonic development. Humans and other vertebrates are animals, as are insects, molluscs, sponges and jellyfish.

Characteristics

Animal cells lack the rigid cell walls of plants and fungi, and animals possess specialised tissues — including muscle and nervous tissue in most groups — that enable movement and rapid responses to stimuli. Almost all animals reproduce sexually, with males producing small motile sperm and females larger eggs, though many also reproduce asexually. Development typically proceeds through a blastula and gastrula stage, controlled by Hox genes shared across the kingdom.

Diversity and classification

More than 1.5 million living animal species have been described, of which around a million are insects; the true total is estimated in the millions. Animals are divided into roughly 35 phyla. The numerically dominant groups are the arthropods (insects, crustaceans, arachnids), molluscs and chordates (which include all vertebrates). The great majority of animal species are bilaterians — animals with bilateral symmetry and three germ layers — split into the protostomes (arthropods, molluscs, annelids and others) and deuterostomes (echinoderms and chordates). Sponges (Porifera) lack true tissues and are among the earliest-diverging lineages.

Evolutionary history

Animals evolved in the oceans, probably more than 700 million years ago; the oldest widely accepted fossils appear in the Ediacaran period (about 635–541 million years ago), and nearly all modern phyla appear in the fossil record during the Cambrian explosion around 541 million years ago. Animals colonised land in stages: arthropods and molluscs by the Silurian–Devonian, and vertebrate tetrapods in the late Devonian.

Ecology and humans

Animals occupy nearly every habitat on Earth and form the consumer levels of food webs as herbivores, predators, parasites and detritivores; they pollinate most flowering plants and shape ecosystems as engineers (beavers, corals, earthworms). Humans depend on animals for food, labour, clothing, companionship and research, and have domesticated species from dogs (at least 15,000 years ago) to silkworms. Animal welfare, conservation and the ethics of animal use are major social concerns; many species are threatened by habitat loss and climate change.
