Robert Stephenson (1803–1859) was an English civil and railway engineer, the only son of George Stephenson, the "Father of Railways." Where his father was largely self-educated, Robert combined practical training with formal study, and became the most celebrated railway engineer of the early Victorian era — a leading figure in the era that also produced the tubular rock-hewn cuttings and bridges of the new lines. He is unrelated to the polar explorers Robert Falcon Scott and Robert Peary.

Early work and the Rocket

After an apprenticeship in coal-mining engineering and a spell in Colombia managing silver mines, Stephenson returned to run the family's Newcastle locomotive works, Robert Stephenson and Company (founded 1823, the world's first locomotive factory). He was the principal designer behind the Rocket, which won the Rainhill Trials of 1829 and set the pattern for steam locomotives with its multi-tubular boiler and separate firebox. He then served as chief engineer of the London and Birmingham Railway (1838), the first intercity line into the capital.

Bridges

Stephenson's greatest civil works were his long-span bridges: the High Level Bridge at Newcastle (1849), the Royal Border Bridge at Berwick (1850), the Victoria Bridge at Montreal (1859, then the longest in the world), and above all the Britannia Tubular Bridge across the Menai Strait (1850), whose trains ran through immense rectangular iron tubes — an audacious design tested with William Fairbairn and Eaton Hodgkinson.

Later life

Stephenson advised on railways internationally, from Norway to Egypt, served as Conservative MP for Whitby from 1847, declined a knighthood, and was president of the Institution of Civil Engineers (1855–57). On his death in 1859 he was buried in Westminster Abbey — a rare honour for an engineer, shared with Thomas Telford.
