Standard of proof

In law, the standard of proof, or burden of proof in the sense of the degree of conviction required, is the level of certainty and the degree of evidence necessary to establish proof in a criminal or civil proceeding. Different proceedings require different standards: the higher the stakes, generally, the higher the standard.

Principal standards

The lowest common standards are "reasonable suspicion" and "probable cause", used to justify searches, seizures and charges in the United States. In civil cases the usual standard is the preponderance of the evidence: the claim is more likely true than not. Some civil matters, such as fraud or the termination of parental rights, require clear and convincing evidence, an intermediate standard. In criminal cases the prosecution must prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt, the most demanding standard in common use, reflecting the principle that it is worse to convict an innocent person than to let a guilty person go free.

Application

The party who bears the burden of proof must persuade the trier of fact—judge or jury—to the requisite standard on each element of the claim or charge. Failure to meet the standard results in judgment for the opposing party. In administrative and other specialized proceedings, other formulations appear, such as "substantial evidence" or the balance of probabilities used in civil cases in many Commonwealth jurisdictions.
