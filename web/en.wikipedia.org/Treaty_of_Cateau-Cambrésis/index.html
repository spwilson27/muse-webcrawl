Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis

Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis
Signed2–3 April 1559
LocationLe Cateau-Cambrésis, France
SignatoriesFrance with Spain; France with England
ContextEnd of the Italian Wars (Habsburg–Valois Wars)

The Treaty of Cateau-Cambrésis (April 1559) ended the Italian Wars between the House of Habsburg and the House of Valois, which had been fought intermittently since 1494 over dominance in Italy. Comprising two agreements — between France and England (2 April 1559) and between France and Spain (3 April 1559) — the settlement confirmed Spain as the dominant power in Italy and reshaped the political order of Europe for decades.

Terms

France renounced its claims in Italy, restoring Piedmont and Savoy to Emmanuel Philibert of Savoy (retaining only the marquisate of Saluzzo and a few fortresses, later mostly surrendered), and returned Corsica to Genoa. Spain kept the Kingdom of Naples, Sicily, Sardinia, the Duchy of Milan and the State of the Presidi, making Philip II master of most of Italy. France nevertheless retained the Three Bishoprics of Metz, Toul and Verdun, seized in 1552, and in the English treaty recovered Calais — England's last foothold in France, lost in 1558 — subject to a payment or restitution clause that lapsed in 1564.

The settlement was sealed by marriages: Philip II of Spain married Elisabeth of Valois, daughter of Henry II of France, and Emmanuel Philibert of Savoy married Henry's sister Margaret. During the tournament held to celebrate the peace in Paris, Henry II was fatally wounded by a lance splinter from Gabriel de Montgomery, captain of his Scottish Guard — an accident that plunged France into the instability preceding the French Wars of Religion.

Significance

Cateau-Cambrésis is often treated as one of the foundational settlements of the European state system. Spain emerged as Europe's leading power, France turned inward toward religious civil war, and Italy remained under Spanish predominance for a century and a half. Freed from their Italian rivalry, both monarchies also came under pressure — and found common cause — in confronting Protestantism, while the treaty's failure to settle Franco-Spanish competition simply displaced it to the Netherlands and the Atlantic world.
