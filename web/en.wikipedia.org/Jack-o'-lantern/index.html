Jack-o'-lantern

A jack-o'-lantern (or jack o'lantern) is a carved lantern, most commonly made from a pumpkin or other root vegetable, associated chiefly with the Halloween holiday. Typically the top of the pumpkin is cut off, the flesh is scooped out, and a monstrous or comic face is carved into the rind, which is then lit from within by a candle or electric light.

History

The practice originates in Ireland and Scotland, where lanterns were carved from turnips, mangelwurzels or potatoes, sometimes with faces, and placed in windows to frighten away evil spirits or to represent them. Irish immigrants brought the custom to North America, where the native pumpkin proved far easier to carve, and the pumpkin lantern became standard.

Etymology and folklore

The name derives from the folk tale of "Stingy Jack", a blacksmith who tricked the Devil several times and, barred from both heaven and hell, was condemned to wander the earth with only a burning coal inside a carved turnip to light his way — hence "Jack of the Lantern". The term was earlier applied to the will-o'-the-wisp phenomenon in English folklore.

Modern practice

Pumpkin carving is a major seasonal activity in the United States and increasingly elsewhere, ranging from simple faces to elaborate stencil designs. Pumpkin festivals and contests, such as those in Keene, New Hampshire, display thousands of lit jack-o'-lanterns. Seeded "Atlantic Giant" pumpkins are grown specifically for carving and competition.
