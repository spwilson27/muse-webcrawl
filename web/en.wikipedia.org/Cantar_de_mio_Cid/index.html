The Cantar de mio Cid ("The Song of my Cid"), also known as El poema de mio Cid, is the oldest preserved Castilian epic poem. It recounts the deeds of the Castilian hero Rodrigo Díaz de Vivar, known as El Cid Campeador, during the Reconquista. It is considered the national epic of Spain and one of the great works of medieval European literature.

A leaf of the unique surviving manuscript of the poem.

The poem

The surviving text is divided into three cantares. In the first, the Cid is exiled by King Alfonso VI and, cut off from royal support, wins wealth and reputation campaigning in the frontier lands. In the second, he conquers Valencia and is reconciled with the king; his daughters are married to the Infantes of Carrión. In the third, the Infantes humiliate and abandon the Cid's daughters in the oak forest of Corpes; the Cid demands justice in a formal legal proceeding, the Infantes are defeated in judicial duels, and the daughters make more honorable marriages.

Authorship and date

The poem is anonymous. A colophon attributes the written version to one Per Abbat, dated 1207 in the Spanish era reckoning (year 1245 of that era), though scholars debate whether he was author or copyist. Ramón Menéndez Pidal argued for composition around 1140 and for strong historical roots; later scholars have proposed later dates and a learned, possibly clerical author. The unique manuscript, now in the Biblioteca Nacional in Madrid, is missing its first leaf and two interior leaves.

Style and themes

Written in irregular lines of assonant verse divided by a caesura, the poem is notable for its realism relative to other epics: its hero wins by strategy, law, and generosity as much as by arms, and money, credit, and legal procedure feature prominently. Central themes include honor lost and recovered, loyalty, and the rise of a hero by merit in a hierarchical society.
