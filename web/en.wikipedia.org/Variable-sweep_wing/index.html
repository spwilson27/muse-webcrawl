Variable-sweep wing

A variable-sweep wing, colloquially known as a "swing wing", is an airplane wing, or set of wings, that may be swept back and then returned to its original position during flight. It allows the aircraft's shape to be modified in flight, combining the low-speed lift of a straight wing with the low drag of a swept wing at high speed.

History

The concept was explored in Germany during the Second World War (the Messerschmitt P.1101) and developed after the war using captured research. The first production swing-wing aircraft included the Soviet Sukhoi Su-17 and MiG-23, and the American General Dynamics F-111 Aardvark (1964). Later examples include the Grumman F-14 Tomcat, the Panavia Tornado, the Rockwell B-1 Lancer, and the Tupolev Tu-160.

Advantages and disadvantages

Sweeping the wings reduces wave drag at transonic and supersonic speeds, while spreading them improves lift, range, and low-speed handling for takeoff and landing. The pivot mechanism is heavy and complex, however, and the center of lift shifts as the wings move. Advances in fly-by-wire controls, relaxed static stability, and computational aerodynamics made fixed wings more efficient for most roles, so no variable-sweep aircraft remain in production.
