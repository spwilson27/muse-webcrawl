Gemstone

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A gemstone (also called a fine gem, jewel, precious stone, or semi-precious stone) is a piece of mineral crystal which, in cut and polished form, is used to make jewellery or other ornamentation. However, certain rocks (such as lapis lazuli, opal, and obsidian) and occasionally organic materials that are not minerals (such as amber, jet, and pearl) are also used for jewellery and are thus also considered to be gemstones. Most gemstones are hard, but some soft minerals are used in jewellery because of their lustre or other physical properties with aesthetic value.

Characteristics and classification

Traditionally, the precious stones are diamond, ruby, sapphire, and emerald, with all other gemstones being semi-precious. This distinction reflects the rarity of the respective stones in ancient times, as well as their qualities: all are translucent with fine colour in their purest forms (except for the colourless diamond) and very hard, with hardness scores of 8–10 on the Mohs scale. Other stones are classified by colour, translucency and hardness. Gemstones are characterised by refractive index, dispersion, specific gravity, hardness, cleavage, fracture, and lustre.

Cutting and polishing

Gemstones are cut and polished by grinding to create flat faces (facets) at precise angles, or shaped and polished into smooth domes (cabochons). The art of cutting and polishing gemstones is called lapidary. Cut quality strongly affects a stone's brilliance and value; the standard round brilliant cut for diamonds has 57 or 58 facets.

Value

The value of a gemstone depends on rarity, colour, clarity, cut and size, measured in carats (1 carat = 0.2 gram). Synthetic and simulated stones, produced in laboratories since the late 19th century, can be physically and chemically nearly identical to natural gems but sell for a fraction of the price.
