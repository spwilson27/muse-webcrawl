Price stability

Price stability is a state in which the general level of prices in an economy does not change significantly over time—neither rising rapidly through inflation nor falling through deflation. Price stability is the primary objective of most modern central banks, which pursue it through monetary policy.

Definition and targets

In practice, price stability does not mean an inflation rate of exactly zero. Because of measurement biases in price indices and the risks of deflation near the zero lower bound on interest rates, many central banks define price stability as a low, positive inflation rate—commonly 2 percent over the medium term, as adopted by the Federal Reserve, the European Central Bank, and the Bank of England. Stable prices allow households and firms to make long-term decisions without uncertainty about the future value of money, protect savers, and prevent the arbitrary redistribution of wealth between borrowers and lenders that unexpected inflation causes.

Measurement

Price stability is monitored using consumer price indices, producer price indices, and measures of underlying or "core" inflation that exclude volatile food and energy components, together with surveys and market-based measures of inflation expectations.
