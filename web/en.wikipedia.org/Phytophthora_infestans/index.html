Phytophthora infestans

Phytophthora infestans is an oomycete (water mould) that causes the serious potato and tomato disease known as late blight or potato blight. It was a major culprit in the 1840s European potato failure and the Great Famine of Ireland (1845–1852), which killed roughly one million people and drove mass emigration. It remains one of the most destructive plant pathogens worldwide, causing billions of dollars in annual losses.

Biology and lifecycle

P. infestans is a hemibiotrophic oomycete, related to brown algae rather than true fungi. It overwinters in infected tubers and debris and spreads by airborne sporangia that release motile zoospores in cool, wet conditions. Under favourable weather it can defoliate a potato crop within days. Its name, given by Heinrich Anton de Bary in 1876, derives from the Greek for "plant destroyer".

Historical impact

The pathogen likely originated in the Toluca Valley of central Mexico. Introduced to Europe in the 1840s, it devastated potato crops on which Irish smallholders depended almost exclusively, precipitating the Great Famine. The outbreak is widely credited with establishing plant pathology as a scientific discipline through de Bary's proof that the mould caused the disease — an early demonstration of the germ theory of disease.

Management

Control relies on resistant cultivars, certified seed, sanitation, and fungicides (historically the copper-based Bordeaux mixture). Breeding programmes cross commercial potatoes with wild relatives such as Solanum demissum, though the pathogen frequently overcomes single resistance genes; genome sequencing (2009) has aided research into durable resistance.
