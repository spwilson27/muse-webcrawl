Card catalog

A card catalog (or card catalogue) is a listing of all the bibliographic items found in a library, recorded on individual cards stored in drawers in a cabinet. Each card describes one item and gives its location in the collection. Card catalogs were the primary finding tool of libraries from the late 19th century until they were replaced by computerised online public access catalogs (OPACs) in the 1980s and 1990s.

[Image: A traditional card catalog cabinet with labelled drawers]

History

Precursors of the card catalog include the revolutionary French cataloguing instruction of 1791, which directed librarians to record books on playing cards. In the United States, Charles Ammi Cutter's Rules for a Dictionary Catalog (1876) standardised practice, and the Library of Congress began selling printed catalog cards to libraries in 1901, greatly promoting uniformity. Cards were filed by author, title and subject headings.

Decline

The MARC machine-readable cataloguing format, developed in the 1960s, allowed catalog records to be shared and eventually displayed online. The Library of Congress froze its card catalog in 1980, and most libraries retired their cabinets over the following two decades, although the furniture is often preserved or repurposed. The card catalog's organisation — one record per card, filed under multiple headings — anticipated many features of modern database design.
