Pitcher plant

Pitcher plants are carnivorous plants whose prey-trapping mechanism consists of a deep cavity formed by a modified leaf, known as a pitfall trap, filled with digestive fluid. Insects and other small animals are attracted by nectar and colour, slip on the waxy rim, fall into the fluid, drown, and are digested, providing the plant with nitrogen and phosphorus in nutrient-poor soils.

Hanging pitchers of a tropical pitcher plant (Nepenthes).

Families

Pitcher traps evolved independently in several plant families. The tropical pitcher plants (Nepenthes) of Asia and Madagascar hang their pitchers from tendrils at leaf tips. The North American trumpet pitchers (Sarracenia) grow upright tubes from rhizomes. Other groups include the cobra lily (Darlingtonia), the sun pitchers (Heliamphora) of South American tepuis, the Australian pitcher plant (Cephalotus), and certain bromeliads.

Trap mechanism

The rim of the pitcher, the peristome, becomes extremely slippery when wet, causing insects to aquaplane into the trap. Downward-pointing hairs and a waxy zone prevent escape, and the digestive fluid — often a community of bacteria and invertebrate larvae as well as plant enzymes — breaks down the prey. Some species form mutualisms: tree shrews and bats use large Nepenthes pitchers as food sources or roosts, fertilising the plant with their droppings.

Cultivation

Pitcher plants are popular with specialist growers, who provide them with bright light, pure water, and nutrient-poor, acidic compost. Many wild species are threatened by habitat loss and poaching for the horticultural trade.
