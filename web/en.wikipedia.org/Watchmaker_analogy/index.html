Watchmaker analogy

The watchmaker analogy is a teleological argument for the existence of God, most famously presented by the English clergyman and philosopher William Paley in his Natural Theology (1802). Paley argued that, just as finding a watch on the ground would compel us to infer an intelligent watchmaker from its complexity and evident purpose, so the complexity and adaptation of living organisms compel us to infer an intelligent designer of nature. The argument became the classic statement of the argument from design.

The argument

Paley opens by contrasting a stone, which might have lain on a heath forever, with a watch, whose parts are "framed and put together for a purpose" — co-ordinated so that it measures time. We cannot rationally believe, he argued, that the watch assembled itself by chance; its existence implies a maker. Likewise the eye, with its transparent lens, retina and coordinated parts fitted for vision, displays design on a far grander scale and so implies a divine designer. Paley held that the inference holds even if we have never seen a watch made, even if the watch sometimes goes wrong, and even if we cannot explain every part.

Criticism

David Hume, writing before Paley, had already undermined the analogy: the universe is not much like a machine, the analogy between human artefacts and the cosmos is weak, and even a successful inference would not establish an infinite, perfect or single creator. Charles Darwin's theory of evolution by natural selection (1859) supplied the decisive scientific response for biology, showing how adaptation and complexity can arise without a designer — though Darwin himself, as a young man, had been convinced by Paley. Richard Dawkins titled his 1986 defence of evolution The Blind Watchmaker, arguing that natural selection is a "blind watchmaker" that produces the appearance of design cumulatively. Modern versions of the argument persist in intelligent-design advocacy and in fine-tuning arguments in cosmology.
