Gas laws

The gas laws are physical laws describing how the pressure, volume, temperature and amount of a gas are related. Developed from experiments between the seventeenth and nineteenth centuries, they combine into the ideal gas law for gases whose particles have negligible volume and intermolecular forces.

Principal laws

Boyle's law: at fixed temperature and amount, pressure is inversely proportional to volume (P ∝ 1/V). Charles's law: at fixed pressure, volume is proportional to absolute temperature (V ∝ T). Gay-Lussac's law: at fixed volume, pressure is proportional to absolute temperature. Avogadro's law: equal volumes of gases at the same temperature and pressure contain equal numbers of molecules.

Ideal gas law

Combining these relations gives PV = nRT, where P is pressure, V volume, n amount in moles, T absolute temperature and R the ideal gas constant. The combined gas law compares a fixed amount of gas between two states.

Real gases

Real gases depart from ideal behaviour at high pressure and low temperature, where molecular volume and attractions matter. Equations such as the van der Waals equation correct for those effects.
