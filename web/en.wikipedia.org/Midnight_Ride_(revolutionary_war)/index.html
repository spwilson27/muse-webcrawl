DateNight of 18–19 April 1775

The midnight ride refers to the alarm rides of Paul Revere, William Dawes and other patriot messengers on the night of 18–19 April 1775, warning Massachusetts militia and colonial leaders that British regular troops were marching from Boston to Concord to seize military stores and arrest Samuel Adams and John Hancock in Lexington. The warnings enabled the militia to assemble and fight the Battles of Lexington and Concord the next morning, opening the American Revolutionary War.

Background and signals

Patriot leaders, alerted by intelligence of the expedition, arranged a signal from Boston's Old North Church — lanterns displayed in the steeple, "one if by land, two if by sea" — to indicate the British route across the Charles River. Dr Joseph Warren dispatched Revere and Dawes by different routes so that at least one would get through.

The rides

Revere rowed across the Charles River to Charlestown, rode through Somerville and Medford, and reached Lexington around midnight, warning Adams and Hancock. Dawes rode out over Boston Neck on a longer route. In Lexington, Revere and Dawes set out for Concord with Dr Samuel Prescott; a British patrol captured Revere, Dawes was unhorsed, and Prescott alone reached Concord. Meanwhile, dozens of other riders spread the alarm across eastern Massachusetts — historians estimate some forty riders in all — and church bells, drums and signal guns mustered thousands of militiamen by dawn.

Legacy

Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's 1861 poem "Paul Revere's Ride" immortalised Revere — and romanticised the event, focussing on him alone — making the midnight ride a founding legend of the United States.
