Giordano Bruno (1548 – 17 February 1600), born Filippo Bruno, was an Italian Dominican friar, philosopher, poet, cosmological theorist and Hermetic occultist known for extending the Copernican model: he proposed that the stars are distant suns surrounded by their own planets, some possibly harbouring life, and that the universe is infinite.

Life

Born in Nola near Naples, Bruno entered the Dominican Order, taking the name Giordano. Suspected of heresy, he left the order and wandered Europe for sixteen years — Geneva, Toulouse, Paris, London, Oxford, Wittenberg, Prague and Frankfurt — teaching the art of memory and publishing dialogues in Italian and Latin works on cosmology, including De l'infinito, universo e mondi (1584).

Trial and execution

Lured back to Italy by the Venetian nobleman Giovanni Mocenigo, Bruno was denounced and arrested by the Inquisition in 1592. After a lengthy trial in Venice and Rome — conducted in its final phase under Cardinal Robert Bellarmine — he refused to recant his doctrines, which included denials of transubstantiation, the Trinity and the virgin birth, alongside his cosmological views. He was burned at the stake in the Campo de' Fiori on 17 February 1600.

Legacy

Bruno has often been cast as a martyr for science, though historians note his trial centred mainly on theological heresies. A statue erected on the site of his execution in 1889 became a symbol of free thought.
