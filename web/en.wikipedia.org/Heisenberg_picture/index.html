In physics, the Heisenberg picture (or Heisenberg representation) is a formulation of quantum mechanics in which the operators (observables) evolve in time while the state vectors remain constant. It contrasts with the Schrödinger picture, in which the states evolve and the operators are fixed, and with the interaction picture, in which both evolve.

The Heisenberg equation

The time evolution of an operator A in the Heisenberg picture is governed by the Heisenberg equation of motion, dA/dt = (i/ħ)[H, A] + (∂A/∂t), where H is the Hamiltonian and [ , ] denotes the commutator. The formal resemblance to Hamilton's equations of classical mechanics — with commutators playing the role of Poisson brackets — makes the picture especially natural for seeing the classical limit of quantum systems.

History and use

The picture grew out of Heisenberg's matrix mechanics of 1925, the original form of quantum mechanics, in which physical quantities were represented by matrices whose entries depended on time. Although expectation values computed in any picture are identical, the Heisenberg picture is often preferred in relativistic quantum field theory, where keeping states fixed and letting fields evolve as operator-valued functions of spacetime fits naturally with relativity.
