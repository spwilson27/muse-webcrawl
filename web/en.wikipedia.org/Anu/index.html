Anu

Anu, supreme sky god of the Mesopotamian pantheon

Anu (Akkadian: 𒀭𒀭 ANU; Sumerian: An, "sky" or "heaven") was the divine personification of the sky and the supreme god in the Mesopotamian pantheon of Sumer, Akkad, Assyria, and Babylonia. He was regarded as the ancestor and king of the gods, source of divine authority, and father of many deities, including Enlil and Enki in the traditional triad of the great gods.

Role and worship

Anu's principal cult centre was the Eanna temple complex at Uruk, shared with the goddess Inanna/Ishtar. Although nominally supreme, Anu was a remote figure in myth, rarely intervening directly; executive authority among the gods was generally exercised by Enlil. Kings derived their legitimacy from Anu, and the concept of "Anu-ship" (anūtu) denoted supreme divine kingship.

Mythology

In Babylonian creation tradition, Anu was the son of Anshar and Kishar and descendant of the primordial waters Apsu and Tiamat. In the Epic of Gilgamesh, it is Anu who, at Ishtar's request, creates the Bull of Heaven. Anu also sometimes appears as the father of the demon Lamashtu and of the Anunnaki gods collectively.

Equivalents

Anu was identified with the Hurrian sky god and, in some syncretic traditions, equated with other supreme deities. His sign in cuneiform, the star-like DINGIR, became the general determinative for divinity.
