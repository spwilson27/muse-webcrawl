Mantle plume

Field: Geophysics / geology
Proposed by: J. Tuzo Wilson (1963); W. Jason Morgan (1971)
Related: Hotspots, large igneous provinces

A mantle plume is a proposed mechanism in geology by which heat and rock rise through the mantle of a planet as a narrow, column-like upwelling. Plumes are thought to originate deep in the Earth, possibly at the core–mantle boundary, and to rise buoyantly until they reach the base of the lithosphere, where they spread out, cause uplift and generate volcanic activity at so-called hotspots.

History

The idea grew out of J. Tuzo Wilson's 1963 suggestion that volcanic island chains such as Hawaii record the motion of tectonic plates over fixed melting anomalies in the mantle. W. Jason Morgan developed the concept in 1971, proposing that plumes rise from the lower mantle and remain relatively stationary while plates move over them, producing age-progressive volcanic tracks.

Characteristics

In classic models a plume consists of a narrow conduit or tail topped by a larger bulbous head. When a new plume head reaches the lithosphere it may produce a large igneous province — a vast outpouring of flood basalt — while the tail sustains a long-lived hotspot track. Candidates for plume-fed volcanism include Hawaii, Iceland, Yellowstone and Réunion, the last associated with the Deccan Traps.

Debate

The existence and depth of mantle plumes remain debated. Seismic tomography has imaged deep low-velocity anomalies beneath some hotspots, but critics argue that many hotspot features can be explained by shallow plate-tectonic processes instead.
