Vitamin K

Vitamin K is a family of structurally similar, fat-soluble vitamers found in foods and marketed as dietary supplements. The human body requires vitamin K for post-synthesis modification of certain proteins involved in blood coagulation (K from koagulation in Danish and German), and for the binding of calcium in bones and other tissues. The two main natural forms are vitamin K1 (phylloquinone), made by plants and abundant in green leafy vegetables, and vitamin K2 (menaquinones), produced by bacteria and found in fermented foods and animal products.

Physiology

Vitamin K is a cofactor for the enzyme gamma-glutamyl carboxylase, which activates clotting factors II, VII, IX, and X, the anticoagulant proteins C and S, and the bone protein osteocalcin. Without adequate vitamin K, these proteins are undercarboxylated and inactive, leading to bleeding. The anticoagulant warfarin works by blocking vitamin K recycling, and vitamin K is its antidote.

Deficiency

Deficiency is uncommon in healthy adults because the vitamin is widespread in food and also produced by gut bacteria, but it can occur in newborns — hence the routine vitamin K injection at birth to prevent haemorrhagic disease of the newborn — and in people with fat-malabsorption disorders or on long-term antibiotics. Sources include spinach, kale, broccoli, soybean and canola oils, cheese, and the fermented soybean food nattō, exceptionally rich in menaquinone-7.

Discovery

The vitamin was identified in 1929 by Danish scientist Henrik Dam, who observed haemorrhages in chickens fed a fat-free diet, and its structure was characterised in the 1930s; Dam and Edward Doisy shared the 1943 Nobel Prize for the work.
