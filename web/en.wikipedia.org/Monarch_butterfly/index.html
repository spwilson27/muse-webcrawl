The monarch butterfly or simply monarch (Danaus plexippus) is a milkweed butterfly in the family Nymphalidae, recognisable by its orange wings laced with black veins and bordered in black with white spots. It is famous for the mass southward migration of the eastern North American population each autumn — a journey of up to 4,800 km (3,000 miles) to overwintering sites in oyamel fir forests of central Mexico, completed by a "super generation" that lives far longer than summer generations and that has never made the trip before.

Life cycle

Females lay eggs singly on milkweed leaves. The caterpillar, banded in yellow, black, and white, feeds on milkweed and sequesters cardiac glycosides (cardenolides), making both larva and adult toxic and distasteful to predators; the bright coloration advertises this defence (aposematism). After about two weeks the larva forms a green, gold-flecked chrysalis and metamorphoses into the adult butterfly. Several generations breed through spring and summer as the population recolonises northern North America; the final generation enters reproductive diapause and migrates south.

Migration and decline

The western population overwinters along the California coast. Both populations have declined sharply since the 1990s — the eastern population by an estimated 80–90% from its mid-1990s peak, the western by more than 99% at its 2020 low — owing to loss of milkweed (linked to herbicide use), logging and storms at overwintering sites, pesticides, and climate change. In July 2022 the IUCN listed the migratory monarch as Endangered. The overwintering colonies in Mexico were scientifically documented only in 1975, after decades of tagging research led by Fred and Norah Urquhart.
