Feynman diagram

Feynman diagram of electron–positron annihilation

In theoretical physics, a Feynman diagram is a pictorial representation of the mathematical expressions describing the behaviour and interaction of subatomic particles. The scheme is named after American physicist Richard Feynman, who introduced the diagrams in 1948. Fundamental interactions between particles are represented by vertices where lines meet: straight lines represent fermions, wavy lines represent gauge bosons such as photons, and coiled lines represent gluons.

Feynman rules

To each diagram one applies the Feynman rules of the theory (for example, quantum electrodynamics) to obtain a mathematical expression contributing to the total probability amplitude for a process. Summing the amplitudes over all relevant diagrams gives the scattering amplitude; the diagrams correspond to terms in a perturbation series expansion of the interaction.

Interpretation

Although often read as showing particles moving through space and time, Feynman diagrams are fundamentally calculation tools. A line pointing "backwards in time" can equivalently be interpreted as an antiparticle moving forwards. Virtual particles appear as internal lines and need not satisfy the usual relations between energy and momentum.
