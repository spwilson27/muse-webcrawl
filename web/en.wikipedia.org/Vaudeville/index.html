Vaudeville

Vaudeville was a theatrical genre of variety entertainment popular in the United States and Canada from the early 1880s until the early 1930s. A vaudeville performance consisted of a series of unrelated acts grouped together on a common bill — including musicians, dancers, comedians, magicians, acrobats, jugglers, trained animals, singers, and one-act plays.

History

The format was shaped by impresarios such as Tony Pastor, who cleaned up variety theatre for family audiences, and by the circuits of B. F. Keith and Edward Albee, whose United Booking Office controlled "big-time" vaudeville from the Palace Theatre in New York and hundreds of theatres across the continent. Performers toured for years on the circuits; stars included Harry Houdini, W. C. Fields, the Marx Brothers, George Burns and Gracie Allen, and Jack Benny.

Decline

Vaudeville declined with the arrival of motion pictures, radio, and talking films in the late 1920s, and the Great Depression. Many vaudevillians carried their material into film, radio, and later television variety shows. The word derives from a French term for satirical songs, from the valley of Vire (vaux de Vire).
