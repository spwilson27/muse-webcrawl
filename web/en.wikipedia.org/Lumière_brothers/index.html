Lumière brothers

Auguste (left) and Louis Lumière

Born: Auguste: 19 October 1862; Louis: 5 October 1864

Birthplace: Besançon, France

Known for: Cinématographe; Autochrome colour photography

The Lumière brothers, Auguste Marie Louis Nicolas Lumière (1862–1954) and Louis Jean Lumière (1864–1948), were French manufacturers of photography equipment, best known for their Cinématographe motion picture system and the short films they produced between 1895 and 1905. They are widely credited as the first filmmakers and the founders of cinema as a public entertainment.

The Cinématographe

Working at their father's photographic plate factory in Lyon, Louis devised the Cinématographe, a lightweight hand-cranked device that functioned as camera, printer, and projector in one, using 35 mm film with round perforations. Patented in February 1895, it was an improvement on Edison's bulky Kinetograph and Kinetoscope peep-box, since it allowed films to be projected to a paying audience.

On 28 December 1895, at the Salon Indien du Grand Café in Paris, the brothers gave the first public screening of projected motion pictures for a paying audience — an event conventionally regarded as the birth of cinema. The programme included Workers Leaving the Lumière Factory, generally considered the first motion picture, and the comedy L'Arroseur arrosé. Arrival of a Train at La Ciotat soon became famous for the (probably apocryphal) story that spectators fled in panic from the onrushing locomotive.

Later work

The brothers sent camera operators around the world, amassing a catalogue of some 1,400 short actuality films, but they soon withdrew from film production, declaring that cinema was "an invention without any future" as a commercial spectacle. Louis went on to perfect the Autochrome process (1907), the first commercially successful industrial colour photography method, and to experiment with stereoscopic and wide-screen cinema.
