Photon

A photon is an elementary particle that is a quantum of the electromagnetic field, including electromagnetic radiation such as light and radio waves, and the force carrier for the electromagnetic force. It is massless, travels at the speed of light in vacuum, and exhibits both wave-like and particle-like properties, a duality central to quantum mechanics.

The energy of a photon is proportional to its frequency: E = hν, where h is the Planck constant. Its momentum is p = h/λ. Photons carry spin angular momentum and are bosons, mediating interactions between charged particles in quantum electrodynamics.

History

In 1905, Albert Einstein proposed that light consists of discrete quanta of energy to explain the photoelectric effect, building on Max Planck's 1900 quantum hypothesis. The term "photon" was coined in 1926 by the chemist Gilbert N. Lewis. Arthur Compton's scattering experiments in 1923 confirmed that light quanta carry momentum.

Properties

Photons are electrically neutral and stable. They are absorbed and emitted by atoms when electrons change energy levels, and are created and destroyed in many processes including particle-antiparticle annihilation, radioactive decay, and synchrotron radiation. In photosynthesis, photons drive the chemistry of life on Earth.

Applications

Photons are exploited in lasers, photovoltaics, telecommunications (fiber optics), imaging, and quantum information science, where individual photons serve as qubits and carriers of quantum entanglement over long distances.
