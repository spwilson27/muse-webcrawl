Fugitive Slave Act of 1850

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Fugitive Slave Act of 1850 was a United States federal law enacted by Congress on September 18, 1850, as part of the Compromise of 1850. It strengthened the earlier Fugitive Slave Act of 1793 by requiring that escaped slaves be returned to their owners even if found in a free state, and it imposed penalties on officials and private citizens who failed to assist in their capture. The law was one of the most controversial measures of the decade before the American Civil War and sharply intensified Northern opposition to slavery.

Provisions

The Act created federal commissioners empowered to issue warrants and decide cases. An alleged fugitive could not testify in their own behalf and had no right to a jury trial; commissioners received a higher fee ($10) for ruling in favour of the claimant than for ruling against ($5), a provision abolitionists cited as a built-in bribe. Citizens could be compelled to join posses, and helping a fugitive brought fines of up to $1,000 and six months' imprisonment, plus civil liability. Federal marshals who refused to enforce the law faced fines themselves.

Resistance and consequences

The Act galvanised the abolitionist movement. High-profile rescues and renditions — including the rescue of Shadrach Minkins in Boston and the rendition of Anthony Burns in 1854, which required federal troops — turned public opinion in the North. Several free states passed "personal liberty laws" obstructing enforcement. The law drove thousands of African Americans, including long-settled free residents, to flee to Canada, and it gave urgency to the Underground Railroad. Harriet Beecher Stowe's novel Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852) was written partly in response. In Ableman v. Booth (1859), the Supreme Court upheld the Act's constitutionality.

Repeal

The Fugitive Slave Acts were repealed on June 28, 1864, during the Civil War, and slavery itself was abolished by the Thirteenth Amendment in 1865.
