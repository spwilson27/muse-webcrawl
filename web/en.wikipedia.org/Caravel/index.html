Caravel

The caravel is a small, highly maneuverable sailing ship developed in the 15th century by the Portuguese to explore along the West African coast and into the Atlantic Ocean. Its lateen sails gave it speed and the ability to sail windward; later, square-rigged ("round") caravels were developed for ocean voyages.

History

Derived from earlier Iberian and Mediterranean fishing and cargo craft, the caravel became the emblematic vessel of the Age of Discovery. Bartolomeu Dias used caravels to round the Cape of Good Hope in 1488; two of Columbus's three ships in 1492, the Niña and the Pinta, were caravels; and Vasco da Gama's fleet included caravels for scouting. Typical caravels were 15–30 meters long with a shallow draft suited to coastal exploration and river mouths.

Design

The lateen-rigged caravela latina excelled at beating to windward, while the square-rigged caravela redonda traded some of that agility for ocean-going stability and cargo capacity. Caravels were progressively superseded by the larger carrack and galleon for long-distance trade.
