Piano quartet

Piano quartet
InstrumentationPiano, violin, viola, cello (standard)
Ensemble typeChamber music
Notable worksMozart K. 478 & K. 493; Brahms Opp. 25, 26, 60; Schumann Op. 47; Fauré Opp. 15, 45

A piano quartet is a chamber ensemble consisting of a piano and three other instruments, or a piece of music composed for such an ensemble. The standard configuration — established in the Classical era and used for nearly all major works in the genre — is piano, violin, viola, and cello: in effect, a string trio joined by piano. The piano quartet should not be confused with the piano quintet, which adds a second violin or another string instrument.

History and repertoire

The genre emerged in the late 18th century as the piano became the dominant domestic instrument. Mozart's two quartets in G minor (K. 478, 1785) and E-flat major (K. 493, 1786) are among the first masterpieces, treating the piano as an equal partner rather than an accompanist. Beethoven wrote early quartets (WoO 36) before turning to other forms, and Mendelssohn composed three youthful quartets.

The Romantic era produced the genre's core repertoire: Schumann's Quartet in E-flat, Op. 47 (1842), and the three quartets of Brahms — Op. 25 in G minor (with its famous Rondo alla zingarese finale), Op. 26 in A major, and Op. 60 in C minor. Dvořák wrote two (Opp. 23 and 87), and Fauré's two quartets (Opp. 15 and 45) are cornerstones of the French repertoire. Later contributions include works by Mahler (a single surviving movement), Webern, Schnittke, and Aaron Copland.

Characteristics

Composers prize the combination for its blend of the string trio's transparency with the piano's harmonic and rhythmic power, though balancing the piano against three string players — and giving the viola a meaningful role — are recurring challenges. Piano quartets are less numerous than piano quintets and string quartets, but the repertoire is disproportionately rich.
