Postsecularism

Postsecularism refers to a set of intellectual and social developments in which religion, far from disappearing as classical secularisation theory predicted, continues to play a significant public role in modern societies — and in which theorists accordingly revise the assumption that modernisation necessarily brings the decline and privatisation of faith. A society may be called "postsecular" when it adjusts to the continued existence of religious communities in an otherwise secularising environment.

Background

Classic secularisation theory, descended from Weber, Durkheim, and others, expected religion to fade with urbanisation, education, and scientific progress. By the late 20th century, the global resurgence of public religion — the Iranian Revolution of 1979, the rise of the religious right in the United States, liberation theology, and the growth of Pentecostalism and Islam worldwide — led sociologists such as Peter Berger, once a leading secularisation theorist, to recant the theory, and José Casanova to document the "deprivatisation" of religion.

Habermas and the postsecular society

The philosopher Jürgen Habermas gave the term wide currency from 2001 onward, arguing that secular citizens and states must take religious voices seriously in the public sphere, while religious citizens in turn accept the "translation" of religious claims into generally accessible reasons at the level of formal political institutions. Habermas's dialogue with Joseph Ratzinger (later Pope Benedict XVI) in 2004 became a prominent example of this engagement. Related work by Charles Taylor (A Secular Age, 2007) re-examined what it means to live in a secular age, distinguishing the separation of church and state from the changed conditions of belief.

Criticisms

Critics question whether "postsecular" implies a prior era that was ever fully secular, note that secularisation continues in Western Europe even as religion grows elsewhere, and warn that the term can be invoked to justify special privileges for religious actors in public life. Others use the term more narrowly in literary and cultural studies for texts that engage religious themes without orthodox belief.
