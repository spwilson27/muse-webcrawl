Essentialism

Plato's theory of Forms is a classic form of essentialism

Essentialism is the view that entities have a set of attributes that are necessary to their identity and function — their "essence" — by which they can be categorised and defined. In early Western thought it was the basis for a lot of scientific and philosophical theorising, with the concept prominently appearing in the work of Plato and Aristotle.

Philosophical essentialism

Plato's theory of Forms held that every object or quality has an ideal, immutable form of which particular instances are copies. Aristotle distinguished the essence (to ti ēn einai, "what it was to be") of a thing from its accidental properties. Essentialism influenced medieval scholasticism and later taxonomy: Linnaean classification long treated species as fixed kinds defined by essential characters. The theory of evolution and population thinking challenged biological essentialism, and philosophers such as Karl Popper attacked "methodological essentialism" in the social sciences.

Psychological essentialism

In developmental psychology, psychological essentialism describes the intuitive human tendency — present from early childhood — to treat categories such as animals, genders, and ethnic groups as if they had hidden, innate essences causing their observable traits. Researchers such as Susan Gelman argue this bias shapes reasoning and can underpin stereotyping.

Other uses

The term is used in education for the doctrine that schools should teach a core of essential knowledge, and in gender studies, where "gender essentialism" — the view that men and women have fixed, inherent natures — is widely criticised. Critics of essentialism, including existentialists and social constructionists, argue that identities are contingent rather than grounded in fixed essences; Jean-Paul Sartre's dictum "existence precedes essence" summarises the opposing position.
