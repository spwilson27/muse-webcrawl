Nicotine is a naturally produced alkaloid in the nightshade family of plants (most predominantly in tobacco and Duboisia hopwoodii) and is widely used recreationally as a stimulant and anxiolytic. As a pharmaceutical drug, it is used for smoking cessation to relieve withdrawal symptoms. Nicotine acts as a receptor agonist at most nicotinic acetylcholine receptors. It is a secondary metabolite produced in the tobacco plant's roots and accumulated in its leaves, where it serves as a potent insecticide.

Effects and addiction

Nicotine is highly addictive. It stimulates the release of neurotransmitters including dopamine, which reinforces tobacco use; tolerance and physical dependence develop with regular consumption. The health harms of smoking arise mainly from other constituents of tobacco smoke — tar, carbon monoxide and carcinogens — though nicotine itself is not classed as a carcinogen by the IARC. Nicotine replacement therapy (patches, gum, lozenges), e-cigarettes and other nicotine products deliver the drug without combustion.

History and chemistry

Nicotine was named after Jean Nicot, the French ambassador who sent tobacco to Paris in the 16th century. It was first isolated from tobacco in 1828 by Wilhelm Heinrich Posselt and Karl Ludwig Reimann, and its structure was determined in 1893. Acute nicotine poisoning, once associated with its use as an insecticide, can cause nausea, convulsions and, at high doses, death, though the lethal doses long quoted in textbooks have been questioned as far too low.
