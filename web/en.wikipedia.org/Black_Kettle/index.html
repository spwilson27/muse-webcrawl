Black Kettle

Black Kettle (Cheyenne: Mo'ohtavetoo'o; c. 1803 – 27 November 1868) was a prominent chief of the Southern Cheyenne who pursued peace with the United States throughout the turbulent decades of westward expansion. Despite his diplomatic efforts, his village was attacked twice by US forces: at the Sand Creek massacre in 1864, which he survived, and at the Battle of Washita River in 1868, where he and his wife were killed.

Peace efforts

Black Kettle emerged as a leading "peace chief" of the Southern Cheyenne and signed the Treaty of Fort Wise in 1861, accepting a reduced reservation in Colorado in exchange for peace. He repeatedly travelled to negotiate with US officials and sought to restrain young warriors from raiding, even as settler encroachment and the Colorado War of 1864 inflamed the frontier.

Sand Creek massacre

In November 1864 Black Kettle camped near Fort Lyon under assurances of military protection, flying a US flag and a white flag over his lodge. On 29 November 1864, Colonel John Chivington's Colorado volunteers attacked the village at Sand Creek, killing an estimated 150–230 Cheyenne and Arapaho, mostly women, children, and the elderly. Black Kettle escaped, but the atrocity was condemned by congressional investigations and is remembered as one of the worst massacres of Native Americans by the US Army.

Death at the Washita

After further treaties, including the Medicine Lodge Treaty of 1867, Black Kettle's band camped on the Washita River in Indian Territory in the winter of 1868. At dawn on 27 November 1868, Lieutenant Colonel George Armstrong Custer's 7th Cavalry attacked the village. Black Kettle and his wife were shot and killed while attempting to cross the river, a controversy that has surrounded Custer's career ever since.
