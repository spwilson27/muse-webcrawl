A tuned mass damper (TMD), also known as a harmonic absorber or seismic damper, is a device mounted in structures to reduce mechanical vibrations. It consists of a mass mounted on one or more damped springs; the mass is tuned to oscillate at the structure's resonant frequency but out of phase, so that energy is transferred from the structure into the damper and dissipated. Tuned mass dampers can reduce the amplitude of vibrations in skyscrapers, bridges, chimneys, and other slender structures excited by wind or earthquakes.

Principle

When a structure vibrates at the frequency to which the absorber is tuned, the damper mass resonates and moves in opposition to the structure's motion. The concept descends from Hermann Frahm's dynamic vibration absorber (1909). Effectiveness depends on the mass ratio, tuning ratio, and the damper's own damping; practical systems typically use masses of a few hundred tonnes in tall buildings.

Notable installations

  • Taipei 101 (Taiwan): a 660-metric-ton steel pendulum sphere suspended between the 87th and 92nd floors, visible to visitors; it reduced wind-induced sway by up to about 40%.
  • Citicorp Center (New York, 1978): a 400-ton concrete block, the first of its kind in a skyscraper.
  • John Hancock Tower (Boston): twin 300-ton lead blocks.
  • Shanghai Tower: a 1,000-tonne damper with an eddy-current damping system.
  • Millennium Bridge (London): dampers installed to stop lateral synchronous excitation ("wobble") by pedestrians.

Applications beyond buildings

Mass dampers are used in automobile engines and suspension ("mass dampers" in Formula One were banned in 2006), power transmission lines (Stockbridge dampers), aircraft, and ships' stabilizers.
