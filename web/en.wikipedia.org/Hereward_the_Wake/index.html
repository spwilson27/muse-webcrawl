Hereward the Wake

Hereward the Wake (c. 1035 – c. 1072) was an Anglo-Saxon nobleman and resistance leader who led opposition to William the Conqueror in the years following the Norman Conquest of England in 1066. His epithet "the Wake" may mean "the watchful" or derive from the later claim of descent claimed by the Wake family.

Hereward
Born: c. 1035, Lincolnshire
Died: c. 1072
Known for: Resistance to Norman rule

Resistance at Ely

From a base in the marshes of the Isle of Ely in the Fens, Hereward joined forces with Danish raiders and local rebels, most famously sacking Peterborough Abbey in 1070 with their help. William besieged Ely in 1071; according to tradition the causeway built across the marshes allowed the Normans to capture the isle after local monks revealed a secret path. Hereward himself is said to have escaped with a few followers into the wild fenland.

Sources and legend

His deeds are recorded in the Gesta Herewardi, a Latin prose narrative drawing on an earlier Old English account, and in the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle. Victorian novelist Charles Kingsley revived his fame in the 1866 novel Hereward the Wake: Last of the English, establishing him as a folk hero of English resistance.
