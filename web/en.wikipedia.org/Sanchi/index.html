Sanchi is a Buddhist complex on a hilltop in the Raisen district of Madhya Pradesh, India, about 46 kilometres north-east of Bhopal. It is famous for the Great Stupa, one of the oldest surviving stone structures in India, originally commissioned by the Mauryan emperor Ashoka in the 3rd century BCE. The site was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1989.

History

Ashoka is said to have built the original brick stupa over relics of the Buddha, along with a polished sandstone pillar bearing one of his edicts. Under the Shunga dynasty (2nd century BCE) the stupa was encased in stone and roughly doubled in size; the elaborate carved gateways (toranas) were added under the Satavahanas around the 1st century BCE–1st century CE. Sanchi flourished as a monastic centre for over a thousand years before falling into ruin, and was rediscovered by British officers in the early 19th century. Major restoration took place under Sir John Marshall in the early 20th century.

Monuments

Stupa No. 1 (the Great Stupa) is a hemispherical dome about 36.6 metres in diameter, crowned by a triple umbrella (chhatra) and enclosed by a stone railing with four richly carved toranas at the cardinal points. The torana reliefs depict Jataka tales and scenes from the Buddha's life, though the Buddha himself is represented only symbolically — by footprints, an empty throne, or the Bodhi tree.

Other structures include Stupas No. 2 and No. 3 (the latter containing relics of the Buddha's disciples Sariputta and Mahamoggallana), numerous monasteries and temples, and the Ashokan pillar. Relics from Sanchi displayed abroad were ceremonially returned to India in the 20th century.

Significance

Sanchi preserves some of the earliest and finest examples of Buddhist art and architecture in stone. Its torana sculpture is a key source for early Indian narrative art, and an image of the Great Stupa appears on the Indian ₹200 banknote.
