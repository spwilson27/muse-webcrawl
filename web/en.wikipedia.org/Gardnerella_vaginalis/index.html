Gardnerella vaginalis is a species of anaerobic bacterium that is a normal, though minor, component of the vaginal flora in many women. It is best known for its association with bacterial vaginosis (BV), in which it commonly becomes a predominant organism alongside other anaerobes.

Characteristics

G. vaginalis is a small, pleomorphic, Gram-variable rod (coccobacillus) that is facultatively anaerobic and non-motile. It was first described in the 1950s by Herman Gardner and Charles Dukes, and was previously classified under the names Haemophilus vaginalis and Corynebacterium vaginale. Modern genomic work has shown that what was long treated as a single species comprises several closely related species within the genus Gardnerella.

Role in bacterial vaginosis

In bacterial vaginosis, the lactobacillus-dominated vaginal flora is displaced by a polymicrobial community in which Gardnerella is frequently a key early coloniser, forming a biofilm on the vaginal epithelium to which other anaerobes adhere. Infection is associated with "clue cells" — vaginal epithelial cells coated with bacteria — visible on microscopy. G. vaginalis produces the cytotoxin vaginolysin and the enzyme sialidase, which contribute to its virulence.

Clinical significance

Although detectable in many women without symptoms, overgrowth is linked with the discharge and fishy odour characteristic of BV, and BV in turn is associated with increased risk of preterm birth and acquisition of sexually transmitted infections. Treatment of symptomatic BV typically uses metronidazole or clindamycin. The bacterium can also, rarely, cause bacteraemia and infections outside the genital tract.
