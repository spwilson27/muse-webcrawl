Helmholtz resonance

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Helmholtz resonance, also known as wind throb, is the phenomenon of air resonance in a cavity, such as when one blows across the top of an empty bottle. The name comes from a device created in the 1850s by Hermann von Helmholtz, the Helmholtz resonator, which he used to identify the various frequencies or musical pitches present in music and other complex sounds.

Qualitative description

When air is forced into a cavity, the pressure inside increases. When the external force is removed, the higher-pressure air inside flows out, overshooting, so that the pressure inside the cavity drops below ambient; the flow then reverses, and the cycle repeats. The air in the neck of the cavity behaves as a mass, and the air in the volume of the cavity as a spring: the system is a harmonic oscillator whose natural frequency depends on the geometry. At resonance, a steady small excitation (such as wind across the opening, or a car's slipstream past an open window) builds up a large oscillating pressure — the throbbing experienced in a car with one rear window open at speed ("wind throb" or buffeting).

Quantitative description

The resonant frequency is approximately

f = (v / 2π) · √( A / ( V · L′ ) )

where v is the speed of sound in air, A the cross-sectional area of the neck, V the volume of the cavity, and L′ the effective length of the neck (its geometric length plus an end correction of roughly 0.6–0.85 times the neck radius per open end). Larger volumes give lower pitches; blowing across a bottle yields frequencies typically between about 100 and 1000 Hz for common bottle sizes.

Applications

  • Acoustic analysis: Helmholtz's original tuned resonators served as narrow-band spectrum analysers before electronic instruments.
  • Musical instruments: the air resonance of guitar and violin bodies, and instruments such as the ocarina, depend on Helmholtz-like behaviour of the body cavity.
  • Architecture and noise control: perforated-panel and cavity absorbers tuned to Helmholtz resonance damp room modes and machinery noise.
  • Automotive and aerospace: intake and exhaust resonators silence engine noise; conversely, designers suppress buffeting by deflectors and window strategies.
  • Loudspeaker design: bass-reflex (ported) enclosures use the port–cabinet Helmholtz resonance to extend low-frequency output.
