King Tubby

Osbourne Ruddock (28 January 1941 – 6 February 1989), better known as King Tubby, was a Jamaican electronics and sound engineer who was a pioneer of dub music and the remix in the 1960s and 1970s. Working at his small studio in the Waterhouse district of Kingston, he transformed the mixing console into a musical instrument.

Career

Trained as an electronics technician, Tubby built and repaired sound system equipment before opening his own studio in 1971, equipped with a four-track mixer salvaged from producer Byron Lee's studio. For producer Duke Reid's Treasure Isle label he began creating instrumental "versions" of reggae tracks, stripping out vocals and applying echo, reverb, and dramatic drops of bass and drums. His 1973–75 sessions with producer Bunny "Striker" Lee and the Aggrovators, and albums such as Dub from the Roots and The Roots of Dub, laid the foundations of dub as a genre.

Innovations and protégés

Tubby pioneered techniques central to modern dance music: remixing, drop-outs, and using effects as compositional elements. His studio trained a generation of engineers, including Scientist (Hopeton Brown), Prince Jammy (later King Jammy), and Prince Philip Smart. He also ran his own sound system, King Tubby's Hi-Fi, and labels such as Waterhouse and Taurus.

Death and legacy

Tubby was shot dead outside his home in Duhaney Park, Kingston, on 6 February 1989, after returning from a studio session; the murder was never solved. His influence extends far beyond reggae: dub's spacious, bass-heavy aesthetic underpins dubstep, trip hop, ambient, and much electronic dance music, and his remix philosophy reshaped popular music production worldwide.
