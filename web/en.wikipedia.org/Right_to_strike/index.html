Right to strike

The right to strike is the right of workers to withhold their labour collectively in pursuit of workplace demands. It is recognised to varying degrees around the world and is regarded by the International Labour Organization's supervisory bodies as an essential corollary of freedom of association, though it is rarely absolute and is often restricted for public servants, essential services and the armed forces.

International law

The right is grounded in instruments such as the ILO's Freedom of Association Convention (No. 87, 1948), the International Covenant on Economic, Social and Cultural Rights (Article 8), the European Social Charter (Article 6(4)) and the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union (Article 28). Many national constitutions, including those of France, Italy, Spain and South Africa, expressly guarantee the right.

History

Strikes were criminalised in many industrialising countries in the 18th and 19th centuries — for example under Britain's Combination Acts and France's Le Chapelier Law — before being gradually legalised as trade unions gained recognition. In the United States, the National Labor Relations Act of 1935 protected most private-sector strike activity, while later statutes such as the Taft–Hartley Act (1947) restricted secondary boycotts and permitted injunctions in national emergencies.

Limitations

Common limitations include bans or minimum-service requirements in essential services such as hospitals, firefighting and policing; requirements for ballots, notice periods and cooling-off periods; prohibitions on political and sympathy strikes; and rules allowing employers to hire replacement workers in some jurisdictions.
