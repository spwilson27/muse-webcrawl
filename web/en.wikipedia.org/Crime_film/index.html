Crime film

Crime films centre on criminals, law enforcement, or both.
Subgenres
Gangster film · Film noir · Police procedural · Heist film · Crime thriller · Legal drama
Notable examples
The Godfather (1972), Scarface (1932/1983), Goodfellas (1990), Pulp Fiction (1994)

Crime film is a film genre inspired by the literary genre of crime fiction. Films of this genre generally involve various aspects of crime and its detection. Stylistically, the genre may overlap and combine with many other genres, such as drama or gangster film, but also include comedy, and is divided into many subgenres.

History

Early crime films drew on stage melodrama and the success of gangster pictures during Prohibition, including Little Caesar (1931), The Public Enemy (1931) and Scarface (1932). The 1940s produced film noir, with its shadowed visual style and morally ambiguous detectives, exemplified by The Maltese Falcon (1941) and Double Indemnity (1944). The 1970s New Hollywood era reinvigorated the genre with The French Connection (1971), The Godfather (1972) and its sequel, widely regarded among the greatest films ever made.

Subgenres

The gangster film follows criminals' rise and fall; the police procedural emphasises investigative method; the heist film centres on the planning and execution of a theft, as in Rififi (1955) and Ocean's Eleven (1960/2001); neo-noir updates noir conventions in contemporary settings, as in Chinatown (1974) and Heat (1995). International traditions include French policiers, Hong Kong heroic bloodshed films of John Woo, Japanese yakuza films, and South Korean crime thrillers.

Themes

Crime films frequently explore loyalty, corruption, the porous boundary between law enforcers and law breakers, and the appeal and cost of life outside the law. Many function as social commentary on urbanisation, immigration, prohibition and organised crime.
