Colonel William Prescott (February 20, 1726 – October 13, 1795) was an American colonel in the Revolutionary War who commanded the rebel forces in the Battle of Bunker Hill. Prescott is known for his order to his soldiers, "Do not fire until you see the whites of their eyes", such that the rebel troops may shoot at the enemy at shorter ranges, and therefore more accurately and lethally, and so conserve their limited stocks of ammunition. It is debated whether Prescott or someone earlier coined the phrase.

Early life and French and Indian War

Prescott was born in Groton, Massachusetts, and inherited a substantial farm at Pepperell. He served as a lieutenant in the provincial forces during King George's War, taking part in the successful siege of Louisbourg in 1745, and as a captain in the French and Indian War, fighting at Fort Beauséjour and in the relief of Fort William Henry.

Revolutionary War

When fighting broke out in 1775, Prescott, then a militia colonel, marched his regiment to Cambridge. On the night of June 16–17, 1775, he led about 1,200 men onto the Charlestown peninsula and supervised the fortification of Breed's Hill. The next day his troops repulsed two British assaults before a third forced them to withdraw for want of powder. Prescott was among the last to leave the redoubt. He went on to command the 7th Continental Regiment, served in the New York campaign of 1776 and at Saratoga in 1777, then retired to his farm because of ill health.

Legacy

Prescott is commemorated by a statue beside the Bunker Hill Monument and by the former town of Prescott, Massachusetts. The Grand Lodge of Massachusetts presented a sword believed to be his to his descendants; it is now displayed at Bunker Hill.
