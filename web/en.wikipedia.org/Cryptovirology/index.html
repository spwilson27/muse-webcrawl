Cryptovirology

Cryptovirology is a field that studies the use of cryptography by malicious software, principally malware such as computer viruses, worms, and ransomware. The term was introduced by Adam L. Young and Moti Yung, who demonstrated how public-key cryptography could be weaponized in a computer virus.

History

Young and Yung presented the concept at the IEEE Symposium on Security and Privacy in 1996 in their paper "Cryptovirology: Extortion-Based Security Threats and Countermeasures." They showed that a virus could use the attacker's public key to encrypt data on a victim's machine such that only the attacker, holding the private key, could decrypt it — a scheme that later became the basis of modern ransomware extortion.

Cryptoviral extortion

In a cryptoviral extortion attack, malware encrypts the victim's files with a symmetric key, then encrypts that key with the attacker's public key and deletes the plaintext key. The victim is told to pay a ransom, increasingly in cryptocurrency, to obtain the decryption key. Notable ransomware families employing this model include CryptoLocker, WannaCry, and LockBit.

Other applications

Beyond extortion, cryptovirology covers deniable password snatching, covert channels in malware communication, and attacks in which cryptography conceals a virus's payload from analysis. The field also studies defensive measures, including monitoring for suspicious cryptographic API usage.
