Thermoreception

Thermoreception is the sense by which an organism perceives temperatures. Thermoreceptors are specialised sensory neurons — mostly free nerve endings in the skin — whose firing rates change with temperature. In mammals, peripheral thermoreceptors are divided into cold receptors and warmth receptors, with separate populations responding to innocuous cooling, innocuous warming, and noxious heat or cold.

Molecular mechanisms

Much of thermoreception is mediated by transient receptor potential (TRP) channels. TRPV1 responds to noxious heat (above about 43 °C) and to capsaicin, the pungent compound in chilli peppers; TRPV3 and TRPV4 respond to warmth; TRPM8 responds to cooling and to menthol; and TRPA1 has been implicated in noxious cold sensation. The discovery of these receptors, particularly by David Julius's laboratory, was recognised by the 2021 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Central processing

Thermal signals travel via the spinothalamic tract to the thalamus and somatosensory cortex, while information about core body temperature, gathered by receptors in the spinal cord, viscera, and hypothalamus itself, feeds into autonomic thermoregulation — sweating, vasomotor changes, and shivering.

In other animals

Some animals possess specialised infrared senses: pit vipers, pythons, and boas have heat-sensitive pit organs that image the infrared radiation of warm prey, and vampire bats have a unique TRPV1-based heat sense in their noseleaves.
