Chuvash language

Chuvash (Чӑваш чӗлхи)
Speakers: ~1 million
Region: Chuvashia, Russia
Family: Turkic — Oghur branch (sole living member)
Script: Cyrillic

Chuvash is a Turkic language spoken by the Chuvash people, mainly in the Chuvash Republic (Chuvashia) in European Russia, where it is co-official with Russian. It is the only surviving member of the Oghur (Bulgaric) branch of Turkic, the sister branch of the Common Turkic languages that include Turkish and Tatar, and it preserves features lost elsewhere in the family, such as the r/l forms corresponding to Common Turkic z/ş (rhotacism and lambdacism).

History

Chuvash descends from the language of Volga Bulgaria. Its speakers adopted Orthodox Christianity in the 18th–19th centuries, and a literary language was developed in the 19th century, notably through the educator Ivan Yakovlev, who devised a Cyrillic-based alphabet in the 1870s and translated the Bible. The modern alphabet, settled in the 1930s–40s, uses additional letters such as Ӑ, Ӗ, Ҫ, and Ӳ.

Features and status

Chuvash is agglutinative and subject–object–verb, with vowel harmony somewhat reduced by long contact with Russian and Tatar, from which it has borrowed heavily. Despite official status and teaching in schools, the number of speakers has been declining — from about 1.6 million in 1989 to roughly a million or fewer — and UNESCO lists the language as vulnerable. Revitalisation efforts include Chuvash-language media, theatre, and online resources.
