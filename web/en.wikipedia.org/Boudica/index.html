Boudica (also spelled Boadicea or Boudicca; c. AD 30 – 60/61) was a queen of the British Celtic Iceni tribe who led a major uprising against the occupying forces of the Roman Empire in AD 60 or 61. She is considered a British national heroine and a symbol of the struggle for justice and independence.

The revolt

When her husband Prasutagus died, Rome annexed the Iceni kingdom outright, seized property, had Boudica flogged and her daughters raped, according to Tacitus. In response Boudica united the Iceni, Trinovantes and others. Her forces destroyed Camulodunum (Colchester), then Londinium (London) and Verulamium (St Albans), burning them and killing an estimated 70,000–80,000 people; destruction layers of red burnt clay remain in the archaeology of all three cities.

Defeat and legacy

The governor Suetonius Paulinus, with about 10,000 men, defeated her much larger army at the Battle of Watling Street, using a narrow defile and disciplined wedges. Tacitus says Boudica poisoned herself; Cassius Dio says she fell ill and died. Forgotten for centuries, she was revived in the Victorian era — Prince Albert championed Thomas Thornycroft's great statue of Boudica in her chariot, erected beside Westminster Bridge in 1902 — and her name, meaning "victory", echoes Queen Victoria's.
