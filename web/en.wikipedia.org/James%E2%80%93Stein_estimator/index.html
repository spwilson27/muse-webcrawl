James–Stein estimator

ProponentsCharles Stein, Willard James
Proposed1956–1961
FieldStatistics, estimation theory

The James–Stein estimator is a biased estimator of the mean of a multivariate normal distribution. It famously shows that the ordinary sample mean is inadmissible when estimating three or more parameters simultaneously — a surprising result known as Stein's paradox.

Background

In 1956 Charles Stein proved that the usual maximum-likelihood estimator for a multivariate normal mean is inadmissible in dimensions three and higher. In 1961 Willard James and Stein gave an explicit estimator that "shrinks" the sample means toward a central value and achieves uniformly lower total mean squared error.

Intuition

Although each coordinate is estimated using only data from all coordinates jointly, borrowing strength across unrelated parameters reduces overall risk. The classic illustration uses baseball batting averages: shrinking early-season averages toward the grand mean predicts later performance better than the raw averages.

Influence

The James–Stein estimator helped launch modern shrinkage estimation, empirical Bayes methods, and regularisation techniques widely used in statistics and machine learning.
