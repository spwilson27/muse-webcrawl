Galaxy morphological classification

Galaxy morphological classification is a system used by astronomers to divide galaxies into groups based on their visual appearance. Several schemes are in use, the most famous being the Hubble sequence, devised by Edwin Hubble in 1926 and often depicted as a "tuning fork" diagram.

The Hubble sequence

Hubble classified galaxies into three broad families: elliptical galaxies (E0–E7, graded by apparent ellipticity), spiral galaxies (S), and lenticular galaxies (S0), which form a transition between the two arms of the fork. Spirals are subdivided into normal spirals (S) and barred spirals (SB), and further by the tightness of their arms and size of their central bulge, from early-type (Sa, tightly wound, large bulge) to late-type (Sc, loosely wound, small bulge). Galaxies that fit none of these categories are called irregular (Irr).

Later systems

Gérard de Vaucouleurs extended Hubble's scheme in 1959, adding finer subdivisions, rings, and the SA/SAB/SB family gradations. Sidney van den Bergh introduced luminosity classes for spirals, and William Wilson Morgan developed the Yerkes scheme, classifying galaxies by the central concentration of light and the spectral type of their stellar populations. Modern surveys, including citizen-science projects such as Galaxy Zoo, continue to use morphology alongside color, star-formation rate, and structural measurements.

Significance

Morphology correlates with other galaxy properties: ellipticals tend to be dominated by old, red stars with little gas, while late-type spirals and irregulars are bluer and actively forming stars. These correlations inform theories of galaxy formation and evolution, including the role of mergers.
