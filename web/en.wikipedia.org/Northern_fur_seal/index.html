The northern fur seal (Callorhinus ursinus) is an eared seal native to the northern Pacific Ocean, Bering Sea and Sea of Okhotsk. It is the only living member of the genus Callorhinus. The species is strongly sexually dimorphic: adult males are up to five times heavier than females and have thick, coarse manes. Both sexes possess an exceptionally dense underfur — historically the cause of intense commercial hunting.

Distribution and life history

The largest breeding colonies are on the Pribilof Islands in the Bering Sea, with others in the Commander Islands, Robben Island, the Kuril Islands and San Miguel Island off California. Adults migrate south in winter as far as California and Japan. Males arrive at the rookeries in May and fight for territories; females come ashore in June or July, give birth to a single pup, and breed again within days, using delayed implantation to time the next pregnancy.

Exploitation and conservation

Commercial sealing in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries killed millions for their pelts, and pelagic (open-sea) hunting drove the species towards collapse by the early twentieth century. The North Pacific Fur Seal Convention of 1911 banned pelagic sealing and allowed recovery to over a million animals. The Pribilof population has declined again since the late twentieth century — for reasons still debated, including prey shifts, entanglement and predation — and the species is listed as Vulnerable.
