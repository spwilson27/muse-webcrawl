The Michelson–Morley experiment was a scientific experiment performed in 1887 by American physicists Albert A. Michelson and Edward W. Morley to detect the relative motion of Earth through the luminiferous aether, the hypothetical medium then thought to carry light waves.

Method

Using an interferometer of Michelson's design, the experimenters split a beam of light into two perpendicular paths, reflected the beams back, and recombined them to produce interference fringes. If Earth moved through the aether, the light travel times along the two arms should differ as the apparatus rotated, shifting the fringe pattern by a measurable amount.

Result and impact

The observed shift was only a tiny fraction of the expected value—effectively null. The result was one of the most famous "failed" experiments in physics: rather than detecting the aether, it provided strong evidence against aether-drift theories and supported the conclusion that the speed of light is the same in all directions regardless of Earth's motion. It became a key empirical foundation for Einstein's special theory of relativity (1905), and increasingly precise repetitions have confirmed the null result ever since.
