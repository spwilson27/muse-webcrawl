A hydrogen atom is an atom of the chemical element hydrogen. The electrically neutral atom consists of a single positively charged proton and a single negatively charged electron bound together by the Coulomb force. Atomic hydrogen constitutes about 75% of the baryonic mass of the universe. Because it is the simplest possible atom, the hydrogen atom has played a central role in the development of atomic theory and quantum mechanics, and its spectrum is described with extraordinary precision by theory.

Structure and energy levels

In the Bohr model (1913), the electron occupies circular orbits whose energies are quantised as En = −13.6 eV / n², successfully reproducing the Rydberg formula for hydrogen's spectral lines. In the full quantum-mechanical treatment, the electron is described by a wavefunction obtained by solving the Schrödinger equation for a central Coulomb potential. Its states are labelled by the quantum numbers n (principal), ℓ (azimuthal), and m (magnetic), plus electron spin. The characteristic size of the ground state is the Bohr radius, a₀ ≈ 5.29 × 10−11 m.

Spectrum

Transitions between energy levels produce the hydrogen spectral series: the Lyman series (ultraviolet, transitions to n = 1), Balmer series (visible, to n = 2), Paschen series (infrared, to n = 3), and further series beyond. The famous red H-alpha line at 656.3 nm dominates the colour of emission nebulae. A very weak hyperfine transition of the ground state at a wavelength of 21 cm (1420 MHz), caused by the interaction of the electron and proton spins, is of enormous importance in radio astronomy for mapping neutral hydrogen across the universe.

Isotopes

The most common isotope, protium (1H), has no neutrons. Deuterium (2H) has one neutron and tritium (3H) has two and is radioactive. Fine structure, the Lamb shift, and hyperfine structure reveal effects beyond the basic Schrödinger solution, including relativity and quantum electrodynamics.
