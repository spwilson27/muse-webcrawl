Urban forestry
FocusCare and management of trees in urban settings
ComponentsStreet trees, parks, urban woodland, greenways
Related fieldsArboriculture, urban ecology, landscape architecture

Urban forestry is the care and management of single trees and tree populations in urban settings for the purpose of improving the urban environment. It involves both planning and management, including the programming of care and maintenance operations for the urban forest, which comprises trees in streets, parks, public and private gardens, cemeteries, and remnant woodlands within and around cities.

Benefits

Urban trees provide a wide range of ecosystem services. They shade buildings and pavements, reducing the urban heat island effect and energy use for cooling; intercept rainfall and reduce stormwater runoff; sequester carbon and filter air pollutants; provide habitat for wildlife; and contribute to public health, recreation and property values. Studies commonly find that the benefits of well-maintained urban trees exceed their planting and maintenance costs.

Practice

Urban foresters inventory tree populations, assess canopy cover, select species suited to site conditions and a changing climate, and manage risks from pests, diseases and storms. Common threats include invasive insects such as the emerald ash borer, soil compaction, limited rooting space, and conflicts with utilities and infrastructure. Many municipalities set canopy-cover targets and maintain tree ordinances governing planting, pruning and removal.

History

Although cities have planted trees for millennia — from avenue plantings in early modern Europe to the 19th-century parks movement and Arbor Day (first celebrated in Nebraska in 1872) — urban forestry emerged as a named professional discipline in the 1960s, notably at the University of Toronto, and became institutionalized through professional bodies such as the International Society of Arboriculture.
