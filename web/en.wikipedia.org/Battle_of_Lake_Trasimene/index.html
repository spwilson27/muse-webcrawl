The Battle of Lake Trasimene was a major battle of the Second Punic War, fought on 21 June 217 BC between the Carthaginian army of Hannibal and a Roman army under the consul Gaius Flaminius. Hannibal laid an ambush along the northern shore of Lake Trasimene in Umbria — often described as the largest ambush in military history — in which the Roman army, strung out in marching column along the lakeside road, was attacked from the hillsides in morning mist before it could deploy. The army was virtually annihilated; Flaminius was killed, some 15,000 Romans died, and thousands more were captured.

Background

After crossing the Alps in 218 BC and winning at the Trebia, Hannibal wintered in Cisalpine Gaul, recruiting Gallic allies. In spring 217 BC he crossed the Apennines, traversed the marshes of the Arno (losing an eye to infection), and deliberately ravaged the countryside of Etruria to provoke Flaminius, the consul shadowing him from Arretium, into a rash pursuit. Flaminius, eager for a victory and unwilling to let Hannibal devastate allied territory before his eyes, followed closely without proper reconnaissance.

The ambush

During the night of 20–21 June, Hannibal stationed his heavy infantry on the hills east of the defile along the lake, his cavalry and Gallic infantry concealed in the folds of the shore hills, and light troops to seal the road's exit; campfires lit on distant hills suggested his army was far away. At dawn, in thick mist, Flaminius's column marched along the narrow shore road into the defile. When the Roman vanguard reached the far end, Hannibal's trumpets sounded and the Carthaginians attacked from all sides. The Romans, unable to form their battle line, fought where they stood; a group of 6,000 cut its way out through the attackers only to surrender to Maharbal's cavalry the next day. The fighting lasted about three hours. An earthquake during the battle, recorded by Livy, reportedly went unnoticed by the combatants.

Aftermath

The disaster, following the Trebia, threw Rome into panic and led to the appointment of Quintus Fabius Maximus as dictator, whose delaying strategy — avoiding pitched battle with Hannibal — became known as the Fabian strategy. Hannibal, unable to take Rome without siege equipment or a secure supply base, marched south into Apulia, freeing his Italian and Greek prisoners without ransom in a bid to detach Rome's allies. The Roman catastrophe at Trasimene was surpassed the following year at the Battle of Cannae.
