Microplastics

Microplastics are fragments of plastic less than 5 millimetres (0.20 in) in length, according to the definition used by the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA). They enter ecosystems from a variety of sources and have been detected in oceans, soils, drinking water, air, and the bodies of many organisms, including humans. Because plastics degrade extremely slowly, microplastics accumulate in the environment and are regarded as a persistent pollutant of growing concern.

Classification

Primary microplastics are manufactured at small size. They include plastic pellets (nurdles) used as industrial feedstock, microbeads formerly added to cosmetics and toothpaste as abrasives, and fibres shed from synthetic textiles during washing. Many jurisdictions, including the United States (Microbead-Free Waters Act of 2015) and the United Kingdom, have banned microbeads in rinse-off cosmetics.

Secondary microplastics arise from the breakdown of larger plastic items — bottles, bags, fishing gear and tyres — through ultraviolet radiation, mechanical abrasion and weathering. Tyre wear particles are among the largest single sources of microplastic entering waterways in many studies.

Distribution

Microplastics have been found from the surface waters of the open ocean, where they concentrate in subtropical gyres such as the Great Pacific garbage patch, to deep-sea sediments, Arctic sea ice, mountain lakes and agricultural soils. They have been reported in seafood, sea salt, bottled and tap water, beer, honey, household dust and human blood, lung tissue and placenta, though the health significance of these findings remains under investigation.

Effects on organisms

Marine animals from zooplankton to whales ingest microplastics, which can cause physical blockage, false satiation and reduced feeding. Plastics can also adsorb persistent organic pollutants from seawater and may leach chemical additives such as plasticisers; whether this materially increases pollutant exposure in wild food webs is debated. Laboratory studies show effects on growth, reproduction and behaviour in some species at high exposure levels.

Human health

People are exposed to microplastics through food, water and inhalation. As of the early 2020s, major reviews by the World Health Organization and the European Food Safety Authority concluded that evidence of harm to humans at current exposure levels was limited and that further research was needed, while studies have continued to detect particles in human tissues.

Mitigation

Responses include bans on single-use plastics and microbeads, improved wastewater and stormwater treatment, washing-machine filters for synthetic fibres, extended producer responsibility schemes, and cleanup of legacy plastic debris. Reducing overall plastic production and leakage is considered the most effective long-term strategy.
