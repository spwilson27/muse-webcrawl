Social learning theory

Much human learning occurs through observing and imitating models

Social learning theory is a theory of learning and social behaviour which proposes that new behaviours can be acquired by observing and imitating others. Developed principally by Albert Bandura, it states that learning is a cognitive process that takes place in a social context and can occur partly through observation alone — without direct reinforcement — via processes such as modelling and vicarious reinforcement.

The Bobo doll experiments

In Bandura's famous experiments of 1961–63, children who observed an adult model acting aggressively toward a large inflatable "Bobo doll" were more likely to imitate that aggression than children who saw a non-aggressive model or no model. Children were also influenced by whether the model was rewarded or punished, demonstrating vicarious reinforcement.

Key concepts

Bandura identified four requirements for observational learning: attention to the model, retention of what was observed, the ability to reproduce the behaviour, and the motivation to perform it. He also developed the concept of self-efficacy — belief in one's own capability to succeed — and the model of reciprocal determinism, in which personal factors, behaviour, and the environment continually influence one another. In 1986 Bandura renamed the framework social cognitive theory to emphasise the cognitive processes involved.

Applications

Social learning theory has been applied in education, health promotion, criminology (where it helps explain the transmission of criminal behaviour), and media studies, where it informs debates about the effects of violent media on children.
