French Gothic architecture

French Gothic architecture is the style of architecture prevalent in France from 1140 until about 1500, which largely divided into four styles: Early Gothic, High Gothic (including Rayonnant), and Late Gothic (Flamboyant). It originated in the Île-de-France and Picardy regions of northern France and spread across Europe, becoming the dominant style for cathedrals and churches for three centuries.

Origins

The style emerged with the rebuilding of the chevet of the Abbey of Saint-Denis by Abbot Suger, consecrated in 1144, which combined the pointed arch, the rib vault, and large stained-glass windows to fill the church with light. Early Gothic cathedrals include Sens, Noyon, Laon, and Notre-Dame de Paris (begun 1163).

High Gothic and Rayonnant

High Gothic, beginning with Chartres Cathedral (1194), Amiens, Reims, and Bourges, achieved greater height, larger windows, and more unified interiors. The Rayonnant style, exemplified by the Sainte-Chapelle in Paris (1248), pushed walls toward near-total glazing with elaborate bar tracery.

Flamboyant

The Late Gothic or Flamboyant style, named for its flame-like tracery, flourished in the late 14th and 15th centuries in works such as the church of Saint-Maclou in Rouen, before giving way to Renaissance architecture.

Characteristics

Defining features include pointed arches, ribbed vaults, flying buttresses, rose windows, and a skeletal stone structure permitting tall, light-filled interiors.
