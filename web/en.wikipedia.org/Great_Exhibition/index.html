Great Exhibition

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Great Exhibition of the Works of Industry of All Nations, also known as the Great Exhibition or the Crystal Palace Exhibition, was an international exhibition held in Hyde Park, London, from 1 May to 15 October 1851. It was the first in a series of world's fairs and a landmark celebration of Victorian industrial technology and design.

Organisation

The exhibition was organised by Henry Cole and Prince Albert, husband of Queen Victoria, together with a Royal Commission. More than 100,000 objects from some 15,000 contributors — roughly half of them British and imperial — were displayed, from steam engines, looms, and railway locomotives to fine crafts, furniture, and raw materials from the colonies.

The Crystal Palace

The exhibits were housed in the Crystal Palace, a revolutionary cast-iron and plate-glass building designed by Joseph Paxton and erected in Hyde Park in a matter of months. About 564 metres long and enclosing some 92,000 square metres, it enclosed the park's mature elm trees and became an icon of industrial modernity. After the exhibition the palace was dismantled and re-erected in Sydenham, south London, where it stood until destroyed by fire in 1936.

Impact

Some six million visitors — equivalent to a third of Britain's population — attended, travelling by the new railways on cheap excursion tickets; admission on "shilling days" opened the exhibition to working-class visitors. The event made a profit of £186,000, which was used to purchase land in South Kensington and fund the Victoria and Albert Museum, the Science Museum, the Natural History Museum, and other institutions of "Albertopolis." Profits also endow the 1851 Research Fellowships, still awarded today.
