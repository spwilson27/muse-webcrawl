Alfred Hershey

Alfred Day Hershey (December 4, 1908 – May 22, 1997) was an American Nobel Prize–winning bacteriologist and geneticist. He is best known for the Hershey–Chase experiment of 1952, performed with Martha Chase, which provided strong evidence that DNA, not protein, is the genetic material.

Alfred Hershey
Born: December 4, 1908, Owosso, Michigan, U.S.
Died: May 22, 1997, Syosset, New York, U.S.
Known for: Hershey–Chase experiment
Awards: Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (1969)

Career

Hershey earned his PhD from Michigan State College (now Michigan State University) in 1934 and worked at Washington University in St. Louis before moving in 1950 to the Carnegie Institution of Washington's Department of Genetics at Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory, New York. He was a central figure in the informal "phage group" of scientists studying bacteriophages.

Hershey–Chase experiment

In 1952, Hershey and Martha Chase labelled the protein and DNA of T2 bacteriophage with different radioactive isotopes and showed that the DNA entered the infected bacterial cells while most of the protein remained outside — demonstrating that DNA carries the genetic instructions for producing new phages.

Nobel Prize

Hershey shared the 1969 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Max Delbrück and Salvador Luria "for their discoveries concerning the replication mechanism and the genetic structure of viruses".
