Bishop

A bishop is an ordained member of the clergy in many Christian traditions who is entrusted with a position of authority and oversight. The word derives from the Greek episkopos (ἐπίσκοπος), meaning "overseer". In churches with episcopal polity — including the Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Anglican, and many Lutheran and Methodist churches — bishops form the highest of the three traditional orders of ordained ministry, above priests (presbyters) and deacons, and are regarded in several traditions as successors of the apostles.

Role and functions

A bishop typically governs a diocese, a territorial district of churches, from a seat (cathedra) in a cathedral city. Episcopal duties include ordaining priests and deacons, confirming the faithful, consecrating churches, teaching and guarding doctrine, and representing the diocese. In the Catholic Church, bishops are appointed by the pope; in Orthodox and many Anglican contexts, they are elected by synods or church bodies. A bishop overseeing an especially important diocese may be styled an archbishop; the Bishop of Rome, the pope, is regarded by Catholics as the chief bishop of the universal Church.

History

Episcopal structures developed during the first centuries of Christianity: by the time of Ignatius of Antioch (early 2nd century), letters attest a single bishop presiding over each city's church, assisted by presbyters and deacons. As Christianity spread, rural congregations came under the city bishop, producing the diocesan system. The doctrine of apostolic succession holds that bishops are consecrated in an unbroken line reaching back to the apostles. The Protestant Reformation abolished bishops in many churches (presbyterian and congregational polities), retained them with altered theology in others, and the office's validity remains a major topic in ecumenical dialogue.

Insignia and dress

Traditional insignia include the mitre (a tall liturgical headdress), the crosier (a pastoral staff), a pectoral cross, and an episcopal ring. Bishops commonly wear purple or scarlet dress depending on tradition and rank. A bishop's coat of arms is distinguished by a galero (wide-brimmed hat) with tassels and a processional cross.

Other uses

The term is also used, by translation or analogy, in some non-Christian contexts and in the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints, where a bishop leads a local congregation (ward) as a lay leader. "Bishop" is additionally the name of a chess piece, and appears in surnames and place names.
