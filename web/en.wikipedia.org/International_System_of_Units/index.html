The International System of Units, internationally known by the abbreviation SI (from French Système international d'unités), is the modern form of the metric system and the world's most widely used system of measurement. Established and maintained by the General Conference on Weights and Measures (CGPM), it is the only system of measurement with official status in nearly every country in the world, employed in science, technology, industry, and everyday commerce.

Base units

The SI comprises a coherent system of units built on seven base units, defined in terms of seven defining constants:

History

The system grew out of the French Revolutionary metric system of the 1790s. The Metre Convention of 1875 created the BIPM and established international prototypes of the metre and kilogram. The SI itself was formally adopted in 1960, building on the metre–kilogram–second system. On 20 May 2019, a major redefinition took effect: the kilogram, ampere, kelvin, and mole were redefined in terms of fixed numerical values of fundamental constants, so that all SI units are now defined by constants of nature rather than physical artefacts or specific experiments.

Derived units

Derived units are formed by combining base units. Twenty-two have special names, including the hertz (frequency), newton (force), pascal (pressure), joule (energy), watt (power), volt (electric potential), ohm (resistance), and degree Celsius (temperature). The system also uses decimal prefixes — from quetta- (1030) down to quecto- (10−30) — to form multiples and submultiples, such as kilometre and millimetre.

Usage worldwide

Only three countries — the United States, Liberia, and Myanmar — have not fully adopted the SI as their primary system, though SI units are widely used in science, medicine, and industry even there. The United Kingdom uses a mixture, retaining miles for road distances and pints for draught beer and milk.
