English auxiliary verbs

The English auxiliary verbs are a small set of verbs, which includes the English modal auxiliary verbs and a few others. Although the definitions have slight variations among authors, they are generally understood as verbs that provide additional meaning to another verb, expressing tense, aspect, modality, voice, emphasis, negativity or interrogative status. Auxiliary verbs generally accompany a main verb (a lexical verb). The main verb provides the main semantic content of the clause.

Primary auxiliaries

The three primary auxiliaries are be, have and do:

  • be forms the progressive aspect (she is running) and the passive voice (it was built);
  • have forms the perfect aspect (they have finished);
  • do is used for questions, negation and emphasis in clauses lacking another auxiliary (Do you agree?, I do not know, I do like it) — the phenomenon known as do-support.

Modal auxiliaries

The central modal verbs are can/could, may/might, shall/should, will/would and must. They express possibility, permission, obligation, prediction and related meanings, take a bare infinitive complement, and lack non-finite forms and third-person singular -s. Marginal modals include ought to, used to, need and dare.

Properties

Auxiliaries are distinguished from lexical verbs by a cluster of behaviours sometimes summarised by the acronym NICE: Negation (they take not directly), Inversion (they invert with the subject in questions), Code (they can stand in for a whole verb phrase, as in Yes, I can), and Emphasis (they carry stress for emphatic polarity). Only the first auxiliary in a chain inverts or takes negation, and auxiliary chains follow a fixed order: modal + have + be (progressive) + be (passive) + main verb, as in it may have been being repaired.

Semi-auxiliaries

Expressions such as be going to, have to, be about to and be able to function much like auxiliaries in meaning, though they retain more of the syntax of ordinary verb phrases.
