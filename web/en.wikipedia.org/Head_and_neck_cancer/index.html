Head and neck cancer

Head and neck cancer is a group of cancers that starts in the mouth, nose, throat, larynx, sinuses, or salivary glands. Symptoms may include a lump or a sore that does not heal, a sore throat that does not go away, trouble swallowing, or a change in the voice. The great majority — more than 90 per cent — are squamous cell carcinomas arising from the mucosal surfaces of the oral cavity, pharynx, and larynx.

Causes

The principal risk factors are tobacco use and alcohol consumption, which act synergistically. Infection with human papillomavirus (HPV), particularly type 16, is an increasingly important cause of oropharyngeal cancers, especially of the tonsils and base of tongue, and HPV-positive tumours have a distinctly better prognosis. Epstein–Barr virus is associated with nasopharyngeal carcinoma, and betel nut chewing, occupational exposures, and poor oral hygiene also contribute.

Diagnosis and staging

Diagnosis involves physical examination and endoscopy, imaging (CT, MRI, PET), and biopsy. Tumours are staged using the TNM system, with the stage guiding treatment; HPV (p16) status is now part of staging for oropharyngeal cancer.

Treatment

Treatment may include surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy such as cetuximab, and immunotherapy such as pembrolizumab or nivolumab for recurrent or metastatic disease. Early-stage disease is often treated with a single modality; advanced disease usually requires combinations, which carry significant effects on speech, swallowing, and appearance, making multidisciplinary care essential.

Epidemiology

Head and neck cancers account for roughly 4 per cent of cancers in the United States and a much higher fraction in parts of South and Southeast Asia; globally there are on the order of 900,000 new cases and about 450,000 deaths per year. Men are affected substantially more often than women.
