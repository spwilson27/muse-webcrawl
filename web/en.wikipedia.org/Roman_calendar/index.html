The Roman calendar was the calendar used by the Roman Kingdom and Republic. It evolved from a lunisolar system traditionally attributed to Romulus and Numa Pompilius into the Julian calendar introduced by Julius Caesar in 45 BC, which became the basis of the modern Gregorian calendar. Roman dates were counted inclusively, backwards from three fixed points in each month: the Kalends (1st), the Nones (5th or 7th) and the Ides (13th or 15th).

Structure

The pre-Julian calendar had 355 days in twelve months, beginning in March. To keep the year aligned with the seasons, an intercalary month, Mercedonius, was supposed to be inserted every other year by the pontifices — but intercalation was often manipulated for political advantage or neglected during wars, so by Caesar's time the calendar was months adrift from the solar year.

Julian reform

As dictator, Caesar reformed the calendar in 46–45 BC, advised by the Alexandrian astronomer Sosigenes. The year 46 BC was extended to 445 days (the "year of confusion"), and from 45 BC the Julian calendar ran with 365 days and a leap day every fourth year. Augustus later corrected errors in leap-year observance and renamed the months Quintilis and Sextilis as July and August.

Days and festivals

Days were classified in the fasti (published calendars) as dies fasti (when legal business was permitted), nefasti (forbidden), and festival days. Major festivals included the Saturnalia in December, the Lupercalia in February and the Ludi Romani in September. Years were named after the two consuls who held office, or counted ab urbe condita ("from the founding of the city", 753 BC).

Legacy

The month names of the modern calendar, the seven-day week's eventual adoption, and expressions such as "the Ides of March" all descend from the Roman system. The Gregorian reform of 1582 adjusted the Julian calendar's leap rule but kept its structure.
