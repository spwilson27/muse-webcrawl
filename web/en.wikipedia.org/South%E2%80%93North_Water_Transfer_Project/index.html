South–North Water Transfer Project

LocationChina
PurposeDivert Yangtze basin water to the arid Yellow River basin and North China Plain
Construction began2002
Planned volume44.8 billion m³ per year (all routes)
StatusEastern and Central routes operating (2013, 2014); Western route planned

The South–North Water Transfer Project, also translated as the South-to-North Water Diversion Project, is a multi-decade infrastructure mega-project in China. Its aim is to channel 44.8 billion cubic metres of fresh water annually from the Yangtze River in southern China to the more arid and industrialised north through three canal and aqueduct systems.

Routes

The Eastern Route upgrades the Grand Canal, pumping water from the Yangtze at Jiangdu through a staircase of pumping stations to Tianjin and Shandong; it began operating in 2013. The Central Route flows by gravity from the enlarged Danjiangkou Reservoir on the Han River to Beijing and Tianjin — including an aqueduct crossing beneath the Yellow River — and began delivering water in December 2014. The Western Route, still in planning, would divert upper Yangtze tributaries across the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau region into the Yellow River headwaters.

Impacts and criticism

The project supplies hundreds of millions of people and has eased groundwater depletion around Beijing, but it required the resettlement of hundreds of thousands of people, raised water-quality and ecological concerns in the donor basins, and is among the most expensive water projects ever undertaken, with costs exceeding $60 billion.

Background

The idea is often traced to Mao Zedong's 1952 remark that "the south has plenty of water, the north lacks it; if possible, borrow some water". Planning studies continued for half a century before construction began in 2002.
