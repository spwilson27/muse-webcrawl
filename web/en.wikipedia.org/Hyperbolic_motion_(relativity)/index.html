Hyperbolic motion (relativity)

Hyperbolic motion
The motion of an object under constant proper acceleration in special relativity; its worldline is a hyperbola in Minkowski spacetime.

In the theory of relativity, hyperbolic motion is the motion of an object with constant proper acceleration — the acceleration felt by an observer riding with the object. It takes its name from the fact that the trajectory of the object in a spacetime diagram (Minkowski diagram) is a hyperbola, in contrast to the parabolas of Newtonian constant acceleration.

Description

For an object with constant proper acceleration α starting from rest, its position as a function of coordinate time is x(t) = (c²/α)(√(1 + (αt/c)²) − 1), which at low speeds reduces to the Newtonian ½αt² but at high speeds approaches the speed of light asymptotically without ever reaching it. As a function of its own proper time τ, the motion is elegant: x = (c²/α)(cosh(ατ/c) − 1), and its rapidity grows linearly with proper time.

Rindler coordinates and horizons

A family of such observers, maintaining constant distances from one another in their own frame (Born-rigid motion), defines Rindler coordinates, in which a uniformly accelerating observer experiences a Rindler horizon — a boundary beyond which events can never send signals to them. This horizon is a flat-space analogue of a black hole's event horizon and is connected with Unruh radiation. The classic thought experiment of the relativistic rocket, and discussions of human spaceflight at constant 1 g acceleration, use hyperbolic motion throughout.

History

The concept was developed in the years after Einstein's 1905 paper, notably by Hermann Minkowski and Max Born (1909), who defined "hyperbolic motion" as the relativistic analogue of uniform acceleration.
