Nouvelle cuisine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nouvelle cuisine (French for "new cuisine") is an approach to cooking and food presentation in French cuisine that emerged in the 1960s and flourished in the 1970s. In contrast to cuisine classique — the elaborate, rich, and heavily sauced tradition codified by Escoffier — nouvelle cuisine emphasises fresh ingredients, lighter sauces, shorter cooking times, smaller portions, and inventive, artistic presentation.

History

The term had been used earlier in French culinary history — notably in the 1730s–40s, and again for the lighter cooking of the 1880s — but in its modern sense it was popularised by the food critics Henri Gault and Christian Millau, founders of the Gault Millau guide. In their 1973 manifesto they set out "ten commandments" of the new cooking, including: no overcooking; use of fresh, quality products; lighter menus; rejection of excessive marinades and heavy sauces; use of regional dishes for inspiration; and receptiveness to new techniques and combinations of flavours.

Chefs associated with the movement include Paul Bocuse, Michel Guérard (who developed the even lighter cuisine minceur), Roger Vergé, the Troisgros brothers, and Alain Chapel. Fernando Point, of La Pyramide in Vienne, was their common mentor. Signature dishes of the era include Troisgros's salmon with sorrel sauce and Guérard's salade gourmande.

Characteristics

Nouvelle cuisine rejected flour-thickened sauces in favour of reductions, juices, and light butter emulsions; it paired ingredients previously kept separate, borrowed from Japanese presentation, and put vegetables at the centre of the plate. Servings were plated in the kitchen on large plates, making presentation a hallmark of the style — and, in its degenerate form, a byword for tiny portions at high prices.

Influence

The movement reshaped fine dining worldwide, shifting haute cuisine toward seasonality, regional produce, and chef-led creativity. Its legacy is visible everywhere in modern cooking, from fusion cuisine to the tasting menu, though many of its leading figures later blended its innovations back into a renewed classical French tradition.
