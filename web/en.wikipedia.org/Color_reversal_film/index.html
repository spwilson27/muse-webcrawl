Color reversal film

Color reversal film, also known as slide film or transparency film, is a type of photographic film that produces a positive image on a transparent base. The developed film, cut and mounted in cardboard or plastic frames, forms slides that can be viewed with a slide projector or magnifier.

Characteristics

Unlike color negative film, which yields an inverted image requiring a second printing step, reversal film is processed so that the original exposure itself becomes a positive transparency. Slides are prized for their vivid color saturation, fine grain, and sharpness, and for decades were the standard medium for professional publication photography.

History

Kodak introduced Kodachrome in 1935, the first commercially successful integral tripack reversal film, followed by Ektachrome in 1946, which photographers could process themselves. Agfa, Fuji, and other manufacturers produced rival stocks such as Fujichrome Velvia, famous for its saturated greens and blues. The rise of digital photography in the 2000s collapsed the market, and most reversal films were discontinued, though a small revival has kept a few emulsions in production.

Processing

The widely used E-6 process develops the film in six baths: a first developer forms a negative silver image, a reversal bath fogs the remaining halide, and a color developer then creates the positive dye image before bleaching away the silver.
