Zooxanthellae

DomainEukaryota
PhylumDinoflagellata
FamilySymbiodiniaceae
Key generaSymbiodinium, Breviolum, Cladocopium, Durusdinium

Zooxanthellae (singular zooxanthella) are photosynthetic dinoflagellates that live in symbiosis with marine invertebrates such as corals, jellyfish, sea anemones, giant clams and nudibranchs. The name derives from their golden-brown colour, caused by photosynthetic pigments including chlorophyll and peridinin. Most zooxanthellae belong to the family Symbiodiniaceae, historically grouped under the genus Symbiodinium.

Zooxanthellae packed within the tissue of a reef-building coral.

Symbiosis with corals

Within a host cell, each zooxanthella is enclosed in a membrane derived from the host's phagosome. Through photosynthesis the algae fix carbon and release oxygen and energy-rich compounds — sugars, glycerol and amino acids — that the host uses for growth, reproduction and, in corals, the calcification needed to build reefs. In return the host provides shelter and the inorganic nutrients (carbon dioxide, nitrogen and phosphorus compounds) that the algae require.

Coral bleaching

When stressed — most commonly by elevated sea temperatures — corals expel their zooxanthellae. Because the algae supply much of the coral's colour, the tissue turns white in the phenomenon known as coral bleaching. Prolonged bleaching starves the coral and can cause widespread reef mortality, making the zooxanthellae–coral partnership a central concern of climate change research. Some zooxanthellae lineages, notably Durusdinium trenchii, confer greater heat tolerance on their hosts.
