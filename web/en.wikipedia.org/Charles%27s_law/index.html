Charles's law

Charles's law
StatementV ∝ T at constant pressure
Named afterJacques Charles
Published byJoseph Louis Gay-Lussac (1802)

Charles's law (also known as the law of volumes) is an experimental gas law that describes how gases tend to expand when heated. A modern statement is: when the pressure on a sample of a dry gas is held constant, the Kelvin temperature and the volume are in direct proportion.

Formula

The law can be written V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂, or equivalently V/T = k, where V is the volume of the gas, T is its absolute temperature, and k is a constant.

History

The law was named after Jacques Charles, who formulated the original law in unpublished work from the 1780s. Joseph Louis Gay-Lussac published it in 1802, crediting Charles, and used it to extrapolate toward absolute zero.

Relation to other gas laws

Together with Boyle's law and Gay-Lussac's law, Charles's law combines into the combined gas law, and with Avogadro's law into the ideal gas law.
