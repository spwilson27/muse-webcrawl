Robert Maynard Hutchins (17 January 1899 – 14 May 1977) was an American educational philosopher and administrator. He was dean of Yale Law School at twenty-eight and, from 1929 to 1945, president of the University of Chicago, then its chancellor until 1951—one of the most influential and controversial university leaders in American history.

At Chicago

Hutchins reshaped the undergraduate curriculum around a core of original texts and discussion, in what became the Great Books approach developed with Mortimer Adler. He abolished varsity football at Chicago in 1939, arguing it distracted from scholarship, eliminated compulsory chapel and elective sprawl, defended academic freedom during periods of anti-communist pressure, and oversaw the university's central wartime role in the Manhattan Project and the first controlled nuclear chain reaction under Enrico Fermi in 1942. His ideas are set out in The Higher Learning in America (1936).

Later life

After Chicago, Hutchins became associate director of the Ford Foundation and then founded the Center for the Study of Democratic Institutions in Santa Barbara (1959), which convened dialogues on civil liberties, world order, and technology. He co-founded, with Adler, the Great Books of the Western World series published by Encyclopædia Britannica in 1952.
