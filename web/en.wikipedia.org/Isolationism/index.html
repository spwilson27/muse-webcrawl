Isolationism

Isolationism is a category of foreign policies institutionalized by leaders who assert that their nation's best interests are best served by keeping the affairs of other countries at a distance. One possible motivation for limiting international involvement is to avoid being drawn into dangerous and otherwise undesirable conflicts. Isolationism typically combines non-interventionism in foreign conflicts with some degree of economic nationalism or protectionism and, in some cases, restrictions on immigration.

United States

American isolationism is conventionally traced to George Washington's Farewell Address (1796), which warned against "permanent alliances" with foreign nations, and to the Monroe Doctrine (1823), which opposed European colonization in the Americas while disclaiming US interference in European affairs. Through the 19th century the United States expanded across its continent while generally abstaining from European wars.

Isolationist sentiment peaked between the world wars. The Senate rejected membership in the League of Nations in 1919–20; the Neutrality Acts of the 1930s sought to keep the US out of future European wars; and the America First Committee (1940–41), whose most famous spokesman was aviator Charles Lindbergh, campaigned against aid to Britain and entry into the Second World War. The attack on Pearl Harbor (7 December 1941) ended the debate, and after 1945 the United States adopted a thoroughly internationalist foreign policy of alliances and overseas bases — although "isolationist" remained a potent label in debates from the Korean and Vietnam wars to the post-Cold War era and the "America First" politics of the 21st century.

Other countries

Historians apply the term to several inward-looking policies, though often with qualifications. Tokugawa Japan's sakoku policy (1630s–1853) severely restricted foreign contact for over two centuries. Qing-dynasty China limited European trade to Canton before the Opium Wars. In Europe, Britain's late-19th-century stance of avoiding continental alliances was dubbed "splendid isolation", though Britain remained globally engaged through its empire and navy — a reminder that isolationism is usually a matter of degree.

Debates

Critics argue that in an interdependent world isolationism is impractical and that withdrawal invites aggression; the interwar period is their standard example. Defenders reply that the term is often used pejoratively to discredit any restraint in foreign policy, and that non-interventionism need not mean total withdrawal — a state may trade widely and maintain diplomacy while refusing military commitments abroad. Political scientists often distinguish isolationism proper from non-interventionism, unilateralism, and protectionism, which overlap but are not identical.
