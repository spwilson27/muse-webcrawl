Moldavia

Moldavia (Romanian: Moldova) is a historical region and former principality in Eastern Europe, roughly corresponding to the territory between the Eastern Carpathians and the Dniester River. Historically the Principality of Moldavia was one of the three principal Romanian principalities, alongside Wallachia and Transylvania. At its greatest extent it encompassed the modern Republic of Moldova, the Romanian region of Moldavia, and parts of present-day Ukraine, including Bukovina and Budjak.

History

The Principality of Moldavia emerged in the mid-14th century, traditionally dated to 1346–1359, under voivodes Dragoș and Bogdan I. Its greatest ruler was Stephen the Great (reigned 1457–1504), who defended the principality against the Ottoman Empire, Hungary and Poland, winning famous victories at Vaslui (1475) and Războieni (1476). From the 16th century Moldavia became a vassal state of the Ottoman Empire while retaining internal autonomy. In 1775 Austria annexed its north-western part, Bukovina, and in 1812 the Russian Empire annexed its eastern half, Bessarabia, under the Treaty of Bucharest.

Union and partition

In 1859 the remaining western Moldavia united with Wallachia under Alexandru Ioan Cuza, forming the nucleus of modern Romania. Bessarabia was briefly reunited with Romania after the First World War, but was annexed by the Soviet Union in 1940 and again in 1944; most of it became the Moldavian SSR and, in 1991, the independent Republic of Moldova. Today "Moldavia" usually denotes the Romanian region in the north-east of Romania, with Iași — the former princely capital — as its principal city. The region is celebrated for its painted churches of southern Bukovina, several of which are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.
