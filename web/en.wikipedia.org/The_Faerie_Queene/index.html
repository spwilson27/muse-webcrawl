The Faerie Queene is an English epic poem by Edmund Spenser. The first three books were published in 1590 and a second installment, with three more books, in 1596. A celebrated work of allegory, the poem follows several knights on quests given by Gloriana, the Faerie Queene, who represents Queen Elizabeth I. It is notable for inventing the Spenserian stanza and for its elaborate archaic diction.

Structure and plan

In a prefatory letter to Sir Walter Raleigh, Spenser explained that he planned twelve books, each centered on a knight embodying a private virtue — Holiness, Temperance, Chastity, Friendship, Justice, and Courtesy in the six completed books — with Prince Arthur, representing Magnificence, linking the whole and standing for England's royal line. Only six books and a fragment (the "Mutabilitie Cantos," published posthumously in 1609) were completed; Spenser died in 1599.

Summary

Book I follows the Redcrosse Knight, the patron of Holiness, who accompanies the lady Una (Truth) to free her parents' kingdom from the dragon Errour's brood and the deceits of Archimago and Duessa. Book II tells of Sir Guyon, the knight of Temperance, who destroys the Bower of Bliss. Book III celebrates Chastity through the female knight Britomart. Book IV treats Friendship, Book V Justice through Sir Artegall and his iron servant Talus, and Book VI Courtesy through Sir Calidore and the Blatant Beast.

Themes and style

The poem weaves Protestant moral allegory with romance, classical epic, and topical political commentary, including a sometimes harsh defense of English rule in Ireland, where Spenser served as a colonial administrator. Its language deliberately imitates Chaucer and medieval romance. The Faerie Queene influenced generations of poets, from Milton and the Romantics to Keats, and its stanza form was adopted by many successors.
