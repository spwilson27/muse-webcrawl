Lycurgus (Greek: Λυκουῦργος, Lykourgos) was the legendary lawgiver of Sparta, credited by the ancient sources with establishing the militarised social system and constitution for which Sparta became famous. According to tradition he instituted the agoge (the rigorous upbringing of Spartan boys), the common messes (syssitia), the council of elders (gerousia), and the redistribution of land into equal lots (kleroi).

Ancient writers disagreed sharply about his dates — estimates ranged from the 9th to the 7th century BC — and modern historians debate whether Lycurgus was a historical figure at all, or a culture hero to whom reforms accumulated over generations were attached. The main sources for his life are Plutarch's Life of Lycurgus, Herodotus, Xenophon's Constitution of the Spartans, and Plato and Aristotle, who both discuss the Spartan system.

Life according to tradition

Plutarch relates that Lycurgus was the younger son of a Spartan king and served as regent and guardian for his nephew, the infant king Charilaus. Maligned by the queen mother, he left Sparta and travelled widely — to Crete, whose laws he studied; to Ionia, where he is said to have first encountered the works of Homer; and, in some accounts, to Egypt, Libya, Iberia and even India.

Recalled by the Spartans, he reformed the state under the authority of the Delphic oracle. The oracle's pronouncement, the Great Rhetra, established the gerousia of twenty-eight elders plus the two kings, regular assemblies of the citizens (the apella), and the division of the people into tribes and villages (obes).

The reforms

The institutions attributed to Lycurgus include:

  • Land reform — the division of Spartan territory into equal, inalienable lots worked by helots, freeing citizens for military life.
  • Currency reform — the replacement of gold and silver coinage with cumbersome iron money, intended to discourage wealth, trade and luxury.
  • Syssitia — compulsory common messes at which all male citizens dined together on simple food, including the famous black broth.
  • The agoge — the state-run training system that took boys from age seven and subjected them to communal living, physical hardship and military discipline.
  • Gerousia and ephorate — the council of elders and (in most accounts) the board of five ephors who supervised the kings and the daily running of the state.

Having bound the Spartans by oath to change nothing until he returned, Lycurgus is said to have departed and starved himself to death abroad, so that the oath would never be released — a self-sacrifice intended to make his laws eternal.

Historicity

No contemporary record of Lycurgus exists; Sparta left no written laws from the archaic period, and the earliest accounts date from centuries after his supposed lifetime. Herodotus already reports two conflicting traditions: that Lycurgus brought the laws from Crete, or that he received them from the Delphic priestess. Many modern scholars treat "Lycurgus" as a personification of a long process of reform, sometimes called the "Spartan revolution," although the tradition's very antiquity keeps the question open.

Legacy

Lycurgus became a canonical figure in political thought: Plato and Aristotle analysed his constitution, Machiavelli praised him in the Discourses on Livy, and Rousseau ranked him among the great legislators in The Social Contract. The Spartan system he supposedly founded became a lasting model — admired and feared — of a society wholly subordinated to the state.
