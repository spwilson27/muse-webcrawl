The University of California, Los Angeles (UCLA) is a public land-grant research university in Los Angeles, California. Founded in 1919 as the Southern Branch of the University of California, it is the second-oldest of the ten-campus UC system. Its 419-acre campus is in the Westwood neighbourhood, near Beverly Hills and the Santa Monica Mountains.

Academics

UCLA comprises the College of Letters and Science and a dozen professional schools including the David Geffen School of Medicine, the School of Law, the Anderson School of Management and the Henry Samueli School of Engineering. It is classified among the United States' most research-intensive universities, and its faculty and alumni include numerous Nobel laureates, Fields medalists and MacArthur fellows. The university receives more applications than any other in the United States and is consistently ranked among the top public universities.

History

The institution began as a teachers college and became a university branch in 1919, moving to the Westwood site in 1929; its first buildings — Royce Hall, Powell Library, Haines Hall and Kinsey Hall — were built in the Romanesque Revival style. Graduate instruction expanded after the Second World War, and the campus became a major centre for computing: the first ARPANET node was installed at UCLA in 1969, and the first message on the network was sent from Boelter Hall.

Athletics

The UCLA Bruins compete in NCAA Division I and have won more than 120 national team championships, among the most of any university. Legendary basketball coach John Wooden won ten national titles between 1964 and 1975. Alumni athletes include Kareem Abdul-Jabbar, Jackie Robinson, Arthur Ashe and Florence Griffith Joyner.
