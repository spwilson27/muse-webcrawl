The 2007–08 Writers Guild of America strike was a labour dispute between the Writers Guild of America, East (WGAE) and Writers Guild of America, West (WGAW) — together representing some 12,000 film and television writers — and the Alliance of Motion Picture and Television Producers (AMPTP). It lasted from 5 November 2007 to 12 February 2008, a total of 100 days, and halted much American television production.

Causes

The central dispute concerned compensation for work distributed over "new media": writers received no residuals for internet downloads and only a small, outdated formula — dating from the 1988 strike settlement — for home video/DVD sales. As studios began streaming programmes online, the Guild sought a percentage of distributors' gross receipts for streamed and downloaded content, along with jurisdiction over reality and animation writing. Talks broke down on 31 October 2007 when the previous contract expired.

Course and effects

Late-night talk shows went into repeats immediately; scripted series shortened their seasons or shut down entirely. The Golden Globe Awards ceremony was cancelled after nominated actors refused to cross picket lines, and the Academy Awards proceeded only after the strike ended. Some shows, notably Late Show with David Letterman, returned under interim agreements negotiated by their production companies. The strike was estimated to have cost the Los Angeles economy over $2 billion. Showrunners and many actors joined picket lines, and fan communities organised support campaigns such as sending pencils to studio executives.

Settlement

Informal talks mediated in early 2008 produced a deal modelled partly on an agreement reached with the Directors Guild. On 12 February 2008 WGA members voted overwhelmingly to end the strike, and the new three-year contract — giving writers residuals for ad-supported streaming and a share of new-media revenues — was ratified later that month. The dispute foreshadowed the streaming-era conflicts that produced the 2023 WGA strike.
