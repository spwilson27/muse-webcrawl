Ernst Chain

Sir Ernst Boris Chain FRS FRSA (19 June 1906 – 12 August 1979) was a German-born British biochemist and a key figure in the development of penicillin. He shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1945 with Alexander Fleming and Howard Florey for the discovery of penicillin and its curative effect in various infectious diseases.

Life and career

Chain was born in Berlin to a Russian-Jewish immigrant family; his father founded a chemical business. He studied chemistry at Friedrich Wilhelm University, Berlin, taking his PhD in 1930, and worked on enzymes at the Charité hospital. After the Nazi rise to power in 1933 he emigrated to England, working first at Cambridge with Frederick Gowland Hopkins and then moving in 1935 to Oxford, where he joined Florey's pathology school.

Penicillin

At Oxford, Chain systematically surveyed antibacterial substances produced by microorganisms. Reading Fleming's 1929 paper on penicillin in 1938–39, he proposed studying it seriously; with Florey and a team including Norman Heatley and Edward Abraham, he purified penicillin, showed it was non-toxic and dramatically effective in infected mice (May 1940), and supervised the first human trials in 1941. Chain's biochemical insight — recognising the instability of the molecule and devising freeze-drying and extraction methods — was essential to turning the mould juice into a drug; he later worked out penicillin's beta-lactam structure. Frustrated by British industry's wartime limitations, production was scaled up in the United States.

Later work

After the war Chain directed the International Research Centre for Chemical Microbiology in Rome (1948–1961), then became professor of biochemistry at Imperial College London, where he built a major department and a fermentation pilot plant. He was knighted in 1969 and retired in 1973, dividing his later years between London and Ireland.
