Compass-and-straightedge construction

Compass-and-straightedge construction, also known as ruler-and-compass construction or classical construction, is the construction of lengths, angles, and other geometric figures using only an idealized ruler and a compass. The idealized ruler, known as a straightedge, is assumed to be infinite in length, have only one edge, and no markings on it. The compass is assumed to "collapse" when lifted from the page, so it may not be directly used to transfer distances — though it can still be used indirectly via the compass equivalence theorem.

History

Ancient Greek mathematicians first studied constructions of this kind, and several ancient problems in plane geometry impose this restriction. The Greeks classified problems into plane (solvable with compass and straightedge), solid (requiring conic sections), and linear classes. Euclid's Elements, from around 300 BC, built plane geometry around constructions with these tools.

Basic constructions

All compass and straightedge constructions consist of repeated application of five basic operations using previously constructed points, lines, and circles:

  • Creating the line through two existing points;
  • Creating the circle through one point with centre another point;
  • Creating the point which is the intersection of two existing, non-parallel lines;
  • Creating the one or two points in the intersection of a line and a circle (if they intersect);
  • Creating the one or two points in the intersection of two circles (if they intersect).

Standard constructions include bisecting a segment or angle, erecting a perpendicular, copying an angle, and constructing regular polygons such as the equilateral triangle, square, regular pentagon, and regular hexagon.

Impossible constructions

Three classical problems proved impossible under these rules are:

  • Squaring the circle — constructing a square with the same area as a given circle;
  • Doubling the cube — constructing the side of a cube with twice the volume of a given cube;
  • Trisecting the angle — dividing an arbitrary angle into three equal parts.

Pierre Wantzel proved the impossibility of doubling the cube and trisecting the angle in 1837 using field theory; Ferdinand von Lindemann's 1882 proof that π is transcendental settled squaring the circle. Carl Friedrich Gauss showed in 1796 that a regular 17-gon is constructible, and Wantzel's criterion states a regular n-gon is constructible exactly when n is a power of two times a product of distinct Fermat primes.
