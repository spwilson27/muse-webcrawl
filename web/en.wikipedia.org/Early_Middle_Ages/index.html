Early Middle Ages

The Early Middle Ages is the period of European history from the 5th century to the 10th century. It began with the fall of the Western Roman Empire and merged into the High Middle Ages around 1000. The term "Late Antiquity" is sometimes used for the transition period. Historically the Early Middle Ages were labelled the "Dark Ages," a term now avoided by most scholars, and the period is better understood as one of transformation: the Roman world gave way to smaller kingdoms, Christianity spread, and new institutions—monasticism, feudalism, and the Carolingian empire—emerged.

Periodisation

The period conventionally opens in 476 with the deposition of Romulus Augustulus, the last Western Roman emperor, though historians now stress continuity: Roman law, the Church, and Latin learning survived. It closes around 1000 with the High Medieval agricultural and urban revival. The mid-period (~500–700) is sometimes called the "Sub-Roman" period.

Barbarian kingdoms

Germanic successor kingdoms—the Ostrogoths and Lombards in Italy, Visigoths in Spain, Franks in Gaul, and Anglo-Saxons in Britain—combined Roman administrative practices with their own customary law. Clovis I (r. c. 481–511) united the Franks and converted to Catholic Christianity, aligning his kingdom with the Roman Church. Monasticism, pioneered by Benedict of Nursia (Rule of Saint Benedict, c. 540), preserved literacy and agriculture through the early centuries.

The Carolingians

Charles Martel's victory at Tours (732) checked the Umayyad advance; his grandson Charlemagne (r. 768–814) built an empire covering most of Western Europe and was crowned Emperor of the Romans by Pope Leo III in 800. The Carolingian Renaissance revived classical learning, standardised the Carolingian minuscule script, and reformed the Church and coinage. The empire fragmented after the Treaty of Verdun (843).

The Byzantine East and the rise of Islam

The Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire survived, reaching its greatest extent under Justinian I (527–565) and enduring until 1453. The rise of Islam in the 7th century transformed the Mediterranean world: Arab armies conquered the Levant, Egypt, North Africa, and Spain, severing the Mediterranean's economic unity and creating a new civilisation centred on Baghdad.

Vikings and invasions

From the late 8th century, Scandinavians raided and settled across Europe; the Magyar and Saracen incursions added pressure in the 9th–10th centuries. These invasions accelerated the growth of localised, militarised lordship that would become feudalism, and they stimulated the formation of new kingdoms, including Alfred the Great's Wessex, Normandy, and the Capetian kingdom of France.
