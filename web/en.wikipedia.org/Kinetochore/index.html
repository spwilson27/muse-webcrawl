Kinetochore

Kinetochore
TypeProtein complex on chromatids
LocationCentromere of each sister chromatid
FunctionAttaches chromosomes to spindle microtubules; drives chromosome movement; hosts the spindle checkpoint
Key componentsCENP-A chromatin; KMN network (Knl1, Mis12, Ndc80 complexes)

The kinetochore is a large protein complex that assembles on the centromere of each sister chromatid during cell division. It is the attachment site for spindle microtubules and the machine that moves chromosomes, ensuring that each daughter cell receives exactly one copy of every chromosome.

Structure

In vertebrates the kinetochore appears under the electron microscope as a trilaminar, plate-like structure about 0.5 micrometres across. Its foundation is specialised chromatin containing the histone variant CENP-A, which marks the centromere. The inner kinetochore (including the CCAN protein network) binds this chromatin throughout the cell cycle, while the outer kinetochore assembles only in mitosis and contacts microtubules — chiefly through the KMN network, whose Ndc80 complex forms the principal microtubule-binding interface. Each human kinetochore attaches to roughly 20 microtubules.

Function

Kinetochores capture microtubules, harness the energy of microtubule depolymerisation and motor proteins such as dynein and CENP-E to move chromosomes to the spindle equator (congression), and then pull sister chromatids apart in anaphase. They also generate the "wait anaphase" signal of the spindle assembly checkpoint: unattached kinetochores catalyse production of the mitotic checkpoint complex, which inhibits the APC/C until every chromosome is correctly attached in a bipolar fashion.

Errors and disease

Faulty attachments — for example, merotelic attachments in which one kinetochore binds microtubules from both poles — can cause chromosome mis-segregation and aneuploidy, a hallmark of cancer cells and a cause of miscarriage and developmental disorders.
