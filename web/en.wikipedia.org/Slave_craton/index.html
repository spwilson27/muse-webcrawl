Slave craton

The Slave craton is an Archean craton in the north-western Canadian Shield, in the Northwest Territories and Nunavut. It is one of the world's oldest and best-preserved fragments of early continental crust, and hosts the Acasta Gneiss, whose rocks — dated to about 4.03 billion years old — are among the oldest known on Earth. The craton takes its name from Great Slave Lake, which lies along its southern margin.

Geology

The craton covers an area of roughly 190,000 square kilometres and is composed principally of granitic and gneissic basement overlain by Archean greenstone belts, including the Yellowknife greenstone belt. Its basement includes ancient tonalite-trondhjemite-granodiorite suites and the Acasta and other gneiss complexes dated between about 2.9 and 4.0 billion years. The craton is bounded by younger Proterozoic orogenic belts, including the Wopmay orogen to the west and the Thelon and Taltson belts to the east and south-east, which formed when the Slave craton collided with neighbouring crustal blocks during the assembly of the Canadian Shield.

Economic geology

The Slave craton is a major diamond province. Kimberlite pipes intruded through the craton's deep, cold lithospheric keel in the late Cretaceous and Eocene, bringing diamonds to the surface; Canada's first diamond mine, Ekati, opened here in 1998, followed by Diavik and Gahcho Kué. The greenstone belts also host significant gold deposits, worked historically at the Giant and Con mines near Yellowknife, as well as base-metal and rare-element occurrences. Mapping of the craton's deep lithosphere from kimberlite xenoliths has made it a key natural laboratory for studying the roots of continents.

Scientific significance

Because its ancient rocks escaped much of the reworking that affected other Archean terranes, the Slave craton is intensively studied for evidence about early Earth: the formation of the first continental crust, the onset of plate tectonics and the conditions of the Hadean–Archean Earth. Isotopic studies of Acasta gneisses hint at crustal material older than the dated rocks themselves, possibly derived from Hadean sources.
