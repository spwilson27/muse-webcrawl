Encyclopédie

Full titleEncyclopédie, ou dictionnaire raisonné des sciences, des arts et des métiers
EditorsDenis Diderot; Jean le Rond d'Alembert
Published1751–1772, Paris
Volumes17 volumes of text; 11 volumes of plates
LanguageFrench

The Encyclopédie, ou dictionnaire raisonné des sciences, des arts et des métiers ("Encyclopedia, or a Systematic Dictionary of the Sciences, Arts, and Crafts") was a general encyclopedia published in France between 1751 and 1772, edited by Denis Diderot and, until 1759, Jean le Rond d'Alembert. It is the most famous work of the Enlightenment: a monumental attempt to assemble all human knowledge, celebrate the mechanical arts, and subject received authority — religious, political, and scholarly — to the scrutiny of reason.

Origins and publication

The project began in 1745 as a planned French translation of Ephraim Chambers's Cyclopaedia. Under Diderot it became something far more ambitious: an original work with contributions from many of the leading philosophes, including Voltaire, Rousseau, Montesquieu, and the economist Quesnay. D'Alembert's "Preliminary Discourse" (1751) set out the work's intellectual programme, organising knowledge according to a "system of human knowledge" adapted from Francis Bacon and memorably illustrated by a figurative tree of knowledge.

Contents and subversion

The finished work comprised 17 volumes of text with some 71,818 articles and 11 volumes containing 2,885 engraved plates, many depicting workshops, tools, and trades in unprecedented detail — a deliberate elevation of artisanal knowledge. Its cross-references (renvois) were a famous device: seemingly innocuous articles directed readers to others that undermined orthodox religious and political positions, allowing the editors to smuggle heterodox ideas past the censors. Articles on theology coexisted with materialist philosophy, and entries on government quietly advanced arguments for tolerance and reform.

Suppression and completion

The Encyclopédie alarmed the authorities. Its privilege was revoked in 1752 after the first two volumes and again in 1759, when it was condemned by the crown and placed on the Catholic Church's Index of Forbidden Books; d'Alembert resigned, and Diderot continued in secret, discovering in 1764 that his publisher Le Breton had secretly censored the proofs. The final text volumes appeared in 1765 under a false Neuchâtel imprint, and the plates were completed by 1772. Cheap reprints spread the work across Europe.

Legacy

The Encyclopédie shaped Enlightenment thought and is often credited with helping to prepare the intellectual ground for the French Revolution. It remains a landmark in the history of publishing, collaborative authorship, and the organisation of knowledge.
