Wheel of Fortune (mythology)

The Wheel of Fortune (Latin: Rota Fortunae) is a concept in ancient Roman religion and in medieval and Renaissance philosophy referring to the capricious nature of Fortune. The goddess Fortuna turns the wheel at random, changing the positions of those on it: some suffer misfortune, others gain windfall. The wheel thereby became a potent symbol of the mutability of earthly success.

Origins and development

The image appears in Cicero and other classical writers, but its classic elaboration is in Boethius's Consolation of Philosophy (c. 524), written in prison while awaiting execution: Lady Philosophy describes Fortune's wheel raising the lowly and casting down the mighty, teaching detachment from worldly goods. Medieval writers and artists adopted the motif widely — it appears in the Carmina Burana ("O Fortuna"), in Chaucer's works, in church carvings, and in the Wheel of Fortune card of the Tarot. Kings and fools alike ride the wheel in illuminated manuscripts, often shown rising on one side, enthroned at the top, falling on the other, and crushed beneath.

Interpretation

Unlike a strictly moralized emblem of pride punished, the wheel stresses the randomness of fate and the instability of worldly rank, a central theme of the medieval de casibus tradition of tragedies of great men.
