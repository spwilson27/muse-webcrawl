A ballad is a form of verse, often a narrative set to music. Ballads derive from the medieval French chanson balladée or ballade, which were originally dance songs. Many ballads were written and sold as single-sheet broadsides. The form was often used by poets and composers from the 18th century onwards to produce lyrical ballads. In the later 19th century, the term took on the meaning of a slow form of popular love song and is often used for any love song, particularly the sentimental ballad of pop or rock music, although the term is also associated with traditional folk music.

Origins

The ballad is a product of the oral tradition of folk song and storytelling. The earliest ballads in English date from the later medieval period, when travelling minstrels carried stories of romance, tragedy, history and the supernatural from place to place. Because they were transmitted orally, ballads exist in many variants, and their authors are almost always anonymous. Tales of Robin Hood, such as Robin Hood ballads collected in early printed garlands, are among the best known of the traditional English and Scottish popular ballads.

Form

The traditional ballad stanza consists of four lines rhyming ABCB (or ABAB), alternating lines of iambic tetrameter and iambic trimeter — a pattern known as ballad meter or common measure, also used in hymns. Ballads frequently employ repetition, incremental refrains, dialogue, and a concentration on a single dramatic episode, often opening abruptly in the middle of the action. Supernatural elements, tragic love, feuds, and historical events are common subjects.

Classification and collections

Francis James Child collected 305 traditional ballads from England and Scotland in The English and Scottish Popular Ballads (1882–98), a corpus known as the Child Ballads. Earlier, Thomas Percy's Reliques of Ancient English Poetry (1765) sparked a revival of interest that influenced Romantic poets. In the literary tradition, poets such as Samuel Taylor Coleridge (The Rime of the Ancient Mariner) and John Keats (La Belle Dame sans Merci) adopted and reshaped the form. In North America, ballads travelled with settlers and evolved into traditions such as the murder ballad and, later, the narrative folk song.

The sentimental ballad

From the late 19th century the word increasingly denoted a slow, sentimental popular song. In 20th- and 21st-century popular music — jazz, pop, rock and R&B — a ballad is typically a slow-tempo love song built around melody and emotional vocal delivery, exemplified by power ballads in rock music.
