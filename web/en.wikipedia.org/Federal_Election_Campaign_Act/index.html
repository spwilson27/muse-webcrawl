Federal Election Campaign Act

The Federal Election Campaign Act of 1971 (FECA, 52 U.S.C. § 30101 et seq.) is a United States federal law that regulates political campaign finance for federal elections. It requires candidates and political committees to disclose contributions and expenditures, limits certain contributions, and provides for public financing of presidential campaigns. The act was substantially amended in 1974 in the wake of the Watergate scandal, creating the Federal Election Commission to enforce it.

Provisions

The original 1971 act consolidated earlier campaign finance statutes and imposed disclosure requirements and limits on media spending. The 1974 amendments, the most sweeping in the law's history, imposed limits on contributions by individuals and political action committees, limits on candidate expenditures, and a system of public funding for presidential primaries and general elections, while establishing the six-member Federal Election Commission (FEC) as an independent enforcement agency.

Legal challenges

In Buckley v. Valeo (1976), the Supreme Court upheld contribution limits and disclosure but struck down mandatory expenditure limits as violations of the First Amendment, reasoning that spending money to disseminate speech is itself protected. Later legislation, notably the Bipartisan Campaign Reform Act of 2002 (McCain–Feingold), amended FECA to restrict "soft money" and electioneering communications; key provisions of that regime were in turn invalidated by Citizens United v. FEC (2010).
