Essential nutrient

CategoriesEssential amino acids, essential fatty acids, vitamins, dietary minerals
Human count9 amino acids, 2 fatty acids, 13 vitamins, and a set of essential minerals
Essential nutrients must come from the diet because the body cannot make them in sufficient quantity.

An essential nutrient is a nutrient required for normal physiological function that cannot be synthesized by the body, either at all or in sufficient quantities, and therefore must be obtained from the diet. Non-essential nutrients can be manufactured by the body from other compounds, although they may also be consumed in food.

Categories

For humans, the essential nutrients include nine amino acids (such as lysine, leucine, and tryptophan), two fatty acids (alpha-linolenic acid, an omega-3, and linoleic acid, an omega-6), thirteen vitamins (A, C, D, E, K, and the eight B vitamins), and a number of minerals including calcium, potassium, iron, zinc, and iodine. Water and oxygen are also indispensable, though they are sometimes treated separately from dietary lists.

Deficiency and excess

Inadequate intake of an essential nutrient produces deficiency diseases: scurvy from lack of vitamin C, rickets from lack of vitamin D, and goitre from lack of iodine are classic examples. Some essential nutrients are toxic in large doses, which is why dietary reference intakes specify both recommended amounts and tolerable upper intake levels. Essentiality can vary by species; for example, most mammals synthesize vitamin C, but humans and other primates cannot because of a nonfunctional gulonolactone oxidase gene.
