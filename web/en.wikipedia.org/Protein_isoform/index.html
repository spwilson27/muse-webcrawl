Protein isoform

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A protein isoform, or "protein variant", is a member of a set of highly similar proteins that originate from a single gene. Different forms of a protein may be produced from related genes, or may arise from the same gene by alternative splicing. A large number of proteins may also be produced through post-translational modifications, though such variants are sometimes distinguished from isoforms proper depending on convention. Isoforms can differ in their amino acid sequence, and consequently in their biological activity, regulation, subcellular localization, and stability.

Mechanisms

Alternative splicing

The primary mechanism producing isoforms is alternative splicing of a pre-mRNA transcript, in which particular exons of a gene may be included within or excluded from the final messenger RNA. Because the human genome contains roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes but the proteome is far larger, alternative splicing is a major source of protein diversity; the vast majority of multi-exon human genes are alternatively spliced, often in tissue-specific patterns.

Alternative promoters and polyadenylation

Use of alternative promoters or transcription start sites can produce transcripts with different first exons, and alternative polyadenylation sites can change the C-terminal sequence or the regulatory untranslated regions of a transcript, yielding distinct isoforms.

Gene duplication

Related genes arising by duplication — such as the globin genes encoding the alpha and beta chains of hemoglobin and their developmental variants — produce closely related proteins that are sometimes grouped as isoforms in a broader sense.

Examples

Well-studied isoform families include the pyruvate kinase isoforms PKM1 and PKM2, produced by mutually exclusive exon splicing and differentially expressed in adult tissues versus proliferating and cancer cells; the isoforms of tau protein in the brain, whose imbalance is implicated in tauopathies; and the many isoforms of tropomyosin and myosin heavy chain that tune muscle contractile properties. The Drosophila gene Dscam can generate tens of thousands of isoforms through alternative splicing of exon clusters, contributing to neuronal self-recognition.

Significance

Isoform diversity allows a limited genome to encode a much richer proteome. Misregulation of splicing is increasingly recognized in disease: mutations that alter splice sites or splicing factors can shift isoform ratios in cancer, spinal muscular atrophy (which involves the SMN2 gene's splicing), and numerous genetic disorders. Modern transcriptomics, particularly long-read RNA sequencing, has greatly improved the cataloguing of isoforms and their expression.
