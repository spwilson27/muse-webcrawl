Ronald Coase

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ronald Harry Coase (29 December 1910 – 2 September 2013) was a British economist and author who spent most of his career at the University of Chicago Law School. He received the Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences in 1991 for his discovery and clarification of the significance of transaction costs and property rights for the structure and functioning of the economy. His two landmark papers, "The Nature of the Firm" (1937) and "The Problem of Social Cost" (1960), reshaped economic thinking about firms, markets, and externalities.

The Nature of the Firm

Written while Coase was an undergraduate travelling in the United States, the 1937 paper asked why economic activity is organized within firms at all, if markets allocate resources efficiently. His answer: using the price mechanism itself is costly — searching for prices, negotiating and enforcing contracts — and firms exist because entrepreneurs can coordinate production at lower cost than the market for some activities. The boundary of the firm settles where the marginal cost of internal organization equals the cost of using the market.

Social cost and the Coase theorem

"The Problem of Social Cost" (1960) argued that, with well-defined property rights and zero transaction costs, parties will bargain to an efficient outcome regardless of the initial assignment of rights — the result George Stigler dubbed the Coase theorem. Coase's deeper point was the opposite emphasis: because transaction costs are positive in the real world, the institutional and legal assignment of rights matters greatly. The paper is one of the most cited in economics and helped found the field of law and economics; Coase also wrote influentially on the allocation of the radio spectrum, questioning the need for administrative licensing.
