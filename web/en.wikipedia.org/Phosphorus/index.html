Phosphorus

Phosphorus is a chemical element with the symbol P and atomic number 15. It is an essential element for life, forming part of DNA, RNA, ATP, and the phospholipids of cell membranes, and it is a major component of bone and teeth. Elemental phosphorus exists in several allotropes, most commonly white and red phosphorus.

History

Phosphorus was discovered in 1669 by the German alchemist Hennig Brand, who isolated it from urine while searching for the philosopher's stone; its glow in the dark made it famous as a "light-bearer" (Greek phōsphóros). It was the first element to be discovered through chemical analysis rather than found in nature.

Allotropes and properties

White phosphorus consists of tetrahedral P4 molecules; it is highly reactive, toxic, and ignites spontaneously in air, so it is stored under water. Red phosphorus is polymeric, far less reactive, and is used on the striking surfaces of safety matches. Black phosphorus, the most thermodynamically stable form, has a layered structure similar to graphite and is studied as a semiconductor.

Biological role and production

Phosphate rock is mined on a vast scale and converted mainly into fertilizers, making phosphorus a strategic agricultural resource with no substitute. In organisms, phosphate groups store and transfer energy (ATP) and form the backbone of nucleic acids.
