The South Pacific Gyre is one of the five great subtropical ocean gyres, a vast rotating system of currents in the South Pacific Ocean. Centred in one of the most remote parts of the world ocean, it is bounded by the East Australian Current, the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, the Humboldt (Peru) Current and the South Equatorial Current. It is often described as the largest "ocean desert" on Earth.

Circulation

Driven by the trade winds and the mid-latitude westerlies, the gyre rotates counterclockwise, as do all Southern Hemisphere subtropical gyres under the Coriolis effect. Ekman transport pushes surface water toward the gyre's interior, producing a slight dome of sea level and a deep, nutrient-poor thermocline. The downwelling at the centre suppresses the upward mixing that fertilises surface waters elsewhere.

An ocean desert

The centre of the gyre is the farthest point from any land — the oceanic pole of inaccessibility, known as Point Nemo. Nutrient scarcity makes its waters among the clearest and least biologically productive in the world, with extremely low chlorophyll concentrations. The sparse life that does exist is adapted to near-starvation conditions; research expeditions have found microbial communities of remarkable frugality, and studies of the seabed beneath the gyre have revealed oxygen penetrating deep into ancient sediments.

Debris accumulation

Like the North Pacific Gyre, the South Pacific Gyre accumulates floating plastic debris transported by its currents, forming the less-studied South Pacific garbage patch. Surveys have found high microplastic concentrations on remote shores such as Henderson Island, one of the world's most isolated islands, which accumulates debris carried across the gyre.
