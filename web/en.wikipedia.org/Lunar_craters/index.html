Lunar craters

Lunar craters are impact craters on Earth's Moon. The Moon's surface has many craters, almost all of which were formed by impacts from asteroids and comets, because the Moon lacks an atmosphere to burn up or erode them and has essentially no geological activity to resurface itself. The lunar crater record preserves a history of bombardment spanning more than four billion years, including the Late Heavy Bombardment.

Characteristics

Craters range in size from microscopic pits on lunar rocks to the South Pole–Aitken basin, about 2,500 km across — one of the largest known impact structures in the Solar System. Simple craters are bowl-shaped; larger complex craters have terraced walls, flat floors and central peaks formed by rebound of the surface. Bright ray systems radiate from young craters such as Tycho (85 km, about 108 million years old) and Copernicus. Prominent named craters include Tycho, Copernicus, Kepler, Aristarchus, Plato and Clavius.

Nomenclature

Crater names are assigned by the International Astronomical Union, following the tradition begun by Giovanni Battista Riccioli in 1651 of naming craters after distinguished scientists, philosophers and explorers. Counts from the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter catalogue list well over a million craters larger than 1 km in diameter.
