Alexander Hamilton (c. 1755/57 – 12 July 1804) was an American military officer, statesman, and Founding Father who served as the first secretary of the treasury of the United States from 1789 to 1795. A Caribbean-born orphan who rose through brilliance and ambition, he was George Washington's aide-de-camp during the Revolutionary War, the principal author of The Federalist Papers, and the architect of the young republic's financial system.

Career

Hamilton arrived in New York in 1772 to attend King's College (now Columbia University), joined the patriot cause, and distinguished himself as an artillery captain and then as Washington's trusted staff officer, finally leading a decisive assault at Yorktown in 1781. As a delegate to the Constitutional Convention of 1787 he advocated a strong central government, and his essays in The Federalist — written with Madison and Jay — were decisive in securing ratification of the Constitution.

Treasury and legacy

As Treasury secretary under Washington, Hamilton established the federal assumption of state debts, a national bank, a mint, the customs service, and the funding system that established American public credit, while championing manufactures and energetic government against the agrarian vision of Jefferson and Madison. His fierce political rivalry with Vice President Aaron Burr culminated in a duel at Weehawken, New Jersey, on 11 July 1804, in which Hamilton was mortally wounded. He appears on the US ten-dollar bill, and his life inspired the 2015 musical Hamilton.
