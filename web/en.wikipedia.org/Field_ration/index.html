Field ration

A field ration (also known as a combat ration, ration pack, or food packet) is a canned or pre-packaged meal, easily prepared and consumed by military troops in the field. They are distinguished from regular military rations by being intended for consumption in the field or combat zones, usually where organized catering facilities are unavailable. Field rations typically provide roughly 3,600–4,200 kilocalories per day in the form of multiple packaged meals.

A modern individual ration: mains, sides, snacks, drinks, and accessories.

History

Armies marched on preserved foods for centuries — Roman legionaries carried grain; navies relied on salt pork, hardtack, and later lime juice against scurvy. Napoleon offered a prize that spurred Nicolas Appert's canning process (1809–1810). The two World Wars produced named systems: British "bully beef" and Maconochie stew, American C-rations and K-rations, and German Eiserne Portionen ("iron rations").

Modern rations

The United States replaced canned MCI rations with the Meal, Ready-to-Eat (MRE) from 1981, packaged in retort pouches with a flameless ration heater and menus covering many cuisines; group feeding uses the Unitized Group Ration. Allies field equivalents such as the British 24-hour ORP, the French RCIR (noted for elaborate menus), the Australian CR1M, and Russian IRP. Rations are designed for long shelf life (typically at least three years), nutritional adequacy, and acceptability to sustain morale, and surplus or retired rations have a civilian following among campers, preppers, and online reviewers.
