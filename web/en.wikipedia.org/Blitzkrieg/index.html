Blitzkrieg

Blitzkrieg
Literal meaning"Lightning war" (German)
EraWorld War II (1939–1941 campaigns)
Key elementsArmored spearheads, motorized infantry, close air support
Notable campaignsPoland 1939, France 1940, Balkans 1941

Blitzkrieg (German for "lightning war") is a word used to describe a combined-arms surprise attack using a rapid, overwhelming force concentration that may consist of armored and motorized or mechanized infantry formations, together with artillery and close air support, intended to break through enemy lines and encircle defenders before they can react.

Concept

Rather than a formal pre-war doctrine, the German methods of 1939–41 built on First World War infiltration tactics, mission-style command (Auftragstaktik), and concentrated Panzer divisions operating with radio communications. The aim was a Schwerpunkt breakthrough followed by deep exploitation and encirclement (Kesselschlacht), paralyzing enemy command and logistics.

Campaigns

The approach produced rapid victories in the invasion of Poland (1939) and the Battle of France (1940), where panzer groups crossed the Ardennes, drove to the Channel, and cut off Allied armies in Belgium. Similar methods featured in the Balkans and the opening of Operation Barbarossa (1941), though vast distances, logistics, and Soviet resistance eventually blunted them.

Legacy

Allied and later armies absorbed the lessons of mobile combined-arms warfare; modern maneuver doctrine and air-land battle concepts trace part of their lineage to the campaigns of 1939–41, even as historians debate how deliberate and doctrinal "blitzkrieg" really was.
