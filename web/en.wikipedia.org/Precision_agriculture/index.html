Precision agriculture

Precision agriculture, also called precision farming or site-specific crop management, is a farm management concept based on observing, measuring and responding to inter- and intra-field variability in crops. The goal of precision agriculture research is to define a decision support system for whole farm management with the goal of optimizing returns on inputs while preserving resources.

Overview

Among such a suite of technologies are Global Positioning System (GPS) and geographic information system (GIS) technologies, remote sensing, variable rate technology, and yield monitoring. Crop variability typically has both a spatial and temporal component, which makes statistical and computational treatments important.

Technologies

GPS and guidance

GPS-guided tractors and auto-steer systems allow farmers to plough, plant and spray with centimetre-level accuracy, reducing overlap and fuel use. Lightbar guidance systems were an early form of this technology.

Remote sensing

Satellites, aircraft and drones collect multispectral imagery that can be used to estimate crop health, often through indices such as the normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI).

Variable rate technology

Variable rate application allows fertilizer, seed and irrigation water to be applied at different rates across a field according to soil maps and sensor data, rather than at a uniform rate.

Yield monitoring

Combine harvesters equipped with yield monitors record the amount of grain harvested at each point in a field, producing yield maps that guide future management decisions.

History

The roots of precision agriculture lie in the 1980s, when researchers began to explore soil sampling on a grid basis and the use of computers to map fields. Commercial GPS availability in the 1990s, following the launch of the Navstar constellation, made field-level positioning practical, and the approach spread widely in the 2000s, especially in the United States, Australia and northern Europe.

Benefits and challenges

Proponents argue that precision agriculture can improve profitability and reduce environmental impacts such as nutrient runoff by matching inputs to crop needs. Critics note the capital cost of equipment, the need for technical skills, and questions about data ownership and privacy associated with farm data collected by machinery manufacturers and service providers.
