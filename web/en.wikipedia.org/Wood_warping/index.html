Wood warping

Wood warping is any deviation from flatness in a piece of lumber, caused mainly by uneven changes in moisture content as wood dries or absorbs water. Because wood shrinks at different rates along and across the grain — and tangentially about twice as much as radially — internal stresses develop that bend the board.

Types of warp

The standard forms are: cup, curvature across the width of the board; bow, curvature along the length of the face; crook (or spring), curvature along the length of the edge; twist, in which the ends rotate in opposite directions, often associated with spiral grain; and kink, a localised crook at a knot or defect. Reaction wood and juvenile wood near the pith are especially prone to distortion.

Causes and prevention

Warping is most likely when green timber is dried too quickly or unevenly. Careful kiln schedules, stacking boards flat with evenly spaced stickers, weighting the pile, sealing board ends, and conditioning finished lumber to the moisture level of its service environment all reduce warp. Selecting straight-grained, quartersawn stock and avoiding pieces cut from near the centre of the log also help. Related topics include wood preservation.
