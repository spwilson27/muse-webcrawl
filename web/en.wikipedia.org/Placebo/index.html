Placebo

Placebo
EtymologyLatin placebo, "I shall please"
FormInert pill, injection, sham procedure
EffectImprovement attributable to belief and context, not pharmacology
OppositeNocebo (harmful expectation effect)

A placebo is a substance or treatment that has no inherent therapeutic effect — such as a sugar pill, saline injection, or sham surgery — but which can nevertheless produce real improvement in a patient's condition when administered in a therapeutic context. The improvement, called the placebo effect (or placebo response), arises from the patient's expectations, the ritual of treatment, and the clinician–patient relationship rather than from any pharmacological action.

The placebo effect

Placebo responses are strongest for subjective, brain-mediated symptoms: pain, nausea, fatigue, anxiety, and depression. Studies using placebo analgesia show genuine neurobiological mechanisms, including the release of endogenous opioids (reversible by naloxone) and dopamine. Remarkably, "open-label" placebos — honestly described as inert — have still shown benefits in some trials of irritable bowel syndrome and chronic pain. The effect is influenced by the form of treatment (injections outperform pills), colour and branding of medication, price, and the warmth of the practitioner. Its negative counterpart, the nocebo effect, produces worsening symptoms from negative expectations.

Use in clinical trials

Since the mid-20th century, the randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial has been the gold standard for testing new treatments: neither patient nor investigator knows who receives the active drug, isolating its true effect from placebo responses, natural recovery, and regression to the mean. Placebo controls raise ethical questions when withholding an effective standard treatment would harm patients, so trials often compare a new drug against standard care instead, with placebo added on top for both groups.

History and criticism

The word entered medicine in the 18th century for treatments given to please rather than benefit the patient. Henry Beecher's influential 1955 paper "The Powerful Placebo" estimated an average 35% response rate, a figure later criticised: analyses by Hróbjartsson and Gøtzsche (2001) found little evidence that placebos produce powerful clinical effects beyond possible small benefits for pain and other continuous subjective outcomes, much of the apparent effect reflecting the untreated course of illness. The debate continues, but the placebo's role as a methodological benchmark is secure.
