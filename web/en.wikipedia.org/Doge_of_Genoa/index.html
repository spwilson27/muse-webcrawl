The Doge of Genoa was the chief magistrate and head of state of the Republic of Genoa, one of the Italian maritime republics. The title derives from the Latin dux ("leader"), as with the better-known Doge of Venice. The office existed, with interruptions, from 1339 until the fall of the republic in 1797.

History

Genoa was initially governed by consuls and then by a podestà. In 1339, amid popular unrest, the Genoese elected Simone Boccanegra as their first doge, acclaiming him in the manner of the Venetian model but with a distinctly popular character. Early doges were chosen for life and often drawn from the great merchant families — Boccanegra, Adorno, and Fregoso (Campofregoso) — whose feuds destabilised the republic; Genoa repeatedly submitted to foreign governors, including the King of France and the Duke of Milan.

The 1528 reform

The reform of Andrea Doria in 1528 stabilised the office: the doge was thereafter chosen by the Grand Council from the Genoese nobility for a two-year term, with elaborate electoral procedures designed to prevent factional capture. Former doges were forbidden immediate re-election and were subject to review of their conduct in office. Under this aristocratic constitution the republic endured until 1797, when Napoleon Bonaparte transformed it into the French-aligned Ligurian Republic.

Ducal palace and insignia

The doge resided in the Palazzo Ducale in Genoa. From 1599 the doge wore a crown and carried a sceptre at his coronation, which took place six months after election, and Genoese doges styled themselves "by the grace of God" — ceremonial claims that rivalled those of Venice and scandalised some contemporaries, given the office's biennial tenure.
