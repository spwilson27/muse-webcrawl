Sarbanes–Oxley Act

Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002
Enacted30 July 2002
SponsorsSen. Paul Sarbanes; Rep. Michael Oxley
Public Law107–204
Short nameSOX

The Sarbanes–Oxley Act of 2002 is a United States federal law that enacted sweeping changes to the regulation of corporate financial practice and reporting. It was passed in response to a series of major accounting scandals, most prominently those of Enron and WorldCom, which had destroyed billions of dollars of shareholder value. The Act is named for its sponsors, Senator Paul Sarbanes and Representative Michael G. Oxley, and is often abbreviated SOX.

The Act being signed into law on 30 July 2002.

Principal provisions

The Act created the Public Company Accounting Oversight Board (PCAOB) to oversee the auditing profession, strengthened auditor independence rules, and increased corporate responsibility and financial disclosure requirements. Section 302 requires chief executive and chief financial officers to personally certify the accuracy of financial reports. Section 404 requires management — and, for larger companies, their external auditors — to assess and report on the effectiveness of internal controls over financial reporting. Section 802 imposes criminal penalties for destroying or falsifying records, and the Act also provides protection for whistleblowers.

Assessment

Supporters credit the Act with restoring investor confidence and improving the reliability of financial statements. Critics argue that its compliance costs, particularly for Section 404 audits, fall disproportionately on smaller companies, and subsequent legislation such as the JOBS Act of 2012 exempted many smaller and newly public companies from some of its requirements. The Act's certification and internal-control regime nevertheless remains the foundation of US public-company reporting practice.
