Inflammatory bowel disease

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a group of inflammatory conditions of the colon and small intestine, with Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis as the main types. Unlike irritable bowel syndrome, IBD causes visible inflammation and damage to the digestive tract. It results from an abnormal immune response to intestinal microbes in genetically susceptible people.

IBD involves chronic inflammation of parts of the digestive tract, most notably in Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis.

Types

Crohn's disease can affect any part of the gastrointestinal tract from mouth to anus, often in discontinuous "skip" segments, and inflammation may extend through the full thickness of the bowel wall, leading to strictures and fistulas. Ulcerative colitis is limited to the colon and rectum, affects only the inner lining, and spreads continuously from the rectum. Cases that cannot be classified are termed indeterminate colitis.

Symptoms and diagnosis

Common symptoms include persistent diarrhoea, abdominal pain, rectal bleeding, weight loss and fatigue, often in a relapsing-remitting course. Extraintestinal manifestations can involve the joints, skin, eyes and liver. Diagnosis combines endoscopy with biopsy, imaging, blood tests and stool markers such as faecal calprotectin.

Causes and treatment

The cause is multifactorial: genetic susceptibility (over 200 associated loci, including NOD2), an altered gut microbiome, environmental factors such as smoking (harmful in Crohn's, paradoxically protective in ulcerative colitis), and immune dysregulation. There is no cure; treatment aims to induce and maintain remission using aminosalicylates, corticosteroids, immunomodulators, biologic drugs (such as anti-TNF agents and vedolizumab) and small molecules (JAK inhibitors). Surgery may be needed for complications or refractory disease; colectomy is curative for ulcerative colitis of the colon. Long-standing colitis increases colorectal cancer risk, prompting surveillance colonoscopy.
