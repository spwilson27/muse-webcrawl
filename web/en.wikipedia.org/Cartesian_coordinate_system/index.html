Cartesian coordinate system

Named afterRené Descartes
ComponentsPerpendicular axes; origin; ordered pairs/triples
Used forAnalytic geometry, graphs, computer graphics

A Cartesian coordinate system in a plane is a coordinate system that specifies each point uniquely by a pair of real numbers called coordinates, which are the signed distances to the point from two fixed perpendicular oriented lines, called coordinate lines, coordinate axes, or just axes of the system. The point where they meet is called the origin, typically denoted O.

The Cartesian plane, with a point at (3, 2).

Description

In two dimensions, the horizontal axis is the x-axis (abscissa) and the vertical axis is the y-axis (ordinate); a point is written (x, y). The axes divide the plane into four quadrants. In three dimensions, a third axis (z) is added, perpendicular to both, and points are specified by triples (x, y, z); the generalization to n dimensions underlies analytic geometry and linear algebra.

History

The system is named for the French philosopher and mathematician René Descartes, who published the idea in 1637 in the appendix La Géométrie to his Discourse on the Method. Pierre de Fermat worked on similar ideas contemporaneously. By fusing algebra with geometry, Descartes' method allowed geometric curves to be expressed as equations — a founding step of modern mathematics, later extended by Leibniz and Newton in the calculus.

Applications

Cartesian coordinates are the default framework for graphs of functions, maps and grids, physics (position and motion), engineering drawing, and computer graphics, where screen and 3D model coordinates are typically Cartesian. Distances follow from the Pythagorean theorem: between (x1, y1) and (x2, y2) it is √((x2−x1)2 + (y2−y1)2).
