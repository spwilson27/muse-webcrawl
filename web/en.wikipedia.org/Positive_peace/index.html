Positive peace

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
Positive peace describes the presence of justice and the absence of structural violence.

Positive peace is a concept in peace studies, most closely associated with the Norwegian sociologist Johan Galtung, that defines peace not merely as the absence of war or direct violence — which Galtung called negative peace — but as the presence of social justice, equality, and the conditions that allow human beings to develop their full potential. Where negative peace is the cessation of direct physical violence, positive peace requires the elimination of structural violence: the systematic ways in which social structures harm people by preventing them from meeting their basic needs.

Positive peace

Field: Peace and conflict studies

Key theorist: Johan Galtung

Contrast: Negative peace

Related measure: Positive Peace Index (Institute for Economics and Peace)

Origins

Galtung introduced the distinction between negative and positive peace in the 1960s, notably in his 1964 editorial in the Journal of Peace Research and his 1969 article "Violence, Peace, and Peace Research". The framework grew out of his broader theory of violence, in which he distinguished direct violence (physical harm by an identifiable actor), structural violence (harm built into social arrangements), and later cultural violence (beliefs and symbols that legitimise the other two). Because structural violence produces unequal life chances — for example through poverty, discrimination, or political exclusion — a society could be entirely free of war yet still be "unpeaceful" in Galtung's terms.

Measurement

The Institute for Economics and Peace (IEP) publishes a Positive Peace Index, which attempts to quantify the concept across countries. The IEP framework identifies eight "pillars" of positive peace: a well-functioning government, equitable distribution of resources, free flow of information, good relations with neighbours, high levels of human capital, acceptance of the rights of others, low levels of corruption, and a sound business environment. Countries scoring highly on positive peace also tend to score highly on the related Global Peace Index and to be more resilient to shocks.

Criticism

Critics have argued that positive peace is so broad that it becomes nearly synonymous with social justice or good governance generally, losing analytical precision. Others question whether the elimination of all structural violence is an achievable or even coherent goal. Defenders respond that the concept deliberately broadens the peace agenda beyond the mere management of armed conflict, directing attention to the root causes of violence.
