Conifer cone

A conifer cone or strobilus (plural: strobili) is the reproductive organ of conifers and other gymnosperms in the division Pinophyta. Cones are typically woody structures composed of scales arranged around a central axis; the familiar "pine cone" is the female, seed-bearing cone. Male cones, which produce pollen, are usually smaller, softer, and short-lived.

Structure

The female cone consists of an axis bearing overlapping scales. Botanically, each scale is part of a structure derived from a modified shoot: a sterile bract scale and an ovuliferous scale that carries (usually) two ovules on its upper surface. After pollination, the ovules develop into winged seeds. In many pines the cone takes two or three years to mature; the scales then dry and spread apart to release the seeds. Some species, such as lodgepole and jack pines, have serotinous cones sealed with resin that open only after the heat of a forest fire.

Size and variety

Cones range from the small, berry-like cones of junipers — whose fused scales form the "juniper berry" — to the enormous cones of the sugar pine, which may exceed 50 cm (20 in) in length, and the heavy cones of the bunya pine. Male cones are borne in clusters, shed their wind-borne pollen in spring, and fall soon afterwards.

Uses

Cones yield edible seeds such as pine nuts, and the cones themselves are used ornamentally and as fire starters; they also feature prominently in art and symbolism as emblems of fertility and regeneration.
