British Columbia (commonly abbreviated as BC) is the westernmost province of Canada, situated between the Pacific Ocean and the Rocky Mountains. With a population of about five million, it is Canada's third most populous province. The capital is Victoria, located at the southeastern tip of Vancouver Island, while the largest city is Vancouver, the third-largest metropolitan area in Canada.

History

Indigenous peoples have inhabited the region for at least 10,000 years, developing rich cultures — including the Haida, Nuu-chah-nulth, and Coast Salish peoples — famed for cedar longhouses, totem poles, and potlatch ceremonies. European exploration began with Spanish voyages in the 1770s and Captain George Vancouver's surveys of 1792–1794. Fur traders of the Hudson's Bay Company established posts in the early 19th century; the Crown colonies of Vancouver Island (1849) and British Columbia (1858) were created amid the Fraser Canyon Gold Rush, and united in 1866. The colony joined Canadian Confederation in 1871 on the promise of a transcontinental railway, completed with the ceremonial last spike at Craigellachie in 1885.

Geography and economy

The province is dominated by mountain ranges — the Rockies, Columbia Mountains, and Coast Mountains — with a deeply indented fjord coastline and temperate rainforests on the coast, including parts of the Great Bear Rainforest. Forestry, mining, hydroelectric power, tourism, film production (Vancouver is nicknamed "Hollywood North"), and technology are major industries, and the Port of Vancouver is Canada's largest port and its principal gateway to Asia. British Columbia also has the mildest winters in Canada along its south coast, and is known for strong environmental movements and, in recent years, severe wildfires and the 2021 "heat dome" and atmospheric-river flooding.

Government

British Columbia is a constitutional monarchy and parliamentary democracy within the Canadian federation, governed by a premier and the unicameral Legislative Assembly in Victoria. Indigenous land title remains a central issue: most of the province was never ceded by treaty, and modern treaties and court decisions (such as Delgamuukw and Tsilhqot'in) have progressively recognized Aboriginal title.
