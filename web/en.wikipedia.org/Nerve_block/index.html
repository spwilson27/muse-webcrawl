Nerve block

Nerve block
SpecialtyAnesthesiology, pain medicine
MethodInjection of local anesthetic adjacent to a nerve or plexus
UsesSurgical anesthesia, postoperative analgesia, chronic pain management and diagnosis
GuidanceUltrasound, nerve stimulation, anatomical landmarks

A nerve block is a deliberate interruption of signals traveling along a nerve, achieved by injecting a local anesthetic (such as bupivacaine or ropivacaine) close to the nerve or nerve plexus that supplies a region of the body. Nerve blocks provide surgical anesthesia or prolonged pain relief for a limb or body region, alone or combined with general anesthesia, and are a mainstay of modern regional anesthesia and multimodal, opioid-sparing analgesia.

Technique

The anesthetic is deposited around the target nerve through a needle, nowadays most commonly under real-time ultrasound guidance, which allows direct visualization of the nerve, needle, and spread of injectate; a peripheral nerve stimulator (producing a motor twitch at low current) or landmark/paresthesia techniques were historically standard. A single injection lasts hours; for prolonged analgesia, a perineural catheter can infuse local anesthetic for days. Additives such as epinephrine, clonidine, or dexamethasone may prolong the block, and long-acting formulations (e.g., liposomal bupivacaine) extend duration.

Common blocks

  • Upper limb: interscalene, supraclavicular, infraclavicular, and axillary brachial plexus blocks; forearm blocks of the median, ulnar, and radial nerves.
  • Lower limb: femoral, adductor canal, sciatic, popliteal, and ankle blocks.
  • Trunk: transversus abdominis plane (TAP), paravertebral, erector spinae plane, and intercostal blocks.
  • Other: cervical plexus blocks, stellate ganglion block, and neurolytic blocks (with alcohol or phenol) for cancer pain; neuraxial techniques (spinal and epidural) are usually classified separately from peripheral blocks.

Risks

Complications include failed or patchy block, bleeding and hematoma, infection, local anesthetic systemic toxicity (LAST) from intravascular injection — a rare but potentially fatal event treated with intravenous lipid emulsion — and nerve injury, usually transient. Ultrasound guidance has reduced several of these risks.

History

Regional blockade followed the introduction of cocaine as a local anesthetic in the 1880s; William Halsted performed early nerve blocks in New York, and August Bier described intravenous regional anesthesia in 1908. The field was transformed in the 2000s by the widespread adoption of ultrasound.
