Sous vide

Sous vide
French for "under vacuum"
Method: vacuum-sealed food cooked in a temperature-controlled water bath
Typical range: 50–85 °C (122–185 °F)

Sous vide (French for "under vacuum") is a method of cooking in which food is placed in a plastic pouch or glass jar, vacuum-sealed, and cooked in a water bath for longer than usual cooking times at an accurately regulated temperature. The temperature is much lower than normally used for cooking and is intended to cook the item evenly, ensuring the inside is properly cooked without overcooking the outside, while retaining moisture.

History

Low-temperature cooking was described as early as 1799 by Benjamin Thompson, Count Rumford. The modern method was developed in the 1970s: French chef Georges Pralus used it at the Restaurant Troisgros in Roanne to cook foie gras without losing fat, and Bruno Goussault pioneered the precise time–temperature combinations and trained many leading chefs. The technique spread through fine dining in the 2000s and reached home cooks with affordable immersion circulators in the 2010s.

Method

Food is seasoned, sealed in a vacuum bag (or a zipper bag with air displaced by water), and immersed in water held at the target serving temperature by an immersion circulator. A steak, for example, may be held at 54 °C (129 °F) for several hours to achieve medium-rare throughout; it is often finished with a brief sear for a browned crust via the Maillard reaction. Because the bath cannot exceed the target temperature, food cannot overcook in the conventional sense, though texture changes and food-safety considerations limit holding times.

Food safety

Because sous vide temperatures can fall in the bacterial "danger zone", cooks must follow validated time–temperature pasteurization tables (such as those published by Douglas Baldwin) and chill food rapidly in an ice bath if storing it after cooking.
