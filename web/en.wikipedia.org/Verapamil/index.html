Verapamil

Verapamil, sold under various trade names including Isoptin and Calan, is a calcium channel blocker medication used to treat high blood pressure, angina (chest pain from insufficient blood flow to the heart), and certain abnormal heart rhythms, particularly supraventricular tachycardias. It is also used for the prevention of cluster headaches and migraine. It is on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines and is available as a generic medication.

Medical uses

Verapamil is used for the management of hypertension, for prophylaxis of angina pectoris, and for rate control in supraventricular tachyarrhythmias such as atrial fibrillation. Intravenous verapamil can be used to terminate certain acute supraventricular tachycardias. Off-label, high-dose verapamil is a first-line preventive treatment for cluster headache.

Mechanism of action

Verapamil blocks L-type voltage-gated calcium channels in vascular smooth muscle and in the heart. This relaxes arterial smooth muscle, reducing blood pressure, and slows conduction through the atrioventricular node while reducing the force of cardiac contraction. Unlike dihydropyridine calcium channel blockers such as amlodipine, verapamil has substantial direct cardiac effects.

Side effects

Common side effects include constipation (a distinctive and frequent problem), headache, flushing, dizziness, ankle swelling and low blood pressure. Because of its negative inotropic and chronotropic effects, verapamil is generally avoided in people with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction, and combining it with beta blockers can cause dangerous slowing of the heart and AV block. Verapamil inhibits the liver enzyme CYP3A4 and the transporter P-glycoprotein, leading to many drug interactions, including with grapefruit juice.

History

Verapamil was developed in Germany in the early 1960s at Knoll AG and was the first calcium channel blocker to enter widespread clinical use. It was approved for medical use in the United States in 1981.
