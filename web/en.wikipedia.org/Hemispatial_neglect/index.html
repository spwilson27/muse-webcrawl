Hemispatial neglect

Hemispatial neglect is a neuropsychological condition in which, after damage to one hemisphere of the brain (classically the right posterior parietal lobe), a deficit in attention to and awareness of one side of space is observed. It is also known as unilateral neglect, hemineglect, or spatial neglect. The patient fails to attend to objects, people, and even parts of their own body on the side opposite the lesion — usually the left — despite intact basic vision, sensation, and movement.

Presentation

A person with left neglect may eat only from the right half of a plate, shave only the right side of the face, collide with objects on the left, or read only the right halves of words. When copying drawings they omit left-side details, and on line-bisection tests they mark well to the right of centre. Some patients deny the deficit entirely (anosognosia) or deny ownership of a limb (somatoparaphrenia). Neglect can also affect imagined scenes: in the famous Piazza del Duomo study, patients describing a familiar square from memory omitted landmarks on the imagined left from one viewpoint and on the imagined right when the viewpoint was reversed.

Causes and mechanisms

Neglect most often follows stroke affecting the right inferior parietal lobule and temporoparietal junction, but also arises from frontal, thalamic, and basal-ganglia lesions and from an imbalance between large-scale attention networks. It must be distinguished from hemianopia, in which a visual field cut, unlike neglect, is usually noticed and compensated for.

Treatment

Rehabilitation approaches include visual scanning training, prism adaptation, limb activation, neck-muscle vibration, caloric stimulation, and non-invasive brain stimulation; spontaneous recovery is common in the first weeks but persistent neglect predicts poorer functional outcome after stroke.
