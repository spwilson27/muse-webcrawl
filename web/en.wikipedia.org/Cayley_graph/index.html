Cayley graph

Named afterArthur Cayley
Defined forA group and a generating set
FieldsGroup theory, graph theory, geometric group theory

In mathematics, a Cayley graph, also known as a Cayley colour graph or group diagram, is a graph that encodes the abstract structure of a group. Given a group G and a generating set S (closed under inverses and not containing the identity), the Cayley graph has one vertex for each group element and an edge from g to gs for each generator s in S; edges are often coloured by the generator they represent.


Cayley graph of the free group on two generators — a 4-regular tree

Examples

The Cayley graph of the cyclic group Zn with generator 1 is an n-cycle. The Cayley graph of the infinite cyclic group is an infinite path. For the free group on two generators it is an infinite 4-regular tree. The Cayley graph of a direct product of groups is the Cartesian product of the factors' Cayley graphs.

Properties and applications

Cayley graphs are vertex-transitive, and a graph is a Cayley graph of some group if and only if its automorphism group contains a subgroup acting regularly on the vertices (Sabidussi's theorem). Because the graph depends on the generating set but its large-scale geometry does not (different generating sets give quasi-isometric graphs), Cayley graphs are the central objects of geometric group theory, where the word metric turns groups into geometric spaces. Cayley graphs with strong expansion properties — expander graphs — are built from groups such as SL(2, Zp) and are used in computer science and network design.

History

Cayley graphs were introduced by Arthur Cayley in 1878 to visualise group presentations. They were revived by Max Dehn in the early twentieth century in his work on the word problem, and became a major research tool with Gromov's programme in geometric group theory.
