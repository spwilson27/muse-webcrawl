Life expectancy

Life expectancy is a statistical measure of the average time an organism is expected to live, based on the year of its birth, its current age, and other demographic factors. Most commonly it means life expectancy at birth: the average number of years a newborn would live if mortality rates at each age remained as observed in the reference period. It is calculated from life tables by demographers and actuaries.

Global patterns

World life expectancy at birth was about 73 years in the early 2020s, having risen from roughly 32 years in 1900 thanks to sanitation, nutrition, vaccines, and medicine. The highest values — in places such as Japan, Switzerland, Spain, and Singapore — exceed 83 years, while the lowest, in parts of sub-Saharan Africa affected by poverty and disease, are in the 50s. Women outlive men by about five years on average worldwide.

History

For most of human history, life expectancy at birth was between 20 and 40 years, largely because of high infant and child mortality; individuals who survived childhood often lived into their 60s or beyond. John Graunt's life table of 1662 began the quantitative study of longevity. In wealthy countries the figure passed 50 around 1900 and 70 by the mid-20th century. Gains have occasionally stalled or reversed — during the HIV/AIDS epidemic in southern Africa, from opioids and "deaths of despair" in the United States, and during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Interpretation

Life expectancy is a period summary, not a prediction for any individual; related measures include healthy life expectancy (years lived in full health), remaining life expectancy at a given age, and maximum lifespan, which for humans is about 122 years (Jeanne Calment).
