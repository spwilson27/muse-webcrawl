The MAPK/ERK pathway (also known as the Ras–Raf–MEK–ERK pathway) is a chain of proteins in the cell that communicates a signal from a receptor on the surface of the cell to the DNA in the nucleus of the cell. The pathway includes many proteins, such as mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs), originally called extracellular signal-regulated kinases (ERKs), which communicate by adding phosphate groups to neighbouring proteins, thereby acting as an "on" or "off" switch.

When one of the proteins in the pathway is mutated, it can become stuck in the "on" or "off" position, a necessary step in the development of many cancers. Components of the MAPK/ERK pathway were first discovered in the 1970s and 1980s in connection with retroviral oncogenes, and the pathway is now known to regulate fundamental processes including cell division, differentiation, and survival in most multicellular organisms.

Signalling cascade

The signal starts when a signalling molecule binds to the receptor on the cell surface and ends when the DNA in the nucleus expresses a protein and produces some change in the cell, such as cell division. The pathway includes many proteins, including MAP kinases, which mediate intracellular signalling.

Receptor activation and Ras

Most ligands that activate the pathway bind to receptor tyrosine kinases (RTKs) such as the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR). Ligand binding causes receptor dimerisation and autophosphorylation of tyrosine residues, which creates docking sites for adaptor proteins such as Grb2. Grb2 recruits the guanine nucleotide exchange factor SOS, which activates the small GTPase Ras by promoting exchange of GDP for GTP.

The kinase cascade

Activated Ras recruits the serine/threonine kinase Raf (MAPKKK) to the plasma membrane, where Raf is activated. Raf then phosphorylates and activates the dual-specificity kinases MEK1 and MEK2 (MAPKK), which in turn phosphorylate ERK1 and ERK2 (MAPK) on both threonine and tyrosine residues. Activated ERK translocates to the nucleus, where it phosphorylates transcription factors such as Elk-1, c-Fos, and c-Myc, altering gene expression.

Regulation

The pathway is tightly regulated by negative feedback: ERK phosphorylates upstream components including SOS and Raf, dampening further signalling. Phosphatases such as the dual-specificity phosphatases (DUSPs) dephosphorylate ERK and switch the signal off. Scaffold proteins, including KSR (kinase suppressor of Ras), organise the components of the cascade and influence signal duration and intensity.

Role in cancer

Deregulation of the MAPK/ERK pathway is a hallmark of many human cancers. Activating mutations in Ras occur in roughly a quarter of all tumours, and the BRAF V600E mutation is found in about half of melanomas. Drugs targeting the pathway — including BRAF inhibitors such as vemurafenib and MEK inhibitors such as trametinib — are used clinically, although resistance commonly develops through reactivation of the pathway.
