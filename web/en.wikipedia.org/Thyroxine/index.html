Thyroxine

Thyroxine, also known as T4, is a hormone produced by the follicular cells of the thyroid gland. It is the principal thyroid hormone secreted into the blood and acts largely as a prohormone of the more active triiodothyronine (T3). Thyroxine contains four atoms of iodine per molecule — hence T4 — and is formed from the amino acid tyrosine within the protein thyroglobulin.

Synthesis and regulation

The thyroid takes up iodide from the blood and organifies it onto tyrosyl residues of thyroglobulin stored in the follicle colloid; coupling of iodinated residues yields T4 (and some T3), which is released under the control of thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) from the pituitary, itself regulated by thyrotropin-releasing hormone from the hypothalamus. In the blood, more than 99 percent of T4 is bound to transport proteins such as thyroxine-binding globulin. In peripheral tissues, deiodinase enzymes convert T4 to T3, which binds nuclear thyroid hormone receptors and regulates gene expression.

Function

Thyroid hormones raise the basal metabolic rate, increase oxygen consumption and heat production, and are essential for normal growth and development, particularly of the brain in infancy. They also influence heart rate, gut motility and protein, fat and carbohydrate metabolism.

Clinical significance

Underproduction of thyroxine causes hypothyroidism, treated with levothyroxine, a synthetic form of T4 that is among the most widely prescribed medicines in the world. Excess production causes hyperthyroidism (thyrotoxicosis). Blood tests for free T4 and TSH are the standard means of assessing thyroid function. Iodine deficiency, historically a major cause of goitre and congenital hypothyroidism, impairs thyroxine synthesis and is prevented by iodised salt.
