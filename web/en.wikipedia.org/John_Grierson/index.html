John Grierson CBE (26 April 1898 – 19 February 1972) was a pioneering Scottish documentary maker, often considered the father of British and Canadian documentary film. In 1926, Grierson coined the term "documentary" in a review of Robert J. Flaherty's Moana. He defined documentary as the "creative treatment of actuality", a phrase that became a touchstone of the movement he founded.

Early life and education

Grierson was born in Deanston, near Doune in Stirlingshire, Scotland, the son of a schoolmaster. He studied at the University of Glasgow, graduating with an MA in English and moral philosophy in 1923. During the First World War he served in the Royal Navy as an ordinary seaman on a minesweeper. After Glasgow he won a Rockefeller Fellowship to study in the United States, where he researched public opinion and mass media at the University of Chicago, Columbia University and the University of Wisconsin–Madison, and became fascinated by the cinema's potential as an instrument of public education and democratic citizenship.

Documentary film movement

Returning to Britain, Grierson persuaded the Empire Marketing Board (EMB) to fund a film unit. His first film, Drifters (1929), a study of the North Sea herring fleet, premiered alongside Eisenstein's Battleship Potemkin and established his reputation. The EMB Film Unit, and from 1933 the GPO Film Unit, gathered a remarkable generation of filmmakers — including Basil Wright, Harry Watt, Edgar Anstey, Stuart Legg and Alberto Cavalcanti — and produced classics such as Song of Ceylon (1934), Housing Problems (1935) and Night Mail (1936), the latter with verse by W. H. Auden and music by Benjamin Britten.

Canada and later career

In 1939 Grierson was invited to Canada, where he recommended the creation of a national film board; he became the first Commissioner of the National Film Board of Canada in October 1939, building it into a major wartime producer of documentary and propaganda series such as Canada Carries On and The World in Action. After the war he worked for UNESCO as director of mass communications, and later in Scotland, where he presented the long-running television series This Wonderful World. He was appointed CBE in 1946.

Legacy

Grierson's conviction that cinema could serve social purpose and civic education shaped documentary practice worldwide. The Grierson Award for documentary film is presented annually at the Edinburgh International Film Festival, and his name is commemorated by the Grierson Trust and the Grierson Awards for British documentary.
