The United Kingdom of the Netherlands (Dutch: Verenigd Koninkrijk der Nederlanden) was a kingdom that existed from 1815 to 1839, uniting the territories of the former Dutch Republic, the Austrian Netherlands, and the Prince-Bishopric of Liège — roughly the present-day Netherlands, Belgium, and Luxembourg. It was created by the Congress of Vienna, which sought to establish a strong state on France's northern border after the Napoleonic Wars, and it was ruled by William I of the House of Orange-Nassau.

Creation

The Eight Articles of London (1814) laid down the conditions of the union, and the Congress of Vienna confirmed it in 1815. William, who had already assumed sovereignty over the north in 1813, was proclaimed king in Brussels. Luxembourg was made a grand duchy in personal union with the Dutch crown and simultaneously a member state of the German Confederation.

Government and tensions

William I governed as an energetic, authoritarian "merchant king", promoting industry, canals, and trade, especially in the south. But the union was strained by fundamental disparities: the north was predominantly Protestant and Dutch-speaking, the south mostly Catholic; southern liberals and Catholics objected to the king's control of the press and education, to the imposition of Dutch as an administrative language in Flanders, and to the south's under-representation in the States General despite its larger population. Opposition groups — Catholics and liberals — formed a "union of oppositions" against the government in 1828.

Belgian Revolution and dissolution

In August 1830 the Belgian Revolution broke out in Brussels, inspired by the July Revolution in France. The Dutch army failed to retake Brussels, and a provisional government declared Belgian independence on 4 October 1830. A conference of the great powers in London recognised Belgian independence, but William refused to accept the settlement and held out in Antwerp until 1832; he did not accept the terms until the Treaty of London of 1839. That treaty formally partitioned Luxembourg, confirmed Belgian independence and perpetual neutrality, and ended the United Kingdom.
