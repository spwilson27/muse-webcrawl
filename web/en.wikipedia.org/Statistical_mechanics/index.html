Statistical mechanics

Statistical mechanics is a branch of theoretical physics that applies probability theory and statistics to describe the behaviour of large systems composed of many particles. Instead of tracking each particle individually, it predicts macroscopic properties — such as temperature, pressure and entropy — from the statistical distribution of microscopic states. It provides the microscopic foundation of thermodynamics.

History

The field grew out of the kinetic theory of gases developed by James Clerk Maxwell and Ludwig Boltzmann in the 19th century. Maxwell derived the distribution of molecular speeds in a gas, and Boltzmann gave entropy its famous statistical interpretation, S = k log W, relating it to the number of microscopic configurations of a system. Josiah Willard Gibbs systematised the subject in his 1902 Elementary Principles in Statistical Mechanics, introducing ensembles — canonical, microcanonical and grand canonical — as the basic theoretical objects.

Core concepts

  • Microstates and macrostates: a macrostate (defined by a few bulk variables) corresponds to an enormous number of microstates (exact configurations).
  • Ensembles: collections of possible microstates weighted by probability, characterising systems in different conditions of isolation or contact with reservoirs.
  • Partition function: a central quantity from which thermodynamic potentials and averages can be derived.
  • Ergodicity: the assumption that time averages equal ensemble averages for typical systems.

Quantum statistics

In quantum statistical mechanics, particles obey either Fermi–Dirac statistics (fermions, subject to the Pauli exclusion principle) or Bose–Einstein statistics (bosons, which can condense into a single state as in a Bose–Einstein condensate). These distributions explain phenomena from electron behaviour in metals to superfluidity and the blackbody radiation spectrum.

Applications

Beyond gases and solids, statistical mechanics underpins the theory of phase transitions and critical phenomena, polymer and soft-matter physics, and methods widely used in chemistry, biology, information theory and even machine learning.
