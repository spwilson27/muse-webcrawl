Fabry–Pérot interferometer

In optics, a Fabry–Pérot interferometer or etalon is an optical cavity made from two parallel reflecting surfaces (i.e.: thin mirrors). Optical waves can pass through the cavity only when they are in resonance with it. It is named after Charles Fabry and Alfred Pérot, who developed the instrument in 1899.

Principle

Light entering the cavity undergoes multiple reflections between the two partially silvered surfaces. Constructive interference occurs only at specific wavelengths, producing a transmission spectrum of very sharp peaks (the Airy function). The sharpness of the fringes is characterised by the finesse, which depends on the reflectivity of the surfaces.

Applications

  • High-resolution spectroscopy and wavelength measurement
  • Laser resonators (the cavity of a typical laser is a Fabry–Pérot resonator)
  • Optical filters and wavelength-division multiplexing in telecommunications
  • Gravitational-wave detectors such as LIGO, whose arms are Fabry–Pérot cavities

Terminology

Etalon is from the French étalon, meaning "measuring gauge" or "standard". The term "Fabry–Pérot interferometer" is commonly reserved for instruments where the spacing can be varied, while "etalon" is used for fixed cavities.
