Interpretations of quantum mechanics

The major interpretations differ in what they take the quantum state to represent.

An interpretation of quantum mechanics is an attempt to explain how the mathematical theory of quantum mechanics might correspond to experienced reality. Although quantum mechanics has held up to rigorous and extremely precise tests in an extraordinarily broad range of experiments, there exist a number of contending schools of thought over their interpretation.

Major interpretations

The Copenhagen interpretation, associated with Niels Bohr and Werner Heisenberg, treats the wave function as a tool for computing probabilities and holds that measurement causes an apparent collapse of the wave function. The many-worlds interpretation, proposed by Hugh Everett in 1957, denies collapse altogether: every measurement outcome occurs in a separate, non-interacting branch of the universe. De Broglie–Bohm theory (pilot-wave theory) posits particles with definite positions guided by a pilot wave. Objective collapse theories, such as the Ghirardi–Rimini–Weber model, modify the Schrödinger equation so that collapse is a real physical process. QBism interprets quantum probabilities as an agent's subjective beliefs.

The measurement problem

The measurement problem arises because the Schrödinger equation describes systems evolving smoothly and deterministically, yet experiments yield single definite outcomes. Interpretations differ in whether they treat the wave function as physically real (ontic) or as a summary of information (epistemic), and in how they handle quantum entanglement and nonlocality.

History

The interpretational debate began in the 1920s with the Bohr–Einstein debates, including the famous Solvay Conferences and the Einstein–Podolsky–Rosen paradox of 1935. John Bell's theorem of 1964 and subsequent experiments by Alain Aspect and others showed that local hidden-variable theories cannot reproduce all quantum predictions.
