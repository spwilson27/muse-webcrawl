Education in ancient Greece

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Education in ancient Greece varied greatly between city-states. In Athens, education aimed to produce citizens who could govern and participate in public life; in Sparta it aimed to produce soldiers. It was largely private and informal, reserved mainly for free-born boys, while girls' education was usually domestic.

Athens

Athenian boys of well-off families began schooling around age seven under three teachers: the grammatistes, who taught reading, writing and arithmetic; the kitharistes, who taught music and poetry, especially Homer; and the paidotribes, who supervised gymnastics and wrestling. From about twelve they studied geometry, astronomy and rhetoric, and after age eighteen young men underwent two years of military service as ephebes before entering full citizenship. Higher education emerged with the Sophists, the schools of Isocrates, and the philosophical schools founded by Plato (the Academy, c. 387 BC) and Aristotle (the Lyceum).

Sparta

Spartan boys entered the agoge, a state-run training system, at age seven, living in communal barracks and enduring hardship, discipline and physical training. Reading and writing were taught only to a minimal level, and education emphasised obedience, endurance and loyalty to the state. Girls in Sparta, unusually for the Greek world, received physical training and some public education.

Legacy

Greek education shaped the Roman and later Western educational tradition, especially through the paideia — the ideal of forming a complete citizen through literature, philosophy, rhetoric and physical training — and the vocabulary of the liberal arts.
