Capstan (nautical)

Sailors working a ship's capstan (placeholder)

A capstan is a vertical-axised rotating machine developed for use on sailing ships to multiply the pulling force of seamen when hauling ropes, cables, and hawsers. The principle is similar to that of the windlass, which has a horizontal axis. A cable or rope is wound several turns around a vertical drum (the whelp); as the drum rotates, the friction of the turns (the capstan effect) allows a small holding force at the free end to control a very large load, and the hauling part is pulled in.

History and use

Capstans were essential for heavy work aboard large sailing vessels: raising the anchor, hoisting yards and heavy sails, and warping the ship. Early capstans were turned by sailors pushing on long removable bars inserted into sockets in the drum head, walking in a circle — a scene accompanied by sea shanties sung to keep time. Later designs added gearing (including two-speed "double" capstans), ratchets and pawls to prevent slipping back, and eventually steam, hydraulic, and electric drives. The largest warships and merchantmen carried several capstans on different decks, sometimes linked so men on two decks could work together.

Modern capstans

Today, powered capstans are found on ships for mooring and anchor handling, on railway shunting yards, and in industry for pulling cable. The physics of the device is described by the capstan equation (Euler–Eytelwein formula), relating the hold and load forces by the coefficient of friction and the total angle of wrap.
