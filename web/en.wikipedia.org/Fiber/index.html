Fiber

A fiber (or fibre in Commonwealth English) is a natural or artificial substance that is significantly longer than it is wide. Fibers are often used in the manufacture of other materials; the strongest engineering materials, such as carbon fiber and ultra-high-molecular-weight polyethylene, are often incorporated as fibers into composites. Fibers occur throughout biology and technology: in textiles, ropes, paper, optical communications, and the human body.

Natural fibers

Natural fibers are derived from plants, animals, or minerals. Plant fibers include seed fibers (cotton), bast fibers (flax, hemp, jute, ramie), and leaf fibers (sisal, abacá). Animal fibers are protein-based and include wool from sheep and silk from silkworms. The mineral fiber asbestos was once widely used but is now restricted because of its health hazards. Cellulose is the principal chemical constituent of plant fibers.

Synthetic and regenerated fibers

Synthetic fibers are made from petrochemical polymers and include nylon, polyester, acrylic, and spandex; regenerated fibers such as rayon are produced from natural polymers, usually cellulose, that have been dissolved and re-formed. Optical fibers are drawn strands of glass or plastic that transmit light for telecommunications and sensing. Carbon fibers, made by pyrolyzing polymer precursors, combine high strength and stiffness with low weight and are used in aerospace and sporting goods.

Biological fibers

In anatomy, the term describes elongated cellular structures such as muscle fibers and nerve fibers (axons), as well as dietary fiber — the indigestible portion of plant foods that aids digestion. The high aspect ratio of fibers is the unifying feature across these diverse uses.
