Mitral regurgitation

Mitral regurgitation (MR), also known as mitral insufficiency, is a disorder of the heart in which the mitral valve does not close properly when the left ventricle contracts, allowing blood to flow backward from the left ventricle into the left atrium. It is one of the most common valvular heart diseases.

Causes

Primary (degenerative) MR results from abnormalities of the valve itself, most commonly mitral valve prolapse, and also from rheumatic heart disease, infective endocarditis, or calcification. Secondary (functional) MR occurs when a structurally normal valve leaks because the left ventricle is enlarged or distorted, as in ischemic heart disease or dilated cardiomyopathy. Acute MR can follow rupture of a papillary muscle after myocardial infarction or rupture of chordae tendineae, and is a medical emergency.

Signs and symptoms

Chronic MR may be asymptomatic for years as the heart compensates, but eventually causes exertional breathlessness, fatigue, orthopnea, palpitations from atrial fibrillation, and signs of heart failure. The characteristic sign is a holosystolic (pansystolic) murmur heard best at the apex and radiating to the axilla. Acute MR typically presents abruptly with pulmonary edema and cardiogenic shock.

Diagnosis and treatment

Echocardiography is the principal test for confirming the diagnosis and grading severity. Management of chronic disease includes treatment of heart failure and rate control; definitive treatment of severe primary MR is mitral valve repair (preferred) or replacement, increasingly supplemented by transcatheter edge-to-edge repair (such as MitraClip) for selected patients, particularly in secondary MR or high surgical risk.
