Predicate logic

Predicate logic is a system of formal logic that extends propositional logic by introducing predicates, variables and quantifiers. In first-order logic, quantifiers range over individual objects; in higher-order logics they may also range over predicates and functions. Predicate logic can express statements such as "every student passed" or "there is a number greater than all others in the set", which propositional logic cannot analyse.

Syntax and semantics

The language of first-order logic includes constant symbols, function symbols, predicate symbols, variables, the logical connectives, and the quantifiers ∀ (universal) and ∃ (existential). A model interprets these symbols in a domain of discourse, and a sentence is true or false relative to such an interpretation. Key properties include soundness and completeness: Kurt Gödel's completeness theorem (1929) shows that every semantically valid first-order sentence is provable in standard deductive systems.

History

Predicate logic was developed independently by Gottlob Frege in his Begriffsschrift (1879) and by Charles Sanders Peirce and his students in the 1880s. It became the standard framework for the foundations of mathematics and underlies fields from mathematical logic to logic programming languages such as Prolog. Unlike first-order logic, full second-order logic is not complete or compact, and first-order validity is undecidable, as shown by Alonzo Church and Alan Turing in 1936.
