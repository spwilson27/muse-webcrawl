Extended producer responsibility

Extended producer responsibility (EPR) is an environmental policy approach in which a producer's responsibility for a product is extended to the post-consumer stage of its life cycle — that is, to its collection, recycling, and final disposal. The concept was formally introduced by the Swedish academic Thomas Lindhqvist in a 1990 report to the Swedish Ministry of the Environment, and it operationalizes the polluter pays principle for products.

Mechanisms

EPR programmes typically combine take-back requirements, deposit-refund systems, advance disposal fees, and recycled-content or collection targets. Producers may discharge their obligations individually or collectively through producer responsibility organizations. Landmark implementations include Germany's 1991 Packaging Ordinance and its Green Dot (Der Grüne Punkt) system, and the European Union's directives on waste electrical and electronic equipment (WEEE), end-of-life vehicles, batteries, and packaging.

Rationale and criticism

By shifting waste-management costs from municipalities and taxpayers to producers and consumers, EPR aims to create incentives for design for recycling, durability, and reduced material use. Critics question whether collective schemes dilute individual design incentives, whether fees are set at effective levels, and how EPR should apply to imports and online marketplaces. The OECD has published guidance on EPR since 2001 and updated it in 2016, reflecting the approach's spread to dozens of countries.
