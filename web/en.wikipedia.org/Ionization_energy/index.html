Ionization energy

The ionization energy (also called the ionization potential) is the minimum amount of energy required to remove the most loosely bound electron of an isolated neutral gaseous atom or molecule. It is quantitatively expressed as X(g) + energy → X+(g) + e−.

Trends

Ionization energy generally increases from left to right across a period of the periodic table, because the increasing nuclear charge holds electrons more tightly, and decreases down a group as electrons occupy shells farther from the nucleus. The noble gases have the highest ionization energies in each period, reflecting their stable filled electron shells.

Successive ionization energies

The second ionization energy is the energy needed to remove an electron from a singly charged positive ion, and so on. Successive values increase, with a large jump when the removal begins to break into a completed inner shell — a pattern that gives direct experimental evidence for the shell structure of atoms.

Units and measurement

Ionization energies are conventionally measured in kilojoules per mole (kJ/mol) in chemistry, or electronvolts (eV) per atom in physics. The first ionization energy of hydrogen is 1,312 kJ/mol (13.598 eV), while that of helium, 2,372 kJ/mol, is the largest of any element.
