Trapezius

The trapezius is a large paired trapezoid-shaped surface muscle that extends from the occipital bone of the skull to the lower thoracic vertebrae of the back and laterally to the spine of the scapula. Together, the two trapezius muscles form a diamond or trapezoid shape — hence the name — covering much of the upper back and the back of the neck. It moves the scapula and supports the arm.

Structure

The muscle is conventionally divided into three functional parts. The descending (superior) part arises from the occipital bone and cervical vertebrae and inserts on the lateral third of the clavicle; it elevates the scapula. The transverse (middle) part arises from the upper thoracic vertebrae and inserts on the acromion; it retracts the scapula. The ascending (inferior) part arises from the lower thoracic vertebrae and inserts on the spine of the scapula; it depresses the scapula. Working together, the upper and lower parts rotate the scapula upward, as when raising the arm overhead.

Innervation and function

The trapezius is unusual among skeletal muscles in being innervated principally by a cranial nerve, the spinal accessory nerve (CN XI), with contributions from cervical nerves C3 and C4 conveying proprioception and pain. Damage to the accessory nerve — for example during neck surgery — causes drooping of the shoulder and difficulty shrugging. The trapezius is commonly involved in neck and shoulder pain and tension, and is strengthened by exercises such as shrugs and rows.
