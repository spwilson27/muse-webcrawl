Magnavox Odyssey

The Magnavox Odyssey is the first commercial home video game console. Developed by a team led by Ralph H. Baer at Sanders Associates and released by Magnavox in September 1972 in the United States, it displayed simple white blocks and a vertical line on a television screen, using plastic overlays and printed circuit "game cards" to vary the play. It sold roughly 350,000 units and launched the video game console industry.

Development

Baer conceived of playing games on a television in 1966 and, with colleagues Bill Harrison and Bill Rusch, built a series of prototypes at Sanders Associates, culminating in the 1968 "Brown Box" — a wood-grained prototype that played chase, sports, and shooting games. Magnavox licensed the technology in 1971, redesigned the unit using diode–transistor logic (no integrated processor), and marketed it through its television dealer network, which led some consumers to believe it worked only with Magnavox TVs.

Games and accessories

The console shipped with game cards that rewired the internal circuitry to produce variations — table tennis, tennis, hockey, skiing, football, roulette, and others — with translucent colour overlays taped to the TV screen providing courts, fields, and scenery. Players kept score manually with included paper scoreboards, play money, and dice; the system had no sound and no on-screen scoring. A light-gun accessory, sold separately, was among the first of its kind for home play. In all, about 28 games were released for the system.

Legacy

The Odyssey's table-tennis game directly inspired Atari's arcade hit Pong (1972) after Nolan Bushnell played it at a Magnavox demonstration; Magnavox and Sanders sued Atari and many later manufacturers for patent infringement, winning settlements that earned tens of millions of dollars and established Baer's patents as foundational to the industry. The Brown Box prototype is held by the Smithsonian Institution, and Baer received the National Medal of Technology in 2004.
