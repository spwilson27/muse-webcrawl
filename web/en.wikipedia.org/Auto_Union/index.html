Auto Union

The streamlined Auto Union racing cars of the 1930s, designed by Ferdinand Porsche, were famed for their mid-engined layout.

Auto Union AG was a German automobile manufacturer formed in 1932 by the merger of four companies: Audi, DKW, Horch and Wanderer. Its emblem of four interlocking rings — still used by Audi today — represented the four founding firms. The company is best remembered for its state-backed Grand Prix racing cars, the Auto Union "Silver Arrows", which duelled with Mercedes-Benz in the 1930s.

Formation

During the Great Depression, Saxony's four motor manufacturers combined under pressure from the State Bank of Saxony to survive. DKW, then the world's largest motorcycle maker, provided much of the group's volume; Horch was its luxury marque, Audi a mid-range brand, and Wanderer's car division was transferred to the new group.

Grand Prix racing

With funding from Adolf Hitler's government, which wanted German cars to dominate international racing, Auto Union commissioned Ferdinand Porsche to design a mid-engined Grand Prix car. The Type A of 1934 placed its V16 engine behind the driver, decades ahead of standard practice. Drivers such as Hans Stuck, Bernd Rosemeyer, Tazio Nuvolari and Rudolf Caracciola's Mercedes rivals made the 1930s a golden age of racing; Rosemeyer won the European Championship in 1936 and was killed in a land-speed record attempt in 1938. The cars' enormous power and difficult handling made them legendary.

War and aftermath

The company's factories in Saxony fell into the Soviet occupation zone after the Second World War and were dismantled; Auto Union was deleted from the Chemnitz commercial register in 1948. Executives re-founded the company in Ingolstadt, Bavaria, in 1949 as Auto Union GmbH, building DKW two-stroke cars and motorcycles. Daimler-Benz acquired it in 1958 and sold it to Volkswagen in 1964; the revived Audi brand, launched with the Audi F103 of 1965, eventually gave the modern company its name, Audi AG.
