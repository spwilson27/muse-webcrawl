Fort Stanwix National Monument

Fort Stanwix National Monument is a United States National Monument in Rome, New York, preserving the site of Fort Stanwix, a colonial fort at the Oneida Carry on the portage between the Mohawk River and Wood Creek. Administered by the National Park Service, the monument consists of a full-scale reconstruction of the 18th-century fort, built in the 1970s on the original site in downtown Rome.

History of the site

The original Fort Stanwix was built by the British in 1758 during the French and Indian War and hosted the treaties of 1768 and 1784 with the Iroquois Confederacy. During the American Revolutionary War the rebuilt fort, garrisoned by Continental troops under Colonel Peter Gansevoort, withstood a three-week British siege in August 1777 — an episode that helped frustrate the British Saratoga campaign. The fort was abandoned after 1781 and the growing city of Rome was built over it, erasing the remains from view.

Establishment and reconstruction

The site was designated a National Monument in 1935, but for decades it consisted mainly of a marker amid city streets. In the 1970s the National Park Service undertook an unusual urban-renewal project: buildings over the fort's footprint were demolished, archaeologists excavated the site, and from 1974 to 1978 the fort was reconstructed in its original form — earthworks, palisades, barracks, and bastions — opening to the public during the Bicentennial. The Marinus Willett Collections Management and Education Center, opened in 2005, houses the monument's museum exhibits and artifact collections.

Interpretation

Rangers and re-enactors present the fort as it appeared during the 1777 siege, with reconstructed soldiers' quarters, a bake oven, and period demonstrations. The monument interprets the Oneida Carry's role in trade, diplomacy, and war, and the experiences of British, American, and Native peoples in the Mohawk Valley.
