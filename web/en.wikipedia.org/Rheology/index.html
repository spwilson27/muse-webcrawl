Rheology

Rheology (from Greek ῥέω rhéō, 'flow', and -λoγία, -logia, 'study of') is the study of the flow of matter, primarily in a fluid (liquid or gas) state but also as "soft solids" or solids under conditions in which they respond with plastic flow rather than deforming elastically in response to an applied force. Rheology is the branch of physics that deals with the deformation and flow of materials, both solids and liquids.

Behaviour of Newtonian and non-Newtonian fluids

The term was coined by Eugene C. Bingham in 1920, on the suggestion of his colleague Markus Reiner; the science was consolidated with the founding of the Society of Rheology in 1929. The term was inspired by the aphorism of Heraclitus, panta rhei (πάντα ῥεῖ, "everything flows").

Scope

In practice, rheology is principally concerned with extending continuum mechanics to characterise the flow of materials whose behaviour cannot be described by the classical theories of elasticity and (Newtonian) fluid mechanics. It is concerned with materials with complex microstructures, such as muds, sludges, suspensions, polymers and other glass formers, as well as many biological materials whose mechanical properties lie between those of a fluid and a solid. Important properties measured in rheology include viscosity, viscoelasticity (elastic and viscous moduli), yield stress and extensional behaviour.

Newtonian and non-Newtonian fluids

A Newtonian fluid has a viscosity independent of shear rate. Non-Newtonian fluids show shear thinning (viscosity decreasing with shear rate, as in paints and blood), shear thickening (as in dense cornstarch suspensions), thixotropy (viscosity decreasing over time under shear, as in some gels and yoghurt), or a yield stress that must be exceeded before flow begins (as in toothpaste and mayonnaise).

Measurement

Rheological measurements are made with rheometers, instruments that apply controlled stress or strain — typically using rotational parallel-plate, cone-and-plate or concentric-cylinder geometries — and capillary rheometers for high shear rates. Key dimensionless groups in rheology include the Deborah number, comparing material relaxation time to the observation timescale, and the Weissenberg number, characterising elastic effects in steady flows.

Applications

Rheology is applied in polymer processing, food science, cosmetics, pharmaceuticals, paints and coatings, cement and concrete technology, geophysics (including the flow of the Earth's mantle and of ice), and hemodynamics — the study of blood flow.
