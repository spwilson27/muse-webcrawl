Niagara Escarpment

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Niagara Escarpment is a long escarpment, or cuesta, in Canada and the United States that runs predominantly east–west from New York State, through Ontario, Michigan, and Wisconsin to Illinois. The escarpment is the cliff face of a resistant band of Silurian dolomite and limestone that caps softer shales beneath; differential erosion of these rocks formed the scarp. It is most famous as the rock formation over which the Niagara River plunges at Niagara Falls.

Geology

The escarpment traces the rim of a saucer-shaped sedimentary basin centred in Michigan. Its rocks were deposited as sediments in a tropical sea more than 420 million years ago. Where the Niagara River crosses the escarpment at Queenston–Lewiston, the falls have eroded a gorge some 11 kilometres long since the end of the last ice age, and continue to retreat slowly upstream.

In Ontario

In Ontario the escarpment runs from the Niagara Peninsula north through Hamilton — where it gives the city its "mountain" — and up the Bruce Peninsula between Georgian Bay and Lake Huron, continuing through the Manitoulin Islands. It is followed for 885 kilometres by the Bruce Trail, Canada's oldest and longest marked footpath. In 1990, UNESCO designated the Ontario portion a World Biosphere Reserve. The Niagara Escarpment Plan, established under provincial legislation in the 1970s and 80s, controls development along the corridor to protect its natural environment, while the escarpment's slopes support orchards, vineyards of the Niagara wine region, quarries, ski areas, and many waterfalls around Hamilton.
