Nystagmus

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Trace of involuntary eye movements in nystagmus

Nystagmus is a condition of involuntary (or voluntary in some cases) eye movement. It can be congenital or acquired as a result of disease or injury. The eyes move rhythmically, typically with a slow drift in one direction followed by a fast corrective movement in the other (jerk nystagmus), or with a smooth oscillation of roughly equal velocity in both directions (pendular nystagmus).

Causes

Physiological nystagmus is normal: the vestibulo-ocular reflex produces it when the head rotates, and optokinetic nystagmus occurs when watching moving scenery, as from a train window. Pathological nystagmus may result from congenital conditions, albinism, disorders of the vestibular system such as Ménière's disease, stroke, multiple sclerosis, or the effects of alcohol and certain drugs. Police roadside tests exploit horizontal gaze nystagmus as an indicator of intoxication.

Effects and treatment

Because the visual image moves across the retina, nystagmus can reduce visual acuity; people with congenital nystagmus usually do not perceive the world as shaking, whereas acquired nystagmus may cause oscillopsia. Treatment addresses the underlying cause where possible and may include corrective lenses, prisms, medications such as gabapentin, or surgery to reposition the eye muscles.
