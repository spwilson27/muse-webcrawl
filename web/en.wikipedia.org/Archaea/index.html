Archaea (singular archaeon) is a domain of single-celled organisms. These microorganisms lack cell nuclei and are therefore prokaryotic. Archaea were initially classified as bacteria, receiving the name archaebacteria, but this grouping is now obsolete: archaeal cells have unique properties separating them from bacteria and from eukaryotes. Further, archaea are divided into multiple recognised phyla. Classification is difficult because most have not been isolated in a laboratory and have been detected only by analysing their nucleic acids in samples from their environment.

Discovery and classification

In 1977 Carl Woese and George Fox proposed, on the basis of small-subunit ribosomal RNA sequences, that the prokaryotes comprised two fundamentally distinct lineages; in 1990 Woese, Otto Kandler and Mark Wheelis formally proposed the three-domain system of Archaea, Bacteria, and Eucarya. The name, from Greek arkhaîa ("ancient things"), reflected an early assumption that archaea were primitive inhabitants of extreme environments. It is now known that archaea are abundant in ordinary environments — oceans, soils, wetlands, and animal guts — and are major contributors to global carbon and nitrogen cycles.

Biochemistry and genetics

Archaea resemble bacteria in size, shape, and genome organisation (a single circular chromosome), but their molecular machinery often resembles that of eukaryotes: their RNA polymerases, ribosomal proteins, and DNA replication and histone systems are eukaryote-like. Their cell membranes are unique, built from ether-linked isoprenoid lipids rather than the ester-linked fatty acids of bacteria, and some archaea have cell walls of pseudopeptidoglycan or protein S-layers rather than peptidoglycan. Many archaea are extremophiles — thermophiles in hot springs, halophiles in saturated brines, acidophiles and alkaliphiles — but mesophilic archaea dominate the ocean's microbial plankton, and methanogenic archaea are essential members of anaerobic communities, including the digestive tracts of ruminants and humans.

Relation to eukaryotes

The discovery in 2015 of the Asgard archaea (beginning with "Lokiarchaeota", found near a hydrothermal vent in the Arctic) transformed understanding of eukaryote origins: Asgard genomes contain many eukaryotic signature proteins, and the first cultured representative, Prometheoarchaeum syntrophicum (2020), supported models in which the eukaryotic cell arose from within the archaeal radiation through symbiosis with a bacterium that became the mitochondrion.
