The senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus ("decree of the Senate concerning the Bacchanalia") is a decree of the Roman Senate, passed in 186 BC, that suppressed and tightly regulated the Bacchanalia — the ecstatic rites of the wine god Bacchus (Dionysus) — throughout Italy. It is famous as one of the earliest surviving documents of the Latin language and as the centrepiece of the historian Livy's dramatic account of a supposed conspiracy and moral panic.

The affair

According to Livy, the cult had spread from southern Italy, admitting men and women of all classes to secret nocturnal rites allegedly involving crime and sexual misconduct. After an informant, the courtesan Hispala Faecenia, revealed the rites to the consul Postumius, the Senate ordered investigation and repression across Italy; Livy claims more than 7,000 people were implicated and many executed. Modern historians treat the affair as a combination of genuine anxiety about unsupervised associations, elite reassertion of control over religion after the Hannibalic War, and exaggerated rhetoric.

Provisions

The decree, preserved on a bronze tablet found at Tiriolo in Calabria in 1640 and now in Vienna, forbade Bacchic shrines and common funds, barred priests and oath-bound organizations, and permitted rites only with senatorial approval, in groups of no more than five, and without male priests. It was addressed to Rome's Italian allies, demonstrating the Senate's authority over Italy.

Language

Written in Old Latin, the text preserves archaic spellings (such as oinvorsei for universi) and remains a key source for the development of Latin and for early Roman law.
