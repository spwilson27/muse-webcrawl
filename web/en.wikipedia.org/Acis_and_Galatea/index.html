Acis and Galatea

Acis and Galatea are characters from Greek mythology later associated together in Ovid's Metamorphoses. The episode tells of the love between the mortal Acis and the Nereid (sea-nymph) Galatea, and the jealousy of the Cyclops Polyphemus, who also loved Galatea.

Myth

Galatea, a sea nymph, loved the handsome youth Acis, son of Faunus and the nymph Symaethis. The Cyclops Polyphemus, who courted Galatea in vain, discovered the lovers together and crushed Acis beneath a rock torn from Mount Etna. In her grief, Galatea transformed the blood of Acis into a river — the Acis in Sicily — and Acis himself into a river god.

In art and music

The story has inspired many works of art, including paintings by Nicolas Poussin and Claude Lorrain. In music, Handel's Acis and Galatea (HWV 49), with a libretto drawing on John Gay and others, is a masque or pastoral opera first performed in 1718 and remains a popular work. Lully's Acis et Galatée (1686) is an earlier operatic treatment.
