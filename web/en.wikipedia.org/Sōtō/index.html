Sōtō

Sōtō Zen (Japanese: 曹洞宗, Sōtō-shū) is the largest of the three traditional sects of Zen Buddhism in Japan, the others being Rinzai and Ōbaku. The school was established in China as the Caodong school during the Tang dynasty and was transmitted to Japan by the monk Dōgen Zenji in the thirteenth century.

Sōtō is distinguished by its emphasis on shikantaza — "just sitting" — a form of zazen meditation in which the practitioner sits in alert stillness without a specific object of concentration, holding that practice and enlightenment are one. This contrasts with the Rinzai school's emphasis on kōan study.

History

Dōgen (1200–1253) travelled to China in 1223 and, after studying under the Caodong master Rujing, returned to Japan to found the school, establishing the monastery of Eihei-ji in present-day Fukui Prefecture, which remains one of the school's two head temples alongside Sōji-ji. His masterwork, the Shōbōgenzō, is among the most important texts of Japanese Buddhism. Keizan Jōkin, regarded as the school's second founder, broadened its appeal and founded Sōji-ji.

Practice

Alongside zazen, Sōtō life includes liturgy, work practice (samu), and the meticulous observance of monastic form described in Dōgen's writings, including his instructions for the cook (Tenzo Kyōkun) and for communal living.
