The Paris Peace Accords, formally the Agreement on Ending the War and Restoring Peace in Viet-Nam, were signed on 27 January 1973 to establish peace in Vietnam and end direct American military involvement in the Vietnam War. They followed years of public and secret negotiations, notably between Henry Kissinger and Le Duc Tho, both awarded (and Tho declined) the 1973 Nobel Peace Prize.

Background

Talks opened in Paris in 1968 after the Tet Offensive, stalemated over mutual withdrawal and South Vietnam's political future. Secret Kissinger–Tho meetings, the US "Vietnamization" policy, the 1972 Easter Offensive and the US "Christmas bombing" (Operation Linebacker II) preceded a breakthrough in October 1972 and final signature in January 1973. An earlier settlement, the Geneva Accords of 1954 after the First Indochina War, had failed to reunify Vietnam.

Terms

The accords provided for a ceasefire in place, total US withdrawal within 60 days, return of prisoners of war, recognition of the Demilitarised Zone as provisional, and a National Council of National Reconciliation and Concord to organise elections in the South. North Vietnamese troops — over 100,000 — were allowed to remain in the South, a fatal weakness for Saigon in critics' view. Related arrangements ended Laos's formal war, though fighting involving Vang Pao's forces wound down through 1973.

Aftermath

The ceasefire quickly broke down; both sides accused each other of violations. US aid cuts, the Watergate crisis and North Vietnam's 1975 Spring Offensive led to the fall of Saigon on 30 April 1975, reunification of Vietnam in 1976 under the government in Hanoi, and reforms later known as Đổi Mới.
