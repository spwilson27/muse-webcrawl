Viennoiserie

Viennoiseries (French: [vjɛnwazʁi]; "things of Vienna") are French baked goods made from a yeast-leavened dough in a manner similar to bread, or from puff pastry, but with added ingredients (particularly eggs, butter, milk, cream and sugar), which give them a richer, sweeter character that approaches that of pastry. The dough is often laminated.

History

The category takes its name from Vienna, whose baking traditions reached Paris in the late 1830s when August Zang, an Austrian artillery officer, opened a Viennese bakery (Boulangerie Viennoise) in Paris. Bakers such as Zang introduced the kipferl, the crescent-shaped ancestor of the croissant, and steam ovens that gave the goods their glossy crust. By the early 20th century, French bakers had adapted the kipferl using laminated yeast dough, creating the modern croissant.

Examples

Popular viennoiseries include the croissant, brioche, pain au chocolat (chocolatine), pain aux raisins, chausson aux pommes, Danish pastry, brioche Nanterre, and the suisse/brioche aux pépites. Laminated items are made by enclosing butter in dough and repeatedly folding and rolling it ("tourage") to create many thin layers.

Production

Making viennoiserie requires temperature control so the butter remains pliable without melting into the dough, careful proofing, and precise baking. Industrial production uses dough sheeters and pre-laminated frozen dough, while artisanal bakeries often perform lamination by hand.
