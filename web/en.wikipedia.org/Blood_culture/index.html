Blood culture

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A blood culture is a medical laboratory test used to detect bacteria or fungi in a person's blood. A blood sample is obtained by venipuncture and inoculated into bottles containing culture media, which are incubated and monitored for microbial growth. Blood cultures are a cornerstone of diagnosing bloodstream infection, bacteraemia, and sepsis, and they guide antimicrobial therapy.

Method

Typically, two sets of cultures are drawn from different venipuncture sites, each set consisting of an aerobic and an anaerobic bottle, before antibiotics are started. Strict skin antisepsis is essential, as contamination with skin flora — particularly coagulase-negative staphylococci — is the principal source of false positives. Modern automated systems continuously monitor bottles for CO2 production indicating growth; positive bottles are Gram stained, subcultured, and the organism identified, increasingly by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry, followed by antimicrobial susceptibility testing.

Indications and interpretation

Blood cultures are indicated for patients with suspected sepsis, fever of unknown origin, endocarditis, and other deep-seated infections. The number of positive sets, the species isolated, and the time to positivity help distinguish true pathogens from contaminants. Fungal cultures and extended incubation may be requested in special cases.

History

Manual blood culture methods date to the early 20th century; radiometric (BACTEC) and later continuously monitored colorimetric systems transformed the field from the 1970s–1990s onward, shortening detection times and standardising practice.
