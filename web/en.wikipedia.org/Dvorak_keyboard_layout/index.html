Dvorak keyboard layout

CreatorAugust Dvorak and William Dealey
Patented1936 (US Patent 2,040,248)

The Dvorak keyboard layout (US: /ˈdvɔːræk/) is an alternative keyboard layout patented in 1936 by August Dvorak, an educational psychologist at the University of Washington, and his brother-in-law William Dealey. Originally called the Dvorak Simplified Keyboard, it was designed so that the most common letters and letter combinations of English fall under the strongest fingers and on the home row, reducing finger travel compared with the dominant QWERTY layout.

Design

In Dvorak, all five vowels (A O E U I) sit on the left-hand home row and the most frequent consonants (D H T N S) on the right, so that typing English alternates hands frequently and roughly 70% of keystrokes in typical text occur on the home row, against about 32% for QWERTY. Dvorak and Dealey derived the layout from time-and-motion studies of typing and from letter-frequency statistics.

History and reception

Dvorak promoted the layout energetically and cited typing championships won by Dvorak typists in the 1930s and a 1944 US Navy study suggesting retraining paid for itself quickly; a 1956 General Services Administration study, by contrast, concluded that retraining was not worthwhile, a finding Dvorak supporters have long disputed. The layout's failure to displace QWERTY is a standard example in economics and technology studies of network effects and path dependence, popularised by Paul David's 1985 essay on QWERTY — an account challenged by Liebowitz and Margolis in "The Fable of the Keys" (1990).

Availability and variants

Dvorak is built into all major desktop and mobile operating systems as a selectable layout. Variants include one-handed layouts for the left and right hand, the "Programmer Dvorak" for software developers, and international versions accommodating accented characters. World-record-level typists have used Dvorak, though most speed records are still set on QWERTY owing to its ubiquity.
