Haplology

Haplology (from Greek ἁπλόος haplóos, "simple", plus λόγος lógos, "speech") is the elimination of a syllable or segment when it occurs adjacent to an identical or similar syllable or segment in a word. It is a form of dissimilation driven by the avoidance of repetition in pronunciation.

Examples

Classic examples include Latin nutritrix "nurse", expected from *nutritritrix, and English pronunciations such as "libry" for library, "Febuary" for February, and "probably" reduced to "probly". Morphological haplology occurs when two identical morphemes would come together and one is dropped. The term itself is sometimes cited with humorous self-reference: a fully regular formation would have been "haplogology".

In language change

Haplology operates both as a synchronic pronunciation tendency and as a mechanism of historical sound change, contributing to the irregular shapes of some words. It is related to, but distinct from, haplography in writing, where a letter or group of letters written twice in succession is written once.
