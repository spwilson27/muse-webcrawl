Chinese characters (traditional: 漢字; simplified: 汉字; pinyin: hànzì) are logographs developed for the writing of Chinese. They are among the oldest continuously used writing systems in the world, and were adapted for Japanese (kanji), Korean (hanja) and formerly Vietnamese. Each character typically corresponds to a single syllable and a morpheme.

History

The earliest confirmed Chinese writing appears on oracle bones of the late Shang dynasty (c. 1200 BCE), used for divination. Through bronze inscriptions, seal script, and the clerical script of the Han dynasty, characters evolved toward the regular script (kaishu) still used today. In the 1950s–60s the People's Republic of China promulgated simplified characters to promote literacy; traditional forms remain standard in Taiwan, Hong Kong and Macau.

Formation

The traditional analysis, from the 2nd-century dictionary Shuowen Jiezi, classifies characters as pictographs, simple ideographs, compound ideographs, phonetic loans, phonetic compounds, and derivative cognates. The great majority — over 80% — are phono-semantic compounds: one component hints at meaning (the radical), another at pronunciation. Dictionaries list characters under 214 Kangxi radicals; tens of thousands of characters are recorded, though a few thousand suffice for everyday literacy.

Use in other languages

Japanese writing mixes kanji with the kana syllabaries; Korean hanja is now used mainly for disambiguation and scholarship; Vietnam replaced chữ Hán and chữ Nôm with the Latin-based quốc ngữ in the 20th century.
