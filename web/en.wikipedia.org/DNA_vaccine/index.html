DNA vaccine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A DNA vaccine is a type of vaccine that transfects a specific antigen-coding DNA sequence into the cells of an organism as a method of inducing an immune response. The delivered DNA — usually a bacterial plasmid engineered with a strong viral promoter — is taken up by host cells, which then produce the antigen themselves, stimulating both antibody (humoral) and T-cell (cellular) immunity.

Mechanism

After intramuscular or intradermal injection, plasmid DNA enters myocytes and antigen-presenting cells. The antigen is expressed, processed, and presented on both MHC class I and class II molecules, mimicking natural infection without any possibility of the vaccine causing the disease. Unmethylated CpG motifs in the plasmid backbone act as a built-in adjuvant, engaging Toll-like receptor 9.

History

The approach grew out of experiments in the early 1990s, when Margaret Liu, Jeffrey Ulmer, and others showed that injecting plasmid DNA encoding influenza or HIV proteins could protect animals. Delivery methods developed to improve uptake include electroporation, gene guns, jet injectors, and lipid or polymer formulations.

Licensed products

Several DNA vaccines have been licensed for veterinary use, including a vaccine against West Nile virus in horses, one against infectious hematopoietic necrosis virus in salmon, and a therapeutic cancer vaccine for canine melanoma. In August 2021, India's ZyCoV-D became the first DNA vaccine authorised for human use, against COVID-19, delivered needle-free by jet injector.

Advantages and limitations

Advantages include rapid, sequence-only redesign, stability without a strict cold chain, and the inability to revert to virulence. Limitations in humans historically included weak immunogenicity of naked DNA and the need for delivery devices or multiple doses; theoretical concerns about integration into the host genome have not been borne out in studies.
