Letterboxing (filming)

Letterboxing is the practice of transferring film shot in a widescreen aspect ratio to standard-width video formats while preserving the film's original aspect ratio. The resulting image has mattes (black bars) above and below it; these mattes are part of the image, i.e. of each frame of the video signal.

Rationale

Most theatrical films are shot in aspect ratios of 1.85:1 or 2.39:1, wider than the 4:3 screens of early television. The alternatives to letterboxing are cropping the image or pan and scan, which lose parts of the picture. Letterboxing preserves the director's full composition at the cost of a smaller displayed image; the term refers to the shape of a letter box slot.

Related techniques

The vertical counterpart, used when a narrow image is shown on a wider screen, is pillarboxing. "Windowboxing" combines both. Non-anamorphic letterboxed releases on LaserDisc and early DVDs made efficient use of limited resolution compared with anamorphic transfers; modern 16:9 and wider displays have reduced the need for letterboxing except for the widest formats.

Home video history

Letterboxing became common with the rise of LaserDisc in the 1980s and 1990s, when enthusiasts demanded original aspect ratios, and was popularised by releases from the Criterion Collection. Early television broadcasts of films had almost always been pan-and-scanned instead.
