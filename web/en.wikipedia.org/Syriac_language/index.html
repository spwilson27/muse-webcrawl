Syriac language

Syriac
Native name: Leššānā Suryāyā
Family: Afro-Asiatic → Semitic → Aramaic
Era: 1st century AD – present (liturgical)
Scripts: Estrangela, Serto, Madnhaya

Syriac is a dialect of Aramaic that emerged in the 1st century AD from the region of Edessa (modern Şanlıurfa, Turkey). It became a major literary language throughout the Middle East and the principal language of Eastern Christianity, carrying a vast body of theological, philosophical, scientific and historical literature.

History

Classical Syriac flourished between the 4th and 8th centuries, used by writers such as Ephrem the Syrian and Jacob of Serugh. Syriac scholars translated Greek works into Syriac and later into Arabic, playing a key role in transmitting Greek learning. After the Mongol invasions the language declined as a vernacular in favour of Arabic, though Neo-Aramaic descendants such as Turoyo and Suret survive.

Script

Syriac is written right-to-left in three main script forms: the classical Estrangela, the western Serto and the eastern Madnhaya. The Syriac Bible translation, the Peshitta, remains its most influential text, and Syriac remains the liturgical language of churches including the Syriac Orthodox Church, the Church of the East and the Maronite Church.
