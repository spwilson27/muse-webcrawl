Domestic partnership

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A domestic partnership is a relationship between two people who live together and share a domestic life, but are not married to each other or to anyone else. In some jurisdictions, domestic partners receive legal recognition and certain rights and benefits comparable to those of married couples, such as hospital visitation, health-insurance coverage and inheritance rights. The term is used differently in different countries and regions: in some places it is an alternative to marriage open to all couples; in others it is a more limited status, historically created for same-sex couples before marriage equality.

History

Legal recognition of cohabiting couples developed in the late 20th century. Berkeley, California, is often credited with the first municipal domestic-partnership registry in the United States (1984), originally devised so that city employees' partners could receive benefits. Denmark created the world's first national "registered partnership" for same-sex couples in 1989. France introduced the civil solidarity pact (PACS) in 1999, open to same-sex and opposite-sex couples alike. Various US states, including California, Oregon, Washington, Nevada and Wisconsin, created domestic-partnership laws, some granting nearly all state-level rights of marriage.

Forms and rights

Depending on the jurisdiction, a domestic partnership may be created by registering with a city, county or state authority, sometimes after living together for a specified period. Benefits commonly conferred include:

  • Family and bereavement leave, and hospital visitation rights
  • Eligibility for a partner's employer-provided health insurance
  • Rights in medical decision-making and inheritance in the absence of a will
  • In some jurisdictions, community-property and support obligations on dissolution

Domestic partnerships are generally not recognised at the federal level in the United States, so partners do not receive federal marital benefits such as Social Security survivorship or immigration sponsorship.

Relationship to marriage

Since the legalisation of same-sex marriage across the United States (Obergefell v. Hodges, 2015) and in many other countries, domestic-partnership registries remain available in many places and are chosen by some couples who prefer a status with fewer obligations, or who wish to avoid marriage for personal or financial reasons. Some employers and municipalities have phased out domestic-partner benefits now that marriage is available to all, while others retain them.
