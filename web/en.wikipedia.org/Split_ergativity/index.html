Split ergativity

Split systems use different alignments in different grammatical contexts

Split ergativity is a phenomenon in linguistic typology in which a language displays ergative–absolutive morphosyntactic alignment in some contexts and nominative–accusative alignment in others. Such "splits" are conditioned by factors such as tense and aspect, the person or animacy of the participants, or the type of clause.

Types of split

In an aspect-based split, common in Indo-Iranian languages such as Hindi-Urdu and Pashto, perfective clauses follow an ergative pattern (the transitive subject takes ergative marking and the verb agrees with the object), while imperfective clauses are nominative–accusative. In a person-based split, found in many Australian Aboriginal languages such as Dyirbal, first- and second-person pronouns behave accusatively while third-person nouns behave ergatively, reflecting the nominal hierarchy (speech-act participants outrank other referents). Splits may also be conditioned by clause type, with subordinate or nominalised clauses patterned differently from main clauses.

Examples

Hindi-Urdu: in the perfective, a transitive subject is marked with the ergative postposition ne and the verb agrees with the unmarked object; in the imperfective the subject is unmarked and the verb agrees with it. Georgian shows a split by tense series: present-series tenses are accusative, the aorist series ergative, and the perfect series inverted. Mayan languages show aspect-conditioned splits within their verbal agreement systems.

Analysis

Linguists have analysed splits as arising from different sources of case and agreement in different clause types, and historically from the reanalysis of passive or possessive constructions in perfective contexts as active ergative clauses. Split ergativity is a central topic in theories of case and alignment typology.
