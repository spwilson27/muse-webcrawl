Hot spring

A hot spring is produced by geothermally heated groundwater emerging at the surface.

A hot spring, hydrothermal spring, or geothermal spring is a spring produced by the emergence of geothermally heated groundwater onto the surface of the Earth. The groundwater is heated either by shallow bodies of magma or by circulation through faults to hot rock deep in the crust. Hot springs are found on every continent and around many volcanic regions, notably the Yellowstone caldera, Iceland, Japan, and New Zealand.

Formation

Rainwater and groundwater percolate downward through cracks and porous rock. At depth, the water is heated by the geothermal gradient or by contact with magma chambers. The heated, less dense water rises back to the surface through fissures, forming springs, geysers, fumaroles, and mud pots depending on temperature and pressure conditions.

Chemistry and life

Hot spring water typically carries dissolved minerals such as silica, sulfur, calcium, and lithium. The colorful mats around many springs are colonies of thermophilic bacteria and algae that thrive at temperatures lethal to most life. Studies of heat-loving enzymes from these organisms, such as Taq polymerase from Thermus aquaticus, have had major applications in biotechnology.

Human use

People have bathed in hot springs for millennia, believing the mineral waters have therapeutic properties. Japan's onsen, Roman baths, and spa towns such as Bath in England and Baden-Baden in Germany were built around them. Wild hot springs can be hazardous: water may be scalding, acidic, or harbor pathogens such as Naegleria fowleri.
