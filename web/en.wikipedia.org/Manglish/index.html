Manglish

Manglish is an informal name for the variety of colloquial English spoken in Malaysia (and, in closely related form, Singapore, where it overlaps with Singlish). It is characterised by heavy influence from Malay, the national language, as well as from Chinese languages (especially Hokkien, Cantonese, and Mandarin), Tamil, and other languages spoken in Malaysia. Its exact form varies by the speaker's ethnic and linguistic background.

Features

Manglish differs from standard Malaysian English — the acrolect used in formal writing, broadcasting, and education — in vocabulary, grammar, pronunciation, and, above all, its discourse particles.

  • Particles: Sentences are frequently coloured by particles such as lah (the most famous, used for emphasis, reassurance, or exasperation), mah, meh (questioning surprise), lor, hor, and kan (borrowed from Malay for "isn't it?").
  • Vocabulary: Loanwords from Malay include makan (eat), jalan (walk/travel), tapau (takeaway food, from Cantonese), kena (to be subjected to something adverse), and boleh (can/able).
  • Grammar: Manglish often drops pronouns and auxiliary verbs, uses "already" where standard English uses perfect aspect ("I eat already"), topicalises sentences ("This one cannot lah"), and uses "got" for existential "there is" ("Got sale or not?").
  • Reduplication and shortenings: Words are repeated for emphasis or familiarity ("hot hot", "same same"), and phrases are clipped ("How come?" for "Why is that?").

Usage and attitudes

Manglish is the language of everyday informal interaction across ethnic lines in Malaysia, used in markets, kopitiams (coffee shops), among friends, and increasingly in advertising, social media, comedy, and film, where it functions as a badge of shared Malaysian identity. At the same time, governments and educators periodically campaign for standard English, worrying that Manglish habits may hinder proficiency; debates about it recur in Malaysian public life, much like the debates about Singlish in Singapore.

Linguists generally describe Manglish not as "broken English" but as a rule-governed contact variety — a basilect on a continuum with standard Malaysian English — reflecting Malaysia's multilingual history under Portuguese, Dutch, and British influence and its post-independence language policies.
