Latin War

The Latin War (340–338 BC) was a conflict between the Roman Republic and its neighbours, the Latin League, an alliance of Latin cities that had long been Rome's partners under the Foedus Cassianum of 493 BC. The war ended in a decisive Roman victory and the dissolution of the Latin League, establishing Rome as the dominant power in Latium and laying the foundations of Roman expansion in Italy.

Causes

Tensions grew as Rome increasingly treated the Latins as subordinates rather than allies. According to Livy, Latin envoys demanded that one of the two consuls and half the Senate be chosen from the Latins; when the demand was contemptuously rejected in 341 BC, war followed. The Latins were joined by the Volsci, the Campanians of Capua, and other peoples of central Italy, while Rome was supported by the Samnites under their treaty of 354 BC.

Course of the war

The Romans won the Battle of Vesuvius (340 BC), famous for the self-sacrifice (devotio) of the consul Publius Decius Mus and for Titus Manlius Torquatus's execution of his own son for disobeying orders. A further victory at Trifanum and successful campaigns in 339–338 BC, culminating in a naval victory at Antium, broke Latin resistance. Ships' prows (rostra) captured at Antium were mounted in the Roman Forum.

Settlement

The settlement of 338 BC dissolved the Latin League and dealt with each city individually: some, such as Lanuvium, Aricia, and Tusculum, received full Roman citizenship; others received citizenship without the vote (civitas sine suffragio) or retained a subordinate alliance. The Latins were forbidden to hold common councils or to intermarry and trade across city boundaries. This flexible system of graded statuses became a model for Rome's organisation of Italy.
