Horner's syndrome

SpecialtyNeurology, ophthalmology
SymptomsMiosis, ptosis, anhidrosis
CausesDamage to the sympathetic trunk

Horner's syndrome, also known as oculosympathetic paresis, is a combination of symptoms that arises when a group of nerves known as the sympathetic trunk is damaged. The signs are found on the same side as the lesion and classically consist of a constricted pupil (miosis), a drooping upper eyelid (ptosis), sinking of the eyeball into the socket (enophthalmos), and decreased sweating (anhidrosis) on the affected side of the face.

Signs and symptoms

In addition to the classic triad, affected people may show dilation lag of the pupil in darkness, a slightly elevated lower lid ("upside-down ptosis"), and apparent enophthalmos. In children, Horner's syndrome can cause lighter iris colour (heterochromia) on the affected side.

Causes

Causes are divided according to which neuron in the three-neuron oculosympathetic pathway is involved. First-order (central) lesions include stroke and multiple sclerosis; second-order (preganglionic) lesions include Pancoast tumours of the lung apex and carotid artery dissection may be mimicked in the third-order group; third-order (postganglionic) lesions include cluster headache and cavernous sinus pathology. In children, birth trauma and neuroblastoma are important causes.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is aided by pharmacological testing with cocaine or apraclonidine eye drops, which produce characteristic pupil responses. Imaging is directed at finding the underlying lesion. Treatment addresses the cause; the syndrome itself may require no specific therapy, though ptosis can be corrected surgically. The condition is named after Swiss ophthalmologist Johann Friedrich Horner, who described it in 1869.
