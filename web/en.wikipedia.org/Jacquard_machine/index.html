Jacquard machine

The Jacquard machine (French: [ʒakaʁ]) is a device fitted to a loom that simplifies the process of manufacturing textiles with complex patterns. The resulting ensemble of the loom and Jacquard machine is called a Jacquard loom. The machine was patented by Joseph Marie Jacquard in 1804, based on earlier inventions by the Frenchmen Basile Bouchon (1725), Jean-Baptiste Falcon (1728), and Jacques Vaucanson (1740).

Operation

The Jacquard machine is controlled by a "chain of cards": a number of punched cards laced together into a continuous sequence. Multiple rows of holes are punched on each card, with one complete card corresponding to one row of the design. Each position on the card corresponds to a hook in the machine; where a hole is punched, the hook can pass through and lift its thread, and where the card is blank, the hook is pressed back and the thread is not lifted. The pattern of lifted and unlifted warp threads produces the woven design.

Significance

The machine made it possible for individual weavers to produce elaborate patterned fabrics—such as brocade and damask—that had previously required the labour of a draw boy as well as a weaver. It is an important predecessor of computing: the Jacquard punched cards inspired scholars such as Charles Babbage, who planned to use punched cards to control his Analytical Engine, and Herman Hollerith, whose punched-card tabulating machines were used in the 1890 United States census.

Legacy

Jacquard looms remained the major method of controlling figured weaving until electronic control systems displaced them in the late twentieth century. The term "Jacquard" is not specific to any particular loom, but rather refers to the added control mechanism that automates patterning.
