In molecular biology, a transcription factor (TF) is a protein that controls the rate of transcription of genetic information from DNA to messenger RNA by binding to a specific DNA sequence. Transcription factors work alone or in complexes to recruit or block RNA polymerase, thereby switching genes on or off in particular cells and times.

Structure

Transcription factors typically contain a DNA-binding domain — common motifs include the helix-turn-helix, zinc finger, leucine zipper, and helix-loop-helix — plus a transactivation domain that interacts with other proteins, and often a signal-sensing domain responsive to hormones or other cues.

Classes and roles

General transcription factors are required at nearly all promoters for assembling the initiation complex, while sequence-specific factors bind enhancers and promoters of particular genes. Master regulators such as the Hox proteins pattern the body plan in development, and pioneer factors can open closed chromatin. The human genome encodes roughly 1,600 transcription factors.

Medical significance

Mutations in transcription factors cause many developmental disorders and cancers: p53, itself a transcription factor, is the most frequently mutated gene in human cancer. Several factors are also drug targets, and the Yamanaka factors (Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, c-Myc) can reprogram adult cells into induced pluripotent stem cells.
