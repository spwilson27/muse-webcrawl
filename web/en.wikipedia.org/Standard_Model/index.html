Standard Model

The Standard Model of particle physics is the theory describing three of the four known fundamental forces (electromagnetism, the weak interaction, and the strong interaction) and classifying all known elementary particles. It was developed in stages throughout the latter half of the 20th century.

Contents of the theory

The Standard Model describes matter as composed of fermions: six quarks (up, down, charm, strange, top, bottom) and six leptons (electron, muon, tau, and their neutrinos), arranged in three generations. Forces are mediated by gauge bosons: the photon (electromagnetism), gluons (strong force), and the W and Z bosons (weak force). The Higgs boson, discovered at CERN in 2012, arises from the mechanism that gives particles mass.

Successes

The theory has passed an enormous range of experimental tests. It predicted the existence of the W and Z bosons, the top quark, and the Higgs boson before their discovery, and its predictions — for example for the anomalous magnetic moment of the electron — agree with measurement to extraordinary precision.

Limitations

The Standard Model does not incorporate gravity, does not explain the observed matter–antimatter asymmetry, provides no candidate for dark matter, and in its original form required neutrinos to be massless, contrary to oscillation experiments. It also contains many free parameters. These gaps motivate research into physics beyond the Standard Model, including supersymmetry and grand unified theories.
