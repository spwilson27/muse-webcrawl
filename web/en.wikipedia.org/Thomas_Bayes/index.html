Thomas Bayes (c. 1701 – 7 April 1761) was an English statistician, philosopher and Presbyterian minister, best known for formulating a specific case of the theorem that bears his name — Bayes' theorem — published posthumously in 1763.

Life

The son of a Nonconformist minister, Bayes studied logic and theology at the University of Edinburgh and served as minister of the Mount Sion chapel in Tunbridge Wells from 1733 until his death. He was elected a Fellow of the Royal Society in 1742, apparently on the strength of a defence of Newton's calculus ("An Introduction to the Doctrine of Fluxions"), and published little else in his lifetime.

Bayes' theorem

His essay "An Essay towards solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances" was sent to the Royal Society by his friend Richard Price after Bayes's death. It addresses inverse probability: how to update the probability of a hypothesis as evidence accumulates, expressed as P(A|B) = P(B|A)P(A)/P(B). Developed further by Laplace and revived in the 20th century, Bayesian statistics became foundational to fields from machine learning and spam filtering to medical testing and forensic science.
