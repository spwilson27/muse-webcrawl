Tomáš Masaryk

Tomáš Masaryk, full name Tomáš Garrigue Masaryk (7 March 1850 – 14 September 1937), was the founder and first president of Czechoslovakia. A Moravian-born philosopher and politician, he led the movement for Czechoslovak independence during the First World War and headed the new state from 1918 to 1935. Summary

Masaryk grew up in modest circumstances in southern Moravia, worked his way through gymnasium and university in Vienna and Leipzig, and in 1882 became a professor of philosophy in Prague. He entered politics as a deputy to the Austrian parliament, became known for defending historical truth against forged national manuscripts and for opposing antisemitism in the Hilsner trial, and at the outbreak of the First World War committed himself to the destruction of Austria-Hungary and the creation of a joint Czech and Slovak state.

Abroad he founded the Czechoslovak National Council, raised the Czechoslovak Legions, and won Allied recognition — capped by the support of US President Woodrow Wilson and the declaration of independence in October 1918. As president for seventeen years, Masaryk embodied the democratic, humanist character of the First Czechoslovak Republic; his maxim "Truth prevails" is still the motto of the Czech president. He died at Lány Castle in 1937, a year and a half before the Munich Agreement dismantled the republic he had built. His son Jan Masaryk later served as Czechoslovak foreign minister.
