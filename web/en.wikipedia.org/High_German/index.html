High German

High German (German: Hochdeutsch, pronounced [ˈhoːxˌdɔɪ̯tʃ]) is a family of dialects forming the southern part of the West Germanic dialect continuum, distinguished from Low German by the High German consonant shift. "High" refers to the upland, hilly terrain of central and southern Germany, Austria and Switzerland, in contrast to the flat "low" north. Standard High German (Standarddeutsch), based largely on East Central German varieties as shaped by Martin Luther's Bible translation and the chanceries, is the standard written and official language of Germany, Austria, Switzerland and Liechtenstein.

Classification

High German is divided into Central German (including Ripuarian, Moselle Franconian, Hessian, Thuringian and Upper Saxon dialects, and Luxembourgish) and Upper German (including Alemannic — among it Swiss German — Bavarian and East Franconian). Yiddish developed from Middle High German. The Benrath line (machen/maken isogloss) separates High German from Low German, while the Speyer line divides Central from Upper German.

History

Historical stages are Old High German (c. 750–1050), Middle High German (c. 1050–1350), Early New High German (c. 1350–1650) and modern New High German. The High German consonant shift, which occurred roughly between the 3rd and 8th centuries, changed voiceless plosives such as p, t, k into affricates and fricatives (compare English apple with German Apfel).
