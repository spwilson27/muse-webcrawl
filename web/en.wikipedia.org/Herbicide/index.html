Herbicides (US: /ˈɜːrbɪsaɪdz/, UK: /ˈhɜːr-/), also commonly known as weed killers, are substances used to control undesired plants, also known as weeds. Selective herbicides control specific weed species while leaving the desired crop relatively unharmed, whereas non-selective herbicides (sometimes called "total weed killers") kill most plant material they contact. Herbicides are the most widely used class of pesticides in modern agriculture.

History

Before chemical herbicides, weeds were controlled by cultivation and hand-pulling. Inorganic compounds such as sodium chlorate and sulfuric acid were used from the late 19th century. The modern era began in the 1940s with the discovery of the synthetic auxin 2,4-D, the first selective herbicide, which killed broadleaf weeds in cereals. In the 1950s and 1960s came the triazines (atrazine) and ureas; paraquat followed, and in 1974 Monsanto introduced glyphosate (Roundup), a broad-spectrum systemic herbicide that became the world's most heavily used. From 1996, herbicide-tolerant genetically modified crops (notably "Roundup Ready" soybeans) transformed weed management on tens of millions of hectares.

Mode of action

Herbicides act by disrupting processes unique or vital to plants: inhibiting photosynthesis (e.g. atrazine at photosystem II), blocking amino-acid synthesis (glyphosate at EPSPS; sulfonylureas at ALS), mimicking growth hormones (2,4-D and other synthetic auxins cause uncontrolled growth), or destroying cell membranes (paraquat). The Herbicide Resistance Action Committee classifies herbicides by these modes of action to guide rotation and stewardship.

Application and timing

Products may be applied pre-emergence (before weeds sprout) or post-emergence, to soil or foliage, by ground sprayers, aerial application, or wick applicators. Effectiveness depends on weed species, growth stage, weather, and formulation, including surfactants that help droplets spread and penetrate leaves.

Resistance and environmental issues

Repeated use of the same mode of action has selected for herbicide-resistant weed populations; hundreds of resistant weed species are documented, including glyphosate-resistant Palmer amaranth and blackgrass resistant to multiple chemistries. Management relies on rotating modes of action, mixtures, and integrated weed management combining cultural and mechanical methods. Environmental concerns include spray drift (notably with dicamba), runoff into waterways, and effects on non-target plants and ecosystems; the health effects of some herbicides, including glyphosate and atrazine, remain subjects of scientific and legal debate.
