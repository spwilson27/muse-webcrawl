The Belmont Report, formally titled Ethical Principles and Guidelines for the Protection of Human Subjects of Research, is a landmark 1979 report of the United States National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects of Biomedical and Behavioral Research. It is named after the Belmont Conference Center in Maryland, where the commission drafted it in 1976.

Background

The commission was created by the National Research Act of 1974, passed in the wake of public outrage over research abuses — above all the Tuskegee Syphilis Study, in which treatment was withheld from African-American men for decades. The commission was charged with identifying the basic ethical principles that should govern research involving human subjects in the United States.

The three principles

  • Respect for persons — individuals should be treated as autonomous agents, and those with diminished autonomy (such as children or prisoners) are entitled to additional protection. Its main application is informed consent.
  • Beneficence — researchers must not harm subjects and must maximise possible benefits while minimising possible harms, applied through systematic assessment of risks and benefits.
  • Justice — the burdens and benefits of research must be distributed fairly, applied in the selection of subjects, so that vulnerable groups are not exploited and benefits are not confined to the advantaged.

Influence

The Belmont Report shaped the US federal regulations for human-subjects research (the "Common Rule") and the practice of Institutional Review Boards (IRBs). Its three principles remain the standard framework taught in research ethics worldwide.
