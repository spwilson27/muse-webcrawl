Spindle apparatus

In cell biology, the spindle apparatus (or mitotic spindle) is the cytoskeletal structure of eukaryotic cells that forms during cell division to separate sister chromatids between daughter cells. In mitosis it is called the mitotic spindle, and in meiosis the meiotic spindle. It is composed chiefly of microtubules nucleated from centrosomes at opposite poles of the cell.

Structure

The spindle contains three classes of microtubules: kinetochore microtubules, which attach to chromosomes at their kinetochores; polar microtubules, which interdigitate from opposite poles; and astral microtubules, which anchor the poles to the cell cortex. Motor proteins such as kinesins and dynein organise and move the microtubules.

Function

During prometaphase and metaphase, chromosomes are captured and aligned at the metaphase plate. At anaphase, kinetochore microtubules shorten, pulling sister chromatids apart, while polar microtubules lengthen, pushing the poles apart. The spindle assembly checkpoint ensures every chromosome is properly attached before division proceeds.

Clinical relevance

Spindle poisons such as taxanes and vinca alkaloids disrupt microtubule dynamics and are used as chemotherapy agents against rapidly dividing cancer cells.
