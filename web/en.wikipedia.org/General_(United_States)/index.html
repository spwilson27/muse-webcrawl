General (United States)

In the United States Armed Forces, a general is a four-star general officer rank in the United States Army, Marine Corps, Air Force, and Space Force, designated by the pay grade O-10. It is the highest rank normally attainable in peacetime service, above lieutenant general (three stars) and below the wartime five-star ranks of General of the Army and General of the Air Force. The naval equivalent is admiral.

Appointment and authority

United States law strictly limits the number of four-star officers on active duty at any time — a total of a few dozen across the services, tied to specific positions such as the chiefs of staff of the services, the chairman and vice chairman of the Joint Chiefs of Staff, and the commanders of the unified combatant commands. Generals are nominated by the President and confirmed by the Senate, and by law they retire at the end of their assignment or by age 64 unless waived, reverting to a lower permanent rank if not reappointed. Pay for O-10 is capped by statute at the Executive Schedule level.

History

The rank of general in the U.S. Army dates to the Civil War era: Ulysses S. Grant was appointed the first full general (then called lieutenant general, later general) in 1864–1866, followed by William Tecumseh Sherman and Philip Sheridan. The rank lapsed and was revived, and during the Second World War the five-star ranks of General of the Army and Fleet Admiral were created for the most senior commanders. No officer has held five-star rank since Omar Bradley, who died in 1981; the rank of General of the Armies, held by John J. Pershing and retroactively by George Washington and Ulysses S. Grant, ranks above it.

Insignia

A United States general wears four silver stars as insignia of rank. The term "general" is also used generically for all general officers (brigadier general through general), who are collectively called flag officers in the naval services.
