The astronomical unit (symbol: au) is a unit of length defined as exactly 149,597,870,700 metres, approximately the mean distance between the Earth and the Sun. It is used primarily for measuring distances within the Solar System or around other stars.

History

The Earth–Sun distance was estimated in antiquity by Aristarchus of Samos and more accurately by Christiaan Huygens and, from transits of Venus in 1761 and 1769, by astronomers including James Cook's expedition observers. Radar ranging to Venus in the 20th century refined the value. Historically the au was defined via the Gaussian gravitational constant; in 2012 the International Astronomical Union redefined it as an exact number of metres, making it a conventional unit independent of the Sun's changing mass.

Usage

Planetary distances are conventionally given in au: Mercury orbits at ~0.39 au, Jupiter at ~5.2 au, Neptune at ~30 au. Larger scales use the light-year and parsec (1 parsec ≈ 206,265 au).
