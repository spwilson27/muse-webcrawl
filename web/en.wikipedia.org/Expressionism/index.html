Expressionism

Expressionism is a modernist movement, initially in poetry and painting, originating in Northern Europe around the beginning of the 20th century. Its typical trait is to present the world solely from a subjective perspective, distorting it radically for emotional effect in order to evoke moods or ideas. Expressionist artists sought to express the meaning of emotional experience rather than physical reality.

Origins

The movement developed as an avant-garde style before the First World War, particularly in Germany. Groups such as Die Brücke (founded in Dresden in 1905 by Ernst Ludwig Kirchner, Fritz Bleyl, Erich Heckel, and Karl Schmidt-Rottluff) and Der Blaue Reiter (founded in Munich in 1911 by Wassily Kandinsky and Franz Marc) were central to its development. Important expressionist painters include Edvard Munch, whose The Scream (1893) anticipated the movement, Egon Schiele, Oskar Kokoschka, Emil Nolde, and Käthe Kollwitz.

Characteristics

Expressionist works often feature exaggerated distortion, angular forms, jarring contrasts, and intense, non-naturalistic color. The movement extended beyond painting into architecture, literature, theatre, dance, and cinema; German Expressionist cinema produced landmarks such as The Cabinet of Dr. Caligari (1920) and Nosferatu (1922).

Legacy

Expressionism influenced later movements including abstract expressionism, neo-expressionism, and the work of the British School of London painters such as Francis Bacon and Lucian Freud. In 1937, the Nazi regime condemned expressionist art as "degenerate" in the infamous Entartete Kunst exhibition in Munich.
