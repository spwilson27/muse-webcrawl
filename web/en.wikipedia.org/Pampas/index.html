Pampas

The Pampas (from the Quechua pampa, meaning "plain") are fertile South American low grasslands that cover more than 1,200,000 square kilometres and include the Argentine provinces of Buenos Aires, La Pampa, Santa Fe, Entre Ríos and Córdoba, all of Uruguay, and the southernmost Brazilian state of Rio Grande do Sul.

Geography and climate

The region is a vast, nearly featureless plain sloping gently from the Andes foothills to the Atlantic Ocean, interrupted only by the low Ventana and Tandil hills. The climate is temperate and humid in the east (the "Humid Pampa"), becoming drier toward the west (the "Dry Pampa"). Rainfall ranges from about 1,200 mm in the east to under 500 mm at the western margins.

Wildlife

Native wildlife includes the pampas deer, the greater rhea, the pampas fox, the maned wolf, armadillos, and the Geoffroy's cat. The grasslands were historically grazed by large herds and are an important habitat for grassland birds.

Economy and culture

The deep, rich soils make the Pampas one of the world's great agricultural regions, producing wheat, maize, soybeans and beef cattle. The cattle culture gave rise to the gaucho, the nomadic horseman who became a national symbol of Argentina and Uruguay. From the late 19th century, mass European immigration transformed the region, and the capital cities of Buenos Aires and Montevideo grew as ports exporting its produce.
