Beers Criteria

The Beers Criteria (also called the Beers List) is a guideline for healthcare professionals to help improve the safety of prescribing medications for older adults. First published by geriatrician Dr. Mark H. Beers and colleagues in 1991, the criteria provide an explicit list of prescription drugs that may be inappropriate for use in older adults because of an unfavorable balance of risks and benefits relative to alternative treatments, or because their effectiveness is questionable in this population.

History

Beers originally developed the criteria while at the RAND Corporation and the University of California, Los Angeles, publishing the first version in the Archives of Internal Medicine in 1991. The list was initially designed to identify inappropriate medications for nursing home residents. Subsequent revisions extended the criteria to all adults aged 65 and older regardless of care setting. After Dr. Beers's death in 2009, stewardship of the criteria passed to the American Geriatrics Society (AGS), which has published updates in 2012, 2015, 2019, and 2023.

Contents

The AGS Beers Criteria organize potentially inappropriate medication (PIM) use into five broad categories:

  • Potentially inappropriate medications in most older adults (independent of diagnoses)
  • Potentially inappropriate medications due to drug-disease or drug-syndrome interactions
  • Medications to be used with caution in older adults
  • Clinically important potentially harmful drug-drug interactions
  • Medications whose dosages should be adjusted based on kidney function

Common classes on the list include strongly anticholinergic drugs, certain benzodiazepines and nonbenzodiazepine hypnotics ("Z-drugs"), long-acting sulfonylureas such as glyburide, meperidine, sliding-scale insulin, and nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs used chronically without gastroprotection.

Clinical use and limitations

The Beers Criteria are widely used in clinical practice, pharmacy benefit management, quality measurement, and regulatory oversight of nursing homes in the United States. They are intended as a guide rather than an absolute prohibition: the AGS emphasizes that a medication's inclusion does not mean it should never be used, and that clinical judgment, patient preference, and goals of care remain paramount.
