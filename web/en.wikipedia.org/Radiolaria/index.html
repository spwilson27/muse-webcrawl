Radiolaria

Radiolarian skeletons illustrated by Ernst Haeckel, 1904.

The Radiolaria, also called Radiozoa, are single-celled eukaryotic organisms (protozoa) found throughout the world's oceans as zooplankton. They are famous for producing intricate mineral skeletons, usually made of silica (opal), with radially symmetric forms pierced by fine pseudopodia called axopodia, which aid buoyancy and capture of prey such as bacteria and small plankton. Individual cells are typically 0.1–0.2 mm in diameter.

Ecology and fossil record

Many radiolarians harbour symbiotic algae and thus live in sunlit surface waters. When they die their siliceous tests sink and accumulate as radiolarian ooze on deep ocean floors; lithified, this forms radiolarite (chert). Radiolarians have an excellent fossil record extending back to the Cambrian period, making them valuable index fossils for dating marine sediments and studying past oceans.

In art

Ernst Haeckel's exquisite plates of radiolarians in Kunstformen der Natur (1904) influenced Art Nouveau design and remain icons of the beauty of microscopic life.
