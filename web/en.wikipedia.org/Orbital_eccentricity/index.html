Orbital eccentricity

In astrodynamics, the orbital eccentricity of an astronomical object is a dimensionless parameter that determines the amount by which its orbit around another body deviates from a perfect circle. A value of 0 is a circular orbit, values between 0 and 1 form an elliptic orbit, 1 is a parabolic escape orbit (or capture orbit), and greater than 1 is a hyperbola. The term derives its name from the parameters of conic sections, as every Kepler orbit is a conic section.

Definition

For an ellipse with semi-major axis a and semi-minor axis b, the eccentricity e is given by e = √(1 − b²/a²). Equivalently, e = (ra − rp)/(ra + rp), where ra is the apoapsis distance and rp is the periapsis distance. Eccentricity can also be computed from the orbital state vectors using the eccentricity vector.

Examples

Earth's orbit has an eccentricity of about 0.0167, so it is nearly circular. Venus and Neptune have even lower eccentricities, while Mercury's orbit, at about 0.2056, is the most eccentric of the eight planets. Some comets have eccentricities very close to 1; Halley's Comet has an eccentricity of about 0.967. The Moon's orbit around Earth has an eccentricity of about 0.0549. Interstellar objects such as ʻOumuamua have been observed on hyperbolic trajectories with eccentricities above 1.

Climatic effect

Variations in Earth's orbital eccentricity over cycles of roughly 100,000 and 400,000 years form one of the Milankovitch cycles. Although eccentricity changes the total annual insolation only slightly, it modulates the precession-driven contrast between seasons by changing the Earth–Sun distance at perihelion and aphelion, and so contributes to the timing of ice ages.
