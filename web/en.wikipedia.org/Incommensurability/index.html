Incommensurability is the absence of a common measure or standard by which two things can be compared. The term originates in ancient Greek mathematics, where it described magnitudes — most famously the side and diagonal of a square — whose ratio cannot be expressed as a ratio of whole numbers; it was later adopted in the philosophy of science for the claim that competing scientific paradigms cannot be fully compared by a neutral standard, and in ethics for values that cannot be reduced to a single scale.

In mathematics

Two lengths are commensurable if some unit measures each a whole number of times; otherwise they are incommensurable. The discovery — attributed to the Pythagoreans and sometimes to Hippasus — that the diagonal of a unit square (√2) is incommensurable with its side was a foundational shock, proving the existence of irrational numbers and refuting the doctrine that "all is number". Eudoxus' theory of proportion, preserved in Book V of Euclid's Elements, gave Greek mathematics a rigorous way to handle such magnitudes without a number system for irrationals.

In philosophy of science

Thomas Kuhn and Paul Feyerabend independently introduced incommensurability in 1962 to characterise relations between successive scientific paradigms. On their accounts, rival theories may use the same words ("mass", "planet") with different meanings, disagree about which problems matter and what counts as a solution, and perceive the world differently — so no neutral, theory-independent measure ranks them point by point. Critics objected that this implied irrational theory choice; Kuhn replied that proponents of different paradigms can still give reasons and persuade, and later restricted the claim to "local" incommensurability between parts of the theories' vocabularies, comparing translation between languages rather than mutual incomprehension.

In ethics

In value theory, incommensurability denotes goods — such as friendship and money, or liberty and equality — that cannot be measured on a single cardinal scale or traded off without remainder. Isaiah Berlin's value pluralism and Joseph Raz's work develop the idea; it is distinguished from mere incomparability, since incommensurable options may still be rationally chosen between.
