Edmond Albius

Edmond Albius (1829 – 9 August 1880) was a Réunionnais horticulturalist. Born into slavery on the island of Réunion, he discovered at the age of twelve the practical method of hand-pollinating vanilla orchids, a technique that made possible the global vanilla industry and remains in use today.

Life and discovery

Albius was born enslaved in 1829 in Sainte-Suzanne, Réunion (then Île Bourbon). Orphaned young, he was raised and educated in botany by his enslaver, Féréol Bellier Beaumont, on whose estate he worked. Vanilla had been introduced to the island from Mexico, but the plants rarely set fruit because the orchid's natural pollinator, the melipona bee, was absent, and European methods of hand-pollination developed in the 1830s were slow and impractical.

In 1841, at about twelve years old, Albius devised a quick technique that could be performed with a thin stick or blade of grass: lifting the rostellum — the flap separating the male anther from the female stigma — and folding it back so the pollinia could be pressed against the stigma with a simple gesture of the thumb. The method, called le mariage ("the marriage") on Réunion, allowed a worker to pollinate thousands of flowers in a day.

Impact

Albius's technique transformed Réunion into the world's leading vanilla producer and spread to Madagascar, which with the Comoros and other Indian Ocean islands still produces most of the world's vanilla. The technique is essentially unchanged today: nearly all cultivated vanilla worldwide is pollinated by hand using his method.

Later life and recognition

Slavery was abolished in the French colonies in 1848, but Albius received neither payment nor official recognition for his discovery during his lifetime; others, including botanists who had published different pollination methods, were sometimes wrongly credited. He later worked as a kitchen servant in Saint-Denis, was at one point wrongly convicted and imprisoned for theft before being pardoned, and died in poverty in 1880. In the twentieth century his achievement was belatedly recognized on Réunion, where statues and memorials now commemorate him.
