Gastroesophageal reflux disease

Gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD or GORD) is a chronic condition in which stomach contents rise into the oesophagus, causing symptoms such as heartburn and acid regurgitation, and sometimes injuring the oesophageal lining. Occasional reflux is common and harmless; GERD denotes reflux that is frequent, troublesome, or complicated.

Signs and symptoms

Typical symptoms are heartburn (a burning sensation behind the breastbone, worse after meals or when lying down) and regurgitation of sour fluid. Others may experience difficulty swallowing, chest pain, chronic cough, hoarseness, or a sensation of a lump in the throat. Complications include erosive oesophagitis, strictures, Barrett's oesophagus (a precancerous change), and, rarely, oesophageal adenocarcinoma.

Causes and mechanism

GERD arises when the lower oesophageal sphincter relaxes inappropriately or other defences fail, allowing acidic stomach contents to contact the oesophagus. Contributing factors include obesity, hiatal hernia, pregnancy, smoking, delayed gastric emptying, and certain foods and medications.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually clinical; endoscopy, 24-hour pH monitoring, and manometry are used when symptoms are atypical or refractory. Management begins with lifestyle measures — weight loss, smaller evening meals, elevating the head of the bed, and avoiding triggers — and acid-suppressing drugs, especially proton pump inhibitors (PPIs). Antireflux surgery, such as fundoplication, is an option for selected patients.
