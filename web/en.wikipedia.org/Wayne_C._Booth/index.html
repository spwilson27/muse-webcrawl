Wayne C. Booth

Wayne Clayson Booth (February 22, 1921 – October 10, 2005) was an American literary critic and professor of English at the University of Chicago. He is best known for The Rhetoric of Fiction (1961), a landmark study of narrative technique that introduced or popularized the concepts of the implied author and the unreliable narrator.

Life and career

Born: February 22, 1921, American Fork, Utah
Died: October 10, 2005, Chicago, Illinois
Occupation: Literary critic, professor
Notable work: The Rhetoric of Fiction (1961)

Booth was born in American Fork, Utah, and educated at Brigham Young University and the University of Chicago, where he completed his doctorate. After teaching at Haverford College and Earlham College, he returned to Chicago, where he spent most of his career and became the George M. Pullman Professor of English.

Work

In The Rhetoric of Fiction, Booth argued that every narrative embodies the rhetorical choices of an "implied author"—the sensibility a reader reconstructs behind the text—distinct from both the real author and the narrator. His analysis of narration distinguished reliable from unreliable narrators according to how far a narrator's norms depart from those of the implied author, a framework that became foundational for narratology.

Later works include A Rhetoric of Irony (1974), Critical Understanding (1979), and The Company We Keep: An Ethics of Fiction (1988), in which he developed an ethical criticism treating books as companions whose company shapes their readers.

Legacy

Booth's terminology remains standard in the study of narrative. The University of Chicago's journal Critical Inquiry, which he helped to shape, is among the leading venues in literary theory.
