Egyptian temple

Egyptian temples were built for the official worship of the gods and in commemoration of the pharaohs in ancient Egypt and regions under Egyptian control. Temples were seen as houses for the gods or kings to whom they were dedicated. Within them, the Egyptians performed a variety of rituals, the central functions of Egyptian religion: giving offerings to the gods, reenacting their mythological interactions through festivals, and warding off the forces of chaos. Many temples survive today, including the great complexes at Karnak, Luxor, Philae, Edfu, and Abu Simbel.

Functions

Temples were central to the Egyptian conception of the cosmos. They were believed to be the dwelling places of the gods on earth, and their architecture embodied the ordered world (maat) surrounded by the chaotic forces outside. Temple rituals, performed daily by priests on behalf of the king, were thought to sustain the gods and thereby maintain cosmic order. Major festivals, such as the Opet Festival at Thebes, carried the god's image in procession between temples, allowing the populace — who were normally excluded from the inner sanctum — to glimpse the deity.

Layout and architecture

The classic Egyptian temple followed an axis from the entrance to the innermost sanctuary, with spaces becoming progressively darker, smaller, and more sacred. Its elements typically included:

  • a massive trapezoidal pylon gateway flanked by obelisks and flagstaffs;
  • an open peristyle court accessible to more people;
  • a hypostyle hall filled with columns representing the primeval marsh;
  • offering halls and subsidiary chapels;
  • the dark inner sanctuary (naos) housing the cult image of the god.

Temples were often enclosed by mudbrick walls and accompanied by sacred lakes, gardens, granaries, workshops, and priestly housing. Mortuary temples, dedicated to the cult of a deceased king, stood on the west bank of the Nile at Thebes and elsewhere.

Economy and decline

Great temples were major economic institutions, owning vast estates, herds, mines, and workshops and employing thousands of staff; inscriptions claim the temple of Amun at Karnak controlled a substantial fraction of Egypt's arable land at its height. Temples continued to be built and expanded under the Ptolemaic and Roman rulers of Egypt — Edfu, Dendera, and Philae are masterpieces of this late period — but the rise of Christianity led to their closure, most famously when the last hieroglyphic inscription was carved at Philae in AD 394 and the temple there was finally closed in the sixth century.
