NERVA


Labelled image: XE engine test at the Nuclear Rocket Development Station, Nevada

The Nuclear Engine for Rocket Vehicle Application (NERVA) was a program of the United States Atomic Energy Commission and NASA, carried out at Los Alamos and by contractors Aerojet and Westinghouse from the 1960s until 1973, to develop a nuclear thermal rocket engine. It grew out of Project Rover, which began in 1955 to investigate nuclear propulsion.

Design and testing

A nuclear thermal rocket heats a light propellant — liquid hydrogen — in a solid-core fission reactor and expels it through a nozzle, achieving roughly twice the specific impulse of the best chemical engines (around 850–900 seconds in NERVA tests). NERVA engines were tested in the Kiwi, Phoebus, Pewee and NRX series at the Nuclear Rocket Development Station in Jackass Flats, Nevada. The XE ("experimental engine") tests of 1968–69 demonstrated a complete flight-configured engine system, with repeated restarts and throttling, and the program was judged technically ready for flight development.

Cancellation and legacy

NERVA was intended to power crewed missions to Mars in the 1980s as part of Wernher von Braun's post-Apollo plans. With the cancellation of those ambitions and shrinking NASA budgets, the program was terminated in 1973. Nuclear thermal propulsion has been repeatedly revived in studies — including the NTP work of the 1990s and the DRACO demonstration program announced by NASA and DARPA in the 2020s — and NERVA's test data remain the principal empirical foundation for the technology.
