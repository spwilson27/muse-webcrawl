The Habsburg Monarchy, also known as the Habsburg Empire, was the collection of empires, kingdoms, duchies, counties and other polities (composite monarchy) that were ruled by the House of Habsburg. From the 16th century until 1804 its Central European core—Austria, Bohemia, and Hungary, together with territories in what are now Italy, the Low Countries (for a time), and the Balkans—formed one of the great powers of Europe, ruled from Vienna.

Formation

The monarchy is conventionally dated from 1526, when Archduke Ferdinand I was elected king of Bohemia and of Hungary after the death of Louis II at the Battle of Mohács, uniting the three crowns in Habsburg hands. His brother Charles V also ruled Spain and its empire, and after Charles's abdication (1556) the dynasty split into Spanish and Austrian branches. The Austrian Habsburgs also supplied nearly all Holy Roman Emperors from the 15th century to 1806.

Conflicts and consolidation

The monarchy stood on the frontier with the Ottoman Empire for two centuries: Vienna was besieged in 1529 and again in 1683, after which Habsburg armies reconquered Hungary and Transylvania, confirmed by the Treaty of Karlowitz (1699). Internally, the Bohemian revolt of 1618 triggered the Thirty Years' War; victory at White Mountain (1620) made the Bohemian crown hereditary. In the 18th century Maria Theresa's reforms centralised administration, and Joseph II pushed further toward a unitary state. In 1804 Francis II proclaimed the Austrian Empire in response to Napoleon's coronation; the Habsburg Monarchy as a term is often extended to that empire and its 1867 successor, Austria-Hungary, which endured until dissolution in 1918.

Character

The monarchy was a dynastic, multilingual conglomerate rather than a nation-state, held together by the ruling house, a common army and court, and the Catholic Church; its motto, A.E.I.O.U. ("Austriae est imperare orbi universo"—"It is Austria's destiny to rule the world"), expressed dynastic confidence rather than administrative reality.
