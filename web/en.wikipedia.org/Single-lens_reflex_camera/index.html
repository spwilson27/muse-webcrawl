A single-lens reflex camera (SLR) is a camera that typically uses a mirror and prism system (hence "reflex" from the mirror's reflection) that permits the photographer to view through the lens and see exactly what will be captured. With most other types of camera, the photographic scene differs significantly from what is seen in the viewfinder because the viewfinder and lens capture from different positions.

How it works

Light enters through the lens and strikes a mirror angled at 45 degrees, which reflects it upward onto a focusing screen. A pentaprism (or pentamirror) corrects the image laterally and directs it to the photographer's eye. When the shutter is released, the mirror flips up out of the way, the shutter opens, and light reaches the film or sensor. The mirror then returns, causing the characteristic viewfinder blackout and mirror slap of SLR photography.

History

Reflex viewing systems date to the 19th century, but the modern 35mm SLR emerged in the 1930s–1950s with cameras such as the Kine Exakta (1936) and, after World War II, Japanese designs incorporating instant-return mirrors and pentaprisms. The Nikon F (1959) established the professional system camera. Digital SLRs (DSLRs) replaced film with electronic sensors from the late 1990s, dominating professional and enthusiast photography until the rise of mirrorless interchangeable-lens cameras in the 2010s.

Advantages and disadvantages

The SLR design provides accurate framing and focusing through interchangeable lenses, and an optical viewfinder with no lag. Its disadvantages include bulk, mechanical complexity, mirror vibration, and the inability to use the viewfinder during exposure — issues addressed in different ways by mirrorless designs.
