A soft drink (also called soda, pop, fizzy drink, or carbonated drink in different regions) is a non-alcoholic beverage typically containing carbonated water, a sweetener, and a natural or artificial flavouring. The sweetener may be sugar, high-fructose corn syrup, fruit juice, or sugar substitutes in diet drinks. Soft drinks may also contain caffeine, colourings, and preservatives. The term "soft" contrasts the drinks with "hard" (alcoholic) liquor.

History

Carbonated waters were first produced commercially in the late eighteenth century, following Joseph Priestley's experiments with "fixed air," and were sold for supposed medicinal benefits. Pharmacists in nineteenth-century America flavoured soda-fountain drinks with botanicals; iconic brands followed: Dr Pepper (1885), Coca-Cola (1886), and Pepsi (1893). Early colas contained extracts of coca leaf and kola nut. Bottling, crown caps, vending machines, and aluminium cans drove mass consumption in the twentieth century, and the "cola wars" between Coca-Cola and Pepsi defined modern beverage marketing.

Production and consumption

Concentrate or syrup is mixed with treated water and carbonated, then packaged in cans, PET bottles, or fountain systems. Global consumption varies widely; the United States historically had among the highest per-capita intakes, though U.S. soda consumption has declined since the late 1990s as bottled water and other drinks gained share. Major markets such as Mexico have also recorded very high consumption.

Health and regulation

Sugar-sweetened beverages are associated with obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and dental caries, prompting public-health measures including sugar taxes in dozens of jurisdictions (such as the UK Soft Drinks Industry Levy, 2018), advertising restrictions, and warning labels. Diet drinks replace sugar with low-calorie sweeteners, whose long-term effects remain debated. Environmental criticism targets packaging waste and water use, spurring deposit schemes and recycled-content targets.
