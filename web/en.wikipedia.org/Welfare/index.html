Welfare

Welfare, or social welfare, refers to government programmes that provide financial assistance and social services to individuals and families in need, with the aim of ensuring a minimum standard of living and protecting against poverty, illness, disability, unemployment and old age. In a narrower sense, especially in the United States, "welfare" refers to means-tested cash assistance programmes such as Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF). Welfare provision is the practical core of the welfare state.

FormsCash benefits, social insurance, in-kind services (health, housing, food)
FundingGeneral taxation and social insurance contributions
Eligibility modelsUniversal, means-tested, contributory
Key institutionsWelfare state; social security systems

Types of provision

Welfare systems combine several instruments. Social insurance programmes (pensions, unemployment insurance, health insurance) are contributory and earnings-related. Means-tested social assistance provides a safety net for those with low income or assets — for example SNAP food assistance and housing benefits in the United States, and Universal Credit in the United Kingdom. Universal services such as public healthcare and education are available regardless of income. Scholars such as Gøsta Esping-Andersen classify welfare states into liberal, conservative-corporatist and social-democratic regimes, differing in generosity, universality and the role of markets and families.

History

Organised poor relief dates to measures such as the English Poor Laws (1601), which made parishes responsible for their poor. Bismarck's Germany created the first modern social insurance schemes in the 1880s. The Great Depression prompted the United States' Social Security Act of 1935, and after the Second World War the Beveridge Report (1942) in Britain inspired a comprehensive welfare state "from cradle to grave" including the National Health Service. From the 1970s, welfare systems faced criticism over cost, dependency and work incentives, leading to reforms emphasising activation and conditionality, such as the 1996 US welfare reform that replaced AFDC with TANF.

Debates

Research generally finds that welfare programmes substantially reduce measured poverty and material hardship, while debates continue over generosity, benefit traps, administrative error and fraud, and the balance between universal and targeted provision. During economic crises and the COVID-19 pandemic, welfare systems expanded rapidly in many countries as automatic stabilisers.
