Anattā

Not to be confused with Anatomy. For the Hindu concept, see Ātman.

In Buddhism, the term anattā (Pali; Sanskrit: anātman) is the doctrine of "non-self" — that no unchanging, permanent self or essence can be found in any phenomenon. While often interpreted as a doctrine denying the existence of a self, anattā is more precisely a strategy for freedom from suffering: rather than an answer to the question "is there a self?", the teaching holds that clinging to any phenomenon as "I" or "mine" produces dukkha (suffering), and directs attention to the impermanent, conditioned nature of the five aggregates (skandhas) that constitute a person.

In the early texts

The Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta (SN 22.59), traditionally the Buddha's second discourse, delivered to the group of five monks at Sarnath, teaches that each of the five aggregates — form, feeling, perception, mental formations, and consciousness — is "not-self" because it is not subject to one's control and is impermanent. The Dhammapada states that "all phenomena are not-self" (sabbe dhammā anattā). Anattā is one of the three characteristics of existence, along with dukkha and anicca (impermanence).

Interpretation

The Buddha famously declined to answer whether a self exists or not (the unanswered questions), arguing that both eternalism and annihilationism are wrong views. Later traditions developed the doctrine in different directions: Theravāda Abhidhamma analyses persons into ultimate realities (dhammas) that lack selfhood; Mahāyāna philosophy, especially Nāgārjuna's Madhyamaka, extended emptiness (śūnyatā) to all phenomena; and the tathāgatagarbha literature used "self" language for Buddha-nature, generating debate about how to reconcile the two teachings. Scholars continue to debate whether anattā is best read as an ontological claim or a pragmatic, therapeutic one.

Relation to other traditions

Anattā stands in contrast to the Hindu concept of Ātman, the eternal self ultimately identical with Brahman, and to Jain notions of the jīva. Its development is often read as a deliberate Buddhist response to Upanishadic self-doctrine.
