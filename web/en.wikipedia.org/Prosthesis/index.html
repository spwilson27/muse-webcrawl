Prosthesis

In medicine, a prosthesis (plural: prostheses), or a prosthetic implant, is an artificial device that replaces a missing body part, which may be lost through trauma, disease, or a congenital condition. Prosthetic limbs are the best-known examples, but prostheses also include artificial joints, dental prostheses, hearing devices, and artificial heart valves. The field of designing and fitting prostheses is called prosthetics, and its practitioners are prosthetists.

History

The earliest known prostheses include an artificial toe from ancient Egypt dating to roughly 950–710 BC, found to be functional as well as cosmetic. Historically, prosthetic legs were often simple peg-like devices, while articulated iron hands were made for wounded knights such as Götz von Berlichingen in the 16th century. The large numbers of amputee veterans of the American Civil War and the two world wars spurred major advances in prosthetic design and public provision.

Limb prostheses

Lower-limb prostheses include transtibial (below-knee) and transfemoral (above-knee) devices, with components such as prosthetic feet, pylons, knee joints, and sockets fitted to the residual limb. Modern running blades made of carbon-fibre composites allow amputee athletes, such as Paralympic sprinters, to compete at elite level. Upper-limb prostheses range from cosmetic hands to body-powered hooks and myoelectric arms controlled by muscle signals; experimental systems use targeted muscle reinnervation and implanted electrodes to provide more intuitive control and sensory feedback.

Other prostheses

Joint replacement (arthroplasty) of hips and knees is among the most common and successful surgical procedures worldwide. Cochlear implants restore a sense of hearing, breast prostheses follow mastectomy, and prosthetic heart valves replace diseased valves. Osseointegration, anchoring the prosthesis directly to bone, is an increasingly used alternative to socket fitting.
