Rudolf Ludwig Carl Virchow (13 October 1821 – 5 September 1902) was a German physician, anthropologist, pathologist, prehistorian, biologist, writer, editor and politician. He is known as "the father of modern pathology" and as the founder of social medicine.

Scientific work

Virchow's most famous dictum, omnis cellula e cellula ("every cell from a cell", 1855), established that cells arise only by division of pre-existing cells, completing cell theory and refuting spontaneous generation for tissues. His Cellular Pathology (1858) relocated disease from organs and tissues to the level of the cell, transforming medical science. He described and named conditions including leukaemia, thrombosis and embolism, and identified the mechanism of pulmonary embolism.

Public health and politics

Sent to investigate a typhus epidemic in Upper Silesia in 1848, Virchow concluded that social and economic conditions caused the outbreak, famously arguing that "medicine is a social science, and politics is nothing else but medicine on a large scale." He helped build Berlin's sewerage system, sat on the Berlin city council and in the Reichstag as a liberal opponent of Bismarck, and founded the journal Virchows Archiv, still published today.
