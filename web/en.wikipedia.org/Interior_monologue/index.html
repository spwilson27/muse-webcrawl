Interior monologue

Interior monologue is a narrative device that depicts the thoughts of a character as if the reader were overhearing the character's inner speech — the mental discourse that occurs, unspoken, within the mind. In its purest form it presents a character's thought processes directly, in the first person and present tense, without the mediation of a narrator. The term is closely related to, and often used interchangeably with, stream of consciousness, though strictly the latter names the broader technique or effect of which interior monologue is one vehicle.

History

The device's development is associated with Édouard Dujardin's novel Les lauriers sont coupés (1888), which James Joyce cited as a model. It became a hallmark of literary modernism: Joyce's Ulysses (1922), culminating in Molly Bloom's unpunctuated soliloquy; Virginia Woolf's Mrs Dalloway (1925) and To the Lighthouse (1927), which move fluidly between characters' minds; the Benjy section of William Faulkner's The Sound and the Fury (1929); and Arthur Schnitzler's novella Leutnant Gustl (1900), an early sustained example in German. The phrase "stream of consciousness" itself derives from William James's Principles of Psychology (1890).

Forms

Narratologists such as Dorrit Cohn distinguish direct interior monologue (quoted thought, first person, present tense) from free indirect discourse or "narrated monologue", in which a third-person, past-tense narration adopts the character's idiom and viewpoint. Interior monologue should also be distinguished from the dramatic monologue and the soliloquy of drama, which are spoken aloud. On stage and in film, voice-over narration sometimes serves an analogous function.
