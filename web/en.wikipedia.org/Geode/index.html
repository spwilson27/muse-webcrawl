Geode

A geode is a geological secondary formation within sedimentary and volcanic rocks. Geodes are hollow, vaguely spherical rocks in which masses of mineral matter — typically crystals — are secluded inside. The crystals are formed by the slow precipitation of minerals from hydrothermal fluids or groundwater through the walls of the cavity. Geodes can be found all over the world, most commonly in deserts, volcanic ash beds, and limestone regions.

Formation

Geodes begin as hollow cavities in rock — gas bubbles in volcanic lava, voids left by dissolved fossils or tree roots in sedimentary stone, or pockets created by fracturing. Over time, mineral-laden water seeps through the outer shell and deposits successive layers of silica (chalcedony, agate, or quartz) and other minerals on the interior walls. Because the solution enters slowly and the cavity remains partly empty, well-formed free-standing crystals — quartz, amethyst, calcite, celestite, and many others — grow inward from the lining. A related feature, a vug, is a similar cavity that lacks the distinct outer rind, while a solid filled equivalent is a nodule.

Size and notable localities

Most geodes range from a few centimetres to a metre across. Exceptionally large crystal-lined chambers are sometimes described as geode caves: the Pulpí Geode in Almería, Spain, discovered in 1999, is lined with enormous translucent gypsum crystals, and the Crystal Cave at Put-in-Bay, Ohio, is a large celestite geode. Famous collecting localities include the amethyst fields of Artigas, Uruguay and Rio Grande do Sul, Brazil; the Keokuk region of Iowa, Missouri, and Illinois, known for quartz geodes in limestone; and the Hauser geode beds of California.

Color

The colours of geode crystals depend on trace impurities and irradiation: iron produces the purple of amethyst, other impurities yield smoky quartz, citrine, and pink hues, while banded chalcedony linings form agate. Some commercially sold geodes are artificially dyed to enhance their colour.
