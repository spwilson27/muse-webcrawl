Kardecist spiritism, usually called Spiritism (French: spiritisme; Portuguese: espiritismo), is a spiritualist philosophy and movement codified in 19th-century France by the educator Allan Kardec. It holds that human beings are incarnate spirits who survive death, progress through successive reincarnations, and can communicate with the living through mediums.

Origins

Kardec (born Hippolyte Léon Denizard Rivail, 1804–1869), a Pestalozzian educator, investigated the "table-turning" phenomena then fashionable in Paris and systematised answers he attributed to spirit communications in The Spirits' Book (1857). He followed it with The Mediums' Book (1861), The Gospel According to Spiritism (1864), Heaven and Hell (1865), and The Genesis (1868) — the five works known as the Spiritist Codification. Kardec coined the word spiritism to distinguish his doctrine from broader spiritualism, presenting it as simultaneously a science of observation, a philosophy, and a moral teaching centred on charity: "Without charity there is no salvation."

Beliefs and practice

Spiritist doctrine teaches belief in God as supreme intelligence, the immortality of the soul, reincarnation as the mechanism of moral and intellectual progress, the plurality of inhabited worlds, and the possibility of communication between incarnate and discarnate spirits. Study groups, public lectures, mediumistic meetings, "passes" (laying on of hands), and charitable work are typical activities of Spiritist centres; the movement has no clergy, sacraments, or ritual worship.

Spiritism in Brazil

Spiritism found its largest following in Brazil, where the Brazilian Spiritist Federation (founded 1884) coordinates thousands of centres. Figures such as the medium Francisco Cândido "Chico" Xavier, who psychographed hundreds of books, made Spiritism a major part of Brazilian religious culture, influencing charity hospitals, publishing, and popular media, and blending in Afro-Brazilian contexts with Umbanda while remaining doctrinally distinct.
