Constructionism (learning theory)

Constructionism emphasises learning through making tangible artefacts.

Constructionism is a theory of learning developed by Seymour Papert and colleagues at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology. It holds that people learn most effectively when they are actively engaged in constructing tangible, personally meaningful artefacts in the real world, whether a sandcastle, a computer program, or a robot, and reflecting on that process.

Origins

Papert built constructionism on the constructivist psychology of Jean Piaget, with whom he had worked in Geneva. Where constructivism says that learners actively construct knowledge structures in their minds, constructionism adds that this internal construction proceeds especially well when supported by the construction of something external and shareable. Papert set out the theory in his books Mindstorms (1980) and The Children's Machine (1993).

Practice

Papert developed the Logo programming language so that children could learn mathematics by teaching a "turtle" to move and draw, treating the computer as an "object to think with". Constructionist ideas underlie the LEGO Mindstorms robotics kits (named in honour of Papert's book), the Scratch programming environment created by Mitchel Resnick's Lifelong Kindergarten group, and much of the maker movement in education. The related pedagogy stresses open-ended projects, debugging as a normal part of learning, and the teacher as facilitator rather than lecturer.

Critics have questioned whether unguided discovery consistently outperforms direct instruction, and empirical studies of constructionist programmes have produced mixed results, but the approach has been highly influential in educational technology and informal learning.
