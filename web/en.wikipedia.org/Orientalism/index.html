Orientalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Orientalism is the imitation or depiction of aspects of the Eastern world by writers, painters and other artists from the Western world. In the history of art it refers particularly to the works of French and British painters of the nineteenth century who travelled in Southwest Asia and North Africa — artists such as Jean-Léon Gérôme, Eugène Delacroix and John Frederick Lewis — depicting scenes of markets, mosques and harems, often highly romanticised or imagined.

Said's critique

The term took on a critical meaning after Edward Said's book Orientalism (1978), a founding text of postcolonial studies. Said argued that Orientalism was a Western discourse of power: a body of scholarship, literature and imagery that constructed "the Orient" as Europe's irrational, sensual, backward opposite, thereby legitimising colonial domination. His analysis drew on Foucault's theory of discourse and Gramsci's concept of cultural hegemony.

Debate

Said's thesis has been enormously influential across literary studies, history and anthropology, and has also been criticised — by scholars such as Bernard Lewis, Ibn Warraq and Robert Irwin — for selective reading of the scholarly record, for flattening diverse Western views of the East, and for essentialising "the West" in turn. Debates concern its application to German and Russian scholarship, to academic Oriental studies as such, and to contemporary representations of the Islamic world in media and politics.
