Tyndall effect

A light beam is visible in a colloid because of the Tyndall effect.

The Tyndall effect is the scattering of light as it passes through a colloid — a mixture in which microscopically dispersed, insoluble particles are suspended throughout another substance. The suspended particles make the path of a light beam visible, just as dust in the air reveals a sunbeam. The phenomenon is named after the Irish physicist John Tyndall, who studied it extensively in the 1860s.

Characteristics

Individual particles that produce the effect are roughly 40 to 900 nanometres in size, comparable to the wavelength of visible light. The scattering is similar in mechanism to Rayleigh scattering: shorter wavelengths are scattered more strongly, which is why a Tyndall-scattered beam often appears bluish — as in the blue haze over forests or the blue appearance of cigarette smoke before it disperses.

The effect provides a simple test distinguishing a true solution, whose dissolved particles are too small to scatter light visibly, from a colloid or suspension. It is exploited in nephelometry and turbidimetry to measure particle concentration, and in instruments such as the ultramicroscope.

Examples

Everyday examples include headlights visible in fog, the beam of a torch in dusty air, light through milk diluted in water, and the blue colour of some eyes, where scattering in the stroma of the iris produces the appearance of blue without blue pigment.
