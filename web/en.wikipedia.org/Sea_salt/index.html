Sea salt

Sea salt is salt produced from the evaporation of seawater. It is used as a seasoning in foods, cooking, and cosmetics, and historically was an important preservative. Sea salt is typically less processed than refined table salt and retains trace minerals such as potassium, iron, and zinc, which can give it a slightly different flavour and colour.

Production

Sea salt is harvested in shallow ponds, called salterns or salt pans, where seawater is allowed to evaporate under the sun and wind. As the brine concentrates, salt crystals form and are raked into piles for collection. Production depends heavily on climate; dry, sunny regions such as the Mediterranean coast, parts of Australia, and Hawaii are major producers. Well-known varieties include fleur de sel from France and Maldon salt from England.

Composition

Commercially produced sea salt typically consists of roughly 98–99% sodium chloride, with the remainder made up of trace minerals and naturally occurring compounds. This is slightly less pure than refined table salt, which is usually about 99.7% sodium chloride and often contains added iodine and anti-caking agents.

History

Salt production from seawater dates back thousands of years and was a major industry in ancient China, the Roman world, and along the coasts of Europe and Africa. Salt was valuable enough to be used as currency in some societies, and control of salt pans was a source of political power.
