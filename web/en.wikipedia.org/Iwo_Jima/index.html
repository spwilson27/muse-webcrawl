Iwo Jima (Japanese: 硫黄島, Iōtō, literally "Sulfur Island") is a volcanic island in the Volcano Islands group of the Ogasawara Islands, about 1,220 kilometres south of Tokyo. It is best known as the site of the Battle of Iwo Jima in February–March 1945, one of the fiercest battles of the Pacific War, and for the photograph of the second flag raising on Mount Suribachi.

Geography

The island is roughly 8 kilometres long and up to 4 kilometres wide, shaped somewhat like a pork chop, with the volcanic cone of Mount Suribachi at its south-western tip. It is an active volcanic island; uplift from volcanic activity and land reclamation have steadily increased its area. The island has no permanent civilian population, and access is restricted.

History

The island was largely uninhabited before Japanese settlement in the late 19th century, when sulphur mining and sugar cultivation began. By 1944 the population had reached roughly a thousand civilians, who were evacuated as Japanese forces fortified the island under General Tadamichi Kuribayashi.

Battle of Iwo Jima

United States Marine Corps forces landed on 19 February 1945. The battle lasted until 26 March 1945; of roughly 21,000 Japanese defenders, nearly all were killed, while the United States suffered some 6,800 killed and 26,000 wounded. On 23 February 1945, Associated Press photographer Joe Rosenthal took the famous photograph of six Marines raising the second American flag atop Mount Suribachi, an image that became one of the most reproduced of the war and the model for the Marine Corps War Memorial in Arlington, Virginia.

Post-war

The island remained under United States administration until 1968, when it was returned to Japan. It is now used by the Japan Self-Defense Forces and the United States military, with an airfield at its centre. In 2007 the Japanese government officially changed the island's name back to its pre-war name, Iōtō. Annual joint memorial services are held for the war dead of both nations.
