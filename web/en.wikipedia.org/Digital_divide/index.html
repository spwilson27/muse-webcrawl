Digital divide

The digital divide is the unequal access to digital technology, including smartphones, tablets, laptops, and the internet. The digital divide worsens inequality around information, resources, and services. The term became prominent in the mid-1990s, when reports by the United States National Telecommunications and Information Administration documented disparities in telephone, computer and internet access by income, race, age and geography.

Dimensions

Scholars distinguish several levels of the divide. The first-level divide concerns physical access to devices and connections. The second-level divide concerns differences in digital skills and patterns of use. A third-level divide concerns the unequal ability to convert internet use into tangible benefits such as employment, education and health outcomes. Divides exist between countries (the global digital divide) and within them, along lines of income, rurality, disability, gender and age.

Causes

Contributing factors include the cost of devices and subscriptions relative to income, the absence of broadband infrastructure in rural and low-income areas, limited digital literacy, language barriers in content, and social norms restricting technology use for some groups. During the COVID-19 pandemic, reliance on remote schooling and telehealth sharply exposed the consequences of lacking home broadband.

Bridging efforts

Responses include public access points and libraries, subsidised broadband programmes, community networks, low-cost devices, satellite constellations serving remote regions, and training programmes such as India's digital literacy missions. Universal service funds have been adapted from telephony to broadband in many jurisdictions.
