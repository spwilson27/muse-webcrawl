Cornelia de Lange syndrome

Cornelia de Lange syndrome (CdLS) is a genetic disorder present from birth, usually not inherited. It causes a range of physical features, such as distinctive facial appearance, growth delay, and limb differences, together with intellectual disability. The syndrome is named after the Dutch paediatrician Cornelia Catharina de Lange, who described it in 1933. Its prevalence is estimated at between 1 in 10,000 and 1 in 30,000 live births.

Signs and symptoms

Characteristic features include low birth weight and slow growth; a small head (microcephaly); thick eyebrows that often meet in the middle (synophrys); long eyelashes; a small, upturned nose with anteverted nostrils; thin lips with downturned corners; and excessive body hair (hirsutism). Hands and feet may be small, and in more severely affected individuals there are limb reduction defects. Gastroesophageal reflux, feeding difficulties, hearing loss and developmental delay are common. Behavioural features may include self-injury and autistic-like traits.

Cause

Most cases are caused by new (de novo) mutations in genes encoding components or regulators of the cohesin complex — most often NIPBL (about 60% of cases), and also SMC1A, SMC3, RAD21, and HDAC8 — which affect gene regulation during development. Because cohesin is essential for chromosome cohesion, CdLS is classified as a cohesinopathy. Diagnosis is usually clinical, confirmed by genetic testing; there is no cure, so care focuses on treating complications and supporting development.
