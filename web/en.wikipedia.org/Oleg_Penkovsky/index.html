Died16 May 1963 (aged 44), Moscow, Soviet Union

Oleg Vladimirovich Penkovsky (23 April 1919 – 16 May 1963), codenamed HERO, was a colonel in Soviet military intelligence (the GRU) who during the early 1960s supplied the United Kingdom and the United States with crucial information about Soviet nuclear missile capabilities, including material that helped the West interpret reconnaissance photographs during the Cuban Missile Crisis of October 1962.

Career and recruitment

Penkovsky served as an artillery officer in the Second World War and later in intelligence postings including military attaché work in Turkey. Working in Moscow at the State Committee for Science and Technology gave him access to visiting Western businessmen and delegations. In 1960–61 he repeatedly approached Western contacts; through the British businessman Greville Wynne he was taken on jointly by MI6 and the CIA, meeting handlers in London and Paris and photographing thousands of classified documents with a miniature camera.

The Cuban Missile Crisis

Penkovsky's reporting revealed that the Soviet intercontinental ballistic missile programme was far weaker than feared, and his descriptions of missile-site layouts, manuals and deployment patterns allowed American photo-analysts to identify the medium-range ballistic missile sites being built in Cuba in October 1962. Many historians credit his material with giving President John F. Kennedy confidence in the true state of the Soviet arsenal during the crisis.

Arrest and death

Under KGB surveillance, Penkovsky was arrested in Moscow on 22 October 1962, the day Kennedy publicly announced the quarantine of Cuba. Wynne was arrested in Budapest days later. After a public show trial in May 1963, Penkovsky was sentenced to death and executed; Wynne was imprisoned and exchanged in 1964 for the Soviet spy Konon Molody.

Legacy

Penkovsky is often described as one of the most valuable Western agents of the Cold War. His papers were published in edited form as The Penkovsky Papers (1965). Debates continue over his motives — disillusionment, ambition, and fear of Khrushchev's recklessness have all been proposed.
