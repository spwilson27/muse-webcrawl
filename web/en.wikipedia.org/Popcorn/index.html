Popcorn (also called popped corn, popcorns, or pop-corn) is a variety of corn kernel which expands and puffs up when heated; the term also refers to the snack food produced by the expansion. It is one of the oldest forms of maize consumption: evidence of popcorn in Peru dates to about 4700 BCE, and popped kernels thousands of years old have been found in New Mexico.

How it pops

A popcorn kernel consists of a hard, moisture-resistant hull surrounding a dense starchy endosperm containing about 14% water. When heated to roughly 180 °C (356 °F), the water turns to superheated steam; pressure builds to about 135 psi until the hull ruptures, the starch gelatinises and expands, and the kernel turns inside out, cooling instantly into its characteristic irregular flake. Kernels that fail to pop are known as "old maids".

History and consumption

Indigenous peoples throughout the Americas popped corn for food and ornament. In the 19th century, steam-powered popcorn machines made it a street and fairground snack in the United States; it was one of the few treats whose sales rose during the Great Depression, and its association with cinemas became entrenched during the Second World War when sugar rationing limited candy. Americans today consume several billion quarts annually. Popcorn may be air-popped, oil-popped or cooked in microwave bags, and is seasoned with salt, butter, caramel or other flavourings; unflavoured it is a high-fibre whole-grain snack.
