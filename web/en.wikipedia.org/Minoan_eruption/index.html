Minoan eruption

The Minoan eruption was a catastrophic volcanic eruption that devastated the Aegean island of Thera (modern Santorini) in the mid-2nd millennium BCE. It was one of the largest volcanic events on Earth in recorded history, ejecting an estimated 14.5 to 78 km3 of material and attaining a Volcanic Explosivity Index of 6–7. The eruption destroyed the Minoan settlement at Akrotiri, as well as communities and agricultural areas on nearby islands and the coast of Crete, possibly with subsequent tsunamis.

Dating

The date of the eruption is a major point of dispute between radiocarbon-based and archaeologically-based chronologies. Radiocarbon dating of an olive tree buried by the eruption and of seeds from Akrotiri points to the late 17th century BCE, roughly 1627–1600 BCE, while conventional synchronisms with Egyptian chronology have often placed it around 1500 BCE. Ice-core and tree-ring evidence, including a frost event in bristlecone pines and growth anomalies in Irish oaks, has been linked to the eruption.

Sequence of events

The eruption appears to have proceeded through precursor earthquakes, a phreatic phase, pumice fall, pyroclastic surges and flows, and finally caldera collapse, leaving the flooded crescent-shaped caldera visible today. The town of Akrotiri was buried under metres of ash and pumice, preserving frescoes, pottery, and multi-storey buildings in a manner compared to Pompeii; remarkably, no human remains have been found there, suggesting evacuation after warning earthquakes.

Effects on Minoan civilisation

The eruption has often been blamed for the decline of Minoan civilisation on Crete, about 110 km to the south. Ash fall damaged agriculture in eastern Crete, and tsunamis may have wrecked the Minoan fleet and coastal settlements. However, Cretan palaces flourished for some decades after the eruption, and the final collapse came later, possibly connected with Mycenaean invasion. The disaster is also a proposed inspiration for Plato's story of Atlantis.
