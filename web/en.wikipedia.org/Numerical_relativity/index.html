Numerical relativity

Numerical relativity is a branch of general relativity that uses numerical methods and algorithms to solve Einstein's field equations on computers, for situations — such as colliding black holes and neutron stars — where no exact analytic solutions exist.

Development

Early attempts in the 1960s and 1970s struggled with instabilities. The ADM and BSSN formulations of the equations, together with improved gauge conditions and mesh refinement, gradually made long, stable evolutions possible. A breakthrough came in 2005 with the first stable simulations of a binary black hole merger by Frans Pretorius and, shortly after, by other groups.

Applications

Numerical relativity produces the gravitational-wave templates used to interpret signals detected by LIGO and Virgo, beginning with the binary black hole merger GW150914, and models the mergers thought to power short gamma-ray bursts and kilonovae.
