Dysphagia

Dysphagia
SpecialtyGastroenterology, speech-language pathology, neurology
SymptomsDifficulty or inability to swallow
ComplicationsAspiration pneumonia, malnutrition, dehydration

Dysphagia is the medical term for difficulty in swallowing. It is derived from the Greek dys meaning bad or disordered, and the root phag- meaning "eat". Although classified under "symptoms and signs" in the ICD-10, in some contexts it is classified as a condition in its own right. People with dysphagia may be unaware of it—it may be a symptom of a wide range of diseases. Dysphagia is distinguished from odynophagia (painful swallowing) and from globus sensation (feeling of a lump in the throat).

Classification

Dysphagia is broadly divided into oropharyngeal dysphagia, arising from problems in the mouth and pharynx—often neurological, as after stroke, in Parkinson's disease or in multiple sclerosis—and esophageal dysphagia, in which food feels stuck after swallowing, caused by mechanical obstruction (strictures, Schatzki rings, oesophageal cancer) or motility disorders such as achalasia and diffuse esophageal spasm.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms include coughing or choking when eating or drinking, food sticking in the throat or chest, regurgitation, drooling, recurrent chest infections from aspiration, weight loss, and a wet or gurgly voice after swallowing. Complications include aspiration pneumonia, malnutrition and dehydration.

Diagnosis and management

Evaluation may include a bedside swallow assessment, videofluoroscopic swallow study (modified barium swallow), fiberoptic endoscopic evaluation of swallowing, barium esophagram, upper endoscopy and esophageal manometry. Management depends on the cause and includes dietary modification (thickened fluids, texture-modified foods), swallowing therapy by speech-language pathologists, dilation of strictures, medication, or surgery; severe cases may require enteral feeding via gastrostomy.
