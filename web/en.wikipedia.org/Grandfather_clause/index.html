Grandfather clause

A grandfather clause (or grandfather policy, or grandfathering) is a provision in which an old rule continues to apply to some existing situations while a new rule applies to all future cases. Those exempt from the new rule are said to have grandfather rights or acquired rights, or to be grandfathered in. The term originated in United States law on voting rights.

Origin in voting law

After the Fifteenth Amendment (1870) prohibited denying the vote on account of race, several Southern states imposed literacy tests and poll taxes designed to disenfranchise Black voters. To protect poor and illiterate white voters from the same tests, some states adopted clauses exempting anyone entitled to vote before 1867 (or whose father or grandfather had been entitled to vote) — the original "grandfather clauses". The U.S. Supreme Court struck down Oklahoma's grandfather clause in Guinn v. United States (1915) as a violation of the Fifteenth Amendment.

Modern usage

Grandfathering is now common in regulation: existing buildings may be allowed to keep nonconforming features when building codes change; existing licensees may remain under old licensing rules; products already approved may stay on the market under earlier standards; and statutes often exempt pre-existing contracts or facilities. In land-use law the related concept is a legal nonconforming use. The doctrine balances stability and reliance interests against the policy goals of new rules, though critics argue grandfathering can entrench advantages and delay regulatory benefits — for example when old, polluting power plants are exempted from new emissions standards.
