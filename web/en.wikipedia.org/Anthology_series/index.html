Anthology series

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


Each episode of an anthology series is a separate story

An anthology series is a written series, radio, television, film, or video game series that presents a different story and a different set of characters in each different episode, season, segment, or short, although some anthology series have elements that appear in each work, such as a regular host or a common theme.

These usually have a different cast in each episode, but several series in the past, such as Four Star Playhouse, employed a permanent troupe of character actors who would appear in a different drama each week. Some anthology series, such as Studio One, began on radio and then expanded to television.

History and examples

The format flourished in the "Golden Age of Television" of the 1950s, with prestigious live drama anthologies including Kraft Television Theatre, The Philco Television Playhouse, and Playhouse 90. Rod Serling's The Twilight Zone (1959–1964) and its many successors made the anthology the home of science fiction, horror and fantasy stories on television.

Notable later and contemporary examples include The Outer Limits, Tales from the Crypt, American Horror Story (which resets its story each season), Fargo, True Detective, and Black Mirror. In film, anthology (or "omnibus") movies compile short segments by different directors, as in Paris, je t'aime.

Characteristics

Because every episode is self-contained, anthology series need no knowledge of earlier episodes, can attract guest stars for single instalments, and can take risks with tone and subject matter. The trade-off is the loss of continuing characters and story arcs that build long-term audience attachment in conventional series.
