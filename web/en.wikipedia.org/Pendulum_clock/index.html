Pendulum clock

A pendulum clock is a clock that uses a pendulum, a swinging weight, as its timekeeping element. Invented by the Dutch scientist Christiaan Huygens in 1656 and patented the following year, it was the most accurate timekeeping technology in the world for nearly 300 years, until the development of the quartz clock in the 1930s.

History

Galileo Galilei had studied the regular swing of pendulums and sketched a clock design, but the first working pendulum clock was built by Salomon Coster to Huygens' design in 1657. The addition of the anchor escapement around 1670 allowed longer, slower pendulums, leading to the longcase or "grandfather" clock. Precision regulators by makers such as John Harrison and later the Shortt–Synchronome clock (1921), accurate to fractions of a second per year, served as primary time standards in observatories.

Mechanism

The pendulum's period depends mainly on its length—about two seconds for a "seconds pendulum" just under a metre long. An escapement gives the pendulum small impulses to replace energy lost to friction and counts its swings, driving the gear train and hands. Temperature-compensated pendulums, using gridiron or mercury designs or low-expansion materials such as Invar, reduced errors from thermal expansion.
