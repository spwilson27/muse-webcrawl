Facade

A facade (also spelled façade) is generally the front of a building, usually the principal face that fronts a street or open space and contains the main entrance. In architecture, the facade is often the most important aspect of a building's exterior design, establishing its style, character and relationship to its surroundings. The word comes from the French façade, ultimately from the Latin facia, meaning "face".

Design and elements

Facades are composed of elements such as windows, doors, cornices, pilasters, columns and cladding materials. In classical architecture, facades were organised by strict rules of symmetry and proportion; Gothic cathedrals developed elaborate west fronts with portals, rose windows and towers. Renaissance architects such as Leon Battista Alberti treated the facade as a composition in its own right, sometimes applied to an older structure, as at Santa Maria Novella in Florence. Baroque facades introduced dramatic curves and sculptural effects, exemplified by the churches of Borromini and Bernini in Rome.

Modern facades

The steel frame and curtain wall transformed facade design in the twentieth century: the exterior skin no longer carried structural loads and could be made largely of glass, as in the work of Mies van der Rohe. Contemporary facade engineering addresses energy performance, shading, ventilation and maintenance, with systems such as double-skin facades and ventilated rainscreens. Building codes in many cities regulate the facades of historic districts, and "facadism" — preserving only the front of a historic building while redeveloping behind it — is a debated conservation practice.

Figurative use

By extension, a facade is an outward appearance maintained to conceal a less pleasant reality — "a facade of respectability" — and the term is used in computing for the facade pattern, a software design pattern that provides a simplified interface to a complex subsystem.
