An agar plate is a Petri dish containing a growth medium solidified with agar, used to culture and isolate microorganisms such as bacteria and fungi. The dish is partially filled with warm liquid medium, which gels as it cools and provides a stable, nutrient-bearing surface on which microbes form visible colonies.

A streaked agar plate showing isolated bacterial colonies along the streak lines.

History

Solid culture media were pioneered in Robert Koch's laboratory in the early 1880s. Koch initially used gelatin, which melted at incubation temperatures and was digested by some bacteria. In 1882 Fanny Hesse, wife of Koch's associate Walther Hesse, suggested agar — a gelling agent from red algae long used in East Asian cooking — which remains solid at body temperature and resists microbial digestion. The covered glass dish introduced by Julius Richard Petri in 1887 completed the modern agar plate.

Composition and types

A basic plate contains water, agar (about 1.5% by weight) and nutrients such as peptone and beef or yeast extract. Formulations are tailored to purpose:

  • General-purpose media, such as nutrient agar and tryptic soy agar, support a wide range of organisms.
  • Enriched media, such as blood agar and chocolate agar, contain added blood or growth factors for fastidious bacteria.
  • Selective media, such as MacConkey agar, contain inhibitors that suppress unwanted organisms.
  • Differential media contain indicators that distinguish organisms by their metabolic reactions, for example lactose fermentation.

Use

Plates are inoculated by streaking a sample across the surface with a sterile loop so that individual cells are separated and grow into discrete colonies, each descended from a single cell or cluster. Plates are typically incubated upside down to prevent condensation dripping onto the surface. Colony appearance — size, shape, colour and haemolysis — aids identification, and isolated colonies can be picked for further testing, antibiotic-susceptibility assays or molecular analysis. Agar plates remain a foundational tool in clinical diagnostics, food safety, environmental monitoring and research, and are also the medium of agar art.
