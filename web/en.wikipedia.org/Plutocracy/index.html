Plutocracy

Plutocracy denotes rule by the wealthy, directly or through influence.

Plutocracy is a society or system of government in which power is controlled by the wealthy, either directly or indirectly. The term combines the Greek ploutos (wealth) and kratos (power, rule). Unlike an oligarchy, which is rule by any small elite, a plutocracy is defined specifically by the concentration of power in the hands of those with wealth. No modern state formally styles itself a plutocracy; the word is almost always used critically, to argue that economic inequality has translated into unequal political influence.

Mechanisms

Wealth can translate into political power through campaign contributions and lobbying, ownership of media, funding of think tanks and advocacy groups, revolving doors between government and business, and the structural dependence of governments on private investment and employment. Political scientists who use the concept distinguish formal plutocracy — property qualifications for voting or office, common in early modern states — from informal plutocracy, where formally equal citizens have markedly unequal influence over policy. Studies of policy responsiveness, such as the 2014 analysis by Gilens and Page of United States policy-making, have been cited in debates over whether wealthy minorities exercise disproportionate influence, though their conclusions are contested.

Historical examples

Historians have applied the label to merchant republics such as Venice and Genoa, to the Roman Republic in its later phase, to the Carthaginian state, and to industrial-era polities where financiers and industrialists dominated — including Gilded Age America and, in contemporary discussion, post-Soviet "oligarchs" in Russia and Ukraine. The term gained wider currency after the financial crisis of 2008 and in discussions of rising wealth concentration documented by economists such as Thomas Piketty.

Related concepts

Plutocracy is related to, but distinct from, kleptocracy (rule by thieves, in which rulers use office to steal), timocracy (rule by property owners, in Plato's classification), corporatocracy (rule dominated by corporations), and meritocracy (advancement by ability). Critics of the term argue that it can be polemical, since wealth and political influence correlate imperfectly and wealthy groups often disagree among themselves.
