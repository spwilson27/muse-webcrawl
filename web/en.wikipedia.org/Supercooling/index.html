Supercooling

Supercooling, also known as undercooling, is the process of lowering the temperature of a liquid below its freezing point without it becoming a solid. A liquid cooled below its standard freezing point will crystallize in the presence of a seed crystal or nucleus around which a crystal structure can form; in the absence of such nuclei, the liquid phase can be maintained far below the normal freezing point.

Mechanism

Freezing normally begins by nucleation at impurities, container walls, or seed crystals. Very pure, still water can be supercooled to about −42 °C before homogeneous nucleation occurs spontaneously. Disturbing a supercooled liquid, or adding a seed, triggers rapid crystallization, releasing latent heat and warming the mixture back toward the freezing point.

Examples and applications

Supercooled water droplets are common in clouds and are important in aircraft icing and precipitation physics. Some animals survive cold climates using antifreeze proteins that exploit or resist supercooling. Sodium acetate hand warmers rely on a supercooled solution that crystallizes on demand, releasing stored heat.
