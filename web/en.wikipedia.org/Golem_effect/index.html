Golem effect


Low expectations shaping performance.

The Golem effect is a psychological phenomenon in which lower expectations placed upon individuals — by supervisors, teachers or the individuals themselves — lead to poorer performance. It is the negative counterpart of the Pygmalion effect, in which high expectations improve performance.

Origin

The effect is named after the Golem of Jewish folklore, a creature animated from inanimate matter whose nature is shaped by its creator. The term was introduced in research on expectancy effects in organisations and classrooms, following Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson's famous 1968 study of teacher expectations ("Pygmalion in the Classroom").

Mechanisms

Studies by Dov Eden and others in military and workplace settings found that leaders who hold low expectations communicate them through colder behaviour, less feedback, fewer learning opportunities and less challenging assignments, which in turn depress motivation and output — a self-fulfilling prophecy. In education, the Golem effect is a concern in ability grouping and tracking, where placement in low groups can entrench low achievement. Awareness training for managers and teachers is proposed as a countermeasure.
