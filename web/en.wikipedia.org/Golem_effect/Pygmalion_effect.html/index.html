Pygmalion effect

The Pygmalion effect, or Rosenthal effect, is a psychological phenomenon in which higher expectations lead to an increase in performance: people tend to perform as expected of them. It is named after Pygmalion, the sculptor in Greek myth whose statue came to life, and was demonstrated in education by Robert Rosenthal and Lenore Jacobson's 1968 study Pygmalion in the Classroom, in which pupils randomly labelled as likely to "bloom" subsequently gained more IQ points than controls.

Its negative counterpart is the Golem effect, in which low expectations lead to poorer performance. Both effects are forms of self-fulfilling prophecy, and have been studied in classrooms, workplaces and clinical settings, raising ethical concerns about labelling and bias.
