Depraved-heart murder

In United States law, depraved-heart murder, also known as depraved-indifference murder, is a type of murder where an individual acts with a "depraved heart": a mind that disregards the sanctity of human life. It is characterized by conduct that is extremely reckless and creates a grave risk of death, even though the defendant did not specifically intend to kill.

Depraved-heart murder is prosecuted as implied-malice murder in several US states

Doctrine

At common law, murder requires malice aforethought, which may be "implied" from conduct so dangerous and indifferent to life that the law treats it as equivalent to intent. Classic examples include firing a gun into a crowded train or room, or playing "Russian roulette" with another person. Where recognized, depraved-heart murder is typically classified as second-degree murder.

State variations

The doctrine survives, under various names, in states such as Maryland, New York ("depraved indifference to human life"), and California (implied-malice second-degree murder). Some jurisdictions have narrowed it: New York's Court of Appeals ruled in People v. Feingold (2006) that depraved indifference is a culpable mental state rather than a mere circumstance, reshaping prosecutions there.

Notable cases

The concept drew wide attention in the 2015 Baltimore case of Freddie Gray, in which one officer was charged with second-degree depraved-heart murder (the charge did not result in conviction). It is also regularly applied to fatal cases of extreme reckless driving and to deaths caused by reckless handling of firearms.
