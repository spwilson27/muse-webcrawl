Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000

Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000
Act of Parliament
Parliament of the United Kingdom
Citation: 2000 c. 37
Royal assent: 30 November 2000
Status: Amended

The Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 (c. 37), known as the CRoW Act and popularly as the "right to roam" act, is an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that came into force in England and Wales in stages between 2001 and 2005. It implements the statutory right of access, on foot, to certain categories of uncultivated land: registered common land, mountain, moor, heath, and down — collectively "access land."

Provisions

Part I of the Act grants a general right of access for open-air recreation on foot over mapped access land, subject to restrictions such as keeping dogs on short leads near livestock and observance of closures for land management and conservation. The right does not extend to cycling, horse riding, or camping, which remain governed by other law. Later parts of the Act strengthened the protection of Sites of Special Scientific Interest (SSSIs), increased penalties for wildlife offences, improved the law on public rights of way, and gave Areas of Outstanding Natural Beauty (AONBs) conservation boards and management plans.

Implementation

The Countryside Agency and the Countryside Council for Wales mapped more than 865,000 hectares of access land in England alone. The final maps were completed in 2005, when the right came into force nationwide. Landowners initially feared damage and liability, but studies generally found few problems, and the right became a settled feature of countryside law.

Background

The Act fulfilled a long-standing campaign by walkers' organizations, from the Kinder Scout mass trespass of 1932 to the lobbying of the Ramblers' Association, and honoured a Labour Party manifesto commitment. Scotland adopted a broader access regime in the Land Reform (Scotland) Act 2003.
