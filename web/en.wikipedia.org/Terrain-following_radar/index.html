Terrain-following radar

Terrain-following radar (TFR) is a military aerospace technology that allows a very-low-flying aircraft to automatically maintain a relatively constant altitude above the terrain, following its contours. A radar in the aircraft scans the ground ahead and sends commands to the autopilot to climb or descend so as to clear obstacles while staying as low as possible — often at 30 to 60 metres (100 to 200 ft) — to evade enemy radar detection and surface-to-air missiles.

Operation

The classic TFR scans a radar beam in a vertical arc ahead of the aircraft, building a profile of the highest terrain in its path. The system computes a desired flight path that "skis" over obstacles with a set clearance height chosen by the crew, balancing ride quality against concealment, and couples directly to the flight-control system so the aircraft flies the terrain essentially hands-off, even at night and in cloud. Because the radar radiates, modern systems are often combined or replaced with passive terrain-referenced navigation using digital elevation databases (TERCOM/TERPROM) and GPS, and some aircraft use low-probability-of-intercept radar modes.

History and aircraft

Terrain-following radar emerged in the 1950s–60s as air defences made high-altitude penetration suicidal. It equipped aircraft including the General Dynamics F-111 (a pioneer of the fully automatic system), the Panavia Tornado, the F-15E Strike Eagle (via the LANTIRN navigation pod), the B-1B Lancer, and the Sukhoi Su-24. Flying at high speed and very low level in all weathers imposed heavy structural and crew workloads, and TFR-equipped bombers formed the backbone of NATO and Warsaw Pact strike plans in the Cold War. With the advent of stealth aircraft and stand-off precision weapons, low-level penetration became less central, but terrain following remains standard for special-operations and strike aircraft.
