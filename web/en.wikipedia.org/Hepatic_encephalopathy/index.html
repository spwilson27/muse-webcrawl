Hepatic encephalopathy

Hepatic encephalopathy

SpecialtyGastroenterology, hepatology

Hepatic encephalopathy (HE) is a decline in brain function that occurs as a result of severe liver disease. In advanced stages it is called hepatic coma. The failing liver cannot remove toxic substances — above all ammonia produced by gut bacteria — from the blood, and blood bypassing the liver through collateral vessels (portosystemic shunts) delivers these toxins to the brain, where they disturb neurotransmission and cause astrocyte swelling.

Signs and symptoms

HE spans a spectrum graded by the West Haven criteria, from grade 0 (minimal/covert, detectable only on psychometric testing) through grade 1 (mild confusion, reversed sleep cycle, irritability), grade 2 (lethargy, disorientation, asterixis — a flapping tremor of the outstretched hands), grade 3 (somnolence, gross confusion) to grade 4 (coma). Personality change, poor concentration and constructional apraxia may appear early.

Precipitants

Episodes in patients with cirrhosis are usually triggered by an aggravating factor: gastrointestinal bleeding, infection (especially spontaneous bacterial peritonitis), constipation, dehydration, electrolyte disturbances (particularly low potassium), sedative drugs, excess dietary protein loads, or kidney failure. Identifying and treating the precipitant is a cornerstone of management.

Treatment

First-line therapy is lactulose, a non-absorbable disaccharide that acidifies the colon and traps ammonium, dosed to produce two to three soft stools daily; it may be given as an enema in patients who cannot swallow. The poorly absorbed antibiotic rifaximin is added to prevent recurrence. Protein restriction is no longer generally recommended, as it worsens malnutrition. Definitive treatment of refractory HE in cirrhosis is liver transplantation. In acute liver failure, cerebral edema and raised intracranial pressure are the major dangers.

Prognosis

The development of overt HE marks decompensated liver disease and carries a poor prognosis, with one-year survival often quoted below 50% without transplantation; episodes also predict further recurrence and hospitalisation.
