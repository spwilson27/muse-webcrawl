Solid oxide fuel cell

A solid oxide fuel cell (SOFC) is an electrochemical conversion device that produces electricity directly from oxidizing a fuel. It is distinguished by its electrolyte, a solid ceramic material that conducts oxide ions (O2−) at high temperature. SOFCs operate at very high temperatures, typically between 600 and 1,000 °C, which allows them to use a variety of fuels and achieve high electrical efficiencies.

Principle of operation

At the porous cathode, oxygen from air is reduced to oxide ions, which migrate through the dense ceramic electrolyte to the anode. There they react with a fuel such as hydrogen, carbon monoxide, or natural gas (internally reformed), producing water (and carbon dioxide), releasing electrons that flow through an external circuit to do work before returning to the cathode. The electrolyte is commonly yttria-stabilized zirconia (YSZ); the anode is typically a nickel–YSZ cermet, and the cathode a perovskite such as lanthanum strontium manganite (LSM).

Advantages

Because they operate at high temperature, SOFCs do not require precious-metal catalysts, can internally reform hydrocarbon fuels, and produce high-quality waste heat suitable for combined heat and power (CHP) or for driving a gas turbine in hybrid cycles, giving system efficiencies of 60% or more. They are fuel-flexible and emit far less pollution than combustion engines.

Challenges

The high operating temperature imposes long start-up times, demands expensive heat-resistant materials, and causes degradation through thermal cycling, chromium poisoning of cathodes, and sulfur contamination of anodes. Research on intermediate-temperature SOFCs (500–750 °C) uses alternative electrolytes such as gadolinia-doped ceria to reduce these problems.

Applications

SOFCs are used for stationary power generation, from residential micro-CHP units of about 1 kW to multi-megawatt installations. Companies such as Bloom Energy have deployed SOFC servers for commercial customers and data centers. SOFCs have also been demonstrated as auxiliary power units for vehicles and in portable military applications. Run in reverse, the same technology acts as a solid oxide electrolyzer to produce hydrogen.
