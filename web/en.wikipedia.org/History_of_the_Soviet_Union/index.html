History of the Soviet Union

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The history of the Soviet Union (USSR) spans from its founding in December 1922, in the aftermath of the Russian Revolution and Civil War, to its dissolution in December 1991. The Soviet Union became one of the two superpowers of the post-1945 world, transforming a largely agrarian empire into an industrial and military giant at enormous human cost, defeating Nazi Germany in alliance with the West, and then confronting the United States in the Cold War before collapsing under the weight of economic stagnation and reform.

Revolution and founding (1917–1927)

The October Revolution of 1917 brought Lenin's Bolsheviks to power; the ensuing Civil War (1918–21) against White armies and foreign intervention devastated the country. War Communism gave way in 1921 to the New Economic Policy (NEP), a partial retreat to markets. On 30 December 1922 the Russian, Ukrainian, Byelorussian, and Transcaucasian republics formed the Union of Soviet Socialist Republics. After Lenin's death in 1924, Joseph Stalin outmanoeuvred rivals including Leon Trotsky, Bukharin, Zinoviev, and Kamenev to become undisputed leader by the late 1920s.

Stalinism (1927–1953)

Stalin's "Great Turn" launched forced collectivisation of agriculture and breakneck industrialisation through Five-Year Plans. Collectivisation and the liquidation of the kulaks caused catastrophic famine—above all the Holodomor in Ukraine (1932–33)—killing millions across the USSR. The Great Terror of 1936–38 executed some 700,000 people and filled the Gulag camp system. Having first signed the Molotov–Ribbentrop Pact with Nazi Germany (1939) and annexed eastern Poland, the Baltic states, and other territories, the USSR was invaded by Germany in June 1941. The Great Patriotic War cost the USSR an estimated 26–27 million lives; Soviet victories at Stalingrad (1943) and Kursk turned the tide, and the Red Army took Berlin in May 1945. Post-war, the USSR annexed further territory, imposed communist governments across Eastern Europe, tested an atomic bomb in 1949, and entered the Cold War. Stalin died in March 1953.

Thaw and developed socialism (1953–1985)

Nikita Khrushchev's "Secret Speech" of 1956 denounced Stalin's crimes and began de-Stalinisation and the "Thaw". His era saw the launch of Sputnik (1957) and Yuri Gagarin's first human spaceflight (1961), the Virgin Lands campaign, a housing boom—and the Cuban Missile Crisis of 1962, which brought the world close to nuclear war. Khrushchev was ousted in 1964 by Leonid Brezhnev, whose long tenure (1964–82) brought rising living standards, détente with the West, and superpower parity, but also economic stagnation, suppression of dissent (the 1968 invasion of Czechoslovakia ended the Prague Spring), and the disastrous invasion of Afghanistan in 1979. Brief successions by Yuri Andropov and Konstantin Chernenko followed.

Perestroika and dissolution (1985–1991)

Mikhail Gorbachev, chosen in 1985, pursued glasnost (openness) and perestroika (restructuring) to revive the system; reforms instead unleashed national movements, economic disruption, and political pluralism. The Chernobyl disaster (1986) exposed systemic failures. Gorbachev ended the Afghan war, signed arms-control treaties, and declined to prop up Eastern European regimes, which fell in 1989; Germany reunified in 1990. Within the USSR, republics declared sovereignty; a failed hardline coup in August 1991 fatally weakened the centre, and on 25 December 1991 Gorbachev resigned and the USSR ceased to exist, succeeded by fifteen independent states, with Russia inheriting its UN Security Council seat and nuclear arsenal.

Legacy

The Soviet Union industrialised a continent, achieved universal literacy and major scientific feats, and played the decisive land role in defeating Nazism, but at the cost of tens of millions of deaths from famine, terror, and war, and a legacy of environmental damage and political repression. Assessments of the Soviet experiment remain fiercely contested in Russia and the successor states.
