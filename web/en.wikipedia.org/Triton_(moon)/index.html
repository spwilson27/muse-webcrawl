Triton is the largest natural satellite of the planet Neptune and the seventh-largest moon in the Solar System. It was discovered by the English astronomer William Lassell on 10 October 1846, just 17 days after Neptune itself. It is named after Triton, the son of Poseidon in Greek mythology.

Orbit and origin

Triton is unique among large moons in having a retrograde orbit — it circles Neptune opposite to the planet's rotation — and its orbit is tilted significantly to Neptune's equator. These features indicate that Triton did not form around Neptune but is a captured dwarf planet from the Kuiper belt, similar in composition to Pluto. Its capture may have disrupted Neptune's original satellite system.

Surface and geology

Voyager 2, the only spacecraft to visit Triton (in 1989), found a remarkably young, sparsely cratered surface covered in nitrogen, methane and carbon monoxide ices. The southern hemisphere is dominated by a pinkish "cantaloupe terrain" of dimpled frozen lakes, and the probe observed active geyser-like plumes erupting nitrogen gas up to 8 km high — the first active volcanic eruptions seen beyond Earth involving volatile ices. Triton is one of the coldest measured bodies in the Solar System, with a surface temperature near 38 K.

Future

Tidal interactions are slowing Triton and drawing it inward; in roughly 3.6 billion years it is expected to pass within Neptune's Roche limit and be torn apart, forming a ring system.
