Smalltalk is a purely object-oriented programming language and integrated development environment developed at Xerox PARC in the 1970s by a team led by Alan Kay, Dan Ingalls, and Adele Goldberg. It was designed as part of the Dynabook vision of a personal computer for learning, and it pioneered many concepts now standard in software: the graphical user interface with overlapping windows, the model–view–controller pattern, and live, image-based development.

Design

In Smalltalk, everything is an object—including numbers, classes, and blocks of code—and computation proceeds entirely by sending messages to objects. Even control structures are implemented as messages: aBoolean ifTrue: [ ... ]. The language has only a handful of reserved words (true, false, nil, self, super, thisContext) and a highly regular syntax readable, in Kay's words, "on one page".

factorial
    "Answer the factorial of the receiver."
    self = 0 ifTrue: [^ 1].
    ^ self * (self - 1) factorial

Influence

Smalltalk-80, publicly released in 1980 and showcased in a famous 1981 Byte magazine issue, profoundly influenced later languages: Objective-C borrowed its message-passing model, Java its virtual machine and garbage collection, and Ruby its "everything is an object" purity. Modern implementations include Squeak, Pharo, and the commercial VisualWorks and VA Smalltalk. The Smalltalk environment also gave rise to extreme programming practices and the first unit-testing frameworks (SUnit, ancestor of JUnit).
