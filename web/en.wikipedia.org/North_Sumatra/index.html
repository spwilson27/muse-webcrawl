North Sumatra (Indonesian: Sumatera Utara) is a province of Indonesia located on the northern part of the island of Sumatra, facing the Strait of Malacca to the east and the Indian Ocean to the west. Its capital and largest city is Medan, Indonesia's third-largest city and the principal gateway to the country's west. The province is known for Lake Toba, one of the world's largest volcanic lakes, formed by a supervolcanic eruption some 74,000 years ago.

Geography

The Barisan Mountains run along the province's western spine, dropping eastward to a broad coastal plain along the Strait of Malacca. Lake Toba — about 100 km long and 30 km wide, with Samosir Island at its centre — dominates the highlands and is a major tourist destination. North Sumatra also includes the island of Nias and other offshore islands in the Indian Ocean, notable for traditional megalithic culture and surfing.

People and history

The province is home to the Batak peoples (Toba, Karo, Pakpak, Simalungun, Angkola and Mandailing), many of whom are Christian or Muslim, as well as coastal Malays, a large Javanese population descended from plantation labourers, and long-established Chinese Indonesian communities. Under Dutch colonial rule the east coast became one of the world's great plantation belts, producing tobacco (Deli tobacco, prized for cigar wrappers), rubber and palm oil.

Economy

Agriculture and plantation products — palm oil, rubber, coffee, cocoa and tobacco — remain central, alongside manufacturing around Medan and the port of Belawan, and growing tourism centred on Lake Toba, the orangutan rehabilitation centre at Bukit Lawang, and Nias. The province was struck by the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami (chiefly via Nias and the 2005 Nias–Simeulue earthquake) but its east coast was largely spared.
