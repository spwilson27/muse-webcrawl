The British Raj (from Hindi rāj, "reign") was the rule of the British Crown on the Indian subcontinent from 1858 to 1947. The region under British control was commonly called India and included areas directly administered by Britain (British India) as well as the princely states ruled by individual rulers under British paramountcy. At its height it was one of the largest empires in history, covering the territories of modern India, Pakistan and Bangladesh.

Establishment

British power in India had been built by the East India Company since the Battle of Plassey (1757). After the Indian Rebellion of 1857, the Government of India Act 1858 dissolved the Company and transferred its possessions and functions to the Crown. Queen Victoria was proclaimed Empress of India in 1876. Governance ran through the Secretary of State for India in London, the Viceroy in India, and the elite Indian Civil Service.

Administration and economy

The Raj built extensive railways (eventually one of the world's largest networks), telegraphs, canals and ports, unified much of the subcontinent administratively, and codified law. Critics, beginning with Indian nationalists such as Dadabhai Naoroji and his "drain of wealth" thesis, argued that Britain impoverished India, deindustrialized its textile sector and neglected famine relief; catastrophic famines, including the Bengal famine of 1943, killed millions. Defenders of the Raj pointed to infrastructure, legal institutions and political unification.

Nationalism and independence

The Indian National Congress (founded 1885) and the Muslim League (1906) led a growing independence movement. Under Mahatma Gandhi, mass campaigns of non-cooperation (1920–22), civil disobedience (1930–34) and the Quit India movement (1942) mobilised millions. After the Second World War, the exhausted British government of Clement Attlee moved to withdraw.

Partition and end

The Indian Independence Act 1947 partitioned British India into two dominions, India and Pakistan, on 14–15 August 1947. Partition displaced between 10 and 20 million people and unleashed communal violence in which up to two million may have died. The princely states acceded to one or other dominion, ending the Raj.
