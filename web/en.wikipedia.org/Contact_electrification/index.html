Contact electrification

Contact electrification
FieldElectrostatics; surface physics
Also known asTriboelectric effect (when friction is involved)
PhenomenonCharge transfer between materials on contact and separation

Contact electrification is the phenomenon in which two materials acquire opposite electric charges after being brought into contact and then separated. It is the underlying mechanism of much of static electricity: rubbing is not strictly required, but friction increases the true contact area and therefore the amount of charge transferred, in which case the effect is called the triboelectric effect.

History

The effect has been known since antiquity — the Greek word for amber, elektron, gives electricity its name, since rubbed amber attracts light objects. In the 18th century researchers arranged materials into a "triboelectric series" according to the sign and strength of the charge they acquire. Despite this long history, the microscopic mechanism remains incompletely understood.

Mechanism

For metals and semiconductors, charge transfer is explained by electrons flowing until the materials' Fermi levels equalise, governed by their work functions. For insulators, proposed mechanisms include electron transfer between surface states, transfer of ions (such as H+ and OH− from adsorbed water), and transfer of microscopic amounts of material itself. Humidity, surface roughness, strain and prior charge history all influence the outcome, and even contact between two pieces of the same material can produce charging.

Applications and hazards

Controlled contact electrification is essential to xerography (photocopiers and laser printers), powder coating and electrostatic separation. Uncontrolled, it causes nuisance shocks, clinging fabrics, and dangerous sparks that can ignite flammable vapours or damage electronics, motivating anti-static measures in industry.
