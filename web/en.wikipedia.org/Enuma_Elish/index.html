Enuma Elish

The Enūma Eliš (Akkadian Cuneiform: 𒂖𒉡𒌕𒂊𒇺, also spelled "Enuma Elish") is the Babylonian creation myth, named from its opening words, "When on high". Written in Akkadian on seven clay tablets, each of 100–200 lines, it was composed probably in the late second millennium BC and is the longest surviving Akkadian text besides the Epic of Gilgamesh.

Summary

The epic begins with the primordial waters Apsu (fresh water) and Tiamat (salt water), from whom the generations of gods descend. When the younger gods disturb Apsu, he plots their destruction and is killed by Ea. Tiamat, enraged, raises an army of monsters led by Kingu. The god Marduk of Babylon agrees to champion the gods on condition of supreme authority; he defeats Tiamat, splits her body to form the heavens and the earth, fixes the stations of the stars, and creates humankind from Kingu's blood to serve the gods. The epic ends with the building of Babylon and Marduk's temple, Esagila, and a catalogue of Marduk's fifty names.

Discovery and significance

The text was recovered in fragmentary form from the Library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh by Austen Henry Layard and Hormuzd Rassam in the mid-nineteenth century and first published by George Smith in 1876. Its parallels with the creation account in Genesis — primordial waters, the division of the waters, the ordered creation — made it central to debates on the relationship between Babylonian and biblical traditions.
