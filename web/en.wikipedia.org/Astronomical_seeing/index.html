Astronomical seeing


Turbulence blurs the image of a star into a seeing disc.

Astronomical seeing is the distortion of astronomical images caused by turbulence in the Earth's atmosphere. It blurs the light of stars into discs of typically 0.5 to 2 arcseconds across and is the chief limit on the sharpness of images from ground-based telescopes, regardless of their aperture.

Causes and measurement

Seeing arises because pockets of air at differing temperatures have different refractive indices, continually deflecting incoming starlight. The effect is quantified by the Fried parameter r0 — often only 10–20 cm at visible wavelengths at good sites — and by the full width at half maximum of the stellar image. The Antoniadi scale and other empirical scales were historically used by visual observers.

Mitigation

Observatories are sited on high, dry mountain tops above the worst turbulence, such as Mauna Kea and the Atacama Desert. Techniques to overcome seeing include speckle interferometry, lucky imaging, and above all adaptive optics, which uses deformable mirrors driven by wavefront sensors to correct the distortion in real time.
