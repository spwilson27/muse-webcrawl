Rigid body dynamics

Rigid body dynamics is the branch of classical mechanics that studies the motion of systems of interconnected bodies under external forces, treating each body as rigid: the distance between any two of its points remains constant, so it does not deform. The assumption simplifies analysis enormously and is a good approximation for most solid objects in ordinary motion.

Rotation of a rigid body is described by its angular velocity and inertia tensor.

Kinematics

The configuration of a rigid body is fixed by the position of its centre of mass and its orientation, giving six degrees of freedom in three dimensions. The velocity of any point combines the translational velocity of the centre of mass with the rotational contribution ω × r, where ω is the angular velocity.

Kinetics

Linear motion of the centre of mass obeys Newton's second law. Rotational motion is governed by the rate of change of angular momentum about the centre of mass. Resistance to angular acceleration is quantified by the inertia tensor; in a body-fixed frame aligned with the principal axes, the equations of motion take the form of Euler's equations, which famously predict phenomena such as torque-free precession and the instability of rotation about the intermediate axis (the Dzhanibekov effect).

Applications

Rigid body dynamics underlies robotics, vehicle and spacecraft dynamics, biomechanics, computer animation and video-game physics engines, where fast approximate simulation of rigid bodies and their collisions is a core technology.
