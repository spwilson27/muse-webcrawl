Raman scattering

Raman scattering or the Raman effect is the inelastic scattering of photons by matter. When light of a single frequency strikes a material, most photons are elastically scattered (Rayleigh scattering), but a small fraction — roughly one in ten million — exchange energy with the material's vibrational or rotational modes and emerge at shifted frequencies. The phenomenon was discovered by the Indian physicist C. V. Raman and his student K. S. Krishnan in 1928; Raman received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1930 for the work.

Stokes and anti-Stokes scattering

If the scattered photon loses energy to a molecular vibration, it is shifted to a lower frequency (longer wavelength); this is called Stokes scattering. If it gains energy from an already-excited vibration, it shifts to a higher frequency, giving weaker anti-Stokes lines. The magnitude of the shift, expressed in wavenumbers (cm−1), equals the vibrational frequency and is independent of the incident wavelength.

Raman spectroscopy

The spectrum of Raman shifts forms a molecular fingerprint. Raman spectroscopy is used to identify chemicals, characterise materials, study crystal structure, monitor pharmaceutical production and analyse artworks and forensic samples. Because water scatters weakly, aqueous samples can be measured readily. Advances such as surface-enhanced Raman scattering (SERS), coherent anti-Stokes Raman scattering (CARS) and tip-enhanced methods have greatly increased sensitivity, the latter reaching single-molecule detection.

Theory

In classical terms, the incident electric field induces an oscillating dipole whose strength depends on the molecular polarisability; vibrations that modulate the polarisability produce sidebands at the shifted frequencies. Quantum mechanically, scattering proceeds via virtual intermediate states. Selection rules differ from infrared absorption, making the two techniques complementary.
