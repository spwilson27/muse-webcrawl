Ride-hailing

Ride-hailing (also called ride-sourcing) is a service in which passengers book and pay for a private driver through a mobile app or website, typically offered by a transportation network company (TNC). Unlike traditional taxis, ride-hailing drivers generally use their own vehicles, cannot be hailed on the street in most jurisdictions, and are matched to riders by an app that also provides upfront or metered pricing, GPS tracking, driver and passenger ratings, and cashless payment. Major providers include Uber, Lyft, DiDi, Grab, Ola, and Bolt.

The industry grew explosively after Uber's launch in San Francisco in 2010 (as "UberCab," founded 2009), and spread to hundreds of cities worldwide, reshaping urban transport markets and provoking sustained controversy over regulation, driver classification, congestion, and the future of the taxi industry.

How it works

A rider enters a destination in the app; the platform quotes a fare (in many markets an upfront price), matches the request to a nearby available driver, and tracks the trip in real time. Fares may include dynamic ("surge") pricing, which raises prices when demand exceeds supply to attract more drivers. After the trip, both parties may rate each other; low-rated drivers can be removed from the platform, and riders may likewise be deactivated. Companies typically take a commission (the "take rate") of roughly 15–30 percent of the fare, with the remainder going to the driver, who bears vehicle, fuel, and insurance costs.

History

Earlier forms of pre-booked private hire — radio-dispatched minicabs and car services — anticipated the model, but the smartphone, GPS, and app-based payment made matching nearly frictionless. Uber expanded internationally from 2011; Lyft launched in 2012 with a peer-to-peer emphasis; regional champions came to dominate many markets, including DiDi in China (which acquired Uber's China operations in 2016), Grab in Southeast Asia, and Ola in India. Services expanded into food delivery, shared rides (later curtailed after COVID-19), freight, and partnerships with public transit, and companies invested heavily in autonomous vehicles.

Regulation and legal status

Cities and countries have responded with licensing regimes for TNCs, background-check and insurance requirements, airport and congestion fees, data-sharing mandates, and in some places caps on vehicle numbers (New York City, 2018) or outright bans later reversed. Taxi drivers and unions have protested what they regard as unfair competition from less-regulated rivals. In the European Union and elsewhere, courts have ruled that platforms such as Uber are transport services rather than mere information-society intermediaries, subjecting them to transport regulation.

Effects and criticism

Studies of ride-hailing's impacts are mixed: the services improve convenience, serve neglected areas, and may reduce drunk driving, but researchers have found they add vehicle miles travelled, draw riders from public transit, and worsen congestion in dense cities. Critics also cite precarious earnings, algorithmic management and opaque deactivation, safety incidents, data privacy, and discrimination risks in ratings. Supporters emphasize consumer benefit, flexible earning opportunities, and service to people poorly served by taxis.

Driver classification debates

Because platforms classify drivers as independent contractors, drivers generally lack minimum-wage guarantees, overtime, unemployment insurance, and collective bargaining rights. Landmark disputes include California's AB5 law (2019) classifying drivers as employees and the industry-backed Proposition 22 (2020) carving out app-based drivers; the UK Supreme Court's 2021 ruling that Uber drivers are "workers" entitled to minimum wage and holiday pay; and the EU's Platform Work Directive. These battles remain central to the wider gig-economy debate.
