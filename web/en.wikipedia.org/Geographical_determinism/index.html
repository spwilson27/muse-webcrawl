Environmental determinism

Environmental determinism, also known as geographical determinism or climatic determinism, is the doctrine that the physical environment — especially climate and terrain — determines the patterns of human culture and societal development. It was a dominant approach in geography and anthropology from the late nineteenth century into the early twentieth.

History

Ideas linking climate to temperament go back to Hippocrates' Airs, Waters, Places and were developed by thinkers such as Ibn Khaldun and Montesquieu, who argued in The Spirit of the Laws (1748) that climate shapes laws and customs. As an academic doctrine it is associated with Friedrich Ratzel and his student Ellen Churchill Semple, and with Ellsworth Huntington, who argued that temperate climates produced energetic civilisations while tropical climates induced indolence.

Criticism and alternatives

Environmental determinism fell from favour after the Second World War. It was criticised as reductionist, empirically weak, and as having served to rationalise colonialism and racism by treating European dominance as a natural product of climate. Franz Boas and his students emphasised historical particularism; French geographers under Paul Vidal de la Blache advanced possibilism — the view that the environment offers possibilities that cultures choose among — and Carl Sauer stressed how cultures reshape landscapes. Jared Diamond's Guns, Germs, and Steel (1997) revived popular interest in geographic explanation, while drawing criticism from scholars who argue that institutions and history, not geography alone, drive development.
