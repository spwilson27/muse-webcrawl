HiRISE

High Resolution Imaging Science Experiment is a camera on board the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter (MRO). The 65 kg (143 lb), $40 million instrument was built under the direction of the University of Arizona's Lunar and Planetary Laboratory by Ball Aerospace & Technologies Corp. It consists of a 0.5 m (19.7 in) aperture reflecting telescope, the largest so far of any deep-space mission, which allows it to take pictures of Mars with resolutions of 0.3 m/pixel (about 1 foot), resolving objects below a metre across.

Capabilities

HiRISE images a swath about 6 km wide (at 300 km altitude) in three colour bands: visible red, blue-green, and near-infrared, enabling colour composites over narrower strips. Its images are up to about 20,000 × 60,000 pixels. From orbit it has photographed details such as individual boulders, layered sedimentary deposits, dune migration, slope streaks, and fresh impact craters. Pairs of images of the same terrain taken from slightly different angles allow the production of stereo digital terrain models and anaglyphs.

Operations and results

MRO began its primary science phase in November 2006, and HiRISE has returned hundreds of thousands of images since — but, because each image is so large, it has imaged only a small percentage of Mars's surface at the highest resolution. HiRISE has been essential for scouting and certifying landing sites for the Phoenix lander, the Curiosity and Perseverance rovers, and InSight; it has even photographed landers and rovers on the surface and under parachute during descent, famously capturing Phoenix (2008) and Curiosity (2012) as they parachuted down. Other discoveries include recurring slope lineae (seasonal dark flows), evidence of dry-ice (CO2) processes in polar regions, and avalanches caught in progress on north polar scarps.

The public can suggest targets through the HiWish program, and images are released through the University of Arizona's HiRISE website with descriptive captions written by scientists.
