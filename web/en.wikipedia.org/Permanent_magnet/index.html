Permanent magnet

A permanent magnet is an object made from a material that is magnetised and creates its own persistent magnetic field. In contrast to an electromagnet, whose field exists only while an electric current flows, a permanent magnet's magnetisation remains (ideally indefinitely) once established. Everyday examples include the magnets that hold notes to a refrigerator door.

Materials

Permanent magnets are made of "hard" ferromagnetic or ferrimagnetic materials with high coercivity, so they resist demagnetisation. Important classes include ferrite (ceramic) magnets, cheap and widely used; alnico alloys of iron, aluminium, nickel and cobalt; samarium–cobalt magnets, with excellent temperature stability; and neodymium magnets (NdFeB), the strongest type commercially available, developed in the early 1980s and essential to modern electric motors, headphones and hard disk drives.

Properties

The performance of a permanent magnet material is characterised by its remanence (the flux density remaining after the magnetising field is removed), its coercivity (resistance to demagnetisation), and its maximum energy product (BH)max, a figure of merit for magnetic strength. Magnetisation can be lost by heating above the Curie temperature, by strong opposing fields, or by physical shock.

Uses

Permanent magnets are used in electric motors and generators, loudspeakers and microphones, magnetic bearings and couplings, sensors, medical MRI scanners (in some designs), magnetic separators, compasses and countless consumer products.
