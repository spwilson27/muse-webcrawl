Saramaccan language

Saramaccan (autonym: Saamáka) is an English-based creole language spoken by the Saramaka people, a Maroon community in Suriname and French Guiana. With roughly 58,000 speakers, it is one of the most extensively studied creole languages and is famous among linguists for its large African-derived vocabulary and tonal features.

Origins

The Saramaka descend from Africans who escaped slavery on plantations in Dutch Suriname in the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries and founded independent communities in the rainforest interior. Their language developed from contact between English (the early colonial language), Portuguese (spoken by many plantation owners), Dutch, and West and Central African languages such as Kikongo and Gbe languages. About half of the Saramaccan lexicon is of English origin, with unusually large Portuguese and African components.

Features

Saramaccan has a phonology with lexical tone, a rarity among Atlantic creoles, and its deep divergence from English has made it central to debates about creole genesis — whether creoles arise from universal processes of language creation or from gradual restructuring of their lexifiers. Unlike many creoles, its speakers historically had limited contact with Europeans, preserving an early snapshot of creole formation.
