Andreas Capellanus ("Andrew the Chaplain") was a French cleric of the 12th century, known as the author of De amore ("On Love"), a treatise in Latin usually translated as The Art of Courtly Love, written around 1184–1186, probably at the court of Marie de Champagne in Troyes. Little is known of his life beyond what the work itself implies; he may have served as a chaplain at the royal court of Philip II of France.

De amore

The treatise is divided into three books. The first defines love and presents eight dialogues in which men and women of different social ranks debate how love may be sought and won. The second book describes how love is retained, gives sample love letters and judgments attributed to noblewomen such as Eleanor of Aquitaine and Marie de Champagne, and sets out thirty-one "rules of love" — among them "Marriage is no real excuse for not loving" and "He who is not jealous cannot love." The third book abruptly reverses course, denouncing love and women in conventional medieval misogynist terms and urging the reader to abstinence.

Interpretation and influence

Scholars have long debated whether De amore is a serious manual of courtly love, a clerical satire or ironic game, or a condemnation dressed as a guide. The work closely models Ovid's Ars amatoria. Together with the romance literature of Chrétien de Troyes, it remains a primary source for the modern concept of courtly love, which C. S. Lewis analysed in The Allegory of Love (1936), taking Andreas's codification as foundational.
