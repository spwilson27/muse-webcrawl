Hatshepsut (c. 1507–1458 BC) was the Great Royal Wife of Pharaoh Thutmose II and the fifth pharaoh of the Eighteenth Dynasty of Egypt, ruling first as regent, then as pharaoh in her own right for Thutmose III, her stepson and nephew. She was one of the few women to rule ancient Egypt as pharaoh and among its most successful, reigning for more than twenty years in a period of prosperity and extensive building.

Rise to power

Daughter of Thutmose I, Hatshepsut became regent on her husband's early death for the infant Thutmose III. Within a few years she assumed full royal titles, depicting herself with masculine regalia including the ceremonial false beard while retaining feminine pronouns in inscriptions, and legitimised her rule with a divine-birth narrative carved at Deir el-Bahari.

Reign

Her reign emphasised trade and building rather than conquest: the famous expedition to the Land of Punt, recorded in reliefs, brought back myrrh trees, gold and exotic animals. Her architect and steward Senenmut oversaw an ambitious programme including her mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari — one of the masterpieces of Egyptian architecture — twin obelisks at Karnak, and the Red Chapel. She died around 1458 BC, possibly of a bone cancer that had metastasised, according to study of her identified mummy.

Erasure

Late in Thutmose III's reign her statues were smashed and her names chiselled out of inscriptions — an attempted damnatio memoriae whose motive (vengeance, or restoring conventional succession ideology) scholars still debate. She was largely forgotten until 19th-century decipherment of hieroglyphs restored her story.
