Capsaicin (8-methyl-N-vanillyl-6-nonenamide) is an active component of chili peppers, which are plants belonging to the genus Capsicum. It is a chemical irritant for mammals, including humans, and produces a sensation of burning when it comes into contact with tissue. Capsaicin and related compounds, collectively called capsaicinoids, are secondary metabolites produced by chili peppers probably as deterrents against seed predators and fungi — notably, birds are insensitive to capsaicin and disperse the seeds.

Pure capsaicin rates 16 million Scoville heat units, the top of the scale devised by Wilbur Scoville in 1912. It is concentrated mainly in the placental tissue (the white pith) of the fruit rather than in the seeds themselves.

Mechanism

Capsaicin acts by binding to the TRPV1 receptor, a heat- and pain-sensing ion channel normally activated by temperatures above about 43 °C, acidity and abrasion. Activation opens the channel to an influx of cations, depolarising nociceptive neurons and creating the perception of burning heat even though no thermal damage occurs. Prolonged exposure defunctionalises the nerve endings, which is the basis of capsaicin's therapeutic use as an analgesic for neuropathic pain, arthritis and postherpetic neuralgia in creams and high-dose patches.

Uses

Beyond the kitchen, capsaicin is the active agent in pepper spray and other riot-control and personal-defence formulations, in animal repellents for crops and bird seed, and in antifouling paints. Extracts are standard tools in pain research: neonatal treatment of rodents with capsaicin permanently destroys TRPV1-expressing neurons, and ultrapotent analogues such as resiniferatoxin are used experimentally to ablate pain fibres. The identification of TRPV1 formed part of the work recognised by the 2021 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

History

Capsaicin was first extracted in impure form in 1816 by Christian Friedrich Bucholz and isolated as a crystalline substance in 1876 by John Clough Thresh, who gave it its name. Its structure was determined in 1919 by E. K. Nelson, and it was first synthesised in 1930 by Ernst Späth and Stephen F. Darling.
