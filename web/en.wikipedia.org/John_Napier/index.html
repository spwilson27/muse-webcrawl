John Napier of Merchiston (1 February 1550 – 4 April 1617) was a Scottish landowner, mathematician, physicist and astronomer. He is best known as the inventor of logarithms, published in his Mirifici Logarithmorum Canonis Descriptio (1614), which revolutionised calculation and remained an essential tool of science and navigation for three centuries. He also invented the manual calculating device known as "Napier's bones" and popularised the use of the decimal point.

Life

Napier was born at Merchiston Castle, Edinburgh, and briefly attended the University of St Andrews before travelling in Europe. A committed Protestant, he devoted much of his early writing to theology, publishing A Plaine Discovery of the Whole Revelation of St John (1593), an interpretation of the Book of Revelation that he considered his most important work. He also devised secret inventions for defence against Spain, including burning mirrors and early designs resembling a tank and a submarine.

Logarithms

Napier spent some twenty years constructing his logarithm tables, defining the logarithm kinematically by comparing points moving along lines at different rates. His publication brought immediate fame; Henry Briggs of Gresham College visited him and together they developed the common (base-10) logarithms published after Napier's death. Kepler used the tables in preparing the Rudolphine Tables, and Laplace later remarked that logarithms, by shortening the labour, doubled the life of the astronomer.

Other contributions

His posthumous Rabdologiae (1617) described Napier's bones and other calculating aids, and he contributed formulas in spherical trigonometry known as Napier's analogies. The neper, a unit of logarithmic ratio, is named after him, as is a crater on the Moon.
