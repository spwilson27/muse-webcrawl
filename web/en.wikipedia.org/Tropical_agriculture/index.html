Tropical agriculture

Wet-rice terraces, a mainstay of tropical farming

Tropical agriculture is the cultivation of crops and raising of livestock in the tropics, the region between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn, characterised by high year-round temperatures, abundant (often strongly seasonal) rainfall, and the absence of frost. It supplies much of the world's rice, sugarcane, bananas, cassava, coffee, cocoa, tea, and palm oil.

Environment and constraints

Tropical farming faces distinctive challenges. Intense rainfall leaches nutrients from soils, leaving many — such as the oxisols and ultisols of the humid tropics — acidic, weathered, and low in phosphorus. Warmth and humidity favour a heavy burden of pests, weeds, and plant diseases, and harvested produce spoils quickly. Cropping is shaped by monsoon and wet–dry season patterns, and by risks from tropical cyclones and drought.

Systems and crops

Traditional systems include shifting cultivation (slash-and-burn), in which forest is cleared and cropped for a few years before a long fallow restores fertility; home gardens and agroforestry, where trees, crops, and livestock are combined; and the intensive paddy-rice systems of monsoon Asia, in which flooded terraced fields sustain high yields. Plantation agriculture, developed under colonial rule, produces export commodities such as sugar, rubber, tea, coffee, bananas, and oil palm, alongside smallholder farming of staples including cassava, yams, sorghum, millet, and plantains.

Development

The Green Revolution brought high-yielding semi-dwarf rice and wheat varieties, irrigation, and fertilisers to much of tropical Asia and Latin America from the 1960s, sharply raising yields, though with environmental and social costs. Research bodies of the CGIAR network, such as IRRI (rice) and IITA (tropical Africa), continue breeding for drought tolerance, disease resistance, and climate resilience in tropical staples.
