Femme fatale


The femme fatale of film noir

A femme fatale (French: [fam fatal], literally "fatal woman") is a stock character, usually a villainous seductress who uses her sexuality and charm to manipulate men and entrap them into dangerous or deadly situations. She is a recurring archetype in mythology, literature, art, and film. Early archetypes include Lilith, Circe, Delilah, Salome, and Cleopatra; the figure flourished in 19th-century Romantic and Decadent art and literature — for example in John Keats's poem "La Belle Dame sans Merci" (1819) — and found an iconic form in 20th-century film noir.

In film noir

The femme fatale became a defining figure of 1940s Hollywood film noir, in roles such as Phyllis Dietrichson (Barbara Stanwyck) in Double Indemnity (1944), Kathie Moffat (Jane Greer) in Out of the Past (1947), and the title character of Gilda (1946, played by Rita Hayworth). Such characters typically deceive the male protagonist, who is drawn into crime or murder. Later neo-noir films revived and complicated the type, as in Body Heat (1981) and Basic Instinct (1992).

Interpretation

Feminist critics have read the femme fatale in divergent ways: as a misogynistic fantasy expressing male anxiety about female independence and sexuality — often punished by death at the story's end — or as a subversive figure of female agency who refuses the domestic roles assigned to women. The counterpart male figure, the manipulative seducer, is sometimes called an homme fatal. Closely related character types include the vamp, the maneater, and the spider woman.
