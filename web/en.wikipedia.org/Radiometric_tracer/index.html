Radiometric tracer

A radiometric tracer, also called a radioactive tracer or radiotracer, is a chemical compound in which one or more atoms have been replaced by a radioisotope. Because the tracer is chemically nearly identical to the unlabelled compound, it follows the same pathways in a biological, chemical or physical system, while its radioactive decay allows its movement and distribution to be tracked with radiation detectors. Radiotracers are fundamental tools in medicine, biology, chemistry, hydrology and industry.

Principle

The tracer principle was developed by George de Hevesy in the early twentieth century, work for which he received the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1943. A minute quantity of the labelled substance is introduced into the system; because the amount is so small it does not disturb the system, yet the radiation emitted reveals where the substance goes, how fast it moves, and how it is transformed.

Production

Short-lived tracers are produced in cyclotrons or nuclear reactors, or eluted from generators such as the technetium-99m generator. Common tracers include carbon-14 and tritium for laboratory research, fluorine-18 (as fluorodeoxyglucose, FDG) for positron emission tomography, technetium-99m compounds for gamma-camera imaging, and iodine-131 for thyroid studies.

Applications

In medicine, radiotracers enable diagnostic imaging (PET and SPECT) and metabolic studies. In biology they trace nutrient uptake and molecular pathways; autoradiography uses them to localise substances in tissue sections. In hydrology and industry, tracers track water flow, detect leaks, measure wear in machinery, and follow sediment transport. Radiometric dating, while related, relies on natural decay ratios rather than introduced tracers.

Safety

Tracer activities are kept as low as practicable; short half-lives minimise radiation dose, and handling follows radiological protection rules for unsealed sources.
