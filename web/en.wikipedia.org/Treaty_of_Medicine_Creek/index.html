Treaty of Medicine Creek

The treaty was negotiated at Medicine Creek, near the mouth of the Nisqually River.

Signed26 December 1854
LocationMedicine Creek, Washington Territory
PartiesUnited States; Nisqually, Puyallup, and other tribes
U.S. negotiatorGovernor Isaac Stevens

The Treaty of Medicine Creek was an agreement between the United States and several Native American tribes of the Puget Sound region of western Washington, including the Nisqually, Puyallup, Steilacoom, Squawskin (Squaxin Island), S'Homamish, Stehchass, T'Peeksin, Squi-aitl, and Sa-heh-wamish, negotiated by Washington Territory governor Isaac I. Stevens. It was signed on 26 December 1854 by Stevens and 62 tribal representatives.

Terms

Under the treaty, the tribes ceded some 2.24 million acres (906,500 hectares) of land between Tacoma and Olympia in exchange for three small reservations, cash payments spread over twenty years, and the retention of fishing, hunting and gathering rights "at all usual and accustomed grounds and stations." The reservations initially allotted—especially the small, heavily forested Nisqually reservation, much of it unsuitable for farming—were a particular source of grievance.

Consequences

Dissatisfaction with the treaty, and with settlers' conduct, erupted in the Puget Sound War of 1855–56. The Nisqually leader Leschi, who had refused to sign, was hanged for murder after contested trials in 1858; he was exonerated by a Washington historical court in 2004. In 1856 the reservations were renegotiated and enlarged. A century later, the treaty's fishing-rights clause became the basis for the 1974 Boldt decision, which affirmed that treaty tribes were entitled to up to half the harvestable fish in their accustomed areas.
