Sodomy law

Sodomy law
SubjectCriminal law, LGBT rights
Historical penaltiesCapital punishment, imprisonment, fines
Key casesBowers v. Hardwick (1986); Lawrence v. Texas (2003); Dudgeon v. United Kingdom (1981); Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018)
StatusRepealed or invalidated in most Western countries; still in force in dozens of jurisdictions

A sodomy law is a law that defines certain sexual acts as crimes. Typically extending beyond the biblical notion of sodomy, such statutes have criminalised anal and oral sex, and historically other "unnatural" acts, often applying in practice to sexual activity between men. Although exact definitions vary, sodomy laws have been a central legal mechanism for the persecution of homosexual people and remain so in a number of jurisdictions.

History

Prohibitions derive from religious law and were incorporated into European legal codes: England's Buggery Act of 1533 made buggery a capital felony, a penalty retained until 1861 and followed, from 1885, by "gross indecency" offences used to prosecute Oscar Wilde. British colonial codes exported such provisions across the empire, including Section 377 of the Indian Penal Code (1860), whose descendants survived in many post-colonial states. In the United States, every state had a sodomy statute into the mid-twentieth century, though enforcement against consenting adults in private was comparatively rare.

Decriminalisation

Reform began with the Wolfenden Report (1957) in Britain, leading to partial decriminalisation in England and Wales in 1967. The European Court of Human Rights in Dudgeon v. United Kingdom (1981) held Northern Ireland's law breached the right to privacy. The U.S. Supreme Court upheld Georgia's law in Bowers v. Hardwick (1986), but reversed course in Lawrence v. Texas (2003), striking down the remaining state sodomy laws as violations of the liberty protected by the Due Process Clause. India's Supreme Court read down Section 377 in Navtej Singh Johar (2018), and courts and legislatures in countries including Botswana, Angola, and Singapore have since removed similar laws.

Current status

Consensual same-sex activity remains criminal in roughly sixty countries, with punishments ranging from fines to life imprisonment, and capital punishment prescribed or imposed in a small number of states. International human rights bodies, following the UN Human Rights Committee's decision in Toonen v. Australia (1994), regard such laws as violations of privacy and non-discrimination guarantees.
