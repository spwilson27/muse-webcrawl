Charter 77 (Czech and Slovak: Charta 77) was an informal civic initiative in communist Czechoslovakia, named after the document Charter 77 issued in January 1977. The manifesto criticised the government of Gustáv Husák for failing to respect the human rights its own laws and the 1975 Helsinki Accords guaranteed. It became the moral centre of Czechoslovak dissent and inspired similar movements across the Soviet bloc.

Background

After the Warsaw Pact invasion crushed the Prague Spring in 1968, Husák's regime of "normalization" purged reformists and tightened control. Czechoslovakia nevertheless ratified the International Covenants on human rights, and signed the Helsinki Final Act (1975). The 1976 trial of the underground rock band the Plastic People of the Universe galvanised intellectuals to demand that the regime simply obey its own commitments — the spark for the Charter.

The document and signatories

Drafted principally by Václav Havel and the philosopher Jan Patočka, among others, the Charter insisted it was not an organisation or opposition movement but a "free, informal, open community" of citizens monitoring compliance with the law. Its first spokespersons were Havel, Patočka, and former foreign minister Jiří Hájek. Patočka died in March 1977 after a lengthy police interrogation. The regime responded with a furious propaganda campaign — the "Anti-Charter," which artists and workers were pressed to endorse — and with arrests, job losses, and surveillance of signatories, whose number grew only slowly, reaching some 1,900 by 1989.

Activities

Between 1977 and 1989 the Charter issued hundreds of documents on subjects from environmental damage and religious persecution to the economy and the rights of the Roma. In 1978 its members founded the Committee for the Defence of the Unjustly Persecuted (VONS) to publicise political trials. Signatories helped seed samizdat publishing and sustained links to Polish Solidarity and Western human-rights groups.

Legacy

When the Velvet Revolution swept the regime away in November 1989, Charter signatories stood at its head: Havel became president of Czechoslovakia on 29 December 1989. Charter 77 formally dissolved itself in 1992, its mission judged complete. It remains the archetype of "living in truth" — the strategy, articulated in Havel's essay The Power of the Powerless (1978), of resisting a mendacious system by behaving as if one were free.
