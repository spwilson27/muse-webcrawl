Glutinous rice

Glutinous rice (Oryza sativa var. glutinosa), also called sticky rice or sweet rice, is a type of rice grown mainly in Southeast and East Asia. Its grains are opaque and become characteristically sticky when cooked, because the starch consists almost entirely of amylopectin, with very little amylose. Despite its name, glutinous rice contains no gluten; the name refers to its glue-like texture.

Cultivation and use

Glutinous rice is the staple food of Laos and much of northeastern Thailand (Isan), where it is eaten by hand, rolled into balls and dipped into accompanying dishes. Elsewhere it is more often used in snacks, desserts and festive dishes. Well-known preparations include Japanese mochi, made by pounding cooked glutinous rice into a paste; Chinese zongzi, parcels wrapped in bamboo leaves, and tangyuan, sweet filled dumplings; Korean tteok rice cakes; and Thai mango sticky rice served with coconut milk. Ground into flour, it is used for dumplings and sweets throughout Asia, and it is also fermented for rice wines such as Japanese sake (from polished non-glutinous rice in most cases) and various Southeast Asian rice beers.

Characteristics

The near-absence of amylose makes glutinous rice grains lose their shape and cling together on cooking, usually after soaking and steaming rather than boiling. Its high amylopectin content also gives it a high glycaemic index. Coloured varieties exist, including black and purple glutinous rice, prized for desserts.
