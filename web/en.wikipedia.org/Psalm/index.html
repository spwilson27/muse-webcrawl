Psalm

A psalm is a sacred song or poem used in worship, of which the most famous collection is the biblical Book of Psalms in the Hebrew Bible and the Christian Old Testament. The word derives from the Greek psalmos, meaning music sung to a harp, itself a translation of the Hebrew mizmor.

The Book of Psalms

The Book of Psalms contains 150 psalms in the Hebrew and most Protestant traditions (151 in the Septuagint and Eastern Orthodox canon), traditionally attributed largely to King David, though modern scholarship regards the collection as an anthology composed by many authors over centuries. The psalms are conventionally divided into five books, echoing the five books of the Torah.

Types

Form criticism, pioneered by Hermann Gunkel, classifies psalms by genre: hymns of praise, laments (the largest group), thanksgiving psalms, royal psalms, wisdom psalms, and songs of Zion. Many psalms bear superscriptions naming authors, occasions, or musical directions such as "for the choirmaster."

Use in worship

Psalms have been sung and recited in Jewish and Christian worship since antiquity. Monastic communities developed the practice of chanting the entire Psalter weekly in the Liturgy of the Hours. In the Reformation, metrical psalms — rhymed vernacular paraphrases sung to tunes — became central to Reformed worship, while Anglican chant developed for cathedral psalmody. Famous individual psalms include Psalm 23 ("The Lord is my shepherd"), Psalm 51 (the Miserere), and Psalm 137 ("By the rivers of Babylon").

Musical settings

Composers from Josquin to Bach, Handel, Mendelssohn, Brahms, Stravinsky, and Bernstein have set psalm texts; Leonard Bernstein's Chichester Psalms and Igor Stravinsky's Symphony of Psalms are notable 20th-century examples.
