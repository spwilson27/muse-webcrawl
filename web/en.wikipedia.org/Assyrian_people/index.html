Assyrian people

The Assyrian people (Syriac: ܐܬܘܪ̈ܝܐ, romanized: Āṯūrāyē) are an ethnic group indigenous to Mesopotamia, a geographical region in West Asia. Modern Assyrians descend from the ancient Assyrians who established one of the earliest empires in the region, and they speak dialects of Neo-Aramaic, the modern descendants of the Aramaic language that became the lingua franca of the Near East in antiquity.

History

The Assyrians trace their origins to the city-state of Assur, founded in the third millennium BCE on the western bank of the Tigris River in what is now northern Iraq. The Neo-Assyrian Empire (911–609 BCE) became the largest empire the world had yet seen, stretching from Egypt to western Iran. After its fall, the Assyrian heartland passed through Persian, Greek, Parthian and Roman rule, but the population retained a distinct identity, gradually adopting Christianity from the 1st century CE onward.

In the late Ottoman period, Assyrians suffered the Sayfo (Assyrian genocide) of 1915 alongside the Armenian and Greek genocides, in which hundreds of thousands perished. The Simmele massacre of 1933 in Iraq and the persecution following the 2003 Iraq War further reduced the population of the ancestral homeland.

Language

Assyrians speak Northeastern Neo-Aramaic dialects, including Suret (Assyrian Neo-Aramaic) and Turoyo (Central Neo-Aramaic), written in the Syriac script. Classical Syriac remains the liturgical language of their churches. Language retention varies widely between the homeland and the diaspora, and several dialects are considered endangered.

Religion

Most Assyrians are Christians, principally of the Assyrian Church of the East, the Chaldean Catholic Church, the Syriac Orthodox Church, the Syriac Catholic Church and the Ancient Church of the East, with smaller Protestant communities. Christianity reached Assyria in the apostolic age, traditionally through St. Thomas the Apostle and his disciples Addai and Mari.

Diaspora

Today the majority of Assyrians live in the diaspora, with large communities in the United States (notably Chicago and Detroit), Sweden, Germany, Australia, Canada and Russia. Kha b-Nisan, the Assyrian New Year celebrated on 1 April, is the most important national festival, marked by parades and gatherings.

Culture

Assyrian culture is expressed through folk dances such as the khigga, distinctive embroidered clothing, a rich liturgical musical tradition, and cuisine featuring dishes such as dolma, biryani and kleicha cookies served at festivals.
