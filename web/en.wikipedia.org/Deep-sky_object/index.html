A deep-sky object (DSO) is any astronomical object other than individual stars and Solar System objects (such as the Sun, the Moon, planets, and comets). The term is used mainly by amateur astronomers to denote visually observable objects beyond our solar system — chiefly nebulae, star clusters, and galaxies — that appear as extended, fuzzy patches rather than points of light.

Types

Deep-sky objects include nebulae (diffuse emission and reflection nebulae, planetary nebulae, and supernova remnants), star clusters (open clusters such as the Pleiades and globular clusters such as Omega Centauri), and galaxies (spiral, elliptical, and irregular systems beyond the Milky Way, such as the Andromeda Galaxy). Double stars are sometimes included in observing lists but are not strictly deep-sky objects.

Catalogues

The foundational list is Charles Messier's catalogue of 110 objects, compiled in the eighteenth century so that comet hunters would not mistake them for comets. The far larger New General Catalogue (NGC, 1888) and its Index Catalogue supplements list nearly 8,000 objects, assembled largely by John Louis Emil Dreyer from the observations of William and John Herschel. The Caldwell Catalogue of 109 objects, published in 1995, supplements Messier's list with bright objects he omitted, including many in the southern sky.

Observation

Most deep-sky objects are faint and require dark skies and averted vision; observation of them is a central pursuit of amateur astronomy, commemorated in the "Messier marathon," the attempt to view all 110 Messier objects in a single night. Modern observers also image deep-sky objects with long-exposure astrophotography. Light pollution severely limits deep-sky observing near cities.
