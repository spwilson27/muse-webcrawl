Clean room design

Clean room design
Also called: Clean-room implementation, Chinese wall technique
Purpose: Reimplement a product without infringing copyright
Used in: Software, semiconductor design
Famous example: Phoenix BIOS; Compaq PC clone

Clean room design (also known as the Chinese wall technique) is the method of copying a design by reverse engineering it and then recreating it without infringing any of the copyrights associated with the original design. Clean room design is useful as a defense against copyright infringement claims, because it relies on independent creation. However, it cannot protect against patents, which grant rights independent of how a product was developed.

Process

In a typical clean room implementation, one team studies the original product, often through black-box testing and reverse engineering, and writes a detailed specification describing only its observable behaviour and interfaces. A second, separate team — whose members have never seen the original's copyrighted source material — then writes new code or designs a new product to satisfy that specification. Careful documentation of the separation provides evidence of independent development if a dispute arises.

Examples

The technique became prominent in the personal computer industry: Compaq used clean room reverse engineering to create the first fully compatible IBM PC clone BIOS in 1982–83, and Phoenix Technologies subsequently marketed a clean-room BIOS to other manufacturers, enabling the PC clone industry. Clean room reimplementation was also used for the Soviet-era and later microprocessor clones, and figures in open-source projects that reimplement proprietary formats and protocols. In U.S. law, the legitimacy of reverse engineering for interoperability was reinforced by cases such as Sega v. Accolade (1992) and Sony v. Connectix (2000).
