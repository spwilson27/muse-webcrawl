Primorial

In mathematics, and particularly in number theory, a primorial, denoted by "pn#" or "n#", is a function from natural numbers to natural numbers similar to the factorial function, but rather than successively multiplying positive integers, the function only multiplies prime numbers.

The name "primorial", coined by Harvey Dubner, draws an analogy to primes similar to the way the name "factorial" relates to factors. There are two conflicting definitions in the literature: the product of the first n primes, and the product of all primes less than or equal to n.

Values

Using the first definition, the primorials begin 2, 6, 30, 210, 2310, 30030, ... — that is, 2# = 2, 3# = 2·3 = 6, 5# = 2·3·5 = 30, 7# = 210, 11# = 2310, and 13# = 30030. By convention the empty product gives 1# = 1 (and 0# = 1).

Properties and applications

Primorials play a role in the search for prime numbers in arithmetic progressions: every prime in such a progression with common difference d beyond a certain bound requires d to be a multiple of a primorial. In Euclid's classic proof of the infinitude of primes, the number formed by adding 1 to a primorial must have a prime factor larger than any in the product. Primorials also give the maximum orders of elements in symmetric groups for certain sizes, and appear in estimates connected with the Chebyshev function ϑ(x), whose study relates to the distribution of primes. Numbers of the form p# ± 1 are investigated as candidate primorial primes.
