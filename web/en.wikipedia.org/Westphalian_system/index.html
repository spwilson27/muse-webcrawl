Westphalian system

Ratification of the Peace of Münster, 1648 — one of the treaties comprising the Peace of Westphalia

The Westphalian system, also known as Westphalian sovereignty, is a principle in international law and international relations that each state has exclusive sovereignty over its territory and domestic affairs, and shall not intervene in the internal affairs of other states. The principle takes its name from the Peace of Westphalia of 1648, the set of treaties that ended the Thirty Years' War in the Holy Roman Empire and the Eighty Years' War between Spain and the Dutch Republic.

Principles

The Westphalian model of state sovereignty is conventionally summarised by several principles:

— Territoriality: states exercise authority within fixed territorial boundaries.
— Sovereignty: each state is legally equal and recognises no higher political authority above it.
— Non-intervention: external actors must not intervene in another state's domestic affairs.
— Consent: international law arises from the consent of states, expressed through treaties.

Under this framework the modern international system emerged as an order of independent sovereign states interacting through diplomacy, balance of power, and international law. The principle of non-intervention was later codified in the Charter of the United Nations (1945).

Historical debate

Historians and political scientists debate how far the treaties of 1648 actually established these principles. The Peace of Westphalia confirmed the rights (Landeshoheit) of the imperial estates of the Holy Roman Empire, recognised the independence of the Dutch Republic and the Swiss Confederacy, and established mechanisms by which France and Sweden became guarantors of the imperial constitution — arguably a form of intervention. Critics speak of a "Westphalian myth", arguing that the fully developed system emerged only over the following centuries.

Challenges

The Westphalian order has been challenged by globalisation, supranational institutions such as the European Union, humanitarian intervention doctrines such as the Responsibility to Protect, transnational terrorism, and economic interdependence, all of which are argued to limit the practical sovereignty of states.
