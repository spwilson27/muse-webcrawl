Till (or glacial till) is unsorted glacial sediment deposited directly by melting ice, without reworking by meltwater. It is derived from the erosion and entrainment of material by a moving glacier and consists of a chaotic mixture of grain sizes — rock flour, sand, gravel and boulders (including glacial erratics) — in a clay-rich matrix.

Types of till

Geologists distinguish lodgement till, plastered onto the bed beneath moving ice and often densely compacted; ablation (melt-out) till, released as buried ice melts at the glacier surface; deformation till, formed from sheared subglacial sediment; and flow till, redeposited by slumping on ice surfaces. Clasts in till often show preferred orientation (fabric) aligned with former ice flow and bear striations from abrasion.

Landforms and significance

Till blankets vast areas of northern North America and Eurasia scraped by Pleistocene ice sheets, forming the parent material of fertile agricultural soils in places such as the American Midwest. It builds constructional landforms including drumlins and moraines. Lithified ancient till is called tillite; Neoproterozoic tillites found even at low palaeolatitudes are key evidence for the "Snowball Earth" hypothesis.

Because it is poorly sorted and often low in permeability, till matters in engineering geology, groundwater studies and mineral exploration, where indicator minerals dispersed in till are traced back to concealed ore bodies ("drift prospecting").
