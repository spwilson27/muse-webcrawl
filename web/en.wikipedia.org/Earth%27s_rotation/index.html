Earth's rotation or Earth's spin is the rotation of planet Earth around its own axis, as well as changes in the orientation of the rotation axis in space. Earth rotates eastward, in prograde motion; as viewed from the northern polar star Polaris, Earth turns counterclockwise.

Period

Earth's rotation period relative to the fixed stars, called its stellar day, is about 86,164.09 seconds. Its period relative to the Sun—the mean solar day used in everyday timekeeping—is about 86,400 seconds. Because tidal friction from the Moon transfers angular momentum from Earth's spin to the Moon's orbit, the length of the day is gradually increasing, by roughly 1.7 milliseconds per century at present, and leap seconds are occasionally added to coordinated universal time to keep it aligned with solar time.

Effects

The rotation produces the cycle of day and night, the apparent daily motion of the Sun, Moon, and stars across the sky, and a centrifugal effect that makes Earth an oblate spheroid slightly flattened at the poles. Deflection of moving air and ocean currents by the Coriolis effect shapes global weather patterns. Rotation was demonstrated experimentally by Foucault's pendulum in 1851.

Variations

The rotation rate is not perfectly constant: it is affected by earthquakes, glacial rebound, atmospheric winds, and ocean tides, and the axis itself undergoes precession and nutation over long timescales. Precise measurements are maintained by the International Earth Rotation and Reference Systems Service.
