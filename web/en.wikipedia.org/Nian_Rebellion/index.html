The Nian Rebellion (Chinese: 捻亂) was a major uprising in northern China between 1851 and 1868, contemporaneous with the Taiping Rebellion in the south. The Nian (nian meaning local gangs or "bands" in the Huai River region) were loose federations of salt smugglers, bandits, and impoverished peasants organized in the Huaibei region where Anhui, Jiangsu, Henan and Shandong meet.

Course of the rebellion

Under the leadership of Zhang Lexing, the Nian federated formally in 1855, adopting banners, titles and loose alliance with the Taiping Heavenly Kingdom. Highly mobile, the Nian relied on cavalry raiding over the north China plain, avoiding fixed positions and living off plunder; they built walled village bases in their home districts. Zhang was captured and executed in 1863. Qing counterinsurgency under the Mongol general Sengge Rinchen ended with his death in an ambush in 1865.

Thereafter the Qing entrusted the campaign to Han Chinese provincial armies: Zeng Guofan, then Li Hongzhang and Zuo Zongtang adopted a strategy of fortified lines, river blockade and constricting cordons along the Grand Canal and Yellow River. The Nian split into Eastern and Western armies; the Western Nian marched toward Xi'an and even threatened Beijing before being destroyed in 1868, and the Eastern Nian were annihilated the same year.

Significance

Coming atop the Taiping and Muslim rebellions, the Nian rising further exposed Qing military decline, accelerated the shift of military power to regional Han-led armies, and devastated the population and economy of the Huai plain.
