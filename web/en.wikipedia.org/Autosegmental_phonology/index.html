Autosegmental phonology

Autosegmental phonology is a framework of phonological analysis proposed by John Goldsmith in his 1976 MIT doctoral dissertation. It represents speech sounds not as a single linear string of segments but as parallel tiers of features — for example, a tonal tier and a segmental tier — connected by association lines.

Motivation

The theory was developed to explain phenomena that linear models handled awkwardly, notably the behavior of tone in African languages, where a single tone can spread across several vowels or persist when its vowel is deleted ("stability"). Vowel harmony and the templatic morphology of Semitic languages, in which a consonantal root interleaves with a vowel pattern, provided further motivation.

Principles

Association lines link elements on different tiers subject to conditions such as the Well-Formedness Condition, which originally required lines not to cross and every element to be associated. Autosegmental ideas underpin later developments including feature geometry, which organizes phonological features into a hierarchical tree, and much work on intonation.
