Yogachara (Sanskrit: योगाचार, "practice of yoga") is an influential school of Mahayana Buddhist philosophy and psychology that emphasises the study of cognition, perception, and consciousness. It is also known as Vijñānavāda ("the doctrine of consciousness") and is characterised by the teaching of vijñapti-mātra, often translated as "consciousness-only" or "representation-only".

History

The school took shape in India in the 4th–5th centuries CE through the half-brothers Asanga and Vasubandhu, who are traditionally said to have received teachings from the bodhisattva Maitreya. Asanga's Yogācārabhūmi-śāstra is the school's great compendium, while Vasubandhu's Triṃśikā-vijñaptimātratā ("Thirty Verses on Consciousness Only") and Viṃśikā ("Twenty Verses") are concise classic statements. The earlier Saṃdhinirmocana Sūtra is regarded as a foundational scripture.

Doctrine

Yogachara holds that what we experience as an external world is inseparable from the processes of consciousness. Its model of mind includes eight consciousnesses: the five senses, mental consciousness, the defiled mentality (kliṣṭa-manas), and the storehouse consciousness (ālaya-vijñāna), which preserves karmic seeds (bīja) and perfuming impressions (vāsanā). The "three natures" (trisvabhāva) — the imagined, the dependent, and the perfected — analyse experience and the path to awakening, which culminates in a "revulsion of the basis" (āśraya-parāvṛtti). Yogachara teachings spread to East Asia, where the school became known as Faxiang in China and Hossō in Japan, and it strongly influenced Tibetan Buddhism.
