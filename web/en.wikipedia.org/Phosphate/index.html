Phosphate

The phosphate anion, PO43−

In chemistry, a phosphate is an anion, salt, functional group, or ester derived from a phosphoric acid. It most commonly consists of the tetrahedral anion PO43−, composed of a central phosphorus atom surrounded by four oxygen atoms. Phosphates are essential to life: they form the backbone of DNA and RNA, store and transfer energy in ATP, and are key structural components of bones and teeth.

Chemical properties

Phosphoric acid (H3PO4) can lose up to three protons, giving the dihydrogen phosphate (H2PO4−), hydrogen phosphate (HPO42−), and phosphate (PO43−) ions, which coexist in proportions determined by pH. This stepwise ionization makes phosphate solutions an important buffer system in cells and blood. Condensed phosphates, such as pyrophosphate and polyphosphates, contain chains or rings of linked PO4 units joined by shared oxygen atoms.

Occurrence and mining

Phosphates occur naturally in phosphate rock, mainly sedimentary deposits of apatite minerals formed from ancient marine sediments. The world's largest reserves are in Morocco and Western Sahara, with other major deposits in China, the United States (notably Florida), Russia, and Jordan. Mined phosphate rock is the primary commercial source of phosphorus, an element for which there is no substitute in agriculture.

Uses

About 90% of phosphate rock production goes to fertilizer—superphosphate, triple superphosphate, and ammonium phosphates (MAP and DAP)—supplying the phosphorus that, together with nitrogen and potassium, is one of the three primary plant nutrients. Phosphates are also used in detergents (though their use has been curtailed because phosphate runoff causes eutrophication of lakes and rivers), food additives, water treatment, metal finishing, and flame retardants. In biology, phosphorylation—the addition of a phosphate group to proteins—is a central mechanism of cellular regulation.
