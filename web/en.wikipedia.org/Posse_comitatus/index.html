Posse comitatus

Posse comitatus (Latin for "power of the county") is the common-law or statutory power of a county sheriff or other law officer to conscript able-bodied people to assist in keeping the peace or pursuing felons. The term also gives its name to the United States Posse Comitatus Act of 1878, which restricts the use of the federal military for domestic law enforcement.

Origins

The institution dates to early English law, under which the sheriff could summon the men of the county to form a posse to suppress riots, execute writs, or pursue criminals — closely related to the hue and cry and the frankpledge system. Colonists carried the practice to America, where it was codified in many states.

Modern use

Many U.S. states retain statutes authorizing sheriffs to summon a posse, although the practice is now rare. In the American West the sheriff's posse became a staple of frontier law enforcement and later of Western fiction. Some jurisdictions retain organized volunteer posses that assist with search and rescue and ceremonial duties.
