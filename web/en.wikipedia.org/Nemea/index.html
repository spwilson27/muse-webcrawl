Nemea (Greek: Νεμέα) is an ancient site in the northeastern part of the Peloponnese in Greece, in the regional unit of Corinthia. It was the site of a sanctuary of Zeus and of the Nemean Games, one of the four Panhellenic festivals of ancient Greece. In Greek mythology, Nemea was the home of the Nemean lion, slain by Heracles as the first of his twelve labours.

Mythology

The valley was sacred to Zeus Nemeios. Myths connect the games' foundation to the death of the infant Opheltes, killed by a serpent while his nurse Hypsipyle led the Seven against Thebes to a spring; the seer Amphiaraus renamed the child Archemoros ("beginning of doom"), and funeral games held in his honor were said to be the origin of the Nemean Games. Alternatively, Heracles was credited with founding the games after killing the lion whose impenetrable hide he thereafter wore.

The sanctuary and games

The Nemean Games, held every two years (in the years before and after the Olympic Games), were administered first by Kleonai and later by Argos. Athletes competed in events including the stadion sprint, wrestling, boxing, pankration, and chariot racing; victors received a crown of wild celery. The surviving Temple of Zeus, built around 330 BC with three standing columns re-erected in modern times, replaced an earlier archaic temple. Excavations, directed for many years by Stephen G. Miller of the University of California, Berkeley, uncovered the stadium (with its vaulted entrance tunnel and athletes' graffiti), a large bathhouse, a hostel (xenon), and an early Christian basilica.

Modern Nemea

The modern town of Nemea lies near the ancient site and is known for its wines, especially the red Agiorgitiko grape, sometimes marketed as the "Blood of Heracles." Since 1996, the Society for the Revival of the Nemean Games has organized modern revivals of the ancient footraces, open to the public.
