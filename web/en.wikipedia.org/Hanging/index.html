Hanging

Hanging is the suspension of a person by a noose or ligature around the neck. It has historically been one of the most common methods of capital punishment and judicial execution, and is also a method of suicide and, occasionally, of murder or lynching. Death by hanging typically results from strangulation, constriction of the airway and blood vessels, or — in the calculated "long drop" — fracture of the cervical vertebrae.

Judicial hanging

Hanging was the standard method of execution in much of Europe and its colonies for centuries. Early methods amounted to slow strangulation ("short drop" or suspension). In the 19th century, Britain and other countries developed the long drop, with official tables correlating the length of drop to the prisoner's weight, intended to break the neck and cause rapid unconsciousness and death. Britain's last executions by hanging occurred in 1964, and capital punishment for murder was abolished in 1965 (permanently in 1969). Hanging remains a legal execution method in a number of countries, including Japan, Singapore, India, and several Middle Eastern and African states.

Mechanism

The causes of death in hanging include asphyxia from airway obstruction, cerebral ischaemia from compression of the carotid arteries and jugular veins, vagal inhibition of the heart, and spinal cord injury in drop hangings. Forensic medicine distinguishes typical (symmetrical, knot at the back of the neck) from atypical hangings, and examines ligature marks and other signs to distinguish suicide from homicide.

Suicide and prevention

Hanging is among the most common methods of suicide worldwide, in part because of the wide availability of ligature points and materials. Public-health guidance emphasises that restricting means, treating mental illness and crisis intervention reduce suicide risk; anyone in crisis should contact local emergency services or a suicide helpline.
