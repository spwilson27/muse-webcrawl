Receiver operating characteristic

ROC curves for three classifiers; the closer a curve approaches the top-left corner, the better the classifier.

A receiver operating characteristic curve, or ROC curve, is a graphical plot that illustrates the performance of a binary classifier as its discrimination threshold is varied. The curve is created by plotting the true positive rate (sensitivity) against the false positive rate (1 − specificity) at various threshold settings. The ROC curve and the summary measure derived from it, the area under the curve (AUC), are widely used in machine learning, medical diagnostics and signal detection theory.

History

The technique originated in the Second World War in the analysis of radar signals, where operators had to decide whether a blip on a screen represented an enemy aircraft or noise. The name reflects the characteristics of the receiver operator making that judgement. It was subsequently developed within signal detection theory in psychology and adopted in medicine from the 1960s onward to evaluate diagnostic tests, before becoming a standard evaluation tool in machine learning.

Area under the curve

The AUC summarises the curve in a single number between 0 and 1. It can be interpreted as the probability that the classifier will rank a randomly chosen positive instance higher than a randomly chosen negative one. An AUC of 0.5 corresponds to chance performance (the diagonal line), while 1.0 represents perfect separation. The AUC is equivalent to the Mann–Whitney U statistic and is related to the Gini coefficient by Gini = 2·AUC − 1.

Choosing a threshold

Any single classifier applied at a fixed threshold corresponds to one point on the curve. Selecting an operating point involves trading sensitivity against specificity according to the relative costs of false positives and false negatives; a common statistical criterion is Youden's J statistic, which maximises the sum of sensitivity and specificity minus one. In clinical settings the choice is guided by the consequences of missed diagnoses versus unnecessary treatment.

Limitations and variants

ROC analysis can be misleading for highly imbalanced datasets, where a large number of true negatives can mask poor performance on the minority class; precision–recall curves are often preferred in such cases. Extensions include multi-class ROC surfaces, partial AUC over a restricted range of false positive rates, and cost curves, which display expected cost directly.
