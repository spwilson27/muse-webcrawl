Islamic terrorism

Islamic terrorism, also called jihadist terrorism, refers to acts of terrorism motivated by or carried out in the name of Islamist ideology. Major groups associated with such violence include al-Qaeda and the Islamic State (ISIS/ISIL), as well as regional movements such as al-Shabaab, Boko Haram, and the Taliban in some of its campaigns. The deadliest acts include the September 11 attacks (2001), which killed nearly 3,000 people.

Terminology

The term is debated: governments and scholars variously prefer "Islamist terrorism", "jihadist terrorism", or "violent extremism", while many Muslim leaders and organizations condemn such attacks and argue they are a distortion of Islam. Most victims of jihadist violence worldwide are themselves Muslims.

Ideological roots

Modern jihadist ideology draws on interpretations of Salafism and Wahhabism and on the writings of figures such as Sayyid Qutb, who advocated offensive jihad against regimes deemed apostate. The Soviet–Afghan War (1979–1989) provided a formative mobilization for transnational networks of Arab volunteers.

Major organizations and attacks

Al-Qaeda, founded by Osama bin Laden in 1988, carried out the 1998 U.S. embassy bombings and the September 11 attacks, prompting the U.S.-led "War on Terror" and invasions of Afghanistan and Iraq. The Islamic State, an al-Qaeda offshoot, seized large territories in Iraq and Syria in 2014, declared a caliphate, and inspired or directed attacks in Europe and elsewhere before losing its territory by 2019. Other high-casualty attacks include the 2004 Madrid train bombings, the 2005 London bombings, the 2008 Mumbai attacks, and the November 2015 Paris attacks.
