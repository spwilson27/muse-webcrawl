Aid climbing
TypeRock climbing discipline
EquipmentÉtriers (aiders), pitons, copperheads, hooks, daisy chains
GradingA0–A5 (or C0–C5 for clean aid)

Aid climbing is a style of rock climbing in which the climber uses equipment not only for protection but directly to make upward progress — standing in slings (étriers or aiders) attached to pieces of gear placed in the rock. It contrasts with free climbing, where hands and feet use only the natural features of the rock and gear serves only to catch falls.

Technique and grading

Aid climbers ascend steep or featureless walls by placing successive pieces of protection — nuts, cams, pitons, copperheads, or skyhooks — clipping a ladder-like aider to each, and weighting it to move up. Routes are graded from A0 (occasional aid on solid gear) to A5 (long sequences of marginal placements where a fall could be catastrophic). "Clean aid" grades (C) denote routes climbable without a hammer, using only removable gear.

History

Aid techniques made possible the first ascents of the great big walls of Yosemite Valley, including The Nose of El Capitan in 1958. As free-climbing standards rose, many former aid routes were climbed free, but aid climbing remains essential on remote big walls and the hardest extreme routes.
