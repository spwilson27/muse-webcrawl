Bulimia nervosa, also known simply as bulimia, is an eating disorder characterised by recurrent episodes of binge eating followed by compensatory behaviours — most commonly self-induced vomiting — intended to prevent weight gain. People with bulimia typically maintain a weight within or above the normal range, which can make the disorder less visible than anorexia nervosa.

Signs and symptoms

Diagnostic criteria (DSM-5) include recurrent binge eating with a sense of loss of control, recurrent inappropriate compensatory behaviour, and self-evaluation unduly influenced by body shape and weight, occurring at least once a week for three months. Medical complications of purging include electrolyte imbalances (especially low potassium, which can cause cardiac arrhythmia), dental erosion from stomach acid, swollen salivary glands, and gastrointestinal damage.

Causes

The causes are multifactorial: genetic vulnerability, personality traits such as perfectionism, sociocultural pressure toward thinness, dieting, and co-occurring conditions including depression, anxiety and substance use disorders. Bulimia most often begins in adolescence or young adulthood and is more commonly diagnosed in women, though men are also affected.

Treatment

Enhanced cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT-E) is the leading treatment, helping patients normalise eating and address shape and weight concerns. Antidepressants, particularly fluoxetine, can reduce binge–purge frequency. With treatment, many patients recover fully, though relapse is common.
