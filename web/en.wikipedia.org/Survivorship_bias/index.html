Survivorship bias

Survivorship bias (or survival bias) is the logical error of concentrating on entities that passed some selection process while overlooking those that did not, typically because they are no longer visible. It can lead to false conclusions in statistics, finance, medicine, history and everyday reasoning: the successes are observed, the failures are silent.

The classic example

During the Second World War, the statistician Abraham Wald was asked where to add armour to bombers based on the bullet-hole patterns of returning aircraft. Wald reasoned that the holes showed where a plane could be hit and still return; the missing planes must have been hit elsewhere — notably the engines and cockpit, which showed few holes among survivors. Armour belonged where the returning planes had no holes. The damaged-but-returned aircraft were the biased sample.

Other manifestations

In finance, mutual-fund performance averages are inflated when failed funds are dropped from databases. In business literature, studying only successful companies attributes success to their visible practices while ignoring failed firms that did the same things. Similar effects appear in medicine (studying long-term survivors), archaeology and architecture (old buildings that survive are not typical of their era), and in the perception of the past — Diagoras of Melos made the point in antiquity about votive offerings from sailors who survived shipwrecks, noting that the drowned left no offerings.

Avoiding it

Remedies include seeking the full population rather than the surviving sample, asking what data are missing and why, using registries that track failures as well as successes, and being wary of advice drawn only from winners.
