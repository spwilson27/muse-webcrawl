Cotton Mather (February 12, 1663 – February 13, 1728) was a New England Puritan clergyman and author, who ministered at Boston's North Church, the largest congregation in New England. He was the son of Increase Mather and the grandson of both Richard Mather and John Cotton, two towering figures of early colonial Puritanism.

Early life and education

Mather was a child prodigy who entered Harvard College at age eleven, graduating in 1678. He earned a master's degree in 1681 and joined his father as assistant pastor of the North Church in 1685, a post he held for the rest of his life.

Salem witch trials

Mather is best known today for his involvement in the Salem witch trials of 1692–1693. His book The Wonders of the Invisible World (1693) defended the trials, though he had also cautioned the judges against over-reliance on "spectral evidence." His reputation has been shadowed by the episode ever since.

Writings and science

One of the most prolific authors in colonial America, Mather published more than 400 works, including the ecclesiastical history Magnalia Christi Americana (1702). He was elected a fellow of the Royal Society of London and championed smallpox inoculation during Boston's 1721 epidemic, learning of the practice from Onesimus, an enslaved man in his household.
