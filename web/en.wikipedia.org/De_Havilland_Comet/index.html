The de Havilland DH 106 Comet was the world's first commercial jet airliner. Developed and manufactured by de Havilland in the United Kingdom, it first flew in 1949 and entered scheduled service with BOAC in May 1952, inaugurating the jet age for passenger travel.

Design

The Comet featured a pressurized cabin, four de Havilland Ghost turbojet engines buried in the wing roots, large square windows, and quiet, vibration-free flight at altitudes above most weather. It halved journey times on routes such as London–Johannesburg.

Accidents and redesign

In 1953–54, three Comets broke up in flight. Investigations, including water-tank fatigue testing of a full fuselage at Farnborough, found that repeated pressurization caused metal fatigue cracks at the corners of the square windows and apertures. The fleet was grounded, and the redesigned Comet 4 introduced rounded windows and a strengthened structure.

Legacy

The Comet 4 entered service in 1958, but Boeing's 707 and Douglas's DC-8 had overtaken it in size and economics. A military derivative, the Hawker Siddeley Nimrod, served for decades. The accident investigations transformed aircraft certification and fatigue-testing practice.
