ITER (initially the International Thermonuclear Experimental Reactor, iter meaning "the way" or "the path" in Latin) is an international nuclear fusion research and engineering megaproject aimed at creating energy through a fusion process similar to that of the Sun. It is the world's largest experimental tokamak nuclear fusion reactor, being built at the Cadarache facility in southern France by a consortium of 35 nations represented by seven members: China, the European Union, India, Japan, Russia, South Korea, and the United States.

Objectives

ITER is designed to produce 500 megawatts of fusion power from 50 MW of external heating power — a gain factor Q of 10 — sustained for pulses of 400 seconds or longer, demonstrating the scientific and technological feasibility of fusion power at power-plant scale. It will not generate electricity itself; its successor concept, DEMO, is intended to do so. The machine's tokamak vessel confines a deuterium–tritium plasma heated to over 150 million °C using superconducting magnets, including niobium–tin central solenoid and toroidal field coils cooled to about 4 K.

History

The project grew out of a 1985 initiative proposed by Soviet leader Mikhail Gorbachev to U.S. President Ronald Reagan. The ITER Agreement was signed in 2006, and site construction began at Cadarache in 2010. Assembly of the tokamak started in 2020 with the installation of the cryostat base. Costs have risen far beyond the original estimate (now well over €20 billion) and schedules have slipped repeatedly; a revised baseline proposed in 2024 moved the start of research operations to the mid-2030s, with full deuterium–tritium operation around 2039.
