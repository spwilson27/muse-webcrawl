Ground deicing of aircraft

Ground deicing of aircraft is the process of removing frost, ice, snow, or slush from an aircraft's critical surfaces before flight, and, where necessary, applying a protective anti-icing treatment to prevent re-accumulation for a limited period. Even a thin layer of frost can roughen a wing's surface enough to increase drag and reduce lift significantly, so regulations in most countries prohibit takeoff with contamination adhering to wings, tail surfaces, control surfaces, propellers, and engine inlets — the so-called "clean aircraft concept".

Fluids and methods

Deicing is usually performed by spraying heated fluids from specialized trucks equipped with articulated booms and operator baskets or enclosed cabins. Fluids are classified by the Society of Automotive Engineers (SAE) standards:

  • Type I fluids are unthickened, low-viscosity glycol-based fluids, usually heated and diluted, used to remove contamination; they provide only brief protection.
  • Type II, III, and IV fluids are thickened with pseudoplastic agents so they cling to the aircraft during taxi, then shear off during the takeoff roll; they provide longer anti-icing protection, Type IV being the longest-lasting.

Deicing may be done in one step (a single heated fluid application that both cleans and briefly protects) or two steps (deicing followed by a separate anti-icing application). Alternatives include infrared deicing hangars, hot-air systems, and mechanical removal, though fluid spraying remains standard at large airports, often at dedicated deicing pads near the runway.

Holdover time

Once anti-icing fluid is applied, the aircraft must take off within the fluid's holdover time — a guideline range depending on the fluid, its concentration, the temperature, and the type and intensity of precipitation. If the holdover time is exceeded, the aircraft must be re-inspected and, if contaminated, deiced again. Fluid runoff containing glycol is an environmental concern, and many airports collect and recycle it.
