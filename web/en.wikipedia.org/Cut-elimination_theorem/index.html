Cut-elimination theorem

The cut-elimination theorem (or Gentzen's Hauptsatz) is the central result of proof theory for sequent calculi. It states that any proof in a sequent calculus that uses the cut rule can be transformed into a proof of the same conclusion that does not use the cut rule at all. It was proved by Gerhard Gentzen in 1934–35 as part of his work on the consistency of arithmetic.

The cut rule

The cut rule corresponds to the use of lemmas in ordinary mathematics: from a proof of a proposition A and a proof of the desired conclusion using A as an assumption, one derives the conclusion. Eliminating cuts means removing every such detour, producing a direct, "analytic" proof in which every formula appearing is a subformula of the conclusion — the subformula property.

Consequences

Cut elimination yields consistency proofs (an empty conclusion cannot be proved, since a cut-free proof of it is impossible), the subformula property, interpolation theorems, and decision procedures for some logics. Gentzen used it to prove the consistency of first-order logic and, in extended form, of Peano arithmetic.

Proof methods

Gentzen's original argument proceeded by double induction on the complexity of the cut formula and the heights of the derivations. Later proofs, and the analogous normalisation theorem for natural deduction and typed lambda calculus, connect cut elimination with computation: eliminating a cut corresponds to evaluating a program, an instance of the Curry–Howard correspondence.
