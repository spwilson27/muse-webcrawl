Metre

The metre (US spelling meter), symbol m, is the base unit of length in the International System of Units (SI). Since 1983 it has been defined as the length of the path travelled by light in vacuum during a time interval of 1/299,792,458 of a second; the 2019 revision of the SI expressed the definition in terms of the fixed numerical value of the speed of light. One metre is about 39.37 inches, slightly longer than a yard.

History

The metre was introduced in revolutionary France in the 1790s, defined as one ten-millionth of the distance from the equator to the North Pole along a meridian through Paris, and embodied in a platinum bar. In 1889 an international prototype of platinum-iridium became the standard, replaced in 1960 by a definition based on wavelengths of krypton-86 light, and in 1983 by the present light-travel-time definition, which can be realised anywhere with sufficient accuracy.

Usage

The metre, with its decimal prefixes (millimetre, centimetre, kilometre, and so on), is the standard unit of length in science and in everyday life in most of the world. It is used for everything from atomic-scale measurements (in nanometres and picometres) to astronomical distances expressed in millions of kilometres.
