War of the First Coalition

Date20 April 1792 – 17 October 1797
LocationFrance, Central Europe, the Netherlands, Italy, the Caribbean, the Mediterranean
CombatantsFrench Republic vs. the First Coalition: Austria, Prussia, Great Britain, Spain, the Dutch Republic, and others
ResultFrench victory; Treaty of Campo-Formio; survival of the French Republic

The War of the First Coalition (1792–1797) was the first major effort of several European monarchies to defeat Revolutionary France. A loose coalition—at various times including Austria, Prussia, Great Britain, Spain, Portugal, the Dutch Republic, Sardinia, Naples, and other states—made war against the French Republic, which responded with mass conscription and a transformation of warfare. The war ended with France victorious, in possession of the Austrian Netherlands and the left bank of the Rhine, and with the young general Napoleon Bonaparte launched to fame by his Italian campaign.

Origins

Fearful of revolutionary contagion, Austria and Prussia issued the Declaration of Pillnitz (1791), threatening intervention to restore Louis XVI. The French Legislative Assembly, driven by Girondin leaders who expected war to consolidate the Revolution, declared war on Austria on 20 April 1792. Early French defeats exposed the disorganisation of the army, and the Brunswick Manifesto's threats against Paris helped provoke the insurrection of 10 August 1792 that overthrew the monarchy.

Course of the war

The Prussian advance was checked at Valmy (20 September 1792), and French forces won at Jemappes and overran the Austrian Netherlands. The execution of Louis XVI in January 1793 brought Britain, Spain, and the Dutch into the war; France was simultaneously beset by the War in the Vendée and Federalist revolts, and responded with the levée en masse of August 1793, raising armies of unprecedented size. Victories at Hondschoote, Wattignies, and finally Fleurus (26 June 1794) reconquered Belgium and opened the way into Holland, where the Batavian Republic was proclaimed in 1795. Prussia and Spain left the war by the Treaties of Basel (1795).

In 1796 France attacked Austria on two fronts. While the Rhine armies were eventually driven back, Bonaparte's Army of Italy defeated Piedmont and Austria in a brilliant series of battles—Montenotte, Lodi, Arcole, and Rivoli—forcing Austria to sign preliminaries at Leoben and then the Treaty of Campo-Formio (17 October 1797), which ceded Belgium to France, recognized French control of the Rhine's left bank, and partitioned the Venetian Republic between France and Austria. Only Britain remained at war, setting the stage for the War of the Second Coalition.

Significance

The war preserved the French Revolution and reshaped European warfare: mass armies, promotion by merit, and living off the land replaced the limited cabinet wars of the 18th century. It also spread revolutionary legal and administrative reforms into conquered territories while provoking the counter-revolutionary resistance that would define the next two decades.
