Arete

Arete
Greekἀρετή (aretḗ)
Literal senseExcellence, virtue, goodness of a thing
Key sourcesHomer, Plato, Aristotle
Related conceptsVirtue ethics, kalokagathia, eudaimonia

Arete (Greek: ἀρετή, aretḗ) is a central concept in ancient Greek thought signifying excellence of any kind — the quality of fulfilling a thing's purpose or function to the highest degree. Applied to people it is often translated "virtue", but its scope is broader: land, tools, animals and even gods could possess arete, meaning their characteristic excellence.

Homeric usage

In Homer's epics, arete belongs chiefly to the warrior aristocracy: physical prowess, courage in battle and the honour and fame (kleos) won thereby. Achilles and Odysseus embody different facets of heroic arete — martial supremacy and cunning excellence respectively. The education of the hero, through exemplars and competition (as at funeral games), was directed at cultivating it.

Philosophical development

The sophists of the fifth century BC claimed to teach arete — increasingly understood as political skill and success in civic life — a claim interrogated throughout Plato's dialogues. Socrates pressed the question of whether virtue can be taught and whether it is a kind of knowledge. For Aristotle in the Nicomachean Ethics, human arete consists in the excellent activity of the rational soul, divided into virtues of character (such as courage and temperance, each a mean between extremes) and virtues of thought (such as practical wisdom, phronesis). Excellence of character arises from habit; hence moral education was central to the Greek city.

Later use

Arete underlies the virtue ethics tradition revived in modern philosophy, and the concept coloured Roman virtus and later ideals of gentlemanly excellence. It also gave its name, through Greek mountaineering usage, to the French loanword arête for a sharp mountain ridge.
