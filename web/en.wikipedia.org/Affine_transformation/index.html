Affine transformation

FieldGeometry, linear algebra
Formf(x) = Ax + b, with A a linear map and b a translation vector

IncludesTranslations, rotations, scalings, reflections, shears and their compositions

In Euclidean geometry, an affine transformation (or affinity) is a geometric transformation that preserves lines and parallelism, though not necessarily distances or angles. More generally, an affine transformation is an automorphism of an affine space: a bijective mapping of the space onto itself that preserves the dimension of affine subspaces, mapping lines to lines and planes to planes.

Algebraically, any affine transformation of n-dimensional space can be written as f(x) = Ax + b, where A is an invertible linear transformation (matrix) and b a translation vector. The group of such transformations — the affine group — is generated by translations together with rotations, reflections, uniform and non-uniform scalings, and shears. Quantities preserved under affine transformations include collinearity of points, ratios of distances along the same line, midpoints and centroids of point sets, and ratios of areas (volumes) of figures.

Applications

Affine transformations are fundamental in computer graphics and computer vision, where they are implemented with homogeneous coordinates and 4×4 (or 3×3) matrices to position, rotate, scale and shear objects; in image registration and remote sensing, to correct distortions in satellite and medical imagery; and in fractal geometry, where iterated function systems build images such as Barnsley's fern from contractive affine maps. In Klein's Erlangen program, affine geometry is precisely the study of the properties invariant under the affine group, intermediate between Euclidean and projective geometry.
