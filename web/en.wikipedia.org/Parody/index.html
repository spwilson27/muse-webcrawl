Parody

Parody
EtymologyGreek parōidía — "burlesque song", from para- (beside/counter) + ōidḗ (song)
Related formsSatire, pastiche, burlesque, travesty, spoof, lampoon
Ancient exemplarsAristophanes; Batrachomyomachia ("Battle of Frogs and Mice")
Legal statusOften protected as fair use / fair dealing exception

A parody is a creative work designed to imitate, comment on, and/or mock its subject by means of satirical or ironic imitation. Often its subject is an original work, its author, or a genre; more broadly, parody can target cultural and political phenomena. The parodist exploits the distinctive style, tone and mannerisms of the original, exaggerating them to comic or critical effect.

History

Parody is among the oldest literary devices: Aristotle already mentions Hegemon of Thasos as its inventor, and the mock-epic Batrachomyomachia parodied Homer. Aristophanes parodied Euripides on the Athenian stage, and Roman writers such as Lucian practised the form. In early modern Europe, Cervantes' Don Quixote (1605) parodied chivalric romance, and John Phillips and later Alexander Pope (in The Rape of the Lock) perfected mock-heroic verse. The twentieth century added parody film and music — from the Marx Brothers and Mad magazine to Mel Brooks, Monty Python, "Weird Al" Yankovic and The Onion.

Characteristics

Unlike plagiarism, parody depends on the audience recognising the work being imitated; unlike pastiche, which imitates affectionately or neutrally, parody typically carries a critical or comic edge, though it need not be hostile — many parodies are tributes. Theorists such as Linda Hutcheon define parody as "repetition with critical distance", a form that both legitimises and subverts its target and is central to postmodern art, literature and music, including sampling culture.

Parody and the law

Because parody necessarily copies elements of its target, it frequently collides with copyright law. In the United States, the Supreme Court held in Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc. (1994) that a commercial parody (2 Live Crew's version of "Oh, Pretty Woman") could qualify as fair use. Many other jurisdictions, including the European Union, provide a specific copyright exception for caricature, parody or pastiche, though moral rights and defamation law may still limit parodic works.
