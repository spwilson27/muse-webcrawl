Perjury trap

A perjury trap is a form of prosecutorial misconduct that occurs when a prosecutor calls a witness before a grand jury solely to compel testimony with the intention of provoking the witness into committing perjury — that is, lying under oath — rather than to investigate any underlying crime or matter within the grand jury's remit.

Doctrine

The perjury trap is a proposed defence to a perjury prosecution in United States law. The argument is that where the government calls a witness not for the legitimate purpose of obtaining truthful testimony relevant to an investigation, but solely in the expectation that the witness will lie so that the lie itself can be prosecuted, due process is violated and the indictment should be dismissed. Commentators often trace the concept to Judge Edward Weinfeld's opinion in United States v. Herr (1978) and related district court decisions.

Reception by courts

Federal appellate courts have generally been highly sceptical of the defence. Several circuits have either rejected it outright or confined it to truly extraordinary circumstances, reasoning that a witness always has the lawful options of testifying truthfully, invoking the Fifth Amendment privilege against self-incrimination, or accepting a contempt sanction, and that perjury is not immunised merely because prosecutors suspected it would occur. The doctrine is therefore discussed widely in legal commentary but has rarely, if ever, succeeded on appeal.
