Inductive reasoning

Inductive reasoning is a method of reasoning in which conclusions are drawn from observed patterns or particular instances, generalizing to broader claims that are probable rather than certain. It contrasts with deductive reasoning, in which a conclusion follows necessarily from its premises. Induction is the foundation of the empirical sciences: observing that the sun has risen every day supports — but does not prove — that it will rise tomorrow.

Types of induction

Common forms include generalization (inferring that a whole population shares a property observed in a sample), statistical syllogism, causal inference (inferring cause and effect, classically organized by John Stuart Mill's methods), analogy, and prediction. In statistics and machine learning, inductive inference is formalized in statistical learning theory and Bayesian methods, which quantify how evidence updates degrees of belief.

The problem of induction

Philosopher David Hume argued in the 18th century that inductive reasoning cannot be rationally justified without circularity: past regularities guarantee nothing about the future, and appealing to induction's past success is itself an induction. This "problem of induction" remains central in philosophy of science. Karl Popper proposed replacing induction with falsification: scientific theories, he argued, are never confirmed, only provisionally retained after failing attempts to refute them. Later philosophers, following Nelson Goodman's "new riddle of induction" (the grue paradox), have explored subtleties about which generalizations evidence supports.

Everyday use

Despite philosophical puzzles, induction pervades everyday reasoning — forecasting weather, learning from experience, and judging character — and its statistical machinery underlies polling, clinical trials, and much of artificial intelligence, where systems learn patterns from data.
