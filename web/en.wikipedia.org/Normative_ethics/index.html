Normative ethics

Normative ethics is the philosophical study of ethical action. It is the branch of ethics that investigates the set of questions that arise when considering how one ought to act, morally speaking. Normative ethics is distinct from meta-ethics because it examines standards for the rightness and wrongness of actions, while meta-ethics studies the meaning and nature of moral language and moral facts. It is also distinct from descriptive ethics, which is an empirical investigation of people's moral beliefs, and from applied ethics, which concerns the treatment of moral problems in specific fields such as medicine, law, and business.

Virtue ethics

Virtue ethics describes the character of a moral agent as a driving force for ethical behavior. Originating with Plato and Aristotle, it was resurrected in the 20th century by philosophers such as Elizabeth Anscombe and Alasdair MacIntyre. Rather than asking "what should I do?", virtue ethics asks "what kind of person should I be?", emphasizing virtues such as courage, temperance, justice, and practical wisdom (phronesis).

Deontology

Deontological ethics holds that the morality of an action should be based on whether the action itself is right or wrong under a series of rules, rather than on its consequences. Immanuel Kant's categorical imperative is the most famous deontological principle: one should act only according to maxims that one can at the same time will to become universal laws, and should treat humanity always as an end and never merely as a means.

Consequentialism

Consequentialism is the class of normative ethical theories holding that the consequences of one's conduct are the ultimate basis for judgment about the rightness or wrongness of that conduct. Utilitarianism, associated with Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill, is the best-known consequentialist theory; it holds that the right action is the one that maximizes overall well-being or happiness.

Other theories

Other normative approaches include ethical egoism, divine command theory, natural law theory, contractarianism, care ethics, and moral particularism. Normative ethicists also debate the relationship between moral reasons and self-interest, the demandingness of morality, and whether moral judgments can be derived from facts about human nature.
