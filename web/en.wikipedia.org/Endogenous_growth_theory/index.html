Endogenous growth theory

Endogenous growth theory
FieldMacroeconomics, growth economics
Key figuresPaul Romer, Robert Lucas Jr., Robert Barro, Philippe Aghion
Foundational papersRomer (1986, 1990); Lucas (1988)
ContrastSolow–Swan model

Endogenous growth theory holds that economic growth is primarily the result of endogenous forces — investment in human capital, innovation, and knowledge — rather than unexplained technological progress arriving from outside the economic system. Developed in the 1980s and 1990s, the theory arose in response to the neoclassical Solow model, in which long-run growth depends on an exogenous rate of technical change and saving rates affect only the level, not the long-run growth rate, of output.

Models

The earliest framework, the AK model, simply assumed constant returns to a broad measure of capital, so that saving and investment permanently raise growth. Paul Romer's 1986 paper introduced increasing returns through knowledge spillovers: firms' research raises the general stock of knowledge, from which all firms benefit. His influential 1990 model made innovation explicit, with a research sector producing new designs under monopolistic competition — growth driven by intentional R&D and the non-rival nature of ideas. Robert Lucas (1988) emphasized human capital accumulation and its externalities. Other strands model quality ladders and Schumpeterian creative destruction (Aghion and Howitt, 1992) and public spending (Barro, 1990).

Policy implications and criticism

Because growth in these models responds to incentives, endogenous growth theory supports policies such as subsidies to education and research, patent protection balanced against diffusion, and openness to trade and foreign investment. Development policy accordingly shifted toward institutions, schooling, and innovation systems. Critics note that strong "scale effects" in early R&D models (larger economies should grow faster) fit the data poorly, prompting semi-endogenous variants in which long-run growth is pinned down by population growth; others question whether the models explain the huge income differences between countries better than institutional accounts do. Paul Romer shared the 2018 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for integrating technological innovations into long-run macroeconomic analysis.
