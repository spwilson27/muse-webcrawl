Herbert Paul Grice (March 13, 1913 – August 28, 1988), usually publishing as H. P. Grice, was a British philosopher of language. He is best known for his theory of implicature and the cooperative principle, the maxims of conversation derived from it, which became foundational to the modern field of pragmatics. Much of his influence spread through lectures and manuscripts circulated long before his major papers were collected in Studies in the Way of Words (1989).

Life and career

Grice was educated at Clifton College and Corpus Christi College, Oxford. After wartime service in the Royal Navy, he taught at Oxford, becoming a fellow of St John's College and a leading figure of post-war "ordinary language philosophy" alongside J. L. Austin. In 1967 he moved to the University of California, Berkeley, where he remained until his death, with a later period at Oxford as a visiting professor. He gave the John Locke Lectures at Oxford and the William James Lectures at Harvard (1967), the latter introducing his theory of conversation.

Theory of meaning

In his 1957 paper "Meaning", Grice distinguished natural from non-natural meaning and analysed speaker meaning in terms of intentions to produce effects in an audience by means of their recognition of those intentions — an analysis elaborated in "Utterer's Meaning and Intentions" (1969). With P. F. Strawson he co-authored "In Defense of a Dogma" (1956), a reply to Quine's attack on the analytic–synthetic distinction.

Implicature and the maxims

Grice proposed that conversation is governed by a Cooperative Principle: "Make your conversational contribution such as is required, at the stage at which it occurs, by the accepted purpose or direction of the talk exchange in which you are engaged." From it he derived four categories of maxims — Quantity (be as informative as required), Quality (be truthful), Relation (be relevant) and Manner (be clear and orderly). When a speaker conspicuously flouts a maxim while apparently remaining cooperative, hearers infer a conversational implicature — meaning beyond what is said. The framework shaped linguistics, philosophy, cognitive science and later theories such as relevance theory.
