Harry Nyquist

Harry Nyquist (born Harry Theodor Nyqvist, February 7, 1889 – April 4, 1976) was a Swedish-American physicist and electrical engineer who made important contributions to communication theory. Working at AT&T's Bell Telephone Laboratories, he laid foundations for sampling theory, feedback amplifier stability, and the theory of thermal noise, all central to modern telecommunications and information theory.

Life and career

Harry Nyquist

Born: February 7, 1889, Nilsby, Sweden

Died: April 4, 1976 (aged 87), Harlingen, Texas

Nationality: Swedish-American

Fields: Electrical engineering, physics

Institutions: Bell Telephone Laboratories

Known for: Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem; Nyquist stability criterion; Johnson–Nyquist noise

Nyquist emigrated from Sweden to the United States in 1907. He studied electrical engineering at the University of North Dakota and received his Ph.D. in physics from Yale University in 1917. He joined AT&T in 1917 and remained with Bell Telephone Laboratories from its founding until his retirement in 1954.

Contributions

In papers of 1924 and 1928 on telegraph transmission theory, Nyquist showed that a signal channel of bandwidth B can transmit at most 2B independent samples per second, the basis of the Nyquist rate; Claude Shannon later built the sampling theorem and modern information theory on this result. His 1932 paper "Regeneration Theory" introduced the Nyquist stability criterion, a graphical method using the Nyquist plot that remains fundamental in control theory for assessing the stability of feedback systems. In 1928 he also provided the theoretical explanation of thermal noise in conductors — Johnson–Nyquist noise — deriving its power from thermodynamic arguments in parallel with John B. Johnson's experimental work.

Nyquist also worked on facsimile transmission, television, and the theory of telegraph distortion, and he held numerous patents. He received the IEEE Medal of Honor in 1960 "for fundamental contributions to a quantitative understanding of thermal noise, data transmission and negative feedback."
