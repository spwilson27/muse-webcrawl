Winnowing

Winnowing is a process by which chaff is separated from grain. It can also be used to remove pests from stored grain. Winnowing usually follows threshing in the preparation of grain. In its simplest form, it involves throwing the mixture into the air so that the wind blows away the lighter chaff, while the heavier grains fall back down for recovery. This method, practised since antiquity, gave rise to the expression "separating the wheat from the chaff".

Methods

Traditionally, the threshed grain is tossed from a shallow basket or winnowing tray — known in various cultures as a winnowing fan, vann, or winnowing machine precursor — so that a natural breeze or air fanned by hand carries off the husks. In another common indoor method, grain is poured slowly from a height in front of a fan, and the chaff is blown to one side.

Mechanisation

The winnowing machine, developed in China by the Han dynasty era and later adopted in Europe in the 18th century, used a hand-cranked rotary fan inside a wooden housing to produce a controlled airstream, combined with sieves and shakers. Winnowing was later incorporated into the threshing machine and then into the combine harvester, where fans and sieves separate grain from chaff continuously as part of harvesting.

Cultural significance

Winnowing appears frequently as a metaphor for judgement and separation in religious and literary texts, including in the Bible, where John the Baptist describes one whose "winnowing fork is in his hand" to clear the threshing floor. The winnowing basket has also served as a ritual object in several cultures.
