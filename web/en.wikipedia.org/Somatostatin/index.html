Somatostatin, also known as growth hormone-inhibiting hormone (GHIH), is a peptide hormone that regulates the endocrine system and affects neurotransmission and cell proliferation via interaction with G protein-coupled somatostatin receptors. Broadly, somatostatin acts as a universal inhibitor: it suppresses the release of numerous other hormones and secretions.

Sources and forms

Somatostatin is produced by neurons in the hypothalamus, by delta cells of the pancreatic islets of Langerhans, and by endocrine cells scattered through the stomach and intestine. A single precursor is cleaved to two active forms: somatostatin-14 (predominant in the brain and pancreas) and somatostatin-28 (predominant in the gut).

Actions

From the hypothalamus, somatostatin travels by the portal circulation to the anterior pituitary, where it inhibits secretion of growth hormone and, to a lesser degree, thyroid-stimulating hormone. In the pancreas it acts locally to suppress insulin and glucagon. In the gut it slows gastric emptying, reduces acid and digestive secretion, and decreases absorption of nutrients. It also functions as an inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system.

Clinical use

Natural somatostatin has a half-life of only a few minutes, so longer-acting analogues — octreotide, lanreotide, and pasireotide — are used clinically to treat acromegaly, neuroendocrine tumours and their hormone-excess syndromes, and bleeding esophageal varices.
