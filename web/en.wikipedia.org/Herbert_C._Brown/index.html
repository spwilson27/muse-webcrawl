Herbert C. Brown

Herbert Charles Brown (May 22, 1912 – December 19, 2004) was an American chemist and recipient of the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 1979 for his work with organoboranes.

Life and career

Brown was born Herbert Brovarnik in London, to Ukrainian Jewish immigrants. His family moved to Chicago in June 1914, when he was two years old. His father died when Brown was 14, and he had to leave school to run the family hardware store, though he returned and graduated, eventually entering the University of Chicago, where he earned his bachelor's degree in 1936 and his PhD in 1938, studying under H. I. Schlesinger. He married Sarah Baylen in 1937.

After a postdoctoral period and a position at Wayne University (now Wayne State University) in Detroit, Brown joined the faculty of Purdue University in 1947, where he spent the rest of his career. He was named the R. B. Wetherill Professor of Chemistry.

Work

During World War II, working with Schlesinger on the production of sodium borohydride for the war effort, Brown laid the groundwork for organoboron chemistry. In the 1950s he and his student B. C. Subba Rao discovered hydroboration — the syn-addition of diborane across carbon–carbon double bonds — while investigating the reducing properties of borohydrides. Hydroboration–oxidation became a standard, exceptionally versatile method for converting alkenes to alcohols with anti-Markovnikov regioselectivity, and organoboranes became a major class of synthetic intermediates, later also central to the Suzuki coupling.

Brown was also a central figure in the famous "nonclassical ion" controversy with Saul Winstein over the structure of the 2-norbornyl cation, which Brown maintained could be explained by classical rapidly equilibrating structures; the dispute lasted decades and was largely resolved in Winstein's favour by later spectroscopic and computational work.

In 1979, Brown shared the Nobel Prize in Chemistry with Georg Wittig "for their development of boron-containing and phosphorus-containing compounds, respectively, into important reagents in organic synthesis."

Death

Brown died on December 19, 2004, in Lafayette, Indiana, of a heart attack. He published more than 1,300 scientific papers and trained a large school of organoboron chemists.
