Trinidad Scorpion

SpeciesCapsicum chinense
OriginTrinidad and Tobago
HeatExceptionally hot
Scoville scale~1.2–2 million SHU (Butch T)

The Trinidad Scorpion is a group of exceptionally hot chili pepper cultivars of the species Capsicum chinense, native to Trinidad and Tobago. The peppers are named for the pointed "tail" or stinger at the blossom end of the fruit, resembling a scorpion's stinger.

Varieties and records

The best-known variety is the Trinidad Scorpion Butch T, propagated by Butch Taylor. In 2011 it was recognised by Guinness World Records as the world's hottest chili pepper, with a measured heat of about 1,463,700 Scoville heat units (SHU), surpassing the Naga Viper. The Trinidad Moruga Scorpion was later measured at up to about 2 million SHU in a 2012 study by the Chile Pepper Institute of New Mexico State University, though individual pods vary greatly. Both were subsequently surpassed by the Carolina Reaper.

Characteristics

The plants grow relatively tall for C. chinense and produce wrinkled, lantern-shaped pods that ripen from green to bright red (or yellow and chocolate in some variants). Beyond their extreme pungency — driven by high concentrations of capsaicin — the peppers are noted for a fruity, floral flavour that appears briefly before the heat builds.

Culinary use

Because of their extreme heat, Trinidad Scorpion peppers are used sparingly, mainly in hot sauces and as a heat-boosting ingredient. Handlers typically wear gloves, and the peppers have also been explored for use in non-lethal applications and marine anti-fouling paints.
