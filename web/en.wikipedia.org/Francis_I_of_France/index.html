Francis I (French: François Ier; 12 September 1494 – 31 March 1547) was King of France from 1515 until his death in 1547. He was the son of Charles, Count of Angoulême, and Louise of Savoy. He succeeded his first cousin once removed and father-in-law, Louis XII, who died without a legitimate son.

A prodigious patron of the arts, Francis promoted the nascent French Renaissance, attracting many Italian artists to his court, including Leonardo da Vinci, who brought the Mona Lisa to France and died in the king's arms at Clos Lucé in 1519. Francis's reign saw important cultural and institutional changes: the rise of absolute monarchy, the dissemination of humanism and Protestantism, the beginning of French exploration of the New World, and the development of a French empire in the Americas.

Reign

Francis began his reign with a spectacular victory at the Battle of Marignano (1515), which recovered the Duchy of Milan for France. Much of his reign was consumed by the Italian Wars against the Habsburg emperor Charles V, a rivalry that dominated European politics. Francis was defeated and captured at the Battle of Pavia in 1525 and held prisoner in Madrid until he signed the Treaty of Madrid (1526), which he repudiated on his release. In 1520 he met Henry VIII of England at the lavish Field of the Cloth of Gold.

Seeking allies against the Habsburgs, Francis concluded a Franco-Ottoman alliance with Suleiman the Magnificent — a scandalous but durable partnership in Christian Europe — and also sought Protestant allies among the German princes, even while persecuting Protestants at home after the Affair of the Placards (1534).

Patronage and reforms

Francis built or enlarged the great châteaux of the Loire Valley, most famously Chambord and Fontainebleau, and began the rebuilding of the Louvre. He founded the Collège de France (1530) to promote humanist scholarship, established the royal library that became the Bibliothèque nationale, and in the Ordinance of Villers-Cotterêts (1539) required the use of French in official documents, an important step in the development of the French language and administration. He sponsored the voyages of Jacques Cartier, who claimed Canada for France, and of Giovanni da Verrazzano along the North American coast.

Francis died at Rambouillet on 31 March 1547 and was succeeded by his son Henry II. He is often called le Roi-Chevalier ("the Knight-King") and is remembered as France's first Renaissance monarch.
