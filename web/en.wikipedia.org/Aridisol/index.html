Aridisol

Aridisols (from Latin aridus, "dry", and solum, "soil") are a soil order in USDA soil taxonomy. They are the dry soils of deserts and semi-arid regions, occupying about 12% of Earth's ice-free land surface and roughly 19% of the land area of the United States, chiefly in the Southwest.

Characteristics

Aridisols form under arid or semi-arid climates where evapotranspiration greatly exceeds precipitation for most of the year. They typically have low organic matter content, light-coloured surface horizons, and subsurface accumulations of calcium carbonate, gypsum, or soluble salts leached downward by infrequent rains. Common diagnostic horizons include calcic, gypsic, and salic horizons; some develop a hardened caliche or duripan layer. Because water is scarce, soil-forming processes are slow, and profiles are weakly developed.

Use and management

Without irrigation, Aridisols support mainly desert vegetation and extensive grazing. With irrigation and careful management of salinity and drainage, they can be productive agricultural soils, as in parts of the southwestern United States, though salinisation is a persistent risk. Aridisols are subdivided into suborders including Argids, Calcids, Cambids, Cryids, Durids, Gypsids, and Salids.
