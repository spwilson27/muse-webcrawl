Whistleblower

Whistleblowing
DefinitionDisclosure of wrongdoing within an organization to outsiders or authorities
Key protectionsWhistleblower Protection Act (US, 1989); EU Whistleblower Directive (2019)

A whistleblower is a person, often an employee, who reveals information about illegal, immoral, illicit, unsafe, or fraudulent activities within a private or public organization. The information is typically disclosed to authorities, the media, or the public, often at considerable personal and professional risk to the whistleblower.

Origins of the term

The term derives from referees and police officers blowing a whistle to signal wrongdoing. It was popularized in its modern sense in the 1970s by consumer advocate Ralph Nader, who sought a word free of the negative connotations of "informer" or "snitch."

Legal protection

Many jurisdictions protect whistleblowers from retaliation. In the United States, protections include the Civil Service Reform Act of 1978, the Whistleblower Protection Act of 1989, the Sarbanes–Oxley Act (2002) for corporate employees, and the Dodd–Frank Act (2010), which created financial rewards for reporting securities violations. The European Union adopted a Whistleblower Directive in 2019 requiring member states to protect reporting persons. Protection remains uneven worldwide, and whistleblowers frequently face dismissal, prosecution, or exile.

Notable whistleblowers

Famous cases include Daniel Ellsberg, who released the Pentagon Papers in 1971; the Watergate source "Deep Throat," revealed in 2005 to be FBI associate director Mark Felt; Jeffrey Wigand, who exposed tobacco industry practices; Edward Snowden, who disclosed global surveillance programs in 2013; and Frances Haugen, who released internal Facebook documents in 2021.
