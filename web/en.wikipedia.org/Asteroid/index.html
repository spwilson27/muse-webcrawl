Asteroids are minor planets — rocky, airless remnants of the early Solar System that never accreted into a planet. Most orbit the Sun in the main asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter, but asteroids are found throughout the inner Solar System, including near-Earth asteroids whose orbits cross that of Earth, and Trojan asteroids sharing the orbit of Jupiter and other planets.

Discovery and naming

The first asteroid, Ceres, was discovered by Giuseppe Piazzi in 1801 and initially classed as a planet. After many similar bodies were found, they were reclassified as asteroids (from the Greek asteroeidēs, "star-like", a term coined by William Herschel). Discoveries accelerated with astrophotography in the late nineteenth century; today automated surveys have catalogued well over a million asteroids. Numbered asteroids receive names approved by the International Astronomical Union.

Composition and classification

Asteroids are classified by their spectra and albedo. C-type (carbonaceous) asteroids are dark, primitive and the most numerous in the outer belt. S-type (silicaceous) asteroids are made of stony material and dominate the inner belt. M-type asteroids are largely metallic, thought to be fragments of the cores of differentiated planetesimals shattered by collisions. Many asteroids are "rubble piles" — loose aggregates held together by gravity rather than solid monoliths.

Exploration

Spacecraft have visited numerous asteroids. NASA's NEAR Shoemaker landed on 433 Eros in 2001; Japan's Hayabusa and Hayabusa2 returned samples from Itokawa (2010) and Ryugu (2020); NASA's OSIRIS-REx returned a sample of Bennu in 2023. In 2022 the DART mission deliberately impacted Dimorphos, the moon of 65803 Didymos, demonstrating asteroid deflection for planetary defence.

Impact hazard

Impacts by large asteroids have shaped Earth's history — most famously the Chicxulub impact 66 million years ago, associated with the extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs. Space agencies maintain surveys of near-Earth objects and track potentially hazardous asteroids larger than about 140 metres.
