Cognitive behavioral therapy

Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) is a form of psychotherapy that aims to reduce symptoms of various mental health conditions, primarily depression and anxiety disorders, by challenging and changing cognitive distortions (such as thoughts, beliefs, and attitudes) and their associated behaviors, to improve emotional regulation, and to develop personal coping strategies that target solving current problems.

History

CBT grew out of two traditions: behavior therapy, rooted in the work of Pavlov, Watson, and Skinner and developed clinically by Joseph Wolpe and Hans Eysenck in the 1950s; and cognitive therapy, developed by Aaron T. Beck in the 1960s when he identified patterns of distorted automatic thinking in depression. The two approaches merged during the 1970s and 1980s, and Albert Ellis's rational emotive behavior therapy formed a related precursor. Later "third wave" approaches, including acceptance and commitment therapy and dialectical behavior therapy, extended the model with mindfulness and acceptance strategies.

Method

Treatment is usually structured, time-limited, and problem-focused. Therapist and client identify specific difficulties, set goals, and use techniques such as cognitive restructuring, behavioral activation, graded exposure, activity scheduling, and homework assignments in which the client tests new interpretations and behaviors between sessions. Sessions typically number between five and twenty depending on the condition.

Evidence and uses

CBT has been studied in hundreds of clinical trials and is recommended in treatment guidelines for depression, anxiety disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder, obsessive–compulsive disorder, eating disorders, insomnia, and chronic pain, among other conditions. It is usually considered to have comparable effectiveness to medication for several disorders, sometimes with more durable effects after treatment ends. Delivery formats include individual and group therapy, guided self-help, and computerized or internet-delivered CBT.
