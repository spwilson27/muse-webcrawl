The Missa Papae Marcelli, or Pope Marcellus Mass, is a mass setting by Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina, scored for six voices a cappella. It is the best-known of Palestrina's masses and is frequently taught as the archetype of Renaissance polyphony. It was published in Palestrina's second book of masses in 1567 and is generally thought to honour Pope Marcellus II, who reigned briefly in 1555.

The legend

According to a long-repeated story, the Council of Trent considered banning polyphonic music from the liturgy because its complexity made the sacred text unintelligible. Palestrina was said to have composed the mass to demonstrate that polyphony could remain clear and reverent, thereby "saving" church music. The tale, popularised in the nineteenth century and dramatised in Hans Pfitzner's opera Palestrina, is now regarded by scholars as largely legendary, though a commission of cardinals in 1564–65 did test masses for textual clarity.

Music

The mass consists of the Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus (with Benedictus) and Agnus Dei. The Gloria and Credo favour chordal, syllabic declamation so that the words can be understood, while the Kyrie is more melismatic. The Agnus Dei II expands to seven voices. The work derives partly from Palestrina's earlier motet and mass material.

Legacy

The mass became a model of counterpoint instruction, studied by composers from the Baroque era onward, and was adapted and edited by figures including Johann Joseph Fux, whose Gradus ad Parnassum codified the "Palestrina style".
