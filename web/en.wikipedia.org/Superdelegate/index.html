Superdelegate

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Superdelegates — formally automatic delegates — were delegates to the Democratic National Convention in the United States who were seated automatically rather than being chosen through primaries or caucuses. The category comprised Democratic governors, senators, members of Congress, former presidents and vice presidents, and other party leaders and officials.

History

The superdelegate system was created after the 1980 election by the Hunt Commission, as part of reforms intended to restore a role for elected officials and party leaders who had been marginalised by post-1968 reforms, while keeping them a minority of the convention. Superdelegates historically numbered around 15–20% of all delegates and were free to vote for any candidate.

Criticism and reform

Critics argued that superdelegates could override the popular will of primary voters, and their early endorsements were widely reported as a running tally that shaped perceptions of races in 2008 and 2016. Following the 2016 election, the Unity Reform Commission recommended reducing their power; in August 2018 the Democratic National Committee adopted rules barring superdelegates from voting on the first nominating ballot unless the outcome was already determined by pledged delegates.

The Republican Party has never had a comparable large class of unbound delegates, though some state parties award automatic seats to party officials who are usually bound by their state's results.
