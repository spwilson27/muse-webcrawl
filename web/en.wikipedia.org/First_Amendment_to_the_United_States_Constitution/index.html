First Amendment to the United States Constitution

First Amendment
Part ofUnited States Bill of Rights
Proposed25 September 1789
Ratified15 December 1791
ProtectsReligion, speech, press, assembly, petition

The First Amendment to the United States Constitution prevents Congress from making laws respecting an establishment of religion; prohibiting the free exercise of religion; or abridging the freedom of speech, the freedom of the press, the freedom of assembly, or the right to petition the government for redress of grievances. It was adopted on 15 December 1791 as one of the first ten amendments composing the Bill of Rights.

Congress shall make no law respecting an establishment of religion, or prohibiting the free exercise thereof; or abridging the freedom of speech, or of the press; or the right of the people peaceably to assemble, and to petition the Government for a redress of grievances.

Religion clauses

The Establishment Clause and the Free Exercise Clause together govern religion. The Supreme Court has held that the Establishment Clause, applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, forbids government establishment or endorsement of religion, while the Free Exercise Clause protects religious belief absolutely and religious practice subject to neutral laws of general applicability, as in Employment Division v. Smith (1990), partly answered by Congress in the Religious Freedom Restoration Act.

Speech and press

Freedom of speech is among the most litigated rights in American law. Landmark cases include Schenck v. United States (1919), Brandenburg v. Ohio (1969), which protects advocacy unless directed to inciting imminent lawless action and likely to produce it, New York Times Co. v. Sullivan (1964) on defamation of public officials, and New York Times Co. v. United States (1971), which rejected prior restraint of the Pentagon Papers. Certain categories — obscenity, defamation, fraud, true threats and incitement — receive reduced or no protection, while commercial speech and content-based regulation occupy intermediate ground.

Assembly and petition

The rights of assembly and petition protect peaceful protest, association for expressive purposes, and appeals to government, and underpin much of American labour, civil rights and protest law.
