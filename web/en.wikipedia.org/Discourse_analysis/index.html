Discourse analysis

Analysts examine patterns of language as used in real contexts.

Discourse analysis (DA) is a broad set of approaches for studying written, spoken, or signed language in use — discourse — beyond the level of the individual sentence, with attention to social, political, and cultural context. The term was introduced by linguist Zellig Harris in 1952. Rather than a single unified method, discourse analysis is an interdisciplinary field spanning linguistics, anthropology, sociology, psychology, communication studies, and literary studies.

Approaches

Major traditions include: conversation analysis, which studies the sequential organization of talk (turn-taking, repair, adjacency pairs) in recordings of natural interaction; discursive psychology, which examines how psychological categories are constructed in talk; the ethnography of communication associated with Dell Hymes; pragmatic and functional analyses of speech acts and coherence; and corpus-based discourse studies, which use large text collections to identify recurrent patterns of wording and collocation.

Critical discourse analysis

Critical discourse analysis (CDA), developed by scholars including Norman Fairclough, Teun A. van Dijk, and Ruth Wodak, explicitly links linguistic analysis to power, ideology, and inequality, examining how texts and talk reproduce or challenge social dominance — for example in political speeches, news coverage, and institutional communication. In the social sciences the Foucauldian tradition treats "discourses" as systems of thought that define what can be said and known about a topic, shaping institutions and subjectivities.

Applications

Discourse analysis is applied in education, healthcare communication, media studies, law (forensic linguistics), marketing, political science, and research on social media. Its practitioners caution that interpretation must be grounded in detailed attention to the text or talk itself, in its context of production and reception.
