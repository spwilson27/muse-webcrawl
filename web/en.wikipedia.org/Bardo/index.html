Bardo

In some schools of Tibetan Buddhism, bardo (Tibetan: བར་དོ་, Wylie: bar do) is an intermediate state or liminal state that exists between death and rebirth. The concept arose soon after the Buddha's death, with a number of earlier Buddhist groups accepting the existence of such an intermediate state, while other schools rejected it. Used more loosely, the term can also denote any transitional or in-between state of existence and experience.

The six bardos

According to the tradition popularised by the Bardo Thodol (often translated as the Tibetan Book of the Dead), attributed to Padmasambhava, six bardos are commonly enumerated: the bardo of this life (birth and waking life), the bardo of dreaming, the bardo of meditation, the bardo of the moment of death, the bardo of dharmata (the luminosity and visions following death) and the bardo of becoming, in which the consciousness seeks a new rebirth in one of the realms of samsara. The intermediate state is traditionally said to last a maximum of 49 days after death.

Practice

The Bardo Thodol is read aloud to the dying and the recently deceased to guide awareness through the visions of peaceful and wrathful deities, understood as projections of one's own mind. Recognising the clear light of dharmata offers liberation; failing that, the deceased is guided towards a favourable rebirth. Related practices such as dream yoga and phowa (transference of consciousness) prepare practitioners for the bardo states.

Influence

The term entered Western popular culture through translations of the Tibetan Book of the Dead, notably W. Y. Evans-Wentz's 1927 edition, and influenced writers, psychologists and artists; Carl Jung wrote a psychological commentary on the text.
