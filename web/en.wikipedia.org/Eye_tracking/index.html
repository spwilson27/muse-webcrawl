Eye tracking

Eye tracking is the process of measuring either the point of gaze (where one is looking) or the motion of an eye relative to the head. An eye tracker is a device for measuring eye positions and eye movement. Eye trackers are used in research on the visual system, in psychology, psycholinguistics, marketing, as an input device for human–computer interaction, and in product design.

History

Early eye tracking in the 19th century was done by direct observation. In the 1950s, Alfred L. Yarbus showed that scan paths over a picture depend on the viewer's task. Modern video-based trackers use the pupil centre and infrared corneal reflections (Purkinje images) to compute gaze direction; head-mounted glasses and remote screen-based trackers are now common.

Measures

The basic units of analysis are fixations (periods when the eyes are relatively still, typically 200–300 ms) and saccades (rapid movements between fixations). Derived visualizations include gaze plots, heat maps, and areas of interest (AOI) statistics such as time to first fixation and dwell time.

Applications

Applications include usability testing of websites and software, advertising and packaging research, reading research, assistive communication devices for people with motor disabilities (such as those with ALS), pilot and driver monitoring, and foveated rendering in virtual reality headsets, which renders detail only where the user is looking to save computation.
