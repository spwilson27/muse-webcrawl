The Papal Basilica of St. Peter in the Vatican (Italian: Basilica Papale di San Pietro in Vaticano), or simply St. Peter's Basilica, is a church built in the Renaissance style located in Vatican City, the papal enclave that is within the city of Rome, Italy. It was initially planned in the 15th century by Pope Nicholas V and then Pope Julius II to replace the aging Old St. Peter's Basilica, which was built in the fourth century by Roman emperor Constantine the Great. Construction of the present basilica began on 18 April 1506 and was completed on 18 November 1626.

Designed principally by Donato Bramante, Michelangelo, Carlo Maderno, and Gian Lorenzo Bernini, St. Peter's is one of the most renowned works of Italian Renaissance architecture and is the largest church in the world by interior measure. While it is neither the mother church of the Catholic Church nor the cathedral of the Diocese of Rome (titles held by the Archbasilica of Saint John Lateran), St. Peter's is regarded as one of the holiest Catholic shrines.

Burial site of Saint Peter

Catholic tradition holds that the basilica is the burial site of Saint Peter, chief among Jesus's apostles and the first Bishop of Rome (pope). Saint Peter's tomb is supposedly directly below the high altar of the basilica, also known as the Altar of the Confession. For this reason, many popes have been interred at St. Peter's since the Early Christian period.

Architecture

The basilica's dome, designed by Michelangelo and completed after his death, rises to 136.6 metres and long served as a model for domes across the Western world. Carlo Maderno extended the nave and added the facade, and Bernini created the vast elliptical St. Peter's Square and the bronze baldachin over the high altar, cast partly from bronze taken from the Pantheon's portico. Inside are works including Michelangelo's Pietà and Bernini's Chair of Saint Peter (Cathedra Petri).
