Interlaced video

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Interlaced video (also known as interlaced scan) is a technique for doubling the perceived frame rate of a video display without consuming extra bandwidth. The interlaced signal contains two fields of a video frame captured consecutively. This enhances motion perception to the viewer and reduces flicker by taking advantage of the phi phenomenon.

This effectively doubles the time resolution (also called temporal resolution) as compared with non-interlaced footage (for frame rates equal to field rates). Interlaced signals require a display that is natively capable of showing the individual fields in a sequential order. CRT displays and plasma displays are capable of displaying interlaced signals.

Fields

Interlaced video consists of two fields. In a typical system, one field contains all the odd-numbered horizontal lines of the picture and the other field contains all the even-numbered lines. The two fields are captured and displayed alternately, so that the eye perceives a complete picture. Common designations include 480i, 576i and 1080i, where the number is the count of vertical lines and the "i" indicates interlaced scanning.

Interlacing and computers

Interlacing can cause problems when video is displayed on progressive-scan devices such as computer monitors and modern flat-panel televisions. Because only one field is present in the signal at a time, displaying an interlaced signal without deinterlacing produces combing artefacts: adjacent lines from different fields appear offset, giving moving edges a serrated, comb-like appearance. Deinterlacing algorithms attempt to reconstruct full frames from the fields.

History

Interlaced scanning was developed in the 1920s and 1930s, notably through work associated with RCA engineer Randall Ballard, whose 1932 patent described interlaced scanning for television. It allowed early television systems to achieve acceptable flicker performance within limited channel bandwidth and became standard for analogue broadcast systems such as NTSC, PAL and SECAM.
