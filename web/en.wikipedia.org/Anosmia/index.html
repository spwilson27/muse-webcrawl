Anosmia

Anosmia is the loss of the sense of smell.

Anosmia is the loss of the sense of smell, either total or partial. It may be temporary or permanent, and can result from problems in the nose, the olfactory nerves, or the brain. A reduced ability to smell is termed hyposmia, while a heightened sense of smell is hyperosmia. Because smell contributes greatly to the perception of flavour, anosmia also diminishes the sense of taste, and it can pose safety risks because sufferers cannot detect smoke, gas leaks, or spoiled food.

Causes

Common causes include upper respiratory tract infections, chronic rhinosinusitis and nasal polyps, allergic rhinitis, and head trauma that shears the olfactory filaments passing through the cribriform plate. Anosmia attracted widespread attention during the COVID-19 pandemic, when sudden loss of smell became a hallmark symptom of SARS-CoV-2 infection, thought to arise from damage to supporting cells of the olfactory epithelium. Anosmia can also be an early sign of neurodegenerative diseases such as Parkinson's disease and Alzheimer's disease, and it occurs congenitally in syndromes such as Kallmann syndrome. Certain medications, smoking, ageing, and exposure to toxic chemicals can also impair smell.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis involves history-taking, nasal endoscopy or imaging, and standardised smell tests such as the University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test (UPSIT). Treatment depends on the cause: inflammatory causes may respond to corticosteroids, and olfactory training—repeatedly sniffing a set of odours over several months—has evidence of benefit for post-infectious loss. Many cases, particularly post-viral ones, improve spontaneously over weeks to months, though some deficits persist.

Impact

Studies link anosmia to depression, reduced quality of life, and increased mortality in older adults, in part because smell is tied to appetite, memory, and hazard detection. Patient organisations such as Fifth Sense and AbScent advocate for recognition of smell disorders as significant health conditions.
