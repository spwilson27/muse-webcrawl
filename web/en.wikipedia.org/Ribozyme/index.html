Ribozyme

Ribozymes (ribonucleic acid enzymes) are RNA molecules that have the ability to catalyze specific biochemical reactions, including RNA splicing in gene expression, similar to the action of protein enzymes. The most common activities of natural or in vitro-evolved ribozymes are the cleavage or ligation of RNA and DNA and peptide bond formation.

Discovery

Before ribozymes were discovered, enzymes—catalytic molecules—were assumed to be exclusively proteins. In 1981–1982, Thomas Cech showed that the RNA intron of the protozoan Tetrahymena thermophila could splice itself out without any protein, and Sidney Altman demonstrated that the RNA component of RNase P is the catalytic subunit. Cech and Altman shared the 1989 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for the discovery of catalytic RNA.

Types

Well-characterised natural ribozymes include the hammerhead, hairpin, hepatitis delta virus (HDV), Varkud satellite and glmS ribozymes, as well as the large ribozymes: the self-splicing group I and group II introns, RNase P, and the ribosome itself — whose peptidyl transferase centre is an RNA catalyst, making the ribosome the largest and most important ribozyme.

Significance

The discovery of ribozymes was central evidence for the RNA world hypothesis, which proposes that early life used RNA both to store genetic information and to catalyse chemical reactions, before DNA and proteins took over those roles. Artificial ribozymes evolved in the laboratory are studied as tools for gene therapy, biosensors and synthetic biology.
