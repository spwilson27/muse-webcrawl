Deadweight loss

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A deadweight loss, also known as excess burden or allocative inefficiency, is a loss of economic efficiency that occurs when the equilibrium outcome for a good or service is not achieved or is not achievable. It represents the loss of total welfare or total surplus (the sum of producer and consumer surplus) arising from market distortions such as taxes, subsidies, price controls, externalities and monopoly pricing.

Causes

Deadweight loss can arise whenever the quantity traded in a market differs from the efficient quantity at which marginal benefit equals marginal cost:

  • Taxes drive a wedge between the price paid by buyers and the price received by sellers, discouraging mutually beneficial trades. The resulting loss grows approximately with the square of the tax rate.
  • Monopoly and market power: a monopolist restricts output to raise price above marginal cost, so trades that would benefit both sides do not occur.
  • Price ceilings and floors, such as rent control and minimum wages, prevent markets from clearing.
  • Externalities: when prices do not reflect external costs (e.g. pollution) or benefits, too much or too little of a good is produced.
  • Subsidies can cause overproduction, generating a deadweight loss on the extra units whose cost exceeds their value to consumers.

Harberger triangles

The welfare loss is conventionally drawn as a triangle between the supply and demand curves — the "Harberger triangle", after economist Arnold Harberger, whose 1954 study estimated the deadweight loss from monopoly in American manufacturing. For a small distortion, the loss is approximated by ½ × (price distortion) × (change in quantity), which is why the loss rises with the square of a tax: doubling a tax both doubles the wedge and roughly doubles the reduction in quantity traded.

Example

Consider a market for nails where the efficient price is $1 per box. If a tax of $0.50 per box reduces the quantity sold, consumers pay more, producers receive less, and the government collects revenue on the remaining sales. The tax revenue itself is a transfer, not a loss; the deadweight loss consists only of the value of the trades that no longer happen — purchases buyers would have valued above the cost of production but which the tax makes unprofitable.
