An imprimatur (Latin for "let it be printed") is a declaration by a bishop of the Roman Catholic Church that a written work contains nothing contrary to Catholic faith or morals and may be published. It is traditionally printed in the front matter of the book, together with the nihil obstat of the diocesan censor who reviewed the text.

Meaning and scope

The imprimatur is not an endorsement of the author's opinions: it certifies only that the work is free of doctrinal or moral error, and the author remains responsible for its contents. Under canon law, certain categories of books — including editions of Scripture, liturgical books, catechisms, and theology textbooks — require the approval of the local ordinary before publication. The practice developed in the era of print censorship after the Reformation, when the Church also maintained the Index Librorum Prohibitorum (Index of Prohibited Books, abolished in 1966).

Metaphorical use

In general usage, "imprimatur" has come to mean any official sanction or approval stamp — for example, a senior figure's endorsement lending legitimacy to a policy or candidate.
