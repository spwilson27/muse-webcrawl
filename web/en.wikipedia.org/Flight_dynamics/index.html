Flight dynamics

Flight dynamics is the science of air vehicle orientation and control in three dimensions. The three critical flight dynamics parameters are the angles of rotation in three dimensions about the vehicle's centre of gravity, known as pitch, roll and yaw. These are collectively known as the aircraft's attitude.

Engineers develop flight control systems to govern a vehicle's attitude, and pilots use them to control the forces of flight and the aircraft's configuration. In fixed-wing aircraft, changes in attitude are brought about by deflecting control surfaces, which alter the distribution of aerodynamic force about the centre of gravity.

The three axes

The longitudinal axis runs from nose to tail; rotation about it is roll, controlled by the ailerons. The lateral axis runs from wingtip to wingtip; rotation about it is pitch, controlled by the elevator. The vertical (normal) axis runs from top to bottom through the centre of gravity; rotation about it is yaw, controlled by the rudder. The intersection of the three axes is the centre of gravity, the point at which the aircraft's mass is considered concentrated.

Stability and control

Flight dynamics also encompasses the study of stability: static stability is the initial tendency of an aircraft to return to its original attitude after a disturbance, while dynamic stability describes the motion over time, including oscillatory modes such as the phugoid and the short-period mode in pitch, and the Dutch roll and spiral modes in lateral-directional motion. Aircraft designers balance stability against manoeuvrability: highly stable aircraft are easy to fly but respond sluggishly, while unstable designs depend on active flight control systems.
