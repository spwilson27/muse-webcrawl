Superconductivity

Superconductivity is a set of physical properties observed in certain materials where electrical resistance vanishes and magnetic fields are expelled from the material (the Meissner effect) below a characteristic critical temperature. It was discovered by Heike Kamerlingh Onnes in 1911, when he found that mercury's resistance dropped to zero at 4.2 K.

Theory

The phenomenon was explained microscopically by Bardeen, Cooper, and Schrieffer in 1957 (BCS theory): electrons bind into Cooper pairs mediated by lattice vibrations (phonons), and these pairs condense into a single quantum state that flows without scattering. Superconductors are classified as type I, which expel fields completely, and type II, which admit quantized magnetic flux vortices (fluxons) above a lower critical field.

High-temperature superconductors

In 1986 Bednorz and Müller discovered superconductivity at around 35 K in a ceramic copper oxide (cuprate), opening the era of "high-temperature" superconductivity; yttrium barium copper oxide (YBCO) superconducts at 92 K, above the boiling point of liquid nitrogen. Iron-based superconductors followed in 2008, and in 2015 hydrogen sulfide under extreme pressure reached 203 K. The mechanism of cuprate superconductivity remains incompletely understood.

Applications

Superconducting magnets power MRI scanners, particle accelerators such as the Large Hadron Collider, and fusion experiments; other applications include SQUID magnetometers, Josephson-junction electronics and qubits, maglev trains, and high-capacity power cables.
