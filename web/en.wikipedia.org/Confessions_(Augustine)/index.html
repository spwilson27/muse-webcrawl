Confessions (Augustine)

Confessions (Latin: Confessiones) is an autobiographical work by Saint Augustine of Hippo, consisting of 13 books written in Latin between AD 397 and 400. The work outlines Augustine's sinful youth and his conversion to Christianity, and is widely regarded as the first Western autobiography ever written. It was influential for later Christian writers and philosophers and is considered a literary masterpiece of late antiquity.

Content

The first nine books are autobiographical, recounting Augustine's childhood in Thagaste, his time as a student and teacher of rhetoric, his years as a Manichaean, the influence of Ambrose of Milan, and his conversion in a Milanese garden in 386 — the famous tolle, lege ("take up and read") episode. The last four books turn philosophical and theological, treating memory, time and eternity, and an exegesis of the opening of Genesis.

Legacy

The Confessions shaped the Western tradition of introspective self-examination and influenced figures from Petrarch to Rousseau (who borrowed the title for his own autobiography) and Wittgenstein, who admired the work. Its discussion of time — "What then is time? If no one asks me, I know; if I wish to explain it, I do not know" — remains a starting point in the philosophy of time.
