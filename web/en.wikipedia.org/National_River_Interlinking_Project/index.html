The National River Interlinking Project (NRLP) is a proposed large-scale civil engineering programme in India that aims to connect the country's rivers by a network of reservoirs and canals, transferring water from water-surplus basins to water-deficit regions. The scheme, formalised by the National Water Development Agency (NWDA) under the National Perspective Plan of 1980, envisages 30 links — 14 under the Himalayan component and 16 under the peninsular component.

Rationale

Supporters argue interlinking could bring some 35 million hectares under additional irrigation, generate roughly 34,000 MW of hydroelectric power, mitigate the simultaneous floods and droughts that afflict different regions, and provide drinking water security. The idea has roots in proposals by the engineer Sir Arthur Cotton in the 19th century and K. L. Rao and Captain Dinshaw Dastur in the 1970s.

Progress

Progress has been slow owing to cost, inter-state water disputes, and environmental objections. The Ken–Betwa link in Madhya Pradesh and Uttar Pradesh, long the priority project, received cabinet approval in December 2021 and entered construction in the mid-2020s. In Andhra Pradesh, the Pattiseema lift scheme (2015) linked the Godavari and Krishna ahead of the larger Polavaram project.

Criticism

Hydrologists and environmentalists question the assumption of permanent "surplus" basins, the ecological damage from dams and submergence (including to Panna Tiger Reserve in the Ken–Betwa case), displacement of communities, seismic and sedimentation risks, and the scheme's enormous cost — estimated at several lakh crore rupees. Opponents advocate watershed development, groundwater recharge, and demand management instead.
