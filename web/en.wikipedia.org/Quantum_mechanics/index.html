Quantum mechanics

Quantum mechanics is a fundamental theory in physics that describes the behaviour of nature at and below the scale of atoms. It departs from classical mechanics by quantizing energy, momentum and other quantities, and by describing particles with wave functions that exhibit wave–particle duality.

Overview

The theory was developed in the 1920s by physicists including Werner Heisenberg, Erwin Schrödinger, Max Born, Niels Bohr, Wolfgang Pauli and Paul Dirac, building on Max Planck's 1900 quantum hypothesis and Albert Einstein's 1905 explanation of the photoelectric effect. The state of a system is represented by a wave function whose evolution is governed by the Schrödinger equation; measurement outcomes are probabilistic, with probabilities given by the Born rule.

Key concepts

Central ideas include superposition, quantisation of energy levels, the uncertainty principle, entanglement, tunnelling and spin. The theory underlies the periodic table and chemical bonding, and technologies such as lasers, transistors, MRI scanners and — under development — quantum computers. Its interpretation (Copenhagen, many-worlds, pilot-wave and others) remains debated. For a related concept from statistics, see Fisher's exact test; for the older revolution in cosmology, see Nicolaus Copernicus.

History

Key milestones include Planck's black-body law (1900), Bohr's model of the atom (1913), de Broglie's matter waves (1924), Heisenberg's matrix mechanics and Schrödinger's wave mechanics (1925–26), Dirac's relativistic equation (1928) and the EPR paper and Bell's theorem (1935, 1964), whose experiments confirmed entanglement.
