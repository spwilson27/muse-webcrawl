Cobden–Chevalier Treaty

Signed23 January 1860
PartiesUnited Kingdom and France
NegotiatorsRichard Cobden; Michel Chevalier
SubjectTariff reductions and free trade

The Cobden–Chevalier Treaty was a free trade agreement signed between the United Kingdom and France on 23 January 1860. It is named after its principal negotiators, the British free-trade campaigner Richard Cobden and the French economist Michel Chevalier. The treaty is regarded as a landmark in the nineteenth-century movement toward liberalized trade.

Terms

France undertook to reduce its tariffs on British goods, abolishing prohibitions and capping duties at a maximum of 30 percent, later lowered further; Britain in turn reduced duties on French products such as wine and brandy and removed duties on manufactured goods. Crucially, the treaty included a most-favoured-nation clause, so concessions granted to one trading partner would extend to others, multiplying the treaty's liberalizing effect.

Effects

The agreement triggered a network of similar treaties across Europe in the 1860s, as France and Britain each concluded most-favoured-nation agreements with Belgium, the German Zollverein, Italy, and other states, substantially lowering European tariffs. Trade between Britain and France grew rapidly after 1860. The liberal era the treaty inaugurated faded after the late 1870s, as economic depression and competition revived protectionism on the continent, though Britain remained committed to free trade into the twentieth century.

Background

The treaty followed Britain's repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846 and reflected Napoleon III's desire to modernize the French economy and improve relations with Britain. It was negotiated partly in secret because of strong protectionist opposition in France.
