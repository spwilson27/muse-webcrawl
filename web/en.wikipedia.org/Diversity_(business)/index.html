Diversity (business)

Diversity, in the context of business and the workplace, refers to the variety of characteristics that make individuals different from one another within an organisation, including race, ethnicity, gender, age, religion, disability, sexual orientation, education, socioeconomic background and national origin. Workplace diversity programmes aim to create and maintain an environment that recognises, respects and values these differences, and diversity management has become a significant field within human resource management.

History

The modern concept of workplace diversity developed in the United States largely in response to the civil rights movement and the legislation that followed it, including the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and affirmative action policies. Early efforts focused mainly on legal compliance and the avoidance of discrimination. By the 1980s and 1990s, consultants and academics increasingly argued that diversity, if properly managed, could improve creativity, decision-making and organisational performance, shifting the emphasis from compliance toward a "business case" for diversity. In 1990, R. Roosevelt Thomas Jr. published an influential Harvard Business Review article arguing that organisations should move "from affirmative action to affirming diversity".

Concepts and approaches

Diversity is closely related to, but distinct from, the concepts of equity and inclusion, and the three are often grouped together as diversity, equity and inclusion (DEI). Diversity concerns the composition of the workforce; inclusion concerns whether employees feel valued, respected and able to participate fully; equity concerns fairness in treatment, access and opportunity. Common organisational practices include diversity training, mentoring programmes, employee resource groups, supplier diversity initiatives, and the measurement of representation at different levels of management and leadership.

Business case

Advocates argue that diverse teams bring a wider range of perspectives and information, which can improve problem-solving and innovation, help companies understand diverse customer markets, and broaden the pool of available talent. Studies by consulting firms such as McKinsey & Company have reported correlations between executive-team diversity and financial outperformance, although the strength and interpretation of such findings are debated by researchers, and correlation does not by itself establish causation.

Criticism

Critics of corporate diversity programmes argue that they can become a form of tokenism, that mandatory training may be ineffective or produce backlash, and that an excessive focus on demographic categories can obscure questions of class or individual merit. Since the early 2020s, diversity programmes in the United States have also become politically contested, and some companies have revised or rebranded their initiatives.
