Digital intermediate

Digital intermediate (DI) is a motion picture finishing process which classically involves digitising a motion picture and manipulating the colour and other image characteristics. It often replaces or augments the photochemical timing process and is usually the final creative adjustment to a film before distribution. The DI process is also a stage in the migration from physical film media to digital distribution.

Workflow

In a typical DI workflow, the original camera negative is scanned at high resolution (commonly 4K or higher) by a film scanner. Editing, conforming, visual effects, colour grading and title insertion are performed digitally, and the finished master is recorded back to film with a film recorder for theatrical prints, or delivered as a digital cinema package. Films shot digitally skip the scanning step but otherwise follow the same finishing process.

History

Early experiments scanned and manipulated individual shots for effects, but full-feature DIs emerged around 2000. O Brother, Where Art Thou? (2000) is widely credited as the first Hollywood feature to undergo a complete digital intermediate for colour grading, using the process to achieve its distinctive desaturated sepia look. Pleasantville (1998) had earlier scanned most of a feature for its selective-colour effects. Within a decade DI became the default finishing route, and the colourist's role expanded from photochemical timing to sophisticated digital grading.

Colour grading and creative impact

Digital grading allows selective adjustment of hue, saturation and contrast in isolated regions or colours, power windows tracking moving subjects, and consistent matching across material from different cameras. Critics argue the process encouraged fashionable looks such as heavy orange-and-teal palettes, while archivists note that DI masters, colour decisions encoded in lookup tables, and digital files complicate long-term preservation compared with a timed negative.
