Cavity magnetron
TypeVacuum tube (microwave oscillator)
Invented1940 (Randall and Boot, Birmingham)
Typical frequency~2.45 GHz (domestic ovens); various radar bands
ApplicationsRadar, microwave ovens, industrial heating

The cavity magnetron is a high-power vacuum tube that generates microwaves using the interaction of a stream of electrons with a magnetic field while moving past a series of resonant cavities. It made centimetric radar practical during the Second World War and is now the standard microwave source in microwave ovens.

History

Earlier magnetrons produced only low power at useful frequencies. In 1940, John Randall and Harry Boot at the University of Birmingham built a multi-cavity design producing kilowatts at a wavelength of about 10 cm. The device was taken to the United States by the Tizard Mission and mass-produced, transforming airborne and naval radar and contributing significantly to the Allied war effort.

Operation

A heated cathode emits electrons into an evacuated space surrounded by a cylindrical anode block containing resonant cavities. Permanent magnets apply a magnetic field perpendicular to the electric field, causing electrons to spiral outward in bunches that excite oscillations in the cavities; an antenna probe extracts the microwave energy.

Applications

Beyond wartime radar, magnetrons power most microwave ovens (at 2.45 GHz), some industrial heating and plasma systems, and older radar installations, though solid-state sources have replaced them in many modern radars.
