Habitability

FieldAstrobiology; ecology
Key conceptCapacity of an environment to support life
Classic criterionPresence of liquid water, energy and nutrients

Habitability is the capacity of an environment — a habitat, planet or other astronomical body — to support and sustain life. In astrobiology, planetary habitability is most often assessed by whether conditions allow liquid water to persist on a surface, since all known life requires water, together with an energy source and the chemical elements needed for organic chemistry (carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus and sulphur).

The habitable zone

The circumstellar habitable zone (or "Goldilocks zone") is the range of orbital distances around a star where an Earth-like planet with sufficient atmospheric pressure could maintain liquid water at its surface. For the Sun this zone extends roughly from just inside Earth's orbit to around the orbit of Mars, depending on assumptions about greenhouse warming. Planets such as Kepler-186f and Proxima Centauri b were identified as lying in their stars' habitable zones.

Beyond the classical picture

Habitability need not mean Earth-likeness. Subsurface oceans on icy moons such as Europa and Enceladus, heated by tides, may be habitable far outside the classical zone. On Earth, extremophile organisms in deep mines, hydrothermal vents and hypersaline lakes demonstrate that life tolerates conditions once thought prohibitive. Conversely, a planet in the habitable zone may still be uninhabitable — for example if it has lost its water or lacks a protective atmosphere or magnetic field, as may have happened to early Mars and Venus.

Measurement

Researchers use indices such as the Earth Similarity Index and the Planetary Habitability Index to rank exoplanets, while emphasising that habitability is not the same as being inhabited.
