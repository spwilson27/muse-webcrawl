Quadratic reciprocity

The symmetry expressed by the law of quadratic reciprocity

In number theory, the law of quadratic reciprocity is a theorem about modular arithmetic that gives conditions for the solvability of quadratic equations modulo prime numbers. It relates the Legendre symbols (p/q) and (q/p) for odd primes p and q: the two symbols are equal unless both primes are congruent to 3 modulo 4, in which case they have opposite signs.

Statement

Let p and q be distinct odd primes. Then (p/q)(q/p) = (−1)((p−1)/2)·((q−1)/2). Together with the supplementary laws giving (−1/p) and (2/p) in terms of p modulo 4 and 8, the law reduces the evaluation of any Legendre symbol to routine computation, and hence determines which numbers are quadratic residues modulo a prime.

History

Versions of the result were conjectured by Euler and by Legendre, who introduced the symbol and published an incomplete proof. Carl Friedrich Gauss gave the first complete proof in his Disquisitiones Arithmeticae (1801), calling it the theorema aureum — the "golden theorem" — and returned to it repeatedly, publishing several distinct proofs over his lifetime. More than two hundred proofs are now known. The law was the starting point of class field theory: its generalisations describe reciprocity laws for higher powers in algebraic number fields.
