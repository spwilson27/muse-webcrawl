Trickle-down economics

"Trickle-down" is a pejorative label for policies said to favour the wealthy in the expectation that benefits reach everyone.

Trickle-down economics is a term used in critical references to economic policies that disproportionately favour the upper tiers of the wealthy. The term has been used broadly by critics of supply-side economics and tax cuts for high earners and corporations to suggest that such policies ultimately, but indirectly, benefit the wider population through investment, business growth and job creation.

The phrase was popularised in the 1930s by humorist Will Rogers, who wrote during the Great Depression that money appropriated for the top "hopefully will benefit the man below" — coining the "trickle down" image as a joke at the policy's expense. Critics in the 1980s applied it to the tax reductions of the Reagan administration in the United States and the Thatcher government in the United Kingdom; supporters of those policies generally rejected the label and described them instead as supply-side or growth-oriented economics.

Debate

Advocates argue that lower marginal tax rates and reduced capital taxes increase incentives to work, save and invest, raising productivity and wages. Critics argue that such policies primarily increase income inequality and asset prices, pointing to research finding weak links between top-rate tax cuts and broad income growth. The term remains largely a political epithet: economists who support the underlying policies rarely self-describe them as "trickle-down."
