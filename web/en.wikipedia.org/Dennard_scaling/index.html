Dennard scaling

Dennard scaling, also known as MOSFET scaling, is a scaling law based on a 1974 paper co-authored by Robert H. Dennard, which originally stated that, as transistors get smaller, their power density stays constant. In other words, as each generation of technology shrank transistor dimensions and lowered voltages, circuits could run at higher speeds while consuming roughly the same power per unit area.

Under Dennard scaling, smaller transistors kept constant power density

The law

Dennard's rules held that if transistor dimensions and supply voltage both scale down by a factor of about 0.7 per generation, delay falls, frequency rises, and power per transistor drops by about half while the number of transistors per chip doubles — leaving power density unchanged. This underwrote decades of progress alongside Moore's law: chips got faster mainly by raising clock speeds.

Breakdown

Around 2005–2007, Dennard scaling broke down. Threshold voltages could not keep shrinking without excessive leakage current, so supply voltages stopped falling proportionally; power density then rose with each generation. Clock frequencies plateaued near 3–4 GHz, and the industry pivoted to multicore processors and specialized accelerators instead of ever-faster single cores.

Consequences

The end of Dennard scaling produced the phenomena known as dark silicon — portions of a chip that must remain powered off to stay within thermal limits — and shifted computer architecture toward parallelism, energy efficiency, and domain-specific hardware such as GPUs and neural-network accelerators.
