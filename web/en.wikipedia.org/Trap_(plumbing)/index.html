Trap (plumbing)

Trap (plumbing)
PurposeRetain water to block sewer gases
Common typesP-trap, S-trap, bottle trap, U-bend
Typical seal depth50–100 mm of water

In plumbing, a trap is a device, usually a U-, S- or J-shaped section of pipe, that retains a small quantity of water after each use of a fixture. This retained water forms a seal that prevents sewer gases — including methane, hydrogen sulfide and other noxious or hazardous gases — from entering buildings through drain lines, while still allowing wastewater and solids to pass through. Traps are fitted beneath or within sinks, basins, baths, showers, floor drains and water closets.

Types

The P-trap, named for its shape when combined with a horizontal outlet, is the most common form in modern installations: waste turns through a U-bend and exits horizontally into the wall. The older S-trap exits vertically downward through the floor, but is prohibited in many jurisdictions because it is prone to siphoning its own seal dry. A bottle trap uses an internal dip tube within a cylindrical body and is often used where appearance matters, under exposed basins. Water closets have an integral trap cast into the porcelain, visible as the S- or P-shaped passage of the bowl. Deep-seal traps retain extra water for fixtures used infrequently, and trap primers can replenish seals that might evaporate in floor drains.

Loss of seal

A trap protects occupants only while its water seal survives. Seals can be lost by self-siphonage (a full-bore discharge pulling the water out), induced siphonage from other fixtures, back-pressure from the sewer, evaporation in rarely used drains, capillary action over debris lodged in the bend, and wind effects at the vent terminal. Building codes therefore require traps to be protected by vent pipes that equalise pressure in the drainage system, and specify minimum seal depths (commonly 50 mm, or 75 mm for some fixtures). Dry traps in little-used floor drains became a public-health concern after investigations linked dried-out U-traps to the spread of SARS through a Hong Kong housing block in 2003, and traps are also checked in studies of aerosolised pathogen transmission in hospital drainage.

History

The principle of the water-sealed trap is often credited to Alexander Cummings, who patented an S-shaped trap in 1775, with Thomas Crapper popularising reliable trap designs for water closets in the Victorian era as indoor plumbing and sewers spread. The connection between sewer gas and disease, debated through the nineteenth century, made the trap a standard requirement in plumbing codes worldwide.
