Wren

Wrens are a family, Troglodytidae, of small, brown, insectivorous songbirds. The family includes about 96 species in some 19 genera, overwhelmingly confined to the New World, with the greatest diversity in Central and South America. The sole Old World representative is the Eurasian wren (Troglodytes troglodytes), widespread across Europe and Asia.

Description

Wrens are small — typically 10–22 cm long — with brown, often barred plumage, slender downcurved bills and short rounded wings; many habitually hold their tails cocked upright. Despite their size, they are famous for extraordinarily loud, complex songs, among the most powerful per unit of body mass of any bird. The name Troglodytes ("cave-dweller") refers to their habit of creeping into crevices and building domed nests.

Behaviour

Most wrens are inconspicuous birds of dense undergrowth, feeding on insects and spiders gleaned from vegetation and leaf litter. Males of many species build several "cock nests", one of which the female selects and lines. Some species, such as the cactus wren of the American Southwest, inhabit deserts; the canyon wren haunts rocky gorges, while the marsh wrens live in reedbeds. The house wren is among the most widely distributed birds in the Americas.

Evolution and distribution

The family appears to have originated in North America, radiating southward; the single Eurasian species reached the Old World via Beringia. The New Zealand "wrens" (Acanthisittidae) are an unrelated, more ancient lineage despite the shared name.
