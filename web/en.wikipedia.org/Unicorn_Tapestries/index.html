The Unicorn Tapestries

The Unicorn Tapestries, formally known as The Hunt of the Unicorn, are a series of seven tapestries woven in the Southern Netherlands around 1495–1505. They are among the most celebrated works of late medieval art and are displayed at The Cloisters, the medieval branch of the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York City.

Description

Woven from wool, silk, and silver- and gold-wrapped thread, the tapestries depict a group of noblemen and hunters pursuing a unicorn through dense, flower-filled woodland in the millefleurs style. The sequence shows the unicorn found, attacked, defended, tamed (traditionally by a virgin), killed, and finally — in the most famous panel, The Unicorn in Captivity — alive again and contentedly fenced within a garden.

History and interpretation

The tapestries belonged for centuries to the La Rochefoucauld family of France; their early history is uncertain, and the repeated cipher "A" and "E" woven into them may identify the original patron. John D. Rockefeller Jr. purchased them in 1922 and donated them to the Met in 1937. Scholars have read the hunt as an allegory of courtly love, of the Passion of Christ, and of marriage.
