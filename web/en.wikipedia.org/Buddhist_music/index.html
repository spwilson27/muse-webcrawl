Buddhist music is music created for or inspired by Buddhism and includes many ritual and devotional traditions across Asia. The most widespread form is chant — the melodic recitation of sutras, mantras, and verses of praise — used in monastic and lay practice for meditation, merit-making, and ceremony. Although early Buddhist monastic rules were ambivalent about music as entertainment, chanting scriptures became a central and accepted part of practice.

Forms by tradition

In Theravada countries such as Sri Lanka, Thailand, and Myanmar, chanting is usually in Pali, often in a restrained, syllabic style. In Chinese Buddhism, fanbai hymnody developed elaborate melodic chanting, which in Japan became shōmyō, the ritual chant of Tendai and Shingon schools. Tibetan Buddhism features deep overtone chanting by monks, accompanied in rituals by instruments such as long horns (dungchen), oboes (gyaling), conch shells, bells, and drums. Korean beompae chant is a highly ornamented ritual vocal art.

Instruments and ritual

Temple instruments mark ritual time and accompany chant: wooden fish drums, temple blocks, gongs, handbells, and the large bell struck before services. In Japan, gagaku-derived and shōmyō traditions preserve some of the oldest continuous chant repertoires in the world.

Modern Buddhist music

In the modern era, composers and popular musicians have produced explicitly Buddhist music, from the hymns set by the Chinese monk Hong Yi to contemporary recordings of mantras, meditation music, and artists such as Imee Ooi, who composes Buddhist songs and chants in several languages. Buddhist themes also appear in Western classical and popular music.
