Atomic nucleus

Schematic of a nucleus: protons (red) and neutrons (blue) bound together at the atom's centre.

The atomic nucleus is the small, dense region consisting of protons and neutrons at the centre of an atom, discovered in 1911 by Ernest Rutherford based on the gold foil experiment performed by Hans Geiger and Ernest Marsden. Protons and neutrons are bound together to form a nucleus by the nuclear force.

The diameter of the nucleus is in the range of about 1.7 fm (femtometres) for hydrogen to about 15 fm for uranium — roughly 100,000 times smaller than the atom as a whole — yet the nucleus contains more than 99.97% of the atom's mass. The collective name for protons and neutrons is nucleons; the number of protons (the atomic number, Z) determines the chemical element, while nuclei of the same element with different neutron numbers are called isotopes.

History

Rutherford's scattering experiments overturned J. J. Thomson's plum pudding model and established the nuclear model of the atom, elaborated by Niels Bohr in 1913. James Chadwick discovered the neutron in 1932, resolving the discrepancy between atomic number and atomic mass. Hideki Yukawa proposed in 1935 that nucleons interact via the exchange of mesons (pions), the first theory of the strong nuclear force.

Structure and models

Nuclear physicists describe the nucleus with several complementary models: the liquid drop model treats it as an incompressible charged fluid and explains binding energies via the semi-empirical mass formula; the nuclear shell model, developed by Maria Goeppert Mayer and J. Hans D. Jensen, explains the enhanced stability of "magic numbers" of nucleons (2, 8, 20, 28, 50, 82, 126). The semi-empirical mass formula is based on calculus-driven fits to measured masses and underlies understanding of fission and fusion.

Stability and decay

Only certain combinations of protons and neutrons form stable nuclei. Unstable nuclei undergo radioactive decay — alpha, beta or gamma emission — or spontaneous fission. The energy released in nuclear reactions, governed by the mass–energy equivalence E = mc², powers nuclear weapons, reactors, and the stars through fusion.
