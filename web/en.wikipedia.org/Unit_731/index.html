Unit 731

Unit 731 (Japanese: 731部隊) was a covert biological and chemical warfare research and development unit of the Imperial Japanese Army that engaged in lethal human experimentation and the production of biological weapons during the Second Sino-Japanese War (1937–1945) and World War II. Based at Pingfang, near Harbin in Japanese-occupied Manchuria, it was headed by Lieutenant General Shirō Ishii. It is estimated that between 3,000 and 12,000 prisoners — mostly Chinese civilians and prisoners of war, but also Koreans, Mongolians, Russians and others — died in experiments at Pingfang alone; broader estimates including field attacks with biological agents run far higher.

Activities

Prisoners, referred to by staff as "logs" (maruta), were subjected to vivisection without anaesthesia, deliberate infection with plague, cholera, anthrax and other pathogens, frostbite experiments, weapons tests, and pressure-chamber and centrifuge experiments. The unit mass-produced plague-infected fleas and ceramic bombs for dissemination, and Japanese forces used biological agents against Chinese cities and villages, including attacks on Ningbo and Changde, and contamination of wells and farmland during the 1942 Zhejiang-Jiangxi campaign — attacks that also killed large numbers of Japanese troops.

End of the war and immunity

As Soviet forces invaded Manchuria in August 1945, the unit destroyed its facilities and killed its remaining prisoners; Ishii ordered staff to take the secret "to the grave." After Japan's surrender, United States authorities granted Ishii and other senior members immunity from prosecution in exchange for their experimental data, which was channelled into the American biological weapons programme at Fort Detrick. As a result, Unit 731's crimes were not tried at the Tokyo Tribunal; twelve members of related units were tried by the Soviet Union at the Khabarovsk trial of 1949. The episode remained little known in the West until journalists and historians brought it to wider attention in the 1980s and 1990s.

Legacy

The Pingfang site is now a museum in China. Japan's wartime human experimentation remains a source of diplomatic tension, and the immunity deal is frequently cited in discussions of wartime justice and the ethics of using data obtained through atrocity.
