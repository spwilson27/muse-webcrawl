Khanqah

Courtyard of a historic khanqah in Central Asia.

A khanqah (Persian: خانقاه; also khanaqah, and related terms such as ribat, zawiya, and tekke in other regions) is a building designed specifically for gatherings of a Sufi brotherhood (tariqa) and a place of spiritual retreat. Khanqahs served as hospices for travelling Sufis, centres of teaching, and sites of communal worship and charity.

History

The institution developed in the eastern Islamic world, especially Khurasan, by around the 10th–11th centuries, as Sufism became organized into orders. Supported by endowments (waqf) from rulers and wealthy patrons, khanqahs spread across Persia, Central Asia, Anatolia, Egypt, and India. Famous examples include the khanqah of Baybars II in Cairo and the many lodges founded around the shrines of saints such as Ahmad Yasawi in Turkestan.

Functions

A khanqah typically included cells for resident dervishes, a prayer hall, a kitchen providing free food (a forerunner of the langar tradition), and rooms for guests. Residents followed a rule of life under a shaykh, practising dhikr (remembrance of God), receiving visitors, and offering instruction. Khanqahs were important in spreading Islam in frontier regions and in sustaining networks of learning and hospitality across the Islamic world.
