Sensitive period


Imprinting in goslings is a classic example of a sensitive period.

In developmental psychology and developmental biology, a sensitive period is a time window during development when the effects of experience on the brain are unusually strong. A sensitive period that is essential for the proper development of a trait is more strictly called a critical period: if the organism does not receive the appropriate stimulus during this time, the corresponding function may fail to develop normally, and later learning may be difficult or impossible.

Examples

Classic examples include imprinting in young birds, first described by Konrad Lorenz: goslings follow the first moving object they see during a short window after hatching. In humans, sensitive periods are studied in the development of binocular vision, where early visual deprivation from congenital cataracts can cause lasting amblyopia, and in language acquisition, where children deprived of linguistic input in early life — or who learn a second language later — typically show reduced proficiency in phonology and grammar. The critical period hypothesis for language, popularised by Eric Lenneberg, proposes that language is acquired most readily before puberty.

Mechanisms

Sensitive periods reflect heightened neural plasticity: synapses are overproduced early in development and then pruned according to experience. Molecular "brakes" on plasticity, including the maturation of inhibitory circuits and the formation of perineuronal nets, close sensitive periods; research in animals suggests they can sometimes be reopened pharmacologically or through environmental manipulation.

Sensitive versus critical periods

The term sensitive period is often preferred because most developmental windows are not absolutely rigid: learning outside them is harder but rarely impossible, and the boundaries are gradual rather than sharp. Stronger critical periods apply to basic sensory capacities such as stereoscopic vision, whereas language and social behaviour show graded sensitivity.
