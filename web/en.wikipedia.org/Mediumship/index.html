Mediumship

Mediumship is the practice of purportedly mediating communication between familiar spirits or spirits of the dead and living human beings. Practitioners are known as "mediums" or "spirit mediums". They claim to hear, see, or feel messages from spirits, or to allow a spirit to control their body during a trance. Mediumship is central to modern Spiritualism, which arose in the mid-19th century, and also appears in traditions such as Kardecist spiritism, Umbanda, and various shamanic practices.

History

Attempts to communicate with the dead occur in many cultures, from the Witch of Endor in the Hebrew Bible to Chinese ancestor veneration. Modern mediumship is usually dated to 1848, when the Fox sisters of Hydesville, New York, reported mysterious rappings they attributed to a spirit. The movement spread rapidly through the United States and Europe, attracting figures such as Arthur Conan Doyle, while sceptics including Harry Houdini campaigned against fraudulent mediums.

Types of mediumship

Mental mediumship involves clairvoyance, clairaudience and clairsentience — impressions received in the medium's mind. Physical mediumship purportedly produces phenomena such as ectoplasm, materialisations, levitation and direct voice. Trance mediumship or channeling involves the medium speaking while in an altered state, allegedly under a spirit's control. Séances are sittings in which sitters gather to receive messages.

Criticism and investigation

The scientific community generally regards mediumship as a pseudoscientific practice explained by cold reading, confirmation bias, ideomotor effects and, in some cases, deliberate fraud. Investigations by the Society for Psychical Research (founded 1882) and later researchers found some mediums, such as Leonora Piper, difficult to explain by fraud alone, but no demonstration of mediumship has been reproducible under controlled conditions.
