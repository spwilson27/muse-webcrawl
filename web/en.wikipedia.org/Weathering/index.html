Weathering

Weathered rock in a desert environment, broken down in place by temperature changes and salt crystallization.

Weathering is the breaking down of rocks, soils and minerals as well as wood and artificial materials through contact with the atmosphere, water and biological organisms of the Earth. Weathering occurs in situ (on-site, with little or no movement), and so is distinct from erosion, which involves the transport of rocks and minerals by agents such as water, ice, snow, wind, waves and gravity.

Physical weathering

Physical (mechanical) weathering disintegrates rock without changing its chemistry. Processes include thermal expansion and contraction, frost wedging (water freezing and expanding in cracks), unloading and exfoliation as overlying material erodes away, abrasion, and salt crystallization in pores. Repeated cycles gradually fracture even hard granite into grus and rubble.

Chemical weathering

Chemical weathering alters the composition of minerals. Rainwater, naturally slightly acidic from dissolved carbon dioxide, drives hydrolysis of feldspars into clay minerals, oxidation of iron-bearing minerals into rusty oxides, dissolution of carbonate rocks such as limestone (producing karst landscapes), and hydration reactions. Chemical weathering is fastest in warm, wet climates and is a key long-term regulator of atmospheric carbon dioxide.

Biological weathering

Living organisms contribute through root growth prising apart cracks, burrowing, the production of organic acids by microbes and lichens, and the mixing of soil by earthworms and other fauna — a process Darwin studied in his final book.

Products and significance

Weathering produces regolith and soil, liberates nutrients for ecosystems, shapes landforms such as tors, tafoni, and spheroidally weathered boulders, and degrades buildings and monuments. The rate of weathering depends on rock type, climate, topography, and time.
