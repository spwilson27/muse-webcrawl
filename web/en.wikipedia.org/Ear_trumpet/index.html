A 19th-century ear trumpet.

An ear trumpet is a funnel-shaped acoustic device used by people with hearing loss to collect sound waves and direct them into the ear canal, amplifying speech passively. Ear trumpets were the most common form of hearing aid from the 17th century until the advent of electronic amplification in the early 20th century.

History

Descriptions of ear trumpets appear in the 17th century, including in the works of the physician and polymath Athanasius Kircher. By the late 18th and 19th centuries, firms in London and elsewhere produced trumpets in tin, brass, silver, horn and tortoiseshell, in collapsible and decorative forms — some concealed in fans, canes or furniture ("acoustic chairs") for users conscious of their disability. Ludwig van Beethoven used ear trumpets made for him by Johann Nepomuk Mälzel during his years of worsening deafness.

How it works

The wide bell of the trumpet gathers sound over a larger area than the ear alone and funnels it to the narrow earpiece, increasing sound pressure at the eardrum by a modest amount, typically around 10–15 decibels for speech frequencies. Ear trumpets provided no frequency shaping and limited gain, but they remained in use well into the 1900s before being displaced first by carbon and then vacuum-tube and transistor hearing aids.
