Forensic ballistics

Comparison microscopy is central to firearm identification work.

Forensic ballistics is the science of analyzing firearm usage in crimes. It is a branch of forensic science concerned with the examination of firearms, ammunition and their components, and the reconstruction of shooting incidents. The term is slightly misleading, as ballistics strictly refers to the study of projectile motion, whereas forensic firearm examination covers the identification of weapons and ammunition from the marks they bear, the determination of bullet trajectories, and the estimation of muzzle-to-target distances.

Firearm identification

When a bullet is fired through a rifled barrel, the lands and grooves of the rifling engrave characteristic striations onto the bullet's surface. Firing pins, breech faces, extractors and ejectors likewise leave impressions and marks on cartridge cases. Because of microscopic imperfections introduced during manufacture and subsequent wear, these toolmarks are held to be characteristic of an individual weapon. Examiners compare test-fired bullets and cartridge cases from a suspect weapon with evidence recovered from a crime scene using a comparison microscope, viewing both specimens side by side to assess whether the marks agree.

Databases

In the United States, the National Integrated Ballistic Information Network (NIBIN), administered by the Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives, stores digital images of cartridge-case marks so that evidence from different crime scenes can be linked. Similar systems operate in other countries.

Other examinations

Forensic ballistics also encompasses gunshot residue analysis, the detection of primer and propellant residues on a shooter's hands or clothing; shooting reconstruction, including trajectory analysis using probes, lasers and photogrammetry; and range determination, often from the pattern of unburned powder and stippling around a wound or target. Serial number restoration, using chemical etching to recover obliterated markings on a firearm, is another common task.

Criticism

The individualization claims of firearm identification have been criticized by statisticians and in reports such as the 2009 National Academy of Sciences report on forensic science and the 2016 PCAST report, which questioned the foundational validity of subjective pattern-matching disciplines. Some jurisdictions have limited the language examiners may use in testimony as a result.
