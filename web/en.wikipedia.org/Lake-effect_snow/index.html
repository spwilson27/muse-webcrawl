Lake-effect snow

Lake-effect snow is a weather phenomenon in which cold air passing over the relatively warm waters of a large lake picks up moisture and heat, becomes unstable, and then deposits heavy, localised snowfall on the lee shore as the air rises and cools over land. It produces narrow, intense snow bands that can drop several feet of snow on one community while neighbouring areas remain nearly clear.

The phenomenon is most famous downwind of the North American Great Lakes — around Buffalo and Rochester (New York), Erie (Pennsylvania), and Michigan's Upper Peninsula — where seasonal totals can exceed 300 cm. Similar effects occur downwind of the Great Salt Lake, the Black and Caspian Seas, and in Japan, where moist air from the Sea of Japan produces the heavy "snow country" falls on Honshu's western mountains. Key conditions include a temperature difference of at least about 13 °C between lake surface and air at 850 hPa, long fetch across open water, and favourable wind direction.
