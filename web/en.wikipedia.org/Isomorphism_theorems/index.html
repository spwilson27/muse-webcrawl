Isomorphism theorems

In mathematics, specifically abstract algebra, the isomorphism theorems (also known as Noether's isomorphism theorems) are theorems that describe the relationships between quotients, homomorphisms and subobjects. Versions exist for groups, rings, vector spaces, modules and other algebraic structures; they were formulated by Emmy Noether in the 1920s, building on earlier work by Richard Dedekind and others.

First isomorphism theorem

If φ: G → H is a group homomorphism, then the quotient of G by the kernel of φ is isomorphic to the image of φ: G / ker φ ≅ im φ. In other words, a homomorphism's image is the domain factored by exactly the elements sent to the identity.

Second and third theorems

The second isomorphism theorem relates a subgroup S and a normal subgroup N: S ∩ N is normal in S, and S / (S ∩ N) ≅ SN / N. The third theorem states that if M and N are normal subgroups with M ⊆ N, then (G / M) / (N / M) ≅ G / N — quotients can be "cancelled". A related result, the correspondence theorem, sets up a bijection between subgroups containing the kernel and subgroups of the quotient.
