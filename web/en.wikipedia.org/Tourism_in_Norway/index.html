Tourism in Norway

Geirangerfjord, a UNESCO World Heritage Site

Tourism in Norway centres on the country's dramatic natural scenery — its fjords, mountains, waterfalls, glaciers, and Arctic phenomena such as the northern lights and the midnight sun — together with Viking heritage and the cities of Oslo, Bergen, and Tromsø. Tourism is a significant and growing part of the Norwegian economy, contributing several percent of GDP and employment, with domestic travellers accounting for the majority of trips.

Attractions

The western fjords are the signature draw: Geirangerfjord and Nærøyfjord are UNESCO World Heritage Sites, and the Norway in a Nutshell itinerary links the Flåm Railway — one of the world's steepest standard-gauge railways — with fjord cruises. In the north, Tromsø and the Lofoten Islands attract winter visitors for aurora viewing, whale watching, and skiing; the North Cape is marketed as Europe's northernmost point. Historic sites include Bergen's Bryggen wharf, the stave churches such as Borgund, and the rock art at Alta.

Outdoor recreation

Norway's tradition of friluftsliv (open-air life) and the right of public access to uncultivated land (allemannsretten) underpin hiking, camping, and skiing tourism. Famous hikes include Trolltunga, Preikestolen (Pulpit Rock), and Besseggen. Norway is also a major winter-sports destination and the most successful nation in Winter Olympic history.

Challenges

Rapid growth in cruise traffic and visitors to a few iconic viewpoints has raised concerns about congestion, safety on exposed mountain hikes, and environmental impact, prompting debate over tourist taxes and visitor management in small fjord communities.
