Biogeography is the study of the distribution of species and ecosystems in geographic space and through geological time. It asks why organisms live where they do: how dispersal, extinction, continental drift, glaciation and climate have shaped the patterns of life on Earth. The field combines geography, ecology, evolutionary biology, geology and climatology, and its findings underpin conservation planning in an era of rapid environmental change.

History

Biogeography began as explorers and naturalists documented the world's floras and faunas: Alexander von Humboldt mapped plant distributions and climate zones in the early nineteenth century, while Alfred Russel Wallace — co-discoverer of natural selection — divided the world into faunal regions in The Geographical Distribution of Animals (1876) and drew the famous "Wallace Line" through the Malay Archipelago separating Asian from Australasian faunas. In the twentieth century, plate tectonics explained disjunct distributions by vicariance (the splitting of ancestral ranges), complementing the older emphasis on dispersal.

Concepts

Biogeographers divide the land into realms — Nearctic, Palearctic, Neotropical, Afrotropical, Indomalayan, Australasian, Oceanian and Antarctic — each with characteristic assemblages shaped by history. Endemic species occur only in one place, often islands or ancient refugia. The theory of island biogeography, set out by Robert MacArthur and E. O. Wilson in 1967, predicts species richness on an island from its area and isolation, and became a foundation of conservation biology. Latitudinal diversity gradients — the increase in species richness toward the tropics — remain one of the field's central puzzles.

Applications

Modern biogeography uses phylogenetics, fossils, climate models and GIS to reconstruct past distributions and forecast how species will shift under climate change; it guides the design of protected-area networks, the management of invasive species, and the tracing of emerging diseases.
