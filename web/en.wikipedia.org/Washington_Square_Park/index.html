Washington Square Park

Washington Square Park is a 9.75-acre public park in the Greenwich Village neighbourhood of Lower Manhattan, New York City. One of the city's best-known parks, it is an open space landmark dominated by the Washington Square Arch at the foot of Fifth Avenue, with a large central fountain. It is a gathering place for residents, students of nearby New York University, street performers and chess players, and has a long association with New York's artistic and countercultural life.

History

The land was once marshy ground beside the Minetta Brook and served the city as a potter's field — a burial ground for the poor and for victims of yellow fever epidemics — and as a place of public execution; a large elm in the northwest corner is traditionally known as the Hangman's Elm. In 1826 the city converted the ground into a military parade ground, and it became Washington Square, named for George Washington. Elegant Greek Revival rowhouses, including the surviving "Row" on Washington Square North, made the area fashionable.

The marble Washington Square Arch, designed by Stanford White, was erected in 1892 to replace a temporary wooden arch built for the 1889 centennial of Washington's inauguration. In the 1950s, plans backed by Robert Moses to run a roadway through the park were defeated by local activists including Eleanor Roosevelt and Jane Jacobs, and the park was closed to traffic in 1958. It later became famous as a centre of the folk music revival — and of protests against police bans on folk singing in 1961 — and remains a hub of performance and demonstration.

Features

The park's chess tables in the southwest corner are famous in American chess culture; the central fountain plaza hosts performers; there are separate playgrounds, dog runs, and statues of Washington and of Giuseppe Garibaldi. A renovation completed in phases from 2009 realigned the fountain with the arch and refurbished the landscaping.
