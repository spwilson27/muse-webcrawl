Revolutions of 1848

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Revolutions of 1848, known in some countries as the Springtime of the Peoples or the Springtime of Nations, were a series of republican revolts against European monarchies, beginning in Sicily in January 1848 and spreading to France, the German states, the Austrian Empire, Italy, Denmark, and beyond. They remain the most widespread revolutionary wave in European history, though within a year most had been suppressed by conservative forces.

Background

The uprisings grew out of widespread dissatisfaction with the conservative order established by the Congress of Vienna in 1815. A poor harvest, food price inflation, industrial unemployment, and resentment of autocratic rule — associated above all with Austrian chancellor Klemens von Metternich — combined with liberal demands for constitutions and nationalist aspirations among Germans, Italians, Hungarians, Poles, and others.

Course

Revolt began in Palermo in January 1848. In February, revolution in Paris overthrew King Louis Philippe and proclaimed the French Second Republic. Uprisings followed in Vienna, forcing Metternich's resignation, in Berlin, Milan, Venice, Prague, and Budapest, where Lajos Kossuth led the Hungarian independence struggle. The Frankfurt Parliament convened to draft a constitution for a united Germany.

Failure and legacy

Divisions between middle-class liberals, radical workers, and competing national movements weakened the revolutions. Armies loyal to the old regimes recovered the initiative: the June Days in Paris were crushed, Vienna and Prague were retaken, Russian troops intervened in Hungary, and the Frankfurt Parliament dissolved in 1849. Nevertheless, 1848 permanently ended serfdom in the Austrian Empire, brought universal male suffrage to France, and kept liberal and nationalist programmes alive for later generations.
