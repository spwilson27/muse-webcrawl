Radio-frequency identification

An RFID tag (foreground) is read wirelessly by a reader using radio waves.

Radio-frequency identification (RFID) uses electromagnetic fields to automatically identify and track tags attached to objects. An RFID system consists of a tiny radio transponder (the tag), a radio receiver, and a transmitter (the reader). When triggered by an electromagnetic interrogation pulse from a nearby reader device, the tag transmits digital data, usually an identifying inventory number, back to the reader.

Unlike a barcode, the tag does not need to be within the line of sight of the reader, so it may be embedded in the tracked object. RFID is one method of automatic identification and data capture (AIDC).

Tags

Passive tags collect energy from the reader's radio waves and have no battery; they are cheap and small — some are as thin as a sheet of paper — but have a short read range. Active tags carry a battery and can broadcast signals over tens of metres or more. Read-only tags carry a factory-set serial number, while read/write tags allow user data to be stored and updated.

Applications

RFID is used in supply-chain and inventory management, access control badges, contactless payment cards, electronic toll collection, livestock and pet identification, library systems, passports, and in near-field communication (NFC) applications in smartphones. Retailers use item-level tagging to speed stocktaking and reduce shrinkage.

History and concerns

The technology descends from wartime identification friend-or-foe transponders and a resonant-circuit espionage device built by Léon Theremin in 1945; commercial development accelerated from the 1970s. Privacy advocates have raised concerns about covert tracking of tagged goods and documents, prompting standards for tag deactivation and data protection.
