Resampling (signal processing)

FieldSignal processing
OperationChanging the sampling rate of a discrete signal

In signal processing, resampling is the process of changing the sampling rate of a discrete signal — creating a new representation of the same underlying continuous signal at a different rate. Increasing the rate is called upsampling or interpolation, and decreasing it is called downsampling or decimation. Resampling is essential whenever signals recorded or generated at one rate must be used in a system working at another, as in converting audio from 44.1 kHz (CD) to 48 kHz (video and broadcast).

Theory

According to the Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem, a sampled signal can perfectly represent a band-limited continuous signal if the sampling rate exceeds twice its highest frequency. Resampling exploits this: the discrete samples are conceptually converted back into the underlying continuous waveform and then re-sampled at the new rate. In practice this is done digitally, most directly with an ideal (sinc) interpolation filter, approximated in real systems by finite impulse response filters.

Methods

Upsampling by an integer factor L inserts L − 1 zeros between samples and then applies a low-pass anti-imaging filter to remove spectral copies created by the zero insertion. Downsampling by a factor M first applies an anti-aliasing low-pass filter with cutoff below the new Nyquist frequency, then discards M − 1 of every M samples. A rational-factor conversion (such as 44.1 → 48 kHz, a ratio of 160/147) combines both steps, and efficient polyphase filter structures avoid computing samples that would be thrown away. For arbitrary, non-rational rate changes — as when correcting clock drift — techniques based on polynomial or windowed-sinc interpolation evaluate the reconstructed signal at arbitrary positions.

Applications

Resampling is ubiquitous: sample-rate conversion in audio production and playback, image scaling (the two-dimensional analogue), software-defined radio channelisation, speech and telecommunications systems, and synchronising media streams whose clocks disagree. Poor resampling produces aliasing (false low-frequency content) or imaging artefacts, so filter design quality strongly affects fidelity.
