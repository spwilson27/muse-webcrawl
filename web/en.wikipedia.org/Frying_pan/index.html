Frying pan

Flat-bottomed cookware used for frying, searing and browning

A frying pan, also called a skillet, is a flat-bottomed pan with low, flaring sides and a long handle, used for frying, searing and browning foods. Typical diameters range from 20 to 30 cm (8–12 inches). The shallow, sloped sides let steam escape so food crisps rather than stews, and make it easy to turn food with a spatula or by tossing.

Materials

Frying pans are made from cast iron (heavy, with excellent heat retention and a surface that becomes naturally non-stick when seasoned), carbon steel (favoured in professional kitchens), stainless steel (often layered with aluminium or copper cores for even heating), aluminium, and copper. Non-stick pans carry a coating, most famously polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE, sold as Teflon), which greatly reduces sticking and simplifies cleaning but wears over time and degrades at very high temperatures. Electric frying pans with built-in thermostats are also common.

History

Copper frying pans were used in ancient Mesopotamia, and pans were common in ancient Greece and Rome, where the Latin patella and sartago gave various European languages their words for the vessel. Cast-iron skillets became household staples in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries before lighter aluminium and non-stick pans spread after the 1960s.
