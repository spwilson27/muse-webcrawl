Minimally invasive procedure

Minimally invasive procedures (also known as minimally invasive surgeries) encompass surgical techniques that limit the size of incisions needed, thereby reducing wound healing time, associated pain, scarring, and risk of infection. Surgery by definition is invasive, and many operations requiring incisions of some size are referred to as open surgery. Incisions made during open surgery can sometimes leave large wounds that may be painful and take a long time to heal. Advancements in medical technologies have enabled the development and regular use of minimally invasive procedures.

For example, endovascular aneurysm repair is a minimally invasive procedure for treating an aortic aneurysm; the surgeon accesses the aneurysm through a small incision in the groin and threads instruments through blood vessels, avoiding the large abdominal incision of open repair.

Techniques

Minimally invasive procedures are commonly performed through one or more small incisions, or through natural body openings, using specialised instruments and miniature cameras. Major categories include:

  • Laparoscopy — abdominal or pelvic surgery performed through small ports with a camera (laparoscope)
  • Endoscopy — examination and treatment through natural openings such as the mouth or urethra
  • Endovascular procedures — catheter-based interventions within blood vessels
  • Robotic surgery — surgeon-controlled robotic arms that filter tremor and scale movements
  • Image-guided interventions — procedures directed by fluoroscopy, ultrasound, or CT imaging

Advantages and limitations

Compared with open surgery, minimally invasive approaches generally offer reduced postoperative pain, shorter hospital stays, quicker return to normal activities, and smaller scars. However, they can involve longer operating times, expensive specialised equipment, reduced tactile feedback, and a limited field of view. Not all patients or conditions are suitable; procedures occasionally must be converted to open surgery.

History

The roots of minimally invasive surgery lie in early endoscopy: Philipp Bozzini's Lichtleiter (1805), the cystoscopes of the late 19th century, and Hans Christian Jacobaeus's early laparoscopic work around 1910. Modern laparoscopy took off in the 1980s, notably with laparoscopic cholecystectomy, and robotic platforms such as the da Vinci system were cleared for clinical use in the early 2000s.
