Jazz Age

The Jazz Age was a period in the 1920s and early 1930s in which jazz music and dance styles gained worldwide popularity. It began in the United States after World War I and is closely associated with the Roaring Twenties, an era of economic prosperity, social change and cultural experimentation.

Characteristics

Jazz spread from New Orleans to Chicago, Kansas City and New York, carried by recordings, radio broadcasts and touring bands. The era saw the rise of stars such as Louis Armstrong, Duke Ellington and Bessie Smith, and the flourishing of venues like Harlem's Cotton Club. Social dancing to jazz rhythms, speakeasies during Prohibition, and the flapper image became emblems of the period.

Cultural impact

The term "Jazz Age" was popularized by F. Scott Fitzgerald, whose fiction — including The Great Gatsby (1925) — chronicled the era's glamour and unease. African American culture, including the Harlem Renaissance, stood at the centre of the age's artistic innovation, even as segregation persisted. The age is generally considered to have ended with the Wall Street Crash of 1929 and the onset of the Great Depression.
