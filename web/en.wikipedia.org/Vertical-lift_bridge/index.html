Vertical-lift bridge

A vertical-lift bridge or just lift bridge is a type of movable bridge in which a span, usually a through truss, rises vertically while remaining parallel with the deck. Cables pass over towers at each end of the lift span to counterweights that balance the span's weight, so only a relatively small amount of energy is needed to move it.

Advantages and disadvantages

Compared with bascule and swing bridges, a vertical-lift bridge requires no additional clearance beside the channel and can span longer distances, and the raised span does not obstruct navigation in the way a partially opened bascule may. However, the towers restrict the vertical clearance for vessels when the span is lowered, and the overhead structure can limit use by tall road vehicles.

History and examples

The first vertical-lift bridge is generally credited to J. A. L. Waddell, whose South Halsted Street Bridge in Chicago opened in 1894. Notable examples include the Hawthorne Bridge in Portland, Oregon (1910), the Arthur Kill Vertical Lift Bridge between New Jersey and Staten Island, and the Pont Jacques-Chaban-Delmas in Bordeaux (2013), among the largest lift bridges in Europe. The marine Parkway–Gil Hodges Memorial Bridge in New York and many Dutch canal bridges also use the principle. Some lift bridges carry railways, where the rigidity of the level span is advantageous.
