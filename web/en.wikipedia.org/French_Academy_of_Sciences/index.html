French Academy of Sciences

The French Academy of Sciences (French: Académie des sciences) is a learned society founded in 1666 by Louis XIV at the suggestion of Jean-Baptiste Colbert, to encourage and protect the spirit of French scientific research. It was one of the earliest academies of sciences. It is now one of the five academies of the Institut de France.

History

The Academy began as a small group of scientists meeting in Paris, including Christiaan Huygens and Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz among its foreign members in the early years. Colbert's plan gave the Academy state support and a role advising the Crown. It was reorganised under royal regulations in 1699 and received the name Royal Academy of Sciences. Suppressed during the Revolution in 1793, it was refounded in 1795 within the new Institut de France, and its structure was reformed by Napoleon in 1803.

Role and membership

The Academy advises public authorities on scientific questions, publishes research (notably the Comptes rendus, founded in 1835), awards prizes, and maintains archives of French science. Its members — about 250 French members plus foreign associates, divided into mathematical/physical and chemical/natural sections — are elected for life by existing members when seats fall vacant. Historically restricted in practice, the Academy elected its first woman full member, Marguerite Perey, in 1962 (Yvonne Choquet-Bruhat became the first woman elected to a full seat in 1979).

Notable activities

The Academy played a central role in the geodesic missions of the 1730s that settled the shape of the Earth, in the definition of the metre during the Revolution, and in the evaluation of innovations from steam engines to vaccines.
