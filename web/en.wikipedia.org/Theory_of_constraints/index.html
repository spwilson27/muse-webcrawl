Theory of constraints


A system's throughput is limited by its weakest link

The theory of constraints (TOC) is a management paradigm that views any manageable system as being limited in achieving its goals by a very small number of constraints. There is always at least one constraint, and TOC uses a focusing process to identify the constraint and restructure the rest of the organization around it.

TOC was introduced by the Israeli physicist Eliyahu M. Goldratt in his 1984 novel The Goal and developed in later works such as Critical Chain. Goldratt argued that organizations should be measured by three metrics: throughput, operational expense, and inventory.

The five focusing steps

  1. Identify the system's constraint(s).
  2. Decide how to exploit the constraint(s).
  3. Subordinate everything else to the above decision.
  4. Elevate the system's constraint(s).
  5. If in the previous steps a constraint has been broken, go back to step one, but do not allow inertia to cause a system constraint.

Applications

TOC has been applied to manufacturing (drum-buffer-rope scheduling), project management (critical chain), distribution, accounting ("throughput accounting"), and marketing. Its thinking processes — including the current reality tree and evaporating cloud — are used for analysing and resolving conflicts in organizations.
