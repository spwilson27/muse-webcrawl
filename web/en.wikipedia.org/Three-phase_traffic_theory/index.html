Three-phase traffic theory

Three-phase traffic theory is a theory of traffic flow developed by Boris Kerner between 1996 and 2002. It proposes that traffic on highways exists in three phases: free flow, synchronized flow, and wide moving jam. It contrasts with earlier "fundamental diagram" approaches, which recognized only free flow and congested traffic.

The three phases

In free flow, vehicles travel at or near the speed limit and flow increases with density. In synchronized flow, speeds are reduced and vehicles tend to synchronize across lanes, but flow can remain relatively high; this phase occurs downstream of bottlenecks and does not have a unique flow–density relationship. A wide moving jam is a localized region of near-standstill traffic that propagates upstream through bottlenecks largely unchanged.

Criticism and influence

The theory has been influential in traffic engineering and in the development of some traffic forecasting and driver-assistance approaches, though it has also been debated, with critics questioning the distinctness of synchronized flow as a separate phase.
