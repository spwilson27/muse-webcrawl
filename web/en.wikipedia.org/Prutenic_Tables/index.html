Prutenic Tables

The Prutenic Tables (1551) spread the Copernican system through practical computation.

The Prutenic Tables (Latin: Tabulae prutenicae, "Prussian Tables") are a set of astronomical tables published in 1551 by the German astronomer Erasmus Reinhold. They were the first tables computed from the heliocentric model of Nicolaus Copernicus set out in De revolutionibus (1543), and they were named in honour of Albert, Duke of Prussia, who sponsored the work.

Significance

Reinhold's tables were appreciably more accurate than the older Alfonsine Tables based on Ptolemaic astronomy, and they quickly became the standard tool of European astronomers, astrologers and calendar makers — including the commission behind the Gregorian calendar reform of 1582. By using Copernican models purely as calculating devices, many astronomers adopted the mathematics of heliocentrism while withholding judgement on its physical truth, a stance sometimes called the "Wittenberg interpretation". Tycho Brahe praised Reinhold's accuracy even while rejecting the moving Earth.

The tables were eventually superseded by Kepler's Rudolphine Tables (1627), computed from Tycho's observations and Kepler's elliptical orbits, which proved far more accurate still.
