Celtic field

Celtic field is an old name for traces of early agricultural field systems found in North-West Europe, including Britain, Ireland, Belgium, the Netherlands, Germany, Denmark, France, Sweden, Poland and the Baltic states. The fields themselves are not necessarily "Celtic" in origin — the name is a legacy of antiquarian terminology — and they range in date from the Bronze Age through the Iron Age into the Romano-British period. They are characterised by small, roughly rectangular plots, typically 20–50 m across, whose boundaries are marked by low banks of stone and earth called lynchets.

Formation

Lynchets form through repeated ploughing: soil moved downhill by the ard accumulates against a field edge, producing a positive lynchet (a bank) at the lower boundary and a negative lynchet (a scarp) at the upper boundary. On stony ground, clearance cairns — heaps of stones gathered from the plots — accumulate at corners and along boundaries. Because the plots are small, they were probably worked by hand or with a simple ard rather than a heavy plough, in a system of intensive garden-like cultivation.

Distribution and study

Extensive systems survive on the chalk downland of southern England, for example on the Marlborough Downs and around Fyfield Down in Wiltshire, where they form part of complex landscapes including barrows and settlements. In the Netherlands and Denmark (where they are known as raatakkers), large systems have been mapped from aerial photography and LiDAR. The antiquarian O. G. S. Crawford pioneered their study from the air in the 1920s. In many lowland areas the field patterns were levelled by later medieval and modern ploughing, so survival is best on marginal land never subsequently cultivated.

Interpretation

The regularity of many systems suggests planned land allotment, possibly organised by communities or estates. Crops grown included emmer and spelt wheat, barley and flax. Some fields were manured, and shifting mosaic patterns of use and fallow have been proposed. Despite the traditional name, the ethnic label "Celtic" cannot be reliably attached to field systems whose currency spans many centuries and regions.
