Matrilineality

Matrilineality
Definition: Descent and inheritance traced through the mother's line
Examples: Minangkabau, Mosuo, Khasi, Akan, Haudenosaunee
Contrast: Patrilineality

Matrilineality is a kinship system in which descent, inheritance and group membership are traced through the female line: a person belongs to their mother's lineage (matrilineage), and property, names, titles or succession often pass from mothers and maternal uncles to their daughters and sisters' children. It is one of the principal forms of unilineal descent studied in anthropology, alongside patrilineality.

Characteristics

In matrilineal societies, the most important male authority figure in a child's life is often the maternal uncle rather than the father (the "avunculate"). Residence is frequently matrilocal or avunculocal. Matrilineality does not by itself imply rule by women: political office may be held by men, but it is inherited through women, as among the Akan of Ghana, where chiefs are succeeded by their sisters' sons, or the Minangkabau of West Sumatra, the largest matrilineal society in the world, where rice land and houses pass down the female line.

Distribution and examples

Matrilineal systems are found in a belt across central Africa (the "matrilineal belt" of the Bemba, Yao and Chewa), in Southeast Asia (Minangkabau, and historically among the Nair of Kerala), in northeast India (Khasi and Garo), in southwest China (the Mosuo), and among Indigenous peoples of North America (Haudenosaunee, Hopi, Navajo in part) and the Pacific. Jewish identity by birth is also traditionally matrilineal. Anthropologists associate matrilineality statistically with horticultural economies in which women do much of the farming, though the correlation is debated.
