The Critique of Pure Reason (German: Kritik der reinen Vernunft) is a book by the German philosopher Immanuel Kant, first published in 1781 with a substantially revised second edition in 1787. It is considered one of the most influential works in the history of philosophy and inaugurates Kant's "critical philosophy".

Argument

Kant asks how synthetic a priori knowledge — knowledge that adds to our concepts yet is known independently of experience, as in mathematics and natural science — is possible. His "Copernican revolution" in philosophy proposes that objects must conform to our cognition rather than our cognition to objects: the mind actively structures experience through innate forms of intuition (space and time) and twelve categories of the understanding.

Kant distinguishes phenomena (things as they appear to us) from noumena (things in themselves, which we cannot know). Reason, when it oversteps the bounds of possible experience — seeking knowledge of God, the soul's immortality, or the world as a totality — falls into "antinomies" and paralogisms; these ideas retain a legitimate regulative and practical use but cannot be objects of theoretical knowledge.

Structure and influence

The work divides into the Transcendental Aesthetic (space and time), the Transcendental Analytic (categories and principles), the Transcendental Dialectic (the illusions of reason), and the Doctrine of Method. Together with the Critique of Practical Reason (1788) and the Critique of Judgment (1790), it frames German idealism and remains central to modern philosophy.
