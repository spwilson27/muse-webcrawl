Brahman

Brahman (Sanskrit: ब्रह्मन्, IAST: Brahman) is a key concept in Hinduism, denoting the ultimate, unchanging reality, composed of pure being, consciousness and bliss. It is described in the Upanishads as the material, efficient, formal and final cause of all that exists — the single binding unity behind the diversity of phenomena, and the innermost self (Ātman) of every being. Brahman should not be confused with Brahmā, the creator god of the Hindu trimūrti, nor with Brahmin, the priestly varṇa.

Etymology

The word derives from the Sanskrit root bṛh-, "to grow, expand, swell", giving a sense of "that which grows" or "the vast". In the earliest Vedic texts, brahman could denote prayer, sacred formulation or the power inherent in ritual utterance; by the time of the Upanishads it had come to signify the ground of all reality itself.

In the Upanishads

The Upanishads (c. 800–200 BCE) revolve around the realisation that Ātman, the self, is identical with Brahman — expressed in the great sayings (mahāvākya) such as tat tvam asi ("thou art that", Chāndogya Upanishad), ahaṁ brahmāsmi ("I am Brahman", Bṛhadāraṇyaka Upanishad) and prajñānaṁ brahma ("consciousness is Brahman", Aitareya Upanishad). The Taittirīya Upanishad defines Brahman as satyaṁ jñānam anantam — "truth, knowledge, infinity". The Māṇḍūkya Upanishad identifies Brahman with the syllable Om.

Saguna and nirguna

Hindu traditions distinguish nirguṇa Brahman, the ultimate reality without attributes, beyond description — characterised only negatively, as in the formula neti neti ("not this, not this") — and saguṇa Brahman, reality with attributes, worshipped as God (Īśvara) and as deities such as Vishnu or Shiva. Schools of Vedānta differ on the relationship between the two.

Interpretations in Vedanta

In Advaita Vedānta (Śaṅkara, 8th century CE), Brahman alone is real; the world of plurality is māyā, an appearance arising from ignorance (avidyā), and liberation (mokṣa) is the direct knowledge that the self is Brahman. In Vishishtadvaita (Rāmānuja, 11th–12th century), Brahman is qualified by sentient and non-sentient beings as its body or modes, and is identified with the personal God. In Dvaita (Madhva, 13th century), Brahman (Vishnu) is eternally distinct from individual selves and the world. Despite these differences, all Vedāntic schools treat Brahman as the topic of the Brahma Sūtras and the Upanishads.
