The Liliaceae, or lily family, is a family of flowering plants in the order Liliales. In the modern APG classification the family comprises about 15 genera and roughly 700 species of herbaceous, mostly bulbous or rhizomatous perennials, distributed mainly in temperate regions of the Northern Hemisphere. Members include the true lilies (Lilium), tulips (Tulipa), fritillaries (Fritillaria), and Erythronium.

Description

Liliaceae are monocotyledonous herbs, usually arising from bulbs or contractile roots. Their flowers are typically large and showy, with six undifferentiated tepals in two whorls, six stamens, and a superior ovary of three fused carpels; fruit is usually a capsule or berry. Leaves are alternate or whorled, simple, with parallel venation.

Taxonomy

Historically "Liliaceae" was used as a sprawling catch-all family for petaloid monocots, including onions, hyacinths, asparagus, and daylilies. Molecular phylogenetics led the Angiosperm Phylogeny Group to split the old family into many smaller families: for example, Allium moved to Amaryllidaceae, Hyacinthus and Asparagus to Asparagaceae, and Colchicum to Colchicaceae. The remaining, much reduced Liliaceae is divided into subfamilies including Lilioideae and Calochortoideae (the latter containing Calochortus, the mariposa lilies).

Uses

The family is of great ornamental importance: lilies and tulips are among the world's principal cut flowers and garden bulbs (the 17th-century Dutch "tulip mania" centred on Tulipa). Some species are edible in small quantities (fritillary bulbs in East Asian cuisine), while many members contain alkaloids and cardiac glycosides and are toxic to humans and livestock.
