Battle of Patay

The Battle of Patay (18 June 1429) was the culminating engagement of the Loire Campaign of the Hundred Years' War, following the relief of Orléans. A French army led by La Hire, Jean Poton de Xaintrailles and other captains, with Joan of Arc present in the rearguard, caught the English army of Sir John Fastolf and John Talbot before it could deploy, and destroyed it. Hundreds of years of English reliance on the longbow availed nothing: the English were ridden down before their archers could plant their stakes.

Background

After Joan of Arc raised the siege of Orléans in May 1429, the French recaptured the Loire fortresses of Jargeau, Meung-sur-Loire and Beaugency in rapid succession. The English field army, reinforced from Paris under Fastolf, retreated northward after Beaugency fell, pursued by the French vanguard.

The battle

Near Patay on 18 June, English scouts were betrayed when a stag burst from cover and the hidden longbowmen raised a hunting cry, giving away their position before the defensive line of stakes was ready. The French vanguard of mounted men-at-arms charged at once and overran the archers, while the English men-at-arms under Talbot were cut off and overwhelmed. English losses were around 2,000–2,500 killed, with Talbot and some 200 others captured; French losses were reportedly as low as one hundred. Fastolf escaped with the remnant of the army and was later scapegoated for cowardice, temporarily losing his Order of the Garter.

Aftermath

Patay reversed a generation of English victories and stands as a mirror image of Agincourt: this time it was the French heavy cavalry that caught its enemies unprepared. The victory cleared the road for Charles VII's march to Reims, where he was crowned on 17 July 1429, transforming the political momentum of the war decisively in France's favour.
