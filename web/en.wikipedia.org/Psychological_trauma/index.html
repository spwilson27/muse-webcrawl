Psychological trauma

Psychological trauma (also known as mental trauma or psychotrauma) is damage to a person's mind that occurs as a result of one or more events so distressing that the individual's ability to integrate their emotions with the experience is overwhelmed. Traumatic events include violent assault, sexual abuse, serious accidents, natural disasters, war, childhood neglect, and sudden bereavement, though trauma is defined by the person's response to the event as much as by the event itself.

Symptoms and disorders

Reactions can include intrusive memories and flashbacks, nightmares, hypervigilance, emotional numbness, avoidance of reminders, sleep disturbance, and physical symptoms such as increased heart rate. When symptoms persist and impair functioning, they may be diagnosed as post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) or, where trauma is prolonged and interpersonal, complex PTSD, recognized in the ICD-11. Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are associated in epidemiological studies with elevated lifetime risks of mental and physical illness.

Treatment

Evidence-based treatments include trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy, prolonged exposure therapy, and eye movement desensitization and reprocessing (EMDR). Medications such as selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors are used for some symptoms. Broader approaches known as trauma-informed care emphasize safety, trust, and avoiding re-traumatization in health care, education, and social services. Most people exposed to traumatic events recover without long-term disorder, a phenomenon studied under the heading of psychological resilience.

History

Modern understanding descends from nineteenth-century work on "railway spine" and hysteria, Pierre Janet's studies of dissociation, Freud's early trauma theory, and the recognition of "shell shock" in the First World War. PTSD entered the DSM-III in 1980, shaped by research on Vietnam veterans and survivors of sexual violence.
