Westminster Abbey, formally titled the Collegiate Church of Saint Peter at Westminster, is an abbey church in the City of Westminster, London, England. Since 1066, it has been the location of the coronations of 40 English and British monarchs and a burial site for 18 English, Scottish and British monarchs. At least 16 royal weddings have occurred at the abbey since 1100.

History

According to tradition, a church was founded on the site in the 7th century. A community of Benedictine monks was established there around 960 by Saint Dunstan. Between 1042 and 1052, Edward the Confessor began rebuilding St Peter's Abbey in the Romanesque style; it was consecrated on 28 December 1065, only a week before Edward's death. On Christmas Day 1066, William the Conqueror was crowned there — the first of an unbroken line of coronations.

The present Gothic church was begun by Henry III in 1245, inspired by the great French cathedrals. Henry VII added the magnificent Perpendicular lady chapel at the east end in the early 16th century. The abbey was dissolved under Henry VIII in 1540, briefly restored under Mary I, and made a collegiate church by Elizabeth I in 1560. The western towers, designed by Nicholas Hawksmoor, were completed in 1745. The building suffered bomb damage during the Blitz of 1941.

Coronations

Every English and British monarch since 1066 has been crowned in the abbey, with the exceptions of Edward V and Edward VIII, who were never crowned. The ceremony centres on the Coronation Chair, made in about 1300 to enclose the Stone of Scone, and St Edward's Crown. The most recent coronation was that of Charles III on 6 May 2023.

Burials and memorials

More than 3,300 people are buried or commemorated in the abbey. The south transept, known as Poets' Corner, holds the graves or memorials of Chaucer, Dickens, Tennyson, Handel and many others. Scientists including Isaac Newton, Charles Darwin and Stephen Hawking lie in the nave. The Grave of the Unknown Warrior, buried in 1920, is the only tomb on which it is forbidden to walk.

Today

The abbey is a "royal peculiar" — a church responsible directly to the sovereign rather than to a bishop. It is governed by the Dean and Chapter of Westminster and, together with the Palace of Westminster, forms a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Daily services are open to the public, and the abbey receives over a million visitors a year.
