Homo habilis ("handy man") is a species of archaic human from the Early Pleistocene of East and South Africa, living about 2.3 to 1.65 million years ago. It was first described in 1964 by Louis Leakey, Phillip Tobias, and John Napier, based on fossils found at Olduvai Gorge in Tanzania.

The species was named for its association with stone tools of the Oldowan industry. Whether H. habilis belongs in the genus Homo, and whether it is ancestral to later humans, remains debated.

Discovery and fossils

The first recognized remains, OH 7, were found by Jonathan Leakey at Olduvai Gorge in 1960 and included a jaw, hand bones, and skull fragments. Notable specimens include the cranium KNM-ER 1813 from Koobi Fora, Kenya, and OH 62, a partial skeleton that revealed surprisingly long arms for a member of Homo.

Anatomy

Homo habilis had a cranial capacity of roughly 510 to 660 cubic centimeters, somewhat larger than in australopithecines but much smaller than in later Homo erectus. Body size estimates suggest a height of about 1.0 to 1.3 meters. The teeth were smaller than those of earlier hominins, but the limb proportions retained ape-like features suited to climbing.

Classification debate

Some researchers argue that H. habilis and the similar Homo rudolfensis should be reassigned to Australopithecus, because their body plans differ little from that genus, while others retain them in Homo as a variable early species ancestral to Homo erectus.
