The Indian Ocean is the third-largest of the world's oceanic divisions, covering about 70.6 million square kilometres — roughly a fifth of the total ocean area and of the Earth's water surface. It is bounded by Asia to the north, Africa to the west, Australia to the east and the Southern Ocean (or Antarctica) to the south. Unlike the Atlantic and Pacific, it is largely landlocked in the Northern Hemisphere, and its northern currents reverse seasonally with the monsoon winds.

Geography and oceanography

Principal marginal seas include the Arabian Sea, the Bay of Bengal, the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf. The ocean's great rivers — the Ganges–Brahmaputra, Indus, Zambezi and Irrawaddy — deliver enormous quantities of fresh water and sediment; the Bengal Fan, built from Himalayan erosion, is the largest submarine fan on Earth. Surface circulation is dominated in the south by a subtropical gyre, while north of the equator the Somali Current and the gyres of the Arabian Sea and Bay of Bengal reverse direction between the southwest and northeast monsoons. The ocean is the warmest of the major oceans, and its warming has been linked to changes in monsoon rainfall and to the Indian Ocean Dipole, an oscillation of sea-surface temperatures affecting weather from East Africa to Australia.

History and trade

The Indian Ocean sustained the world's oldest long-distance maritime trade network. Austronesian sailors reached Madagascar; Arab, Persian, Indian and Chinese merchants rode the predictable monsoon winds between East Africa, Arabia, India and Southeast Asia, trading spices, textiles, gold, ivory and slaves; the 1st-century Greek guide Periplus of the Erythraean Sea documents the routes. Vasco da Gama's voyage of 1498 opened a direct sea route from Europe, ushering in Portuguese, Dutch, French and British rivalry, and the ocean became the highway of empire. In the 20th century it was a theatre of both world wars, and it remains the crossroads of global energy shipping: a large share of the world's oil passes through the straits of Hormuz and Malacca.

Natural events and ecology

On 26 December 2004, a megathrust earthquake off Sumatra generated the Indian Ocean tsunami, which killed roughly 230,000 people in fourteen countries — one of the deadliest natural disasters in recorded history — and spurred the creation of an Indian Ocean tsunami warning system. The ocean's ecosystems range from the coral atolls of the Maldives and Chagos to the unique fauna of Madagascar's coasts; its fisheries, especially tuna, are of major economic importance.
