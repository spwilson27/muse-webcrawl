Hospital readmission

Hospital readmission is an episode when a patient who had been discharged from a hospital is admitted again within a specified time interval. Readmissions are widely used as a measure of hospital quality and of the cost-effectiveness of care. A common measure is the 30-day readmission rate. Studies in the United States have found that roughly one in five Medicare patients discharged from hospital is readmitted within 30 days, with common causes including heart failure, pneumonia, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and complications following surgery.

Policy in the United States

The Patient Protection and Affordable Care Act of 2010 established the Hospital Readmissions Reduction Program (HRRP), under which the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services reduce payments to hospitals with higher-than-expected readmission rates for specified conditions, beginning with acute myocardial infarction, heart failure and pneumonia, and later expanded to other conditions and procedures such as COPD and joint replacement. Penalties can reach 3 per cent of a hospital's Medicare payments.

Criticism and interventions

Critics argue that readmission rates partly reflect socioeconomic circumstances outside a hospital's control—poverty, housing, access to primary care—and that penalising safety-net hospitals may worsen disparities; risk-adjustment methods have been modified to account for some of these factors. Some studies controversially suggested that the HRRP period was associated with increased mortality for heart failure patients, prompting ongoing debate. Interventions shown to reduce readmissions include better discharge planning, medication reconciliation, patient education, follow-up appointments scheduled before discharge, transitional care nurses, and post-discharge telephone or home visits.
