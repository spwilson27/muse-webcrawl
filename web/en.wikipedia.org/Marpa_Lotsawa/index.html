Marpa Lotsawa

Marpa Lotsawa (1012–1097), also rendered Marpa Chökyi Lodrö, was a Tibetan Buddhist teacher credited with introducing the Kagyu school of Tibetan Buddhism to Tibet. "Lotsawa" means "translator": Marpa made three journeys to India, studied with the mahasiddha Naropa and other masters, and translated key tantric texts — especially those of the Hevajra and Cakrasaṁvara cycles and the Six Dharmas of Naropa — from Sanskrit into Tibetan. He is famous as the stern guru of Tibet's beloved yogin-poet, Milarepa.

Life

Born Marpa Chökyi Lodrö to a wealthy farming family in Lhodrak in southern Tibet, he was a wild youth whose parents sent him to study at Mangkhar with the translator Drogmi Yeshe, from whom he learned Sanskrit. Selling his inheritance for gold, Marpa travelled to India, where he studied for years under Naropa, former abbot of Nalanda, receiving the transmissions of the Cakrasaṁvara and Hevajra tantras, the practice of tummo (inner heat), dream yoga and the other Six Dharmas. He also studied with Maitripa and the yogini Niguma's tradition through Khyungpo Naljor's milieu. On his third journey Marpa sought out the aged Naropa once more to receive a final transmission.

Back in Tibet, Marpa settled at Drowolung in Lhodrak, married Dagmema, and raised a family while farming — unusual for a Tibetan religious figure of his stature — and teaching a circle of disciples, foremost among them Ngok Chöku Dorje, Tsurton Wang-nge, Metön Tsönpo and Milarepa, who inherited his principal lineages.

Relationship with Milarepa

Marpa's treatment of Milarepa is one of the most celebrated teacher–disciple stories in Buddhism. Milarepa, seeking teachings to expiate the black magic murders he had committed against his aunt's family, was put through years of gruelling trials: Marpa ordered him repeatedly to build stone towers and tear them down, refused him teachings, and appeared harsh and capricious — all, in the traditional account, deliberately to purify Milarepa's karma and test his devotion. Eventually satisfied, Marpa gave him initiation and transmission, and predicted he would attain enlightenment in one lifetime. The biography The Life of Milarepa, composed by Tsangnyön Heruka in the 15th century, remains the classic source for both men's legends and is one of the most widely read works of Tibetan literature.

Legacy

Through Milarepa's disciple Gampopa, Marpa's lineages grew into the Dagpo Kagyu and the "Four Major and Eight Minor" Kagyu schools that shaped Tibetan Buddhism. Marpa is revered as the fountainhead of the Kagyu in Tibet, second in lineage only to the Indian masters Tilopa and Naropa who precede him in the traditional Kagyu "golden rosary" of gurus.
