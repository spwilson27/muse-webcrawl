Electron-beam lithography


A modern electron-beam lithography (direct-write) system

Electron-beam lithography (often abbreviated as e-beam lithography or EBL) is the practice of scanning a focused beam of electrons to draw custom shapes on a surface covered with an electron-sensitive film called a resist (exposing). The electron beam changes the solubility of the resist, enabling selective removal of either the exposed or non-exposed regions of the resist by immersing it in a solvent (developing).

Principles

The primary advantage of electron-beam lithography is that it can draw custom patterns (direct writing) with sub-10 nm resolution, far finer than the diffraction limit of optical lithography. Because no photomask is required, designs can be changed simply by altering the scan pattern data. The key limitation is throughput: the beam writes serially, point by point, so patterning a full wafer is slow and expensive.

Systems

E-beam systems are classified by beam shape — Gaussian (round) beams that raster or vector-scan across the field, and shaped-beam systems that project simple rectangles or characters. They are also classified by the writing strategy: direct-write systems pattern the wafer itself, while mask-writing systems produce the photomasks used by optical steppers. Common resists include PMMA (a positive resist) and HSQ (a negative resist).

Resolution limits

Resolution is limited less by the focused spot size than by electron scattering within the resist and substrate. Forward scattering broadens the beam, while backscattered electrons expose resist far from the target — the proximity effect — which must be corrected in software by adjusting local dose.

Applications

  • Production of photomasks for optical lithography
  • Prototyping of integrated circuits and nanodevices
  • Quantum devices, photonic crystals and research nanostructures
  • Low-volume manufacture of specialised components such as MMICs
