Disability sport

Wheelchair racing at the Paralympic Games.

Disability sport, also called adaptive sports or parasports, are sports played by persons with a disability, including physical and intellectual disabilities. Some are adapted from existing able-bodied sports with modifications to equipment and rules, while others — such as goalball and boccia — were created specifically for athletes with disabilities.

History and organization

Organized sport for deaf athletes began with the first "Silent Games" in Paris in 1924, now the Deaflympics. Systematic physical-activity programmes for injured servicemen after the Second World War, led by Sir Ludwig Guttmann at Stoke Mandeville Hospital in England, led to the Stoke Mandeville Games (1948) and the first Paralympic Games in Rome in 1960. The International Paralympic Committee (IPC), founded in 1989, now governs the Paralympic movement. Special Olympics, founded by Eunice Kennedy Shriver in 1968, provides sport for people with intellectual disabilities worldwide, while organizations such as Virtus govern elite competition for that group.

Classification

Because disabilities vary widely in their effect on performance, athletes are grouped by classification — an assessment of how an impairment affects the specific tasks of a sport — so that competition is decided by skill, fitness, and tactics rather than by the severity of impairment. Sports such as para-athletics, para-swimming, wheelchair basketball, and para-cycling each maintain their own classification systems, which are periodically reviewed for evidence-based fairness.

Major events and sports

The Paralympic Games (summer and winter) are held every four years in the same cities as the Olympic Games. Sports on the Paralympic programme include para-athletics, para-swimming, wheelchair basketball, sitting volleyball, goalball, boccia, para-cycling, wheelchair rugby, para powerlifting, para alpine skiing, and para ice hockey. Beyond elite competition, disability sport includes school, rehabilitative, and recreational programmes, and high-profile athletes such as Oscar Pistorius and Markus Rehm have raised debates about competing against able-bodied athletes with prosthetic technology.
