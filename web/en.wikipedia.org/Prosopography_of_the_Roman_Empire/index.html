Prosopographia Imperii Romani

The Prosopographia Imperii Romani (often abbreviated PIR) is a massive collective biography of the leading figures of the Roman Empire, documenting the senatorial and equestrian aristocracy and other notable persons from the reign of Augustus through to about AD 641. It collects and organizes the literary, epigraphic, papyrological, and numismatic evidence for each individual, mapping family relationships and careers, and remains a standard reference work for Roman social and political history.

Editions

The first edition (PIR1) was published in Berlin in three volumes between 1897 and 1898, edited by Elimar Klebs, Hermann Dessau, and Paul von Rohden under the auspices of the Prussian Academy of Sciences. A completely revised second edition (PIR2) was begun in 1933 under Edmund Groag and Arthur Stein; publication continued for decades under editors including Leiva Petersen and Klaus Wachtel, with the alphabetical sequence reaching its final fascicles in the twenty-first century. The work is written in Latin, following the conventions of the great German Altertumswissenschaft corpora.

Significance

The PIR exemplifies the prosopographical method — the study of history through the collective biographies of an elite — that shaped twentieth-century Roman historiography, notably in Ronald Syme's The Roman Revolution (1939). Entries assign each person a standard reference number (for example, PIR2 A 1405), which scholars cite across the literature. A related project, the Prosopography of the Later Roman Empire (PLRE), continues the record from AD 260 to 641 in English.
