Levellers

The Levellers were a political movement active during the English Civil Wars who were committed to popular sovereignty, extended franchise, equality before the law and religious tolerance. The movement coalesced around 1645–46 among radicals in London and within the New Model Army, led by figures including John Lilburne, Richard Overton, William Walwyn and Thomas Rainsborough. Their programme was set out in a series of manifestoes entitled the Agreement of the People. The name "Leveller" was originally a pejorative term applied by their opponents, suggesting that they sought to "level" all social ranks and property; the Levellers denied wanting to abolish property distinctions, and the name stuck.

Origins and programme

The Levellers grew out of London's radical pamphlet culture and John Lilburne's campaigns against arbitrary imprisonment. Their mature programme, debated at Putney in 1647, demanded a written constitutional settlement: regular parliaments elected by a much wider franchise (manhood suffrage for most adult males, though servants and alms-takers were generally excluded), equality before the law, freedom of conscience, abolition of conscription, tithes and excise, and law reform. At the Putney Debates, Rainsborough famously argued: "the poorest he that is in England hath a life to live, as the greatest he."

Conflict with the grandees

Oliver Cromwell and the army grandees resisted the Leveller programme as a threat to property and order. Leveller-inspired mutinies at Ware (1647) and at Burford and Bishopsgate (1649) were suppressed by Cromwell and Fairfax; mutineers were shot and Lilburne imprisoned in the Tower of London. After 1649 the movement was crushed, though Lilburne's trials and pamphlets continued to influence English radicalism.

Legacy

The Levellers are regarded as pioneers of modern democratic and liberal ideas — government by consent, universal (male) suffrage, and rights reserved against Parliament itself. The Diggers, a contemporary group led by Gerrard Winstanley, went further toward communal ownership and were dubbed "True Levellers". Leveller ideas resurfaced in the Chartists and in later democratic movements, and the Putney Debates remain a classic text of democratic theory.
