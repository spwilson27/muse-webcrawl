Empathy

Empathy is the capacity to understand or feel what another person is experiencing from within their frame of reference — that is, the capacity to place oneself in another's position. Definitions of empathy encompass a broad range of social, cognitive and emotional processes, and the term is used in psychology, philosophy, neuroscience and everyday language with somewhat different emphases.

Definitions

Researchers commonly distinguish cognitive empathy (perspective taking — understanding another's mental state), affective or emotional empathy (sharing another's feelings, sometimes called emotional contagion), and compassionate empathy (being moved to help). Empathy differs from sympathy, which involves feeling for someone rather than with them, and from pity.

The English word derives from the German Einfühlung ("feeling into"), coined in aesthetics by Robert Vischer in 1873 and translated by the psychologist Edward Titchener in 1909.

Development and measurement

Precursors of empathy appear early: newborns cry in response to other infants' cries, and toddlers begin to comfort others in distress. Perspective-taking abilities develop through childhood alongside theory of mind. Empathy is commonly measured by self-report questionnaires such as the Interpersonal Reactivity Index and by behavioural and neuroscientific paradigms.

Neuroscience

Brain regions associated with empathy include the anterior insula and anterior cingulate cortex for shared affect, and the medial prefrontal cortex and temporoparietal junction for perspective taking. Mirror neurons — cells that fire both when an animal acts and when it observes the same action — were proposed as a basis for empathy, though their role in humans remains debated.

Clinical aspects

Reduced empathy is associated with conditions such as autism spectrum disorder (particularly cognitive empathy), narcissistic and antisocial personality disorders, and psychopathy. Excessive empathic arousal can produce personal distress and burnout in caregivers and clinicians, sometimes termed compassion fatigue. Empathy is considered a core skill in psychotherapy since Carl Rogers, who emphasised accurate empathic understanding as one of the conditions for therapeutic change.
