The Williamsburg Bridge is a suspension bridge in New York City across the East River, connecting the Lower East Side of Manhattan at Delancey Street with the Williamsburg neighbourhood of Brooklyn. Completed in 1903, it was the longest suspension bridge span in the world until the Bear Mountain Bridge opened in 1924.

History

Construction began in 1896 under chief engineer Leffert L. Buck. Originally called the East River Bridge, it was renamed for the Brooklyn neighbourhood it served. The bridge opened to traffic on 19 December 1903, at a cost of about $24.2 million. Unlike the earlier Brooklyn Bridge, its towers and stiffening trusses are of steel rather than masonry.

Design and use

The bridge carries eight lanes of traffic, two tracks of the New York City Subway (J, M and Z trains), and walkways for pedestrians and cyclists. Its main span of 1,600 feet (488 m) is suspended from four main cables. The bridge was the second of three suspension crossings built between Manhattan and Brooklyn, alongside the Brooklyn and Manhattan bridges.

Renovation

By the 1980s the bridge had deteriorated severely; cables were corroded and the structure was briefly closed in 1988. A major rehabilitation programme replaced the suspender cables, deck and much steelwork through the 1990s, and the bridge remains a vital artery carrying more than 100,000 vehicles a day.
