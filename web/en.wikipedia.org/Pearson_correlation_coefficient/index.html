Pearson correlation coefficient


Scatter plots illustrating values of r from -1 to +1.

The Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC), denoted r, is a measure of the linear correlation between two sets of data. Developed by Karl Pearson from an idea of Francis Galton, it is the ratio of the covariance of two variables to the product of their standard deviations, and so is a normalised measurement ranging from -1 (perfect negative linear relationship) through 0 (no linear relationship) to +1 (perfect positive linear relationship).

Interpretation

Values are often described as weak, moderate, or strong, though the appropriate reading depends on context. The coefficient measures only linear association: variables can be strongly related non-linearly while r is near zero, and correlation does not imply causation. It is sensitive to outliers, and its use as a test statistic assumes, in its standard form, roughly normally distributed data.

History and variants

Galton developed the underlying concept of regression and correlation in the 1880s; Pearson gave it mathematical form in the 1890s while working with the biometric school. Related measures include Spearman's rank correlation coefficient, which uses ranks rather than raw values, and Kendall's tau.
