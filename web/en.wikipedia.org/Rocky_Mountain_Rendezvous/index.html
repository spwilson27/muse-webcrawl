Rocky Mountain Rendezvous

Years active1825–1840
FrequencyAnnual (summer)
RegionRocky Mountains — present-day Wyoming, Utah, Idaho, Montana, Colorado
FounderWilliam Henry Ashley (Rocky Mountain Fur Company)
ParticipantsMountain men, fur company agents, Native American trading partners

The Rocky Mountain Rendezvous was an annual gathering held from 1825 to 1840 at various locations in the Rocky Mountains of North America, at which mountain men and fur trappers sold their season's furs and hides to company agents in exchange for supplies, equipment, and trade goods. Created by General William Henry Ashley of the Rocky Mountain Fur Company, the rendezvous system revolutionised the western fur trade by eliminating the need for permanent trading posts and fixed forts.

Origins

Before 1825, trappers typically had to carry their furs all the way to St. Louis or trade at fixed posts, losing months of trapping time. After the Arikara War of 1823 disrupted the Missouri River route, Ashley devised a new system: company supply caravans would travel overland each summer to a prearranged mountain valley, where independent trappers and company men alike could trade. The first rendezvous was held in 1825 on Henry's Fork of the Green River in present-day Wyoming/Utah, drawing about 120 trappers.

The gatherings

Rendezvous sites rotated among river bottoms in the Green River valley, Cache Valley, Pierre's Hole, the Wind River country, and other locations in present-day Wyoming, Utah, Idaho, and Montana. A caravan of wagons or pack animals brought trade goods — guns, powder, traps, cloth, tobacco, coffee, and notoriously, whiskey — marked up steeply. Trappers received credit or payment in kind for beaver pelts, the era's most valuable commodity because of demand for beaver felt hats in Europe and America.

The gatherings lasted days or weeks and were as much social festivals as markets: there was drinking, gambling, horse racing, storytelling, and marriage negotiations, and Native American nations — Shoshone, Crow, Ute, Nez Perce, and others — camped nearby in large numbers to trade horses, robes, and provisions. Famous mountain men associated with the rendezvous include Jedediah Smith, Jim Bridger, Kit Carson, Thomas Fitzpatrick, and William Sublette.

Decline

By the late 1830s the system was failing. Beaver were heavily trapped out, and fashion shifted from beaver-felt to silk hats, collapsing prices. The rendezvous of 1840 was the last; the fur companies consolidated, and the trade moved back to permanent posts such as Fort Laramie. Knowledge of the trails, passes, and geography accumulated by the rendezvous-era trappers soon guided emigrant wagon trains along the Oregon and California Trails.

Legacy

The Rocky Mountain Rendezvous is celebrated as the defining institution of the mountain-man era. Modern buckskinning and black-powder enthusiasts hold rendezvous re-enactments across the American West, and the Museum of the Mountain Man in Pinedale, Wyoming, commemorates the gatherings held in the Green River valley nearby.
