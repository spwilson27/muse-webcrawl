Asymptotic freedom

Asymptotic freedom is a property of some gauge theories in which interactions between particles become asymptotically weaker as the energy scale increases and the corresponding length scale decreases. In quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the theory of the strong interaction, asymptotic freedom means that quarks interact almost freely at very short distances, while the force grows so strong at large distances that quarks are permanently confined inside hadrons.

Discovery

Asymptotic freedom was discovered in 1973 by David Gross and Frank Wilczek, and independently by David Politzer. It explained the SLAC deep inelastic scattering results of the late 1960s, in which quarks inside protons behaved as nearly free pointlike particles (partons) when probed at high energy. Gross, Wilczek and Politzer shared the 2004 Nobel Prize in Physics for the discovery.

Mechanism

In non-Abelian gauge theories such as QCD, the beta function is negative provided the number of quark flavours is not too large (fewer than 16½ for three colours). Self-interactions of the gluon field produce "antiscreening" that outweighs ordinary screening, so the effective coupling αs decreases logarithmically with increasing momentum transfer.

Consequences

At high energies, perturbation theory becomes reliable, enabling precise predictions for jet production at colliders. At low energies the coupling grows until perturbation theory fails — the regime of confinement and hadronisation, studied with lattice QCD. The phenomenon does not occur in quantum electrodynamics, whose coupling grows slowly with energy (screening).

Screening versus antiscreening

In QED, virtual electron–positron pairs screen a charge, weakening the observed coupling at distance. In QCD, virtual gluons — which themselves carry colour charge — spread the colour field out, antiscreening it; this effect dominates over quark screening and drives asymptotic freedom.
