In Greek mythology, Clytemnestra was the wife of Agamemnon, king of Mycenae, and the half-sister of Helen of Troy. She is famous for her role in the murder of Agamemnon on his return from the Trojan War, and for her own subsequent murder at the hands of her son Orestes — the central events of Aeschylus's trilogy the Oresteia.

Mythology

Clytemnestra was the daughter of Tyndareus, king of Sparta, and Leda. According to some versions her first husband was Tantalus, king of Pisa, whom Agamemnon killed, along with their infant child, before marrying her himself. With Agamemnon she had four children: Iphigenia, Electra, Chrysothemis and Orestes.

The murder of Agamemnon

Before sailing for Troy, Agamemnon sacrificed Iphigenia at Aulis to obtain favourable winds — the act that, in Aeschylus's telling, fixed Clytemnestra's resolve for revenge. During Agamemnon's ten-year absence she took as a lover Aegisthus, a surviving son of Thyestes with his own grievance against Agamemnon's house. When Agamemnon returned in triumph, Clytemnestra welcomed him with feigned joy, then murdered him — in the Agamemnon she traps him in a robe or net in his bath and strikes him down, also killing his captive concubine Cassandra.

Death and aftermath

Clytemnestra and Aegisthus ruled Mycenae for seven years until Orestes, grown to manhood in exile, returned with his sister Electra and killed them both, acting on Apollo's command. Orestes was then pursued by the Erinyes (Furies) until Athena's court on the Areopagus acquitted him, breaking the cycle of blood vengeance that had cursed the House of Atreus.

In literature

Clytemnestra is one of the great figures of Greek tragedy, treated by Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides, each giving a different weight to her motives — maternal grief, ambition, and adultery. In Homer's Odyssey she already serves as the dark counterpart to the faithful Penelope. She has remained a subject for modern literature, opera and feminist reinterpretation.
