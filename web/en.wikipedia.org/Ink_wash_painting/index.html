Ink wash painting


A landscape rendered in graded ink washes.

Ink wash painting is a technique of painting with black ink — usually the same carbon ink used in East Asian calligraphy — in varying concentrations to produce a range of tonal values, from deep black to pale grey. Known in Chinese as shuimo (水墨, "water and ink") and in Japanese as suiboku-ga or sumi-e, it developed in China during the Tang dynasty and became a dominant art form in China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam. Because it shares tools and training with calligraphy, the brushstroke itself — its speed, pressure and moisture — is treated as an expression of the artist's character.

Technique

The artist grinds an ink stick against an inkstone with water to the desired density, then applies it with a soft animal-hair brush to absorbent paper or silk. Tonal gradation is achieved by diluting the ink, by loading the brush with several densities at once, or by layering washes. Unlike an ink drawing on a legal document or a mere preliminary sketch, a finished ink wash painting relies on irreversible strokes: the absorbent ground permits no correction, so execution demands long practice and decisiveness.

History and schools

The splashed-ink manner is associated with the eccentric Tang painter Wang Mo; monochrome landscape painting matured under the Five Dynasties and Northern Song masters such as Li Cheng and Fan Kuan, and was taken up by Chan (Zen) Buddhist monk-painters. In Japan, Sesshū Tōyō became the pre-eminent master of suiboku-ga. Later Chinese literati painters prized expressive, abbreviated brushwork over descriptive accuracy, and the style's emphasis on tonal wash also influenced the monochrome painting traditions of Korea (sumuk).
