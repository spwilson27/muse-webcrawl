Liquidity trap

A liquidity trap is a situation, described in Keynesian economics, in which, "after the rate of interest has fallen to a certain level, liquidity preference may become virtually absolute in the sense that almost everyone prefers holding cash rather than holding a debt (financial asset) which yields so low a rate of interest." In such a situation, increases in the money supply by the central bank fail to lower interest rates further and fail to stimulate the economy.

Mechanism

When nominal interest rates approach zero — the zero lower bound — money and short-term bonds become near-perfect substitutes, so open market operations merely swap one for the other without changing spending. Monetary policy is said to be "pushing on a string". Keynes developed the idea in analysing the Great Depression; later economists associated it with Japan's "Lost Decade" of the 1990s and with advanced economies after the 2008 financial crisis.

Policy responses

Proposed escapes from a liquidity trap include fiscal policy (government spending and tax cuts), quantitative easing, negative interest rates, raising inflation expectations, and forward guidance about future policy. Economists debate both the trap's empirical relevance and the effectiveness of these remedies.
