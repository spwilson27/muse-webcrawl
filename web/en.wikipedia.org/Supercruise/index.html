Supercruise

Supercruise is sustained supersonic flight of a supersonic aircraft with a useful cargo, passenger, or weapons load, performed efficiently and without the use of afterburners (reheat). Most military aircraft require afterburners to exceed the speed of sound, which consumes fuel rapidly; a supercruising aircraft can cruise supersonically for extended periods, increasing range and intercept speed.

History and aircraft

The term is associated above all with the Lockheed Martin F-22 Raptor, defined by the U.S. Air Force as the ability to cruise above Mach 1.5 without afterburner; the F-22's Pratt & Whitney F119 engines were designed for this capability. The Eurofighter Typhoon can supercruise around Mach 1.2–1.5 in a clean configuration, and the Dassault Rafale and Saab Gripen E also claim supercruise capability.

The first aircraft able to supercruise was the English Electric Lightning in certain configurations, and the Concorde airliner cruised at Mach 2 without afterburners — using reheat only for takeoff and transonic acceleration — making it the only passenger aircraft to supercruise routinely. Early models of the F-14, F-15, and F-16 could exceed Mach 1 in dry thrust in clean configurations but not sustain operationally useful supercruise.

Supercruise confers tactical advantages: faster intercepts, greater missile launch energy, and reduced infrared signature compared with afterburner use.
