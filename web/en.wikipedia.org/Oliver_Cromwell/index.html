Oliver Cromwell (25 April 1599 – 3 September 1658) was an English statesman, politician and soldier who led the Parliamentarian cause to victory in the English Civil War and from 1653 ruled as Lord Protector of the Commonwealth of England, Scotland and Ireland. He is one of the most controversial figures in British and Irish history — hailed as a champion of liberty and condemned as a regicidal dictator and perpetrator of atrocities in Ireland.

Rise

Cromwell, a Huntingdonshire gentleman farmer who underwent an intense Puritan religious conversion, sat in the Long Parliament from 1640. In the Civil War his disciplined cavalry — the "Ironsides" — proved decisive at Marston Moor (1644) and, with the New Model Army he helped create, at Naseby (1645). A leading Independent, he supported the trial and execution of Charles I in 1649 and signed the king's death warrant.

Ireland and Scotland

Cromwell's campaigns in Ireland (1649–50) crushed Royalist and Confederate resistance but involved massacres at Drogheda and Wexford that remain infamous in Irish memory. He then defeated the Scots at Dunbar (1650) and Worcester (1651).

The Protectorate

Frustrated with the Rump Parliament, Cromwell dissolved it in 1653 and, after the failure of Barebone's Parliament, accepted the Instrument of Government, becoming Lord Protector. His government promoted religious toleration for most Protestants, readmitted Jews to England, pursued an assertive foreign policy against the Dutch and Spain, and briefly ruled through regional major-generals. He declined the crown in 1657. He died in 1658 and was succeeded by his son Richard; the Protectorate collapsed and the monarchy was restored in 1660, after which Cromwell's body was exhumed and posthumously executed.
