Quadratic formula

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The quadratic formula is the closed-form solution of the quadratic equation ax2 + bx + c = 0, where a ≠ 0. It expresses the roots of any quadratic polynomial in terms of its coefficients:

x = (−b ± √(b2 − 4ac)) / 2a

The discriminant

The expression under the square root, b2 − 4ac, is called the discriminant. If it is positive, the equation has two distinct real roots; if zero, one repeated real root; if negative, two complex conjugate roots and no real solution. Geometrically, the roots are the x-intercepts of the parabola y = ax2 + bx + c.

Derivation

The formula is obtained by completing the square: dividing by a, moving the constant term to the right-hand side, and adding (b/2a)2 to both sides so the left becomes a perfect square, (x + b/2a)2. Taking square roots and isolating x yields the formula.

History

Babylonian scribes (c. 2000 BCE) solved quadratic problems by procedures equivalent to completing the square, expressed geometrically. Brahmagupta (628 CE) gave an explicit rule for solving quadratics, and al-Khwarizmi (9th century) systematised the method of al-jabr. The Indian mathematician Sridhara (c. 870–930) is often credited with stating the formula in a general form recognisably close to the modern one; the use of ± and the treatment of negative and complex roots were settled in European algebra by the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries.

Equivalent forms

For numerical computation, an algebraically equivalent "citardauq" form, x = 2c / (−b ∓ √(b2 − 4ac)), avoids catastrophic cancellation when one root is much smaller than the other. Vieta's formulas relate the two roots to the coefficients: their sum is −b/a and their product c/a.
