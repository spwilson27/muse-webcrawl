Ecosystem engineer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Beaver dams transform streams into ponds and wetlands used by many other species.

An ecosystem engineer is any species that creates, significantly modifies, maintains or destroys a habitat. These organisms can have a large impact on the species richness and landscape-level heterogeneity of an area. As a result, ecosystem engineers are important for maintaining the health and stability of the environment they inhabit. The concept was formalised by Clive Jones, John Lawton and Moshe Shachak in 1994.

Types

Autogenic engineers modify the environment through their own physical structures — their living and dead tissues — as when trees create forest habitat and coral polyps build reefs. Allogenic engineers transform living or non-living materials from one physical state to another, as beavers do by felling trees and damming streams, or woodpeckers by excavating tree holes later used by other animals.

Examples

Classic examples include beavers, whose dams create wetlands; elephants, which topple trees and maintain savanna grasslands; prairie dogs, whose burrow systems shelter many other species; earthworms, which aerate and mix soil; and reef-building corals and oysters, which construct three-dimensional habitat. Humans are sometimes described as the ultimate ecosystem engineers.

Importance

Because engineers create habitat for other species, their loss can trigger cascades of extinction, and their reintroduction — as in rewilding projects — is used as a conservation tool.
