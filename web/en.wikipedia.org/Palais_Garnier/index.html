Palais Garnier

Palais Garnier
Location: Place Jacques-Rouché, 9th arrondissement, Paris, France
Architect: Charles Garnier
Style: Napoleon III / Beaux-Arts / Second Empire
Construction: 1861–1875
Opened: January 5, 1875
Capacity: 1,979 seats

The Palais Garnier (French: [palɛ ɡaʁnje], Garnier Palace), also known as the Opéra Garnier, is a historic 1,979-seat opera house at the Place Jacques-Rouché in the 9th arrondissement of Paris, France. It was built for the Paris Opera from 1861 to 1875 at the behest of Emperor Napoleon III. It was the primary theatre of the Paris Opera and its associated Paris Opera Ballet until 1989, when a new opera house, the Opéra Bastille, opened at the Place de la Bastille.

The Palais Garnier has been called "probably the most famous opera house in the world, a symbol of Paris like Notre Dame Cathedral, the Louvre, or the Sacré Coeur Basilica." Its opulent Napoleon III style — a synthesis of Baroque exuberance, classical symmetry and Second Empire eclecticism — features the Grand Staircase, the horseshoe-shaped Italian-style auditorium with red and gold décor, and a ceiling painted by Marc Chagall in 1964. The building famously inspired Gaston Leroux's 1910 novel The Phantom of the Opera; its underground lake, created by the high water table encountered during construction, still exists beneath the stage.

History

Architect Charles Garnier won the design competition in 1861. Construction was interrupted by the Franco-Prussian War and the Paris Commune; the building was finally inaugurated on 5 January 1875 with a gala attended by Marshal MacMahon, President of the Republic. In 1969 the theatre was given a new name, the Palais Garnier, and since the opening of the Opéra Bastille it has been used primarily for ballet by the Paris Opera Ballet.
