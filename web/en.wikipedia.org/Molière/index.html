Molière

Molière (1622–1673), France's greatest comic playwright

Jean-Baptiste Poquelin (baptised 15 January 1622 – 17 February 1673), known by his stage name Molière, was a French playwright, actor, and poet, widely regarded as one of the greatest writers in the French language and world literature. His comedies — including Tartuffe, The Misanthrope, The School for Wives, The Bourgeois Gentleman, and The Imaginary Invalid — satirised the hypocrisies of 17th-century French society and remain fixtures of the stage; French is still called "la langue de Molière".

Life and career

Born in Paris to a prosperous upholsterer to the king, Poquelin renounced his inheritance to co-found the Illustre Théâtre in 1643, taking the name Molière. After bankruptcy and years touring the provinces, his company returned to Paris in 1658 and won the patronage of Louis XIV, performing at the Palais-Royal. His satires repeatedly scandalised the devout: Tartuffe, a portrait of religious hypocrisy, was banned from public performance for five years (1664–69) despite the king's private support, and Don Juan was withdrawn after fifteen performances.

Death and legacy

Molière collapsed on stage while playing the hypochondriac Argan in The Imaginary Invalid and died at home hours later, on 17 February 1673. Because actors were denied Christian burial, he was interred at night by royal dispensation. His company merged in 1680 into the Comédie-Française, still known as the "House of Molière". His plays combined farce, commedia dell'arte technique, and sharp social observation, creating enduring stock figures — the miser, the hypocrite, the would-be gentleman — and a comic prose and verse of lasting influence.
