The Huastec or Huaxtec people (in their own language, Teenek) are an indigenous people of Mexico living in the region of La Huasteca along the Gulf Coast, in the modern states of Veracruz, San Luis Potosí, Hidalgo, Tamaulipas, Querétaro, and Puebla. They speak the Huastec language, a Mayan language that separated from the rest of the Maya family at an early date.

History

The Huastec homeland, the Huasteca, is geographically separated from the main Maya region of Yucatán and the Guatemalan highlands. Linguists estimate that the ancestors of the Huastec migrated northward along the Gulf Coast roughly three thousand years ago, making Huastec the earliest-diverging branch of the Mayan languages. Pre-Columbian Huastec civilization built ceremonial centres with circular pyramids, such as Tamtoca and Tamuín, and produced distinctive stone sculpture, including standing figures and depictions of the deity Tlazolteotl-Ixcuina.

Conquest and modern era

The Aztec Empire campaigned repeatedly against the Huasteca in the fifteenth century, and the region was brutally conquered by Spanish forces under Nuño de Guzmán in the 1520s–30s, with many Huastecs sold into slavery in the Caribbean. Huastec communities survived and today number in the hundreds of thousands; many continue to speak Teenek and practise traditional milpa agriculture, embroidery, and music, including the huapango tradition shared across the region.

Culture

Huastec culture is noted for its pottery, shell pectorals, and finely carved stone sculptures. The ancient Huastec were renowned — and sometimes scandalized in Aztec accounts — for their customs, and their region was associated with the goddess of love and purification. Modern Teenek communities maintain strong traditions of oral literature in their language.
