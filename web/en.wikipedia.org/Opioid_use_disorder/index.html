Opioid use disorder

Opioid use disorder
Symptoms: Strong desire to use opioids, tolerance, withdrawal, continued use despite harm
Treatment: Medication-assisted treatment (methadone, buprenorphine, naltrexone), counselling
Complications: Overdose, infectious disease, neonatal withdrawal

Opioid use disorder (OUD) is a substance use disorder involving the compulsive use of opioids despite significant problems related to the use. Signs of the disorder include a strong desire to use opioids, increased tolerance, difficulty fulfilling obligations, withdrawal symptoms when use stops, and continued use despite knowledge of harm. Opioids that may be involved include heroin, morphine, oxycodone, hydrocodone, and fentanyl.

Diagnosis

The DSM-5 defines OUD by a pattern of opioid use leading to clinically significant impairment, meeting at least two of eleven criteria within a year — including craving, unsuccessful efforts to cut down, and use in larger amounts or for longer than intended. Severity is graded mild, moderate, or severe by the number of criteria met.

Treatment

The most effective treatment is medication-assisted treatment (MAT) combining behavioural therapy with one of three approved medications: methadone, a full agonist dispensed through licensed programs; buprenorphine, a partial agonist often combined with naloxone; and naltrexone, an antagonist available as a monthly injection. Such treatment reduces overdose mortality substantially compared with abstinence-only approaches. Harm-reduction measures — naloxone distribution, needle and syringe programmes, and supervised consumption services — reduce deaths and disease transmission among people who continue to use.
