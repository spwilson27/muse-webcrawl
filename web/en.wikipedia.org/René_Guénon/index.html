René Guénon

Born: 15 November 1886, Blois, France
Died: 7 January 1951, Cairo, Egypt
Occupation: Philosopher, writer
Known for: Founding the Traditionalist School

René Guénon (15 November 1886 – 7 January 1951), also known as ʿAbd al-Wāḥid Yaḥyá after his conversion to Islam, was a French philosopher and writer. He is regarded as the founder of the Traditionalist School, a movement in perennial philosophy concerned with what its adherents regard as the loss of traditional knowledge in the modern world.

René Guénon in his later years in Cairo.

Life

Guénon was born in Blois, France, and studied philosophy and mathematics in Paris. In his youth he moved in occultist circles, becoming involved with the Martinist Order and other initiatic organizations, before breaking with them. In 1912 he was initiated into Sufism and took the name ʿAbd al-Wāḥid Yaḥyá. He married an Egyptian woman and moved to Cairo in 1930, where he lived for the rest of his life as a practising Muslim.

Work

Guénon's early books criticized modern esotericism, including Theosophy: History of a Pseudo-Religion (1921) and The Spiritist Fallacy (1923). His mature work developed a metaphysics drawn from Advaita Vedanta and other Eastern traditions: Man and His Becoming According to the Vedanta (1925), Introduction to the Study of the Hindu Doctrines (1921), and The Symbolism of the Cross (1931). His most famous book, The Crisis of the Modern World (1927), diagnosed modernity as the terminal phase of the Kali Yuga, the "reign of quantity" developed further in The Reign of Quantity and the Signs of the Times (1945).

Guénon influenced the Traditionalist thinkers who followed him, including Ananda Coomaraswamy, Frithjof Schuon, Titus Burckhardt, and Julius Evola, though Evola departed from Guénon in important respects.
