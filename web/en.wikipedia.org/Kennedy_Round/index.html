The Kennedy Round was the sixth session of multilateral trade negotiations under the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade (GATT), held in Geneva from 1964 to 1967. Named after United States President John F. Kennedy, the round achieved broad tariff reductions and the first GATT agreements on non-tariff barriers.

Background

The United States Trade Expansion Act of 1962 gave the President authority to negotiate tariff cuts of up to 50 percent, a response in part to the formation of the European Economic Community and its common external tariff. Although Kennedy was assassinated in 1963, the negotiations he had authorised proceeded under Presidents Johnson, and the round was named in his memory.

Negotiations

Earlier GATT rounds had bargained item by item; the Kennedy Round attempted across-the-board linear cuts in industrial tariffs, an approach favoured by the United States but resisted in part by the EEC, which negotiated exceptions for sensitive products. Agriculture proved the most difficult sector, producing only limited results, including arrangements on grains that fed into the later International Grains Agreement.

Results

The final agreements, concluded on 30 June 1967, cut tariffs on roughly $40 billion of trade, reducing duties on industrial goods by an average of about 35 percent, phased in over five years. The round also produced the GATT Anti-Dumping Code, the first multilateral discipline on a non-tariff measure, and special provisions acknowledging the interests of developing countries following the addition of Part IV to the GATT in 1965.

Significance

The Kennedy Round marked the high point of postwar tariff liberalisation by formula rather than product-by-product bargaining, and it set the pattern for the later Tokyo Round and Uruguay Round negotiations that eventually created the World Trade Organization.
