Mathematical economics

Mathematical economics is the application of mathematical methods to represent theories and analyze problems in economics. Often, these applied methods are beyond simple geometry, and may include differential and integral calculus, difference and differential equations, matrix algebra, mathematical programming, or other computational methods. Proponents of this approach claim that it allows the formulation of theoretical relationships with rigor, generality, and simplicity.

History

The use of mathematics in economics grew during the nineteenth century. Antoine Augustin Cournot used calculus in his 1838 work on duopoly, and Léon Walras developed general equilibrium theory in the 1870s. The marginal revolution, associated with William Stanley Jevons, Carl Menger, and Walras, placed marginal analysis at the center of economic theory. In the twentieth century, John von Neumann and Oskar Morgenstern founded game theory, and Paul Samuelson's Foundations of Economic Analysis (1947) systematized the mathematical treatment of consumer and producer theory.

Applications

Mathematical methods are used throughout modern economics: in general equilibrium theory, econometrics, mechanism design, growth theory, and finance. The Arrow–Debreu model formalized the existence of competitive equilibrium, while optimization theory underpins much of microeconomic analysis of choice under constraints.

Criticism

Critics, including some within the Austrian school and proponents of complexity economics, argue that excessive formalization can obscure institutional and historical realities, and that the assumptions required for tractable models may be unrealistic.
