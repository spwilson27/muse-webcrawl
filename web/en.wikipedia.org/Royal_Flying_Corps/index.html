Royal Flying Corps

The Royal Flying Corps (RFC) was the air arm of the British Army before and during the First World War. Formed on 13 April 1912 under the command of Frederick Sykes, it comprised a Military Wing, a Naval Wing, and the Central Flying School at Upavon. The Naval Wing soon separated to become the Royal Naval Air Service. On 1 April 1918 the RFC and RNAS were amalgamated to form the Royal Air Force, the world's first independent air force.

First World War service

The RFC went to France in August 1914 with four squadrons of reconnaissance aircraft. Its initial role was observation and artillery spotting, and its reports during the retreat from Mons and the Battle of the Marne proved the value of military aviation. As the war developed, the RFC expanded enormously into air fighting, photographic reconnaissance, bombing, and ground attack. Periods of German air superiority, such as the "Fokker Scourge" of 1915–16 and "Bloody April" 1917 — when the RFC lost 245 aircraft during the Battle of Arras — inflicted terrible casualties; the life expectancy of a replacement pilot on the Western Front could be measured in weeks.

Aircraft and personnel

Famous RFC types included the Royal Aircraft Factory B.E.2, the F.E.2, the Bristol Scout, the Sopwith Pup and Camel, and the S.E.5a. The corps grew from about 1,800 personnel in 1914 to over 114,000 by 1918, drawing many volunteers from across the British Empire, particularly Canada. Its leading aces included Albert Ball, William "Billy" Bishop, James McCudden, and Edward "Mick" Mannock.

Leadership and legacy

For most of the war the RFC in France was commanded by Hugh Trenchard, who championed an offensive doctrine of relentless patrols over enemy lines and later became the first Chief of the Air Staff of the RAF. The RFC's motto was Per ardua ad astra — "Through adversity to the stars" — inherited by the RAF.
