Synagogue

A synagogue, also called a shul or temple, is a Jewish house of worship, prayer, study and communal assembly. The word derives from the Greek synagōgē, "assembly", a translation of the Hebrew beit knesset, "house of assembly".

Origins

Synagogues emerged during or after the Babylonian exile, when Jews far from the Jerusalem Temple needed local centres for prayer and scripture reading. By the Second Temple period they existed throughout Judea and the diaspora; the oldest known remains include the synagogue at Masada and the Theodotos inscription from Jerusalem.

Design and furnishings

The focal point is the ark (aron ha-kodesh), a cabinet holding the Torah scrolls, placed on the wall facing Jerusalem and surmounted by the eternal light. Readings are chanted from a raised platform, the bimah. Traditional synagogues separate men's and women's seating with a mechitza; liberal congregations sit together. Stained glass, carved ornament and Hebrew inscriptions decorate many historic buildings, from the Old New Synagogue in Prague (1270) to Moorish-Revival and modernist designs.

Services

Prayer services are held daily and on Shabbat and festivals, led by a rabbi or cantor (hazzan), with communal prayer requiring a quorum (minyan) of ten adults. Synagogues also host schools, lifecycle events and social welfare activities.
