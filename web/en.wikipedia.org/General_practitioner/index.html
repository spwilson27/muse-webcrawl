A general practitioner (GP) is a doctor who is a consultant in general practice, also known as family medicine in some countries. GPs provide primary care: they are usually the first point of contact for patients within a health system, treat a wide range of acute and chronic illnesses, provide preventive care and health education, and refer patients to hospital specialists when needed.

Role

Unlike specialists, who focus on a particular organ system or disease, general practitioners care for patients of all ages and both sexes, and for undifferentiated problems — illnesses that present early, before a diagnosis is clear. Continuity of care, in which the same doctor sees a patient over many years, is regarded as a defining feature of the discipline and is associated with better health outcomes.

Training

Training varies by country. In the United Kingdom, GPs complete medical school, two years of foundation training, and then a three-year specialty training programme in general practice. In the United States and Canada, the equivalent role is filled by family physicians, who complete a residency in family medicine after medical school. In Australia, general practice is a specialty with fellowship awarded by the Royal Australian College of General Practitioners.

Practice models

GPs may work in partnerships, group practices, community clinics, or solo practice. In systems such as the British National Health Service, patients register with a practice, and GPs act as gatekeepers to specialist services, a model intended to coordinate care and control costs.
