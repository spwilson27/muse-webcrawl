Hierarchy of angels

Tradition: Christian angelology
Source text: De Coelesti Hierarchia (attr. Pseudo-Dionysius)
Structure: Three triads, nine choirs

A hierarchy of angels is a system of ranking angels in Christian theology, most fully developed by the 5th–6th-century author known as Pseudo-Dionysius the Areopagite in his treatise On the Celestial Hierarchy. He arranged the angels into three triads (spheres) of three choirs each, ordered by closeness to God.

The nine choirs

First triad (contemplating God directly): Seraphim, the burning ones of Isaiah's vision, with six wings; Cherubim, guardians of Eden and bearers of God's throne; and Thrones, associated with divine justice.

Second triad (governors of creation): Dominions, who regulate the duties of lower angels; Virtues, who oversee the movements of the heavenly bodies and bestow grace; and Powers, warrior angels who resist evil spirits.

Third triad (messengers to humankind): Principalities, who watch over nations and earthly rulers; Archangels, such as Michael, Gabriel, and Raphael, who carry the most important decrees; and Angels proper, the lowest rank, including guardian angels assigned to individuals.

Development and influence

The scheme draws on scattered biblical references (Colossians 1:16 and Ephesians 1:21 list thrones, dominions, principalities, and powers) and was elaborated by Gregory the Great, who gave a slightly different order, and by Thomas Aquinas in the Summa Theologiae. It pervades medieval and Renaissance art and literature — Dante's Paradiso follows Dionysius — and parallels exist in Jewish angelology (Maimonides lists ten ranks) and Islamic tradition.
