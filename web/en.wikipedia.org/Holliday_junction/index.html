Holliday junction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A Holliday junction: four DNA strands exchange partners in a cruciform structure.

A Holliday junction is a branched nucleic acid structure containing four double-stranded arms joined together. These arms may adopt one of several conformations depending on buffer salt concentrations and the sequence of nucleobases nearest the junction. The structure is named after Robin Holliday, the molecular biologist who proposed its existence in 1964 to explain genetic recombination in fungi, particularly in Ustilago maydis.

Biological role

Holliday junctions are a key intermediate in homologous recombination, the process by which genetic information is exchanged between similar DNA molecules during meiosis and in the repair of double-strand breaks. After strand invasion and DNA synthesis, the intertwined molecules form a junction that can migrate along the DNA (branch migration) and is finally resolved — cut and re-ligated by resolvase enzymes — either with or without crossing over of the flanking DNA.

In bacteria, the RuvABC complex and the RecG helicase process junctions; in eukaryotes, resolvases such as GEN1 and the BLM–topoisomerase complex (which dissolves double Holliday junctions) carry out equivalent roles. Unresolved or improperly resolved junctions can cause chromosome mis-segregation, so their processing is tightly regulated.

Structure and nanotechnology

In the presence of divalent cations such as Mg²⁺, the junction folds into a stacked X-structure with two quasi-continuous helices; in low salt it adopts an open, square-planar form. Because their branching is controllable, immobile Holliday junctions have become fundamental building blocks of DNA nanotechnology, used since Nadrian Seeman's pioneering work to construct lattices, polyhedra and nanomechanical devices from DNA.
