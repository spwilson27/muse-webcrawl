Mosel is one of 13 German wine regions (Weinbaugebiete) for quality wines (QbA and Prädikatswein), and takes its name from the Moselle River (German: Mosel). Before 1 August 2007 the region was called Mosel-Saar-Ruwer, after the river and its two tributaries, the Saar and the Ruwer. The region is Germany's third largest in terms of wine production but is regarded internationally as its most prestigious, especially for Riesling wines grown on steep slate slopes.

Geography and viticulture

The vineyards line the Moselle from the French and Luxembourg borders upriver of Trier downstream to Koblenz, where the river joins the Rhine. Some slopes, such as the Bremmer Calmont, are among the steepest vineyards in the world, with gradients of up to 65 degrees, worked entirely by hand. The blue and red Devonian slate soils store daytime heat, helping the late-ripening Riesling to mature in this marginal northern climate.

Wines

Riesling accounts for well over half of plantings, followed by Müller-Thurgau and Elbling. Mosel Rieslings are famous for their lightness of alcohol, racy acidity, mineral character, and longevity, ranging from dry (trocken) styles to intensely sweet Trockenbeerenauslese. Celebrated villages include Bernkastel-Kues, Wehlen, Graach, Piesport, Erden, and Trittenheim, with famous vineyards such as Wehlener Sonnenuhr and Bernkasteler Doctor.
