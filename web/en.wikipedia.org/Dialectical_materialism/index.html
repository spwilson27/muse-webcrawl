Dialectical materialism

Dialectical materialism grew out of the writings of Marx and Engels.

Dialectical materialism is a philosophical approach to reality derived from the writings of Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels. It interprets the world as material — matter in motion — and holds that change occurs through the struggle of internal contradictions, developing through quantitative accumulation into qualitative leaps. It formed the official philosophy of the Soviet Union and of Marxist–Leninist parties.

Origins

Marx and Engels never used the phrase "dialectical materialism" themselves; the term was coined by the Russian theorist Georgi Plekhanov in 1891. Its roots lie in Engels's Anti-Dühring (1878) and his unfinished Dialectics of Nature, and in Lenin's Materialism and Empirio-criticism (1909). The doctrine combines the dialectical method of Hegel — the development of reality through contradiction — with philosophical materialism, which takes matter as primary and mind as derivative.

The laws of dialectics

Following Engels, the doctrine is usually summarised in three laws: (1) the unity and struggle of opposites, (2) the transformation of quantitative changes into qualitative changes, and (3) the negation of the negation. These were said to govern nature, society, and thought alike.

Relation to historical materialism

Dialectical materialism supplies the general worldview; its application to society is historical materialism, Marx's theory that the economic base of production shapes law, politics, and ideology. The codification of the doctrine in the USSR — notably in Stalin's 1938 essay Dialectical and Historical Materialism — is often called "Diamat".

Criticism

Critics, including many Western Marxists, argue that the codified doctrine is a crude, deterministic dogma quite different from Marx's own method, and that the supposed laws of dialectics in nature are unfalsifiable. Defenders respond that it expresses a process-oriented, anti-reductionist ontology with lasting influence on the philosophy of science in the socialist world.
