Raiatea

Raiatea or Ra'iatea (Tahitian: Ra'iātea) is the second largest of the Society Islands in French Polynesia, after Tahiti. The island is widely regarded as the ancestral homeland of Polynesian voyaging: in legend it is identified with Hawaiki, the starting point of the great migrations that populated the Polynesian Triangle, including Hawaii, Easter Island, and New Zealand.

Aerial view of Raiatea (foreground), sharing a lagoon with the island of Taha'a.

Geography

Raiatea is an extinct volcanic island surrounded by a coral reef and lagoon, which it shares with the smaller island of Taha'a to the north. Its highest point is Mount Tefatua (To'omaru), rising 1,017 metres above sea level. The island's principal town is Uturoa, the administrative capital of the Leeward Islands. The Faaroa River, on the east coast, is the only navigable river in French Polynesia.

History

The great marae of Taputapuātea, on the southeast coast, was the religious and political center of eastern Polynesia, from which voyaging canoes were dispatched and where an alliance of chiefs, the Ao-tea, was maintained. In 2017 the Taputapuātea complex was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Captain James Cook visited the island in 1769 during the transit of Venus expedition. Raiatea came under French administration in the 19th century; King Tamatoa VI abdicated in 1888, and the island was annexed by France.

Economy

Agriculture (notably vanilla, copra, and the rum-producing sugarcane of the Faaroa valley), pearl farming, fishing, and yacht charter tourism support the island's roughly 12,000 inhabitants. Raiatea is a major base for charter sailing in French Polynesia.
