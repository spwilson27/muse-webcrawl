Gel electrophoresis

Gel electrophoresis is a method for separation and analysis of biomacromolecules (DNA, RNA, proteins) and their fragments, based on their size and charge. It is used in clinical chemistry to separate proteins by charge or size, and in biochemistry and molecular biology to separate a mixed population of DNA and RNA fragments by length, to estimate the size of fragments, or to separate proteins by charge. Nucleic acid molecules are separated by applying an electric field to move the negatively charged molecules through a matrix of agarose or other substances.

Principle

Charged molecules migrate through a gel when an electric current is passed across it. Shorter molecules move faster and therefore travel farther than longer ones because they navigate the pores of the gel more easily, an effect called sieving. Proteins are commonly treated with the detergent SDS (sodium dodecyl sulfate), which coats them with a uniform negative charge so that migration depends almost purely on molecular mass, as in SDS-PAGE.

Types of gel

Agarose gels, cast from a seaweed polysaccharide, have large pores and are used for DNA fragments from about 100 base pairs to tens of kilobases. Polyacrylamide gels have smaller, tunable pores and higher resolution, suiting proteins and short nucleic acids. Variants include pulsed-field gel electrophoresis for very large DNA, two-dimensional gels that separate proteins first by isoelectric point and then by mass, and capillary electrophoresis in automated sequencers.

Procedure

Samples are loaded into wells at one end of a gel submerged in buffer (commonly TAE or TBE for nucleic acids). Applying a voltage drives the negatively charged DNA toward the positive electrode; a molecular-weight ladder in one lane provides size standards. After the run, molecules are visualized with stains such as ethidium bromide or safer fluorescent dyes under ultraviolet light, or with Coomassie blue and silver stain for proteins.

Applications

Gel electrophoresis underpins DNA fingerprinting, forensic analysis, paternity testing, verification of PCR products, preparation for Southern and Northern blotting, and quality control in sequencing. Before mass spectrometry became dominant, two-dimensional gels were the workhorse of proteomics.

History

Electrophoresis in free solution was developed by Arne Tiselius in the 1930s (Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 1948). Starch and later polyacrylamide and agarose gels made the technique a routine bench method; SDS-PAGE was popularized by Laemmli in 1970 and remains among the most cited methods in science.
