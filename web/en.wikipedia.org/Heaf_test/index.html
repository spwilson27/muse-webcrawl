Heaf test

The Heaf test used a spring-loaded gun with six needles to test for tuberculosis immunity.

The Heaf test is a diagnostic skin test formerly used in the United Kingdom to determine whether children needed vaccination against tuberculosis with the BCG vaccine. Named after its inventor, Professor Frederick Roland George Heaf (1894–1973), it was a multiple-puncture form of the tuberculin test, standardised in the 1950s and used in the British schools BCG programme from 1953 until 2005.

Procedure

A Heaf gun — a spring-actuated device with six short needles arranged in a circle — was used to press tuberculin purified protein derivative (PPD) through the skin of the forearm in a single stroke, producing six punctures. The site was examined about seven days later and graded by the reaction: grades 0–1 (no or minimal reaction) were considered negative, indicating the child should receive BCG; grades 2–4 indicated increasing degrees of sensitivity, taken as evidence of prior infection or immunisation, in which case BCG was withheld and stronger reactions prompted referral for chest X-ray and assessment for active tuberculosis.

Replacement

The test required trained staff and consistent technique, and interpretation was partly subjective. In 2005 the UK schools BCG programme was replaced by a targeted programme for high-risk groups, and tuberculin testing moved to the more standardised Mantoux test, with interferon-gamma release assays increasingly used for latent tuberculosis screening. The Heaf gun's needles also raised modern infection-control concerns because of issues with sterilisation between uses.
