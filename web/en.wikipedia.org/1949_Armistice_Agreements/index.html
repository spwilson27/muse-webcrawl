The 1949 Armistice Agreements were a set of armistice agreements signed during 1949 between Israel and neighbouring Egypt, Lebanon, Jordan and Syria, following the end of the 1948 Arab–Israeli War. The agreements ended the official hostilities of the war and established armistice lines between Israeli forces and the forces of its neighbours, lines that later became known as the Green Line.

The negotiations were mediated by the acting United Nations mediator Ralph Bunche and were held on the Greek island of Rhodes beginning in January 1949. Bunche received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1950 in recognition of his work.

Agreements

The agreements were signed separately with each neighbouring state:

  • Egypt — signed 24 February 1949. The Gaza Strip remained under Egyptian control, and a demilitarised zone was established around Auja al-Hafir.
  • Lebanon — signed 23 March 1949. The armistice line largely followed the pre-war international boundary.
  • Jordan (then Transjordan) — signed 3 April 1949. Jordanian forces retained control of the territory that became known as the West Bank, including East Jerusalem and the Old City.
  • Syria — signed 20 July 1949, after protracted negotiations, with demilitarised zones established in disputed areas near the Sea of Galilee.

Iraq, which had also sent forces, signed no agreement and withdrew its troops, handing its sector over to Jordan.

Provisions and character

Each agreement stated explicitly that the armistice demarcation lines were not to be construed as political or territorial boundaries, and that they were delineated without prejudice to the parties' rights and claims in an ultimate peaceful settlement. The agreements were intended as purely military arrangements pending a final peace settlement that, in most cases, did not follow for decades.

Aftermath

The armistice lines remained the de facto boundaries of Israel until the Six-Day War of 1967. The Mixed Armistice Commissions, supervised by the United Nations Truce Supervision Organization (UNTSO), were charged with supervising the agreements, but disputes over the demilitarised zones and infiltrations across the lines were frequent throughout the 1950s.
