Chiang Kai-shek (31 October 1887 – 5 April 1975) was a Chinese politician, revolutionary and general who led the Republic of China from 1928 until his death. As head of the Kuomintang (KMT), he unified much of China in the Northern Expedition, fought the Second Sino-Japanese War, and after losing the Chinese Civil War withdrew his government to Taiwan in 1949.

Rise to power

Trained in Japan and briefly in the Soviet Union, Chiang became commandant of the Whampoa Military Academy and, after Sun Yat-sen's death in 1925, the party's military leader. The Northern Expedition (1926–28) defeated the warlords and established the Nationalist government at Nanjing. Chiang broke violently with the Communists in the Shanghai massacre of 1927, beginning a civil war that would last, with interruptions, for two decades.

War and civil war

After the Xi'an Incident of 1936 forced a united front, Chiang led China's resistance to Japan (1937–45), allied with the United States and Britain. Civil war resumed in 1946; despite American support, corruption, inflation and Communist organisation doomed the Nationalists, and in December 1949 Chiang evacuated to Taiwan with some two million refugees.

Taiwan

In Taiwan Chiang ruled under martial law through the White Terror, while presiding over land reform and rapid economic growth. He maintained that his government was the legitimate government of all China until his death in 1975, when he was succeeded by his son Chiang Ching-kuo. Historians debate his record as both nation-builder and authoritarian.
