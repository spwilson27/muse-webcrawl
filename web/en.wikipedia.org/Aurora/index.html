Aurora

The aurora borealis over high northern latitudes, produced by charged particles colliding with the upper atmosphere.

An aurora (pl. aurorae or auroras), also commonly known as the northern lights (aurora borealis) or southern lights (aurora australis), is a natural light display in Earth's sky, predominantly seen in high-latitude regions around the Arctic and Antarctic. Auroras display dynamic patterns of brilliant lights that appear as curtains, rays, spirals, or dynamic flickers covering the entire sky.

Auroras are the result of disturbances in the Earth's magnetosphere caused by solar wind. Major disturbances result from enhancements in the speed of the solar wind from coronal holes and coronal mass ejections. These disturbances alter the trajectories of charged particles in the magnetospheric plasma; precipitating particles, mainly electrons and protons, excite atmospheric atoms and molecules, which emit light as they return to their ground states.

Colours and forms

The most common auroral colour, a pale yellowish-green, is produced by oxygen molecules at about 100 km altitude; rare all-red auroras come from high-altitude oxygen at up to 200 km. Nitrogen produces blue or purplish-red light. Auroral forms include quiet arcs, rippling curtains with ray structure, coronas overhead, and diffuse pulsating patches. The emissions involve electronic transitions across energy levels analogous to the band gaps that govern light emission in semiconductors.

Occurrence

Auroras are centred on the magnetic poles in annular "auroral ovals" that expand during geomagnetic storms. Strong storms — such as the Carrington Event of 1859 or the May 2024 storms — can push auroras to mid and even low latitudes. Auroras also occur on other planets with magnetic fields and atmospheres, notably Jupiter and Saturn, where they have been imaged by space telescopes and orbiters.
