Stoichiometry quantifies reactants and products in chemical reactions.

Stoichiometry is the study of the quantitative relationships between the amounts of reactants and products in a chemical reaction. The word derives from the Ancient Greek stoicheion "element" and metron "measure"; it was introduced by Jeremias Benjamin Richter in the 1790s, in his work on acid–base titrations.

Principles

Stoichiometric calculations rest on the law of conservation of mass and on the fixed composition of chemical compounds. A balanced chemical equation gives the mole ratios between substances: for example, in CH₄ + 2 O₂ → CO₂ + 2 H₂O, one mole of methane reacts with two moles of oxygen to produce one mole of carbon dioxide and two moles of water. Using molar masses, chemists convert between masses, moles, gas volumes and solution amounts.

Applications

Stoichiometry determines the limiting reagent that caps a reaction's yield, the theoretical and percentage yields of products, empirical and molecular formulas from combustion analysis, and concentrations in titrations. Air–fuel ratios in engines are also described as stoichiometric when exactly enough oxygen is present for complete combustion.

Gas stoichiometry

For gases at the same temperature and pressure, volume ratios equal mole ratios (Avogadro's law), simplifying calculations in industrial processes such as ammonia synthesis.
