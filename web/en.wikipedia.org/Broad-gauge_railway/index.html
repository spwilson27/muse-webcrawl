Broad-gauge railway

A broad-gauge railway is a railway with a track gauge (the distance between the rails) broader than the 1,435 mm (4 ft 8½ in) used by standard-gauge railways. Broad gauge of 1,524 mm (5 ft), commonly known as Russian gauge, is used in the former Soviet Union and Finland; 1,668 mm (Iberian gauge) is used in Spain and Portugal; and 1,676 mm (5 ft 6 in), known as Indian gauge, is the dominant gauge in India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Argentina and Chile.

History

In Britain, the Great Western Railway used Brunel's broad gauge of 7 ft ¼ in (2,140 mm), which offered smoother running at speed, but the Gauge Act 1846 mandated standard gauge for most new construction, and the GWR completed its conversion in 1892. Broad gauges generally allow larger, more stable rolling stock and higher capacities, but suffer from incompatibility at breaks of gauge, requiring transshipment or bogie exchange.

Advantages and disadvantages

Broad gauge can offer greater stability, roomier vehicles and potentially higher speeds and axle loads. However, construction costs are higher — wider formation, larger structures and sharper land take — and through-running with standard-gauge networks is impossible without additional measures.
