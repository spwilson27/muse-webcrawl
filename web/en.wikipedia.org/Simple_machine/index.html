Simple machine

A simple machine is a mechanical device that changes the direction or magnitude of a force, using only one movement and no external source of power beyond the applied force. In general, simple machines can be understood as the simplest mechanisms that exploit mechanical advantage (leverage) to multiply force.

The classical six

Renaissance scientists defined six classical simple machines, ultimately derived from the works of Archimedes and Hero of Alexandria:

  • Lever — multiplies force about a fulcrum;
  • Wheel and axle — a wheel rotating with an axle, trading distance for force;
  • Pulley — changes the direction of a rope's pull and, in block-and-tackle arrangements, multiplies force;
  • Inclined plane — spreads the work of lifting over a longer distance;
  • Wedge — a portable double inclined plane used to split or cut;
  • Screw — an inclined plane wrapped around a cylinder, converting rotation to linear force.

Mechanical advantage

The ratio of output force to input force is the mechanical advantage. An ideal simple machine conserves energy: the gain in force is paid for by a proportional loss in the distance or speed of movement, so the work done on the machine equals the work done by it. Real machines lose some energy to friction, captured by the machine's efficiency.

Compound machines

Combinations of simple machines form compound machines — from scissors (levers and wedges) to the bicycle and complex industrial machinery. The classification remains a standard starting point in school physics for analysing how machines work.
