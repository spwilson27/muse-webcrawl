Radical feminism

Radical feminism is a perspective within feminism that calls for a radical reordering of society in which male supremacy is eliminated in all social and economic contexts, while recognizing that women's experiences are also affected by other social divisions such as race, class, and sexual orientation. The ideology and movement emerged in the 1960s.

A women's liberation march during the second wave of feminism.

Radical feminists view society fundamentally as a patriarchy in which men dominate and oppress women. They seek to abolish the patriarchy in order to "liberate everyone from an unjust society by challenging existing social norms and institutions". This includes opposing the sexual objectification of women, raising public awareness about such issues as rape and violence against women, challenging the concept of gender roles, and challenging what radical feminists see as a racialized and gendered capitalism that characterizes the United States and many other countries.

History

Radical feminism emerged amid the second wave of the feminist movement in the late 1960s, particularly in the United States, Canada, and the United Kingdom. Its proponents were women involved in the New Left and the civil rights movement who grew disillusioned with the sexism they encountered in those movements. Groups such as New York Radical Women, founded in 1967, and the Redstockings, founded in 1969, became central to the early movement.

The movement popularized the slogan "the personal is political" and developed the practice of consciousness-raising, in which women shared experiences of oppression in small groups. Radical feminists organized protests such as the 1968 Miss America protest, which drew national attention to standards of beauty and the treatment of women.

Theory

Radical feminist theory holds that the oppression of women is the most fundamental form of oppression, underlying all others such as racism and class oppression. Key theorists include Shulamith Firestone, whose 1970 book The Dialectic of Sex argued that women's subordination was rooted in biology and reproduction, and Kate Millett, whose Sexual Politics (1970) analyzed patriarchy as a political institution.

Radical feminists have been influential in debates over pornography, prostitution, and reproductive rights. Figures such as Andrea Dworkin and Catharine MacKinnon argued that pornography constitutes a form of violence against women and sought legal remedies against it.

Criticism

Radical feminism has been criticized by other feminists for essentialism, for neglecting the intersections of gender with race and class, and for positions on transgender issues that some branches hold. Intersectional feminists such as bell hooks argued that early radical feminism centered the experiences of white, middle-class women.
