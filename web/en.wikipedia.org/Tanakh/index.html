Tanakh

The Tanakh (Hebrew: תָּנָ״ךְ, romanised Tānāḵ), also known in Hebrew as Miqra (/miːˈkrɑː/; מִקְרָא, 'that which is read'), is the canonical collection of Hebrew scriptures, comprising the Torah (the five books of Moses), the Nevi'im (Prophets), and the Ketuvim (Writings). The name "Tanakh" is an acronym formed from the first Hebrew letter of each division: Torah, Nevi'im, Khetuvim. It is the foundational text of Judaism and, in a somewhat different ordering and scope, corresponds largely to the Christian Old Testament.

Structure

The Torah comprises Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and Deuteronomy. The Nevi'im are divided into the Former Prophets (Joshua, Judges, Samuel, Kings) and the Latter Prophets (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and the Twelve Minor Prophets counted as one book). The Ketuvim include Psalms, Proverbs, Job, the Five Scrolls (Song of Songs, Ruth, Lamentations, Ecclesiastes, Esther), Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah, and Chronicles. Counted this way — with Samuel, Kings, Chronicles, Ezra–Nehemiah, and the Twelve each as single books — the Tanakh contains 24 books, though the same material is counted as 39 books in most Christian Old Testaments.

Text and transmission

The authoritative Hebrew text was standardised by the Masoretes of Tiberias between the 6th and 10th centuries CE, who added vowel points (niqqud) and cantillation marks to the consonantal text; the oldest complete surviving manuscript is the Leningrad Codex (1008 CE). The Dead Sea Scrolls, dating from the 3rd century BCE to the 1st century CE, preserve much earlier witnesses, and ancient translations include the Septuagint (Greek) and the Targums (Aramaic).

Canonisation

The Torah was authoritative by the Persian period; the Prophets by about the 2nd century BCE; and the Writings were gradually accepted, with the full canon settled by roughly the 2nd century CE. Books found in the Septuagint but not the Tanakh — such as Tobit and 1–2 Maccabees — form the deuteroc canon of Catholic and Orthodox churches and the Apocrypha of Protestant tradition.
