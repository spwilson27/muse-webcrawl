Reproductive isolation

The mechanisms of reproductive isolation are a collection of evolutionary mechanisms, behaviours and physiological processes critical for speciation. They prevent members of different species from producing offspring, or ensure that any offspring are sterile. These barriers maintain the integrity of a species by reducing gene flow between related species.

Prezygotic isolation

Prezygotic barriers act before fertilisation. They include habitat isolation (populations occupy different habitats), temporal isolation (breeding at different times), behavioural isolation (different courtship rituals, songs or pheromones), mechanical isolation (incompatible reproductive structures), and gametic isolation (sperm and egg fail to fuse, common in marine species with external fertilisation).

Postzygotic isolation

Postzygotic barriers act after a hybrid zygote forms: hybrid inviability (hybrids die during development, often explained by Dobzhansky–Muller incompatibilities between genes), hybrid sterility (as in the mule, the sterile offspring of a horse and a donkey), and hybrid breakdown (first-generation hybrids are viable but their offspring are weak or sterile).

Evolution of isolation

Isolating mechanisms accumulate as populations diverge in allopatry or, more controversially, in sympatry. Reinforcement is the process by which natural selection strengthens prezygotic isolation where hybrids are unfit. Laboratory experiments with Drosophila by Theodosius Dobzhansky and others demonstrated that isolation can evolve measurably within tens of generations, and that postzygotic isolation evolves roughly as the "snowball" of pairwise incompatibilities.

Genetics

Studies of hybrid sterility and inviability have identified "speciation genes" such as Prdm9 in mice and OdsH in fruit flies. Haldane's rule observes that when one sex of a hybrid is sterile or inviable, it is usually the heterogametic sex (e.g. males in mammals).
