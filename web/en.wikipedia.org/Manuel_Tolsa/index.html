Manuel Tolsá

Born4 May 1757, Enguera, Valencia, Spain
Died24 December 1816, Mexico City, New Spain (aged 59)
NationalitySpanish
OccupationArchitect, sculptor
Known forNeoclassical architecture in Mexico City; El Caballito

Manuel Tolsá (1757–1816) was a Spanish architect and sculptor who worked in New Spain (colonial Mexico). He is regarded as the leading exponent of Neoclassicism in Mexico, and his buildings and sculptures remain defining landmarks of Mexico City.

Life and career

Tolsá was born in Enguera, in the province of Valencia, and trained at the Royal Academy of Fine Arts of San Carlos in Valencia and the Royal Academy of Fine Arts of San Fernando in Madrid, where he became a sculptor of the royal chamber. In 1790 he was appointed director of sculpture at the newly founded Academy of San Carlos in Mexico City, and he arrived in New Spain in 1791, bringing with him casts and copies of classical sculpture for teaching purposes.

In Mexico City Tolsá taught at the Academy, supervised public works for the city council, and received the colony's most prestigious commissions. He designed the completion of the Mexico City Metropolitan Cathedral — including its towers, dome, and facade statuary — finishing in 1813 a project that had been under construction for nearly three centuries. His other major works include the Palacio de Minería (the School of Mines, 1797–1813), considered his masterpiece and one of the finest Neoclassical buildings in the Americas; the Palacio del Marqués del Apartado; the Hospicio Cabañas project in Guadalajara (built to his design); the church of Santo Domingo and the main altar (ciprés) of Puebla Cathedral; and the Palacio de Buenavista, now the Museo Nacional de San Carlos.

El Caballito

Tolsá's most famous sculpture is the equestrian statue of King Charles IV of Spain, cast in bronze in a single pour in 1802 and unveiled in 1803 in the Plaza Mayor (Zócalo) of Mexico City. Known affectionately as El Caballito ("the little horse"), it is one of the largest bronze equestrian statues of its era. After Mexican independence the statue, a symbol of colonial rule, was repeatedly moved and at one point threatened with destruction; it was preserved — legend credits the inscription noting it was kept for its artistic merit — and today stands in the Plaza Manuel Gamio in front of the Palacio de Minería, facing the Museo Nacional de Arte.

Legacy

Tolsá died in Mexico City in 1816. His academy teaching helped form the first generation of Mexican-born architects and sculptors, and his work marks the transition in Mexican architecture from the late Baroque (Churrigueresque) to the Neoclassical style. The plaza in front of the Palacio de Minería, where El Caballito stands, is named Plaza Manuel Tolsá in his honour.
