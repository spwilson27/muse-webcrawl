Prestressed concrete

Prestressed concrete is a form of concrete used in construction in which internal stresses are introduced before the structure carries its service loads, in order to counteract the stresses that will result from applied loading. High-strength steel tendons — wires, strands, or bars — are tensioned and used to place the concrete into compression, a state in which concrete is strong, so that the tensile stresses produced by service loads do not exceed the pre-compression and cracking is controlled or prevented.

Principle

Ordinary reinforced concrete relies on steel reinforcement to carry tension once the concrete cracks. In prestressed concrete, the tendons are deliberately tensioned to a large fraction of their strength, so the concrete member is pre-compressed. Under load, the member first "unloads" this compression before any tension develops, allowing longer spans, shallower sections, and reduced deflection and cracking compared with conventionally reinforced members.

Methods

In pre-tensioning, tendons are tensioned against fixed abutments before the concrete is cast; once the concrete has hardened, the tendons are released and the prestress is transferred by bond. This is typical of factory-produced elements such as bridge girders and hollow-core floor slabs. In post-tensioning, tendons run through ducts cast into the concrete and are tensioned with hydraulic jacks after the concrete has gained strength, then anchored and usually grouted. Post-tensioning may be bonded or unbonded and is common in bridges, parking structures, and floor slabs.

History

The French engineer Eugène Freyssinet is credited with developing practical prestressed concrete in the 1920s and 1930s, recognizing that high-strength steel and low creep losses were essential. Gustave Magnel in Belgium and others spread the technique after the Second World War, when steel scarcity made its efficiency attractive for rebuilding bridges and infrastructure.

Applications

Prestressed concrete is used in bridges, high-rise and long-span floors, parking structures, railway sleepers (ties), water tanks and silos, nuclear containment vessels, offshore platforms, and poles and piles.
