Welfare economics

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Welfare economics is a field of economics that applies microeconomic techniques to evaluate the overall well-being (welfare) of a society. The principles of welfare economics underlie much of public economics, including the theory of cost–benefit analysis, the analysis of market failure and government intervention, and the evaluation of income distribution.

Approaches

There are two fundamental approaches to welfare economics. The neoclassical school, descending from Alfred Marshall and A. C. Pigou, traditionally treated utility as measurable and comparable across persons, so that policies could be judged by their effect on total utility. The new welfare economics, developed in the 1930s by economists such as John Hicks and Nicholas Kaldor, instead relies on Pareto efficiency: an allocation is an improvement if at least one person is made better off and nobody is made worse off.

Fundamental theorems

The two fundamental theorems of welfare economics connect competitive markets with efficiency. The first fundamental theorem states that, under certain conditions, every competitive equilibrium is Pareto efficient. The second fundamental theorem states that any Pareto efficient allocation can be reached as a competitive equilibrium given a suitable redistribution of initial endowments. These theorems, formalised by Kenneth Arrow and Gérard Debreu, define both the power and the limits of market allocation.

Social welfare functions

A social welfare function ranks social states according to the well-being of the members of society, aggregating individual preferences or utilities. Arrow's impossibility theorem showed that no aggregation rule can satisfy a short list of seemingly reasonable conditions simultaneously when there are three or more alternatives, a result with deep implications for democratic decision-making. Compensation criteria such as the Kaldor–Hicks criterion judge a policy by whether winners could in principle compensate losers.

Applications

Welfare economics informs the design of taxation, social insurance, environmental regulation and competition policy. Concepts such as consumer surplus, deadweight loss, externalities and Pigouvian taxes all arise from the attempt to measure how policies change social welfare.
