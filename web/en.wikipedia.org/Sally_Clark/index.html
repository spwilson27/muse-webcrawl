Sally Clark

Sally Clark became a emblematic case in debates about expert statistical evidence.

Sally Clark (August 1964 – 15 March 2007) was an English solicitor who was wrongfully convicted in 1999 of murdering her two infant sons, who had in fact died of sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS). Her conviction was overturned in 2003 after it emerged that prosecution expert evidence was seriously flawed and that material test results had not been disclosed.

The case

Clark's first son, Christopher, died in 1996 at eleven weeks, and her second son, Harry, died in 1998 at eight weeks. She was prosecuted largely on the evidence of paediatrician Professor Sir Roy Meadow, who testified that the chance of two cot deaths in one affluent family was about 1 in 73 million — a figure obtained by squaring the probability of a single cot death, an approach statisticians condemned as the "prosecutor's fallacy". The Royal Statistical Society issued a public statement criticising the misuse of statistics in the case.

Appeals and release

A first appeal failed in 2000, but a second appeal in January 2003 succeeded after it was revealed that a pathologist had failed to disclose microbiology results suggesting Harry had died of a bacterial infection. Clark was freed, and several other convictions resting on similar expert testimony, including those of Angela Cannings and Donna Anthony, were subsequently reviewed or overturned.

Aftermath and death

Clark never recovered from the trauma of imprisonment and the loss of her children; she died at home in 2007 from acute alcohol poisoning. Her case led to reforms in the handling of expert evidence and in the review of sudden infant death prosecutions in the United Kingdom.
