The potato (Solanum tuberosum) is a starchy tuber crop and the world's most important non-grain food, grown from a perennial plant in the nightshade family. Native to the Andes of South America, the potato was domesticated there roughly 7,000–10,000 years ago and was introduced to Europe by the Spanish in the second half of the 16th century. It is now a staple food for more than a billion people, ranking fourth in global production after maize, rice and wheat.

Biology and cultivation

The plant grows to about a metre tall, with white, pink or purple flowers and yellow-green fruit resembling small tomatoes. The tubers are swollen underground stems ("eyes" are their buds) that store starch as an energy reserve. Potatoes are normally propagated vegetatively from "seed potatoes" rather than true seed, so varieties are clones. They grow best in cool climates with loose, well-drained soil. Major diseases include late blight, caused by the oomycete Phytophthora infestans, which destroyed the Irish potato crop in the 1840s and caused the Great Famine.

Nutrition and uses

Potatoes are rich in starch, vitamin C, vitamin B6 and potassium, and contain about 2% protein of good quality. They are cooked in countless ways — boiled, baked, roasted, mashed and fried — and processed into products such as potato chips (crisps), French fries, vodka and starch. Green or sprouted tubers accumulate the glycoalkaloid solanine and can be toxic if eaten in quantity.

History

After its introduction to Europe the potato spread slowly, initially grown as a curiosity and animal fodder, but by the 18th and 19th centuries it had become the backbone of northern European agriculture because it yields more calories per hectare than grain crops, fuelling population growth. Its dependence in Ireland led to catastrophe when blight struck. The United Nations declared 2008 the International Year of the Potato. Thousands of cultivated varieties exist, from early "new potatoes" to floury bakers such as Russet Burbank, and breeding and genetic engineering continue to target blight resistance and nutritional quality.
