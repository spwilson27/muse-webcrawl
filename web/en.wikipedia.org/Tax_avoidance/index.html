Tax avoidance

Tax avoidance is the legal usage of the tax regime in a single territory to one's own advantage to reduce the amount of tax that is payable by means that are within the law. By contrast, tax evasion is the illegal evasion of taxes by individuals, corporations, and trusts. Tax avoidance can involve exploiting deductions, exemptions, and credits as intended by legislators, as well as arranging affairs to take advantage of loopholes, rate differences between jurisdictions, and timing rules.

Methods

Common avoidance techniques include claiming all available deductions and tax credits, deferring income, converting income into more lightly taxed forms such as capital gains, using tax-advantaged savings accounts, and, for multinational companies, transfer pricing and locating intellectual property or debt in low-tax jurisdictions — arrangements popularly known by names such as the "Double Irish" and "Dutch Sandwich". So-called tax havens facilitate many of these structures.

Government responses

Governments counter unwanted avoidance through specific anti-avoidance rules and, increasingly, general anti-avoidance rules that allow authorities to disregard artificial arrangements whose main purpose is a tax benefit. International efforts coordinated by the OECD, notably the Base Erosion and Profit Shifting (BEPS) project and the global minimum corporate tax agreement of 2021, aim to limit profit shifting by multinational enterprises.

Distinction from evasion

The boundary between avoidance and evasion is legality: evasion involves concealment or misrepresentation and is a criminal offence in most countries, whereas avoidance exploits the law as written. Aggressive avoidance schemes occupy a grey area and may be challenged in court; judges have long remarked, in a line attributed to Learned Hand, that there is nothing sinister in arranging one's affairs to keep taxes as low as possible.
