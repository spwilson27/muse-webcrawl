Nictitating membrane

Nictitating membrane
Other namesThird eyelid, haw
FromLatin nictare, "to blink"
Found inMany birds, reptiles, amphibians, fish, and mammals

The nictitating membrane (from Latin nictare, to blink) is a transparent or translucent third eyelid found in many animals that can be drawn across the eye to protect it and moisten it while maintaining vision. Unlike the upper and lower eyelids, the nictitating membrane moves horizontally across the eyeball. In many species any stimulus to the eyeball, such as a puff of air, triggers a reflex contraction that sweeps the membrane across the cornea.

The nictitating membrane of a masked lapwing, visible as a milky covering over the eye

Distribution

Fully developed nictitating membranes are found in many birds, reptiles, amphibians, and some mammals. In birds, the membrane sweeps diagonally and is used constantly during flight to keep the cornea moist; in diving birds it acts like a contact lens underwater. Sharks and some other fish possess nictitating membranes that protect the eyes when attacking prey. In polar bears it filters ultraviolet light.

In mammals

Many mammals, including cats and dogs, have a prominent third eyelid containing a tear gland; its visible appearance in a sick cat is a recognized clinical sign. In primates the membrane is generally reduced. In humans and most other primates, the plica semilunaris, a small fold of conjunctiva at the inner corner of the eye, is the vestigial remnant of the nictitating membrane.

Function

The membrane protects the cornea from mechanical injury and desiccation, spreads the tear film evenly, and can remove debris. In some frogs and lizards it is transparent, allowing the animal to see while the eye is protected; in the aardvark it helps keep dirt out of the eyes while the animal digs.
