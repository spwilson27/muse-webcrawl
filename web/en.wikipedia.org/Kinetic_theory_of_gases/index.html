Kinetic theory of gases

The kinetic theory of gases is a classical model of the thermodynamic behaviour of gases, explaining their macroscopic properties — pressure, temperature and viscosity — in terms of the motion of their constituent atoms and molecules. In the model, a gas consists of a very large number of identical submicroscopic particles moving rapidly in random directions, colliding elastically with each other and with the walls of their container.

History

Daniel Bernoulli gave the first kinetic derivation of pressure in his Hydrodynamica of 1738, but the idea was neglected for a century. John Waterston's fuller treatment of 1845 was rejected by the Royal Society. The theory was established in the 1850s–1870s by Rudolf Clausius, who introduced the mean free path, and by James Clerk Maxwell and Ludwig Boltzmann, who founded statistical mechanics.

Assumptions and results

The basic postulates are that the particles are negligibly small compared with the container and the distances between them, that they move in straight lines between collisions, that collisions are perfectly elastic and of negligible duration, and that no forces act between particles except during collisions. From these assumptions, the pressure of an ideal gas follows as P = (1/3)nmv² averaged over the particles, and comparison with the ideal gas law shows that temperature is proportional to the average translational kinetic energy of the particles: (1/2)mv² = (3/2)kBT.

Distribution of speeds

Maxwell and Boltzmann showed that molecular speeds are not all equal but follow the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution, in which most molecules move at moderate speeds and a few move much faster or slower, with the distribution broadening as temperature rises. The theory also yields quantitative accounts of transport properties — viscosity, thermal conductivity and diffusion — and of effusion (Graham's law). Kinetic arguments applied to Brownian motion by Einstein in 1905 provided decisive early evidence for the reality of atoms.
