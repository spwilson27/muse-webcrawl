Lotus-eaters

The Lotus-eaters (Greek: Λωτοφάγοι, Lotophagoi) were a race of people in Greek mythology, encountered by Odysseus and his crew in Homer's Odyssey. They lived on an island where the inhabitants' primary food was the fruit of the lotus plant; eating it caused a pleasant apathy and made those who tasted it lose all desire to return home.

In the Odyssey

In Book 9 of the Odyssey, Odysseus recounts that after being blown off course he landed among the Lotus-eaters. He sent three scouts inland; the natives offered them the lotus to eat, and those who ate forgot their homes and wished only to stay and feed on the lotus. Odysseus dragged them back to the ships by force, tied them beneath the benches, and ordered the rest of the crew to embark without tasting the fruit.

Identification and interpretation

Ancient and later writers located the Lotus-eaters on or near the North African coast, sometimes identifying their island with Djerba off Tunisia. Herodotus mentioned a people of the same name on the Libyan coast. The lotus itself has been variously identified with the jujube, a date-plum, or other plants with mildly narcotic or intoxicating fruits.

Cultural legacy

The episode became a lasting symbol of blissful forgetfulness and escape from duty. It inspired Alfred, Lord Tennyson's poem "The Lotos-Eaters" (1842), based partly on Odysseus's tale, in which the mariners sing of their weariness and desire to rest, and it has been reworked in many later poems, novels, and popular works.
