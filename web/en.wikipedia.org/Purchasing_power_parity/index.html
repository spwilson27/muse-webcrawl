Purchasing power parity

Purchasing power parity (PPP) is an economic theory and measurement technique that compares the currencies of different countries through the prices of a common basket of goods and services. In its absolute form, based on the law of one price, identical goods should cost the same everywhere once prices are converted at the PPP exchange rate; in its relative form, exchange-rate changes should offset differences in inflation rates over time.

Theory

The idea was developed by the Swedish economist Gustav Cassel after the First World War as a guide for restoring exchange rates. Arbitrage in tradable goods should push prices toward equality, but transport costs, tariffs, and the large share of non-tradable goods and services (such as housing and haircuts) prevent full convergence. The Balassa–Samuelson effect predicts that richer countries have systematically higher price levels because productivity advantages concentrate in tradables while wages equalize across sectors.

Measurement and uses

The International Comparison Program, coordinated by the World Bank, collects prices for hundreds of items across some 170 economies to compute PPP conversion factors. GDP measured at PPP is widely used to compare living standards: by PPP, economies with low domestic prices, including China and India, rank much larger relative to the United States than at market exchange rates. The Economist's Big Mac index is a lighthearted PPP gauge based on a single product. PPP-based measures also underpin the international poverty line and the Human Development Index's income component.

Limitations

PPP estimates are sensitive to basket composition, quality adjustments, and survey coverage, and they do not reflect a country's ability to purchase imports or service foreign debt, for which market exchange rates remain more relevant.
