Coulomb's law

Coulomb's law, or Coulomb's inverse-square law, is an experimental law of physics that calculates the amount of force between two electrically charged particles at rest. This electric force is conventionally called the electrostatic force or Coulomb force. The law states that the magnitude of the force is proportional to the product of the two charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them: F = ke q1q2 / r².

History

The law was first published in 1785 by French physicist Charles-Augustin de Coulomb, based on experiments with a torsion balance. Earlier in the century, Daniel Bernoulli, Joseph Priestley and Henry Cavendish had all anticipated aspects of the inverse-square relationship, but Coulomb's precise measurements established it.

Form and constant

The electrostatic constant ke is approximately 8.988 × 109 N·m²/C², and equals 1/(4πε0), where ε0 is the permittivity of free space. Charges of the same sign repel; charges of opposite sign attract. In vector form the force acts along the line joining the two charges, and Coulomb's law is a special case of Gauss's law and the foundation of electrostatics.

Limitations and uses

The law strictly applies to point charges at rest in a vacuum, but works well for charged spheres and, by superposition, for distributions of charge. It underlies atomic physics — the attraction between electrons and the nucleus — and the definition of the electric field.
