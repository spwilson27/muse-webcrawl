Experimental film

Experimental film or avant-garde film is a mode of filmmaking that rigorously re-evaluates cinematic conventions and explores non-narrative forms, alternative content, and unconventional methods of working. Typically produced outside the commercial industry on small budgets, experimental films often dispense with linear narrative, actors, and even the camera itself, focusing instead on the medium's material and perceptual properties.

History

The 1920s European avant-garde produced foundational works tied to movements such as Dada and Surrealism: Hans Richter's Rhythmus 21, Fernand Léger's Ballet Mécanique (1924), Man Ray's rayograph films, and Luis Buñuel and Salvador Dalí's Un Chien Andalou (1929). Postwar American avant-garde — Maya Deren, Stan Brakhage, Jonas Mekas — built an alternative infrastructure of cooperatives and journals, developing forms such as the trance film, lyrical film, and structural film. Brakhage painted and scratched directly on film stock; Michael Snow's Wavelength (1967) reduced cinema to a single slow zoom.

Techniques and forms

Common techniques include scratching and painting on film, found-footage re-editing, loop printing, time-lapse and single-frame animation, multiple exposure, and expanded cinema performances involving multiple projectors. Experimental film intersects with video art and gallery installation, and its aesthetics — jump cuts, abstraction, chance operations borrowed from composers like John Cage — have fed back into music video and mainstream cinema.
