Aleatoric music

Aleatoric music (or aleatory music, from Latin alea, a die) is music in which some element of the composition or its performance is left to chance. The term was popularized in the 1950s by the composer Pierre Boulez, who distinguished forms of controlled chance, and is most closely associated with John Cage, who used the I Ching to compose works such as Music of Changes (1951) and composed the famous silent piece 4′33″ (1952).

Types of indeterminacy

Scholars distinguish chance in composition (the score itself is produced by random procedures, as in Cage's I Ching works, but is thereafter fixed) from indeterminacy in performance (the score under-specifies the result, as in Earle Brown's graphic scores or Karlheinz Stockhausen's text pieces). Witold Lutosławski developed "limited aleatorism," in which players repeat short passages at individual tempi while the conductor cues larger entrances, producing shimmering, unsynchronized textures.

History and predecessors

Precursors include the eighteenth-century Musikalisches Würfelspiel — dice games for assembling minuets attributed to Mozart's era — and Charles Ives, whose scores sometimes offer alternative passages. In the 1950s and 60s, aleatoric procedures became a hallmark of the musical avant-garde and influenced experimental rock and electronic music.
