Musique concrète

Musique concrète (French for "concrete music") is a type of music composition that uses recorded sounds as raw material. Developed in France in the 1940s by the composer and radio engineer Pierre Schaeffer, it treated recorded sound objects — trains, bells, voices, industrial noise — as musical material to be cut, looped, reversed, and transformed, in contrast to composition beginning from abstract notation.

Origins

Working at French radio's Studio d'Essai, Schaeffer composed the Cinq études de bruits (Five Studies of Noises, 1948), including Étude aux chemins de fer, built from locomotive sounds. With Pierre Henry he produced Symphonie pour un homme seul (1950). The arrival of magnetic tape around 1951 greatly expanded the techniques available. Schaeffer founded the Groupe de Recherches Musicales (GRM) and theorized "reduced listening" — attending to sound in itself, divorced from its source — in his Traité des objets musicaux (1966).

Influence

Musique concrète stood alongside the electronically generated music of the Cologne studio as one of the two founding branches of electroacoustic music. Its sampling and tape-editing techniques anticipate modern sampling culture, sound design in film, and composers from Karlheinz Stockhausen and Edgard Varèse to contemporary electronic artists. Schaeffer's ideas also interacted with the chance-based work of John Cage, who pursued parallel experiments with tape in Williams Mix (1952).
