Jean Rouch

Born31 May 1917, Paris, France
Died18 February 2004 (aged 86), Tahoua, Niger
OccupationFilmmaker, anthropologist

Jean Rouch (31 May 1917 – 18 February 2004) was a French filmmaker and anthropologist, regarded as the father of ethnographic film and one of the founders of cinéma vérité. He made over one hundred films, many in West Africa, where he spent much of his career studying and filming the Songhay and Dogon peoples of Niger and Mali.

Work

Trained first as a civil engineer, Rouch turned to ethnology after the Second World War. His early films recorded possession rituals and migration; Les Maîtres fous (1955), about the Hauka cult in the Gold Coast, remains controversial for its depiction of trance and colonial mimicry. His concept of ethnofiction blurred documentary and improvisation: in Moi, un noir (1958), Nigerian migrants in Abidjan play versions of themselves and narrate their own stories.

With sociologist Edgar Morin he made Chronique d'un été (1961), the canonical vérité film, shot with the new lightweight sync-sound equipment Rouch helped pioneer. Rouch advocated "shared anthropology" — screening films for their subjects and incorporating their responses — and famously walked with the camera in long fluid takes that influenced the French New Wave.

Death and legacy

Rouch died in a car crash in Niger in 2004. His participatory methods reshaped documentary practice and visual anthropology worldwide.
