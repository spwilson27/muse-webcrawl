Experimental film

Experimental film or avant-garde cinema is a mode of filmmaking that rigorously re-evaluates cinematic conventions and explores non-narrative forms or alternatives to traditional narratives or methods of working. Many experimental films, particularly early ones, relate to the arts in other disciplines: painting, dance, literature and poetry, or arise from research and development of new technical resources.

History

Experimental film began in the 1920s, alongside the European avant-garde movements. Key early works include Ballet Mécanique (1924) by Fernand Léger and Dudley Murphy, René Clair's Entr'acte (1924), and Man Ray's Le Retour à la Raison (1923). In the Soviet Union, Dziga Vertov's theories of the "kino-eye" influenced documentary experimentation.

In post-war America, Maya Deren's Meshes of the Afternoon (1943) and the work of Stan Brakhage, who hand-painted film and shot without a camera, defined the American avant-garde. The 1960s saw the rise of structural film (Michael Snow's Wavelength, 1967; Hollis Frampton's Zorns Lemma, 1970) and the flicker films of Tony Conrad and Paul Sharits.

Characteristics

Experimental films are typically distinguished by:

  • Non-narrative or non-linear structures
  • Materialist exploration of the film medium itself (grain, exposure, editing)
  • Low budgets and personal, artisanal production
  • Distribution through festivals, museums and galleries rather than commercial theatres
