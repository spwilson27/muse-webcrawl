Experimental music

Experimental music is a general label for any music or music genre that pushes existing boundaries and genre definitions. Experimental compositional practice is defined broadly by exploratory sensibilities opposed to, and questioning of, institutionalised compositional, performing and aesthetic conventions in music. Elements of experimental music include indeterminate music, in which the composer introduces the elements of chance or unpredictability with regard to either the composition or its performance.

History

The term gained currency in the 1950s around John Cage and his circle, whose works — such as 4′33″ (1952), with its focus on ambient sound during performer silence — challenged definitions of music itself. Earlier antecedents include Charles Ives, Henry Cowell, Edgard Varèse, the Futurists' noise instruments (intonarumori), and Harry Partch. In Europe, musique concrète (Pierre Schaeffer) and electronic studios pursued parallel experiments.

Practices

Experimental approaches include aleatoric and chance procedures, graphic scores, extended instrumental techniques, prepared instruments, found sounds and field recordings, homemade electronics, improvisation, and process-based and minimalist composition.

In popular music

The label "experimental" is also applied to rock, jazz, hip-hop and electronic artists who depart from commercial conventions, from the Velvet Underground and Captain Beefheart to free jazz and noise music.
