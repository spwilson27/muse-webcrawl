Avant-garde

The avant-garde (from French, "advance guard" or "vanguard") refers to works, movements or people that are experimental, radical or unorthodox with respect to art, culture and society. It is frequently characterised by aesthetic innovation and initial unacceptability, and often has political overtones: the term was originally used by Saint-Simonian socialists to describe artists who would lead society toward progress.

Historical movements

Major avant-garde movements of the late 19th and 20th centuries include Impressionism (in its day), Cubism, Futurism, Dada, Surrealism, Constructivism, Abstract Expressionism, Fluxus, Pop Art (often seen as absorbing the avant-garde into mass culture), Minimalism and Conceptual art. Avant-gardes also flourished in film — from the French impressionist cinema and German Expressionism to the postwar "underground film" — and in music, literature and theatre.

Theory

Peter Bürger's Theory of the Avant-Garde (1974) argued that the historical avant-gardes attacked the institution of art itself, seeking to reintegrate art into the praxis of life, and that their failure led the "neo-avant-garde" to institutionalise transgression. Clement Greenberg associated avant-garde with the defence of high culture against "kitsch".

Criticism

The concept has been criticised as Eurocentric, self-mythologising, and — once absorbed by museums and markets — a vehicle for the very institutions it claimed to oppose.
