John Milton Cage Jr. (September 5, 1912 – August 12, 1992) was an American composer, music theorist, artist, and philosopher. A pioneer of indeterminacy in music, electroacoustic music, and the non-standard use of musical instruments, Cage was one of the leading figures of the post-war avant-garde, and critics have called him one of the most influential American composers of the twentieth century.

Career

Cage studied with Arnold Schoenberg and Henry Cowell. In the late 1930s he developed the prepared piano — a piano whose strings are altered with screws, bolts, and rubber — used in works such as the Sonatas and Interludes (1946–48). Influenced by Zen Buddhism and the I Ching, he turned to chance operations to remove personal taste from composition, producing Music of Changes (1951) and the landmark aleatoric piece 4′33″ (1952), in which the performer makes no deliberate sound and the "music" is the ambient noise of the room.

Influence

Through his teaching at Black Mountain College and the New School, and collaborations with choreographer Merce Cunningham and artist Robert Rauschenberg, Cage influenced Fluxus, happenings, conceptual art, and experimental film, as well as generations of composers from Morton Feldman to Brian Eno. His writings, beginning with Silence (1961), remain widely read.
