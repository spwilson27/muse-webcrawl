Direct Cinema

Direct Cinema is a documentary genre originating in North America around 1958–1962, characterized by a striving for pure observational filmmaking: no interviews, no narration, no staged scenes, and minimal intervention by the filmmaker — the "fly on the wall." It was made possible by newly developed lightweight cameras and portable synchronous sound equipment.

Key films and filmmakers

The movement coalesced around Drew Associates in the United States — Robert Drew, Richard Leacock, D. A. Pennebaker, and Albert and David Maysles — producing Primary (1960), which followed Kennedy and Humphrey through the Wisconsin primary; Crisis (1963); and later classics such as Salesman (1969) and Gimme Shelter (1970). In Canada, the National Film Board's Unit B (Colin Low, Terence Macartney-Filgate) pursued a parallel path with the Candid Eye series.

Relation to cinéma vérité

Direct Cinema is often conflated with the French cinéma vérité, but the two differ in stance: vérité filmmakers such as Jean Rouch provoked and participated, while Direct Cinema practitioners sought unobtrusiveness, ideally capturing a "crisis structure" in which events unfold before the camera. Both movements transformed documentary and later influenced journalism, reality television, and observational fiction film.
