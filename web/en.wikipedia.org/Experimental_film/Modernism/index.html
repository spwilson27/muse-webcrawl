Modernism

Modernism is a philosophical and art movement that arose from broad transformations in Western society during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. Driven by industrialization, urbanization, new technologies, and the shock of the First World War, modernists sought a deliberate break with traditional forms — in Ezra Pound's slogan, to "make it new."

Literature and the arts

Modernist literature experimented with stream of consciousness, fragmentation, and unreliable narration in the works of James Joyce, Virginia Woolf, T. S. Eliot, Franz Kafka, and Marcel Proust. In painting, modernism moved from Post-Impressionism through Fauvism, Cubism, Dada, and abstraction; in music, from Debussy and Stravinsky to Schoenberg's atonality and twelve-tone method; in architecture, to the International Style of Le Corbusier and the Bauhaus. Modernist cinema — from German Expressionism to Soviet montage — fed directly into the tradition of experimental film.

Themes and aftermath

Common themes include alienation, the instability of identity and language, scepticism toward grand narratives, and self-consciousness about the medium itself. From the mid-twentieth century, critics increasingly described a successor sensibility, postmodernism; others speak of "late modernism" or metamodernism. The definition and dates of modernism remain contested, but its reshaping of every art form is not.
