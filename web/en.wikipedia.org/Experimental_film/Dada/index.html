Dada

Dada or Dadaism was an art movement formed in reaction to the horror and perceived absurdity of the First World War. Beginning at the Cabaret Voltaire in neutral Zurich in 1916, and spreading to Berlin, Paris, Cologne, Hannover, and New York, Dada rejected the logic, reason, and aestheticism of capitalist society, expressing nonsense, irrationality, and anti-war protest through art that was often deliberately anti-art.

Activities and techniques

Dadaists such as Hugo Ball, Emmy Hennings, Tristan Tzara, Jean Arp, Hannah Höch, Kurt Schwitters, and Marcel Duchamp pioneered or popularized sound poetry, chance-based composition, photomontage, collage, assemblage, and the readymade — ordinary manufactured objects designated as art, most notoriously Duchamp's Fountain (1917), a urinal signed "R. Mutt." Dada publications and riotous performances attacked nationalism and bourgeois culture.

Legacy

By the mid-1920s Dada had largely dissolved or merged into Surrealism, but its influence runs through much of twentieth-century art: conceptual art, performance art, pop art, Fluxus, and the aesthetics of punk all draw on Dada. As an avant-garde movement it also intersected with modernism more broadly and with experimental film-makers such as Hans Richter and Man Ray.
