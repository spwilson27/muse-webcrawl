Cinéma vérité

Cinéma vérité (French for "truthful cinema") is a style of documentary filmmaking combining improvisation with the use of the camera to unveil truth or highlight subjects hidden behind crude reality. The term translates the title of Dziga Vertov's 1920s newsreel series Kino-Pravda ("Film Truth") and was popularized by the French sociologist-filmmaker Edgar Morin.

Characteristics and history

Vérité emerged around 1960, enabled by lightweight 16mm cameras and portable synchronous-sound recorders. Unlike the detached "fly-on-the-wall" of American Direct Cinema, vérité embraces the filmmaker's presence: provoking, interviewing, and intervening, on the theory that the camera catalyzes a deeper truth. The landmark film is Chronique d'un été (Chronicle of a Summer, 1961) by Jean Rouch and Edgar Morin, which ends with the subjects watching and debating the film itself.

Legacy

Vérité techniques — handheld camerawork, location sound, non-actors, reflexive acknowledgment of filming — permeated fiction film and television, from the French New Wave to contemporary reality TV and mockumentary. Critics continue to debate whether provoking subjects reveals truth or manufactures it.
