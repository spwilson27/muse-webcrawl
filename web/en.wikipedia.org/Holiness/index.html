Holiness

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Holiness is associated with the sacred, the pure and the set apart.

Holiness (from Old English hālig, "whole" or "holy") is the state or quality of being holy — set apart for, belonging to, or worthy of God or the divine — and, by extension, of absolute moral purity. The concept appears in many religions, applied to God, persons, places, objects and times. In Hebrew, qodesh conveys separateness and sacredness; the Greek hagios carries similar meaning into the New Testament.

In the Abrahamic religions

In Judaism, holiness (kedushah) is a central attribute of God ("Holy, holy, holy", Isaiah 6:3) and a calling for Israel, commanded to be holy because God is holy (Leviticus 19). It is realised through observance of the commandments, and applies to the Sabbath, the Temple and sacred objects.

In Christianity, holiness is God's essential attribute and the goal of the Christian life. Catholic and Orthodox traditions understand sanctification as a lifelong process of growth in grace, exemplified by the saints; Protestant traditions variously emphasise imputed holiness through faith, and the Wesleyan tradition — the "Holiness movement" of the nineteenth century, which gave rise to denominations such as the Church of the Nazarene — teaches "entire sanctification" or Christian perfection as a definite work of grace. The Holiness movement in turn influenced Pentecostalism.

In Islam, holiness is most fully God's attribute (al-Quddus, "the Most Holy", is one of the names of God), and sacredness attaches to the Quran, Mecca, mosques and the month of Ramadan; Jerusalem is known in Arabic as al-Quds, "the Holy".

In other traditions

Analogous ideas appear elsewhere: in Hinduism, purity and sacred power (puṇya) attach to deities, rivers such as the Ganges, temples and holy persons; in Buddhism, places and relics associated with the Buddha are venerated; in Shinto, kami and purified spaces are treated as sacred. Sociologists such as Émile Durkheim have made the sacred–profane distinction fundamental to the study of religion itself, while Rudolf Otto described the holy as the mysterium tremendum et fascinans — an awe-inspiring and attractive mystery beyond the purely moral.
