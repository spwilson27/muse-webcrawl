Bicycle

A bicycle, also called a bike, push-bike or cycle, is a human-powered or motor-powered assisted, pedal-driven, single-track vehicle, having two wheels attached to a frame, one behind the other. A bicycle rider is called a cyclist, or bicyclist. Bicycles were introduced in the 19th century in Europe; by the early 21st century more than a billion had been produced worldwide, outnumbering cars.

History

The wooden draisine or dandy horse, invented by Karl von Drais in 1817, was propelled by the rider's feet on the ground. Pedals were added in the 1860s, leading to the high-wheeled penny-farthing of the 1870s. The decisive design was John Kemp Starley's Rover "safety bicycle" of 1885, with equal-sized wheels, a chain driving the rear wheel, and a steerable front wheel; combined with Dunlop's pneumatic tire (1888), it produced essentially the modern bicycle and sparked a worldwide cycling craze in the 1890s.

Design and mechanics

The diamond frame of steel, aluminum, titanium, or carbon fiber carries two spoked wheels, a saddle, and handlebars. A chain transmits pedal power to the rear wheel through a gear train — often derailleur gears with a wide range of ratios. Steering geometry, including rake and trail, gives the bicycle its remarkable self-stability at speed.

Uses and impact

Bicycles serve for transport, recreation, sport (road racing, mountain biking, BMX), and utility work, and remain the most efficient self-powered means of transportation. Cycling infrastructure is central to transport planning in the Netherlands and Denmark, and the bicycle played a historic role in women's emancipation in the 1890s — Susan B. Anthony remarked that it had done more to emancipate women than anything else in the world.
