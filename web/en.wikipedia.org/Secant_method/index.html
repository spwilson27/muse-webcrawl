Secant method

The secant method is a root-finding algorithm that uses a succession of roots of secant lines to better approximate a root of a function f. It can be thought of as a finite-difference approximation of Newton's method, in which the derivative is replaced by a quotient of function values at two previously computed points. The secant method requires two initial approximations but, unlike Newton's method, does not require evaluating any derivative.

The secant method uses secant lines through pairs of points to approach a root.

The method

Given two initial approximations x0 and x1, the iteration is defined by the recurrence

xn+1 = xn − f(xn) · (xn − xn−1) / (f(xn) − f(xn−1))

The process is repeated until the approximations converge to a root of f or the difference between consecutive iterates is sufficiently small.

Convergence

If the initial values are close enough to a simple root and the function is sufficiently smooth, the secant method converges superlinearly with order equal to the golden ratio, approximately 1.618. This is faster than the linear convergence of the bisection method but slower than the quadratic convergence of Newton's method. Because each iteration needs only one new function evaluation, the secant method is often more efficient in practice when derivatives are expensive to compute.

Comparison with related methods

The secant method is closely related to the false position method (regula falsi), which brackets the root between two points of opposite sign. Variants such as the Illinois algorithm improve on false position by modifying retained function values. The secant method does not guarantee that the root remains bracketed, and it can fail if the denominator becomes zero or if the iterates diverge.
