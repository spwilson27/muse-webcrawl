Computer-aided manufacturing

Not to be confused with computer-aided engineering.

Computer-aided manufacturing (CAM) is the use of software to control machine tools in the manufacturing of work pieces. This is not the only definition for CAM, but it is the most common. CAM may also refer to the use of a computer to assist in all operations of a manufacturing plant, including planning, management, transportation and storage. CAM software converts engineering designs, typically created in computer-aided design (CAD), into instructions — often G-code — that drive computer numerical control (CNC) machines.

Process

The CAM workflow begins with a CAD model of the part. The programmer selects tools, cutting strategies and machining parameters; the software computes toolpaths and simulates the machining process to detect collisions; finally a post-processor translates the toolpaths into machine-specific code. The result is a program that a CNC mill, lathe, router or other machine tool executes largely automatically.

History

Numerical control originated in the 1940s and 1950s with punched-tape machines developed for the aerospace industry, notably at MIT. The APT programming language allowed part programmes to be generated by computer. From the 1970s onwards, CAM became integrated with CAD in combined CAD/CAM systems, and later with computer-integrated manufacturing.

Applications and benefits

CAM is used in automotive, aerospace, electronics and consumer-product manufacturing, as well as in additive manufacturing (3D printing) and rapid prototyping. Its advantages include greater precision and repeatability, faster changeovers, reduced waste, and the ability to machine complex geometries impossible by hand.
