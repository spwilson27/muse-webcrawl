Equatorial Guinea, officially the Republic of Equatorial Guinea, is a country on the west coast of Central Africa, consisting of a mainland region, Río Muni, and five inhabited islands including Bioko, where the capital Malabo is located. It is the only African country in which Spanish is an official language.

History

The territory was colonised by Portugal and then Spain as Spanish Guinea. Independence in 1968 was followed by the brutal dictatorship of Francisco Macías Nguema (1968–1979), under whom a large fraction of the population was killed or exiled. His nephew Teodoro Obiang Nguema Mbasogo seized power in a 1979 coup and remains president, leading one of the world's longest-ruling governments.

Economy

The discovery of large offshore oil reserves in the mid-1990s transformed the economy and gave the country one of Africa's highest GDPs per capita, though the wealth is concentrated and poverty remains widespread. Oil production has since declined, and the government has sought diversification and built a new planned capital, Ciudad de la Paz, on the mainland.

Geography and demographics

The population is around 1.7 million. The largest ethnic group is the Fang, predominant on the mainland; the Bubi are indigenous to Bioko. The mainland's rainforests are home to gorillas, chimpanzees, and forest elephants; the island of Annobón lies south of the equator.
