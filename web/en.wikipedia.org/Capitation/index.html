Capitation

Capitation pays providers a fixed amount per enrolled patient (placeholder)

Capitation is a payment arrangement for health care service providers, such as physicians or hospitals, in which they are paid a set amount for each enrolled person assigned to them, per period of time, whether or not that person seeks care. The amount is commonly adjusted for the enrollee's age, sex, and health status (risk adjustment). Capitation is a central payment model in managed care and in health maintenance organisations (HMOs), and it contrasts with fee-for-service payment, in which providers are paid for each service rendered.

How it works

Under capitation, a payer (insurer or health plan) pays the provider a fixed per-member per-month (PMPM) sum to cover a defined set of services for an attributed population of patients. The provider assumes financial risk: if patients need less care than expected, the provider keeps the surplus; if they need more, the provider absorbs the cost. Arrangements may be "full" (covering all services) or "partial" (covering, for example, primary care only).

Advantages and criticisms

Supporters argue capitation encourages preventive care and cost control, since providers profit from keeping patients healthy rather than from performing more procedures. Critics warn it can create incentives to under-treat, avoid sicker patients ("cherry-picking"), or skimp on referrals — risks mitigated by risk adjustment, quality metrics, and stop-loss provisions. Many modern "value-based" payment models blend capitation with performance bonuses and shared-savings arrangements.

Other meanings

Historically, "capitation" also refers to a poll tax levied per head, and in some countries to per-pupil funding formulas for schools, in which institutions receive a fixed sum for each enrolled student.
