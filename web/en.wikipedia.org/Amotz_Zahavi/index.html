Amotz Zahavi (Hebrew: אמוץ זהבי; 1 January 1928 – 12 May 2017) was an Israeli evolutionary biologist, a Professor of Zoology at Tel Aviv University, and one of the founders of the Society for the Protection of Nature in Israel. He is best known for proposing the handicap principle, a theory of honest signalling in animal communication.

Handicap principle

Proposed in 1975, the handicap principle holds that reliable signals between animals must be costly to the signaller, so that low-quality individuals cannot afford to fake them. The peacock's cumbersome train, on this view, honestly advertises the male's fitness precisely because only a fit male can survive bearing such a burden. Initially controversial, the idea was later given mathematical support by Alan Grafen and is now widely accepted in signalling theory. Zahavi developed the theory with his wife Avishag Zahavi in the book The Handicap Principle (1997).

Field research

Zahavi studied the Arabian babbler, a cooperatively breeding bird of the Arava valley, for decades. His observations of altruistic behaviour in babblers — such as individuals competing to feed others or act as sentinels — led him to interpret social prestige and costly altruism through the lens of signalling theory.

Conservation

In 1953 Zahavi co-founded the Society for the Protection of Nature in Israel, which became the country's largest environmental organisation; he received the Israel Prize for his contribution to the environment in 1980 (shared with colleagues).
