Soy milk, also known as soya milk or soy drink, is a plant-based beverage produced by soaking and grinding soybeans, boiling the mixture, and filtering out the remaining particles. It is a traditional staple in East Asian cuisines — a by-product and companion of tofu-making for many centuries — and since the 20th century has become a popular dairy alternative worldwide.

History

Soy milk originated in China, where soybeans have been cultivated for millennia; the earliest written references date to the Han era and it was long consumed fresh, often at breakfast with youtiao (fried dough sticks), both savoury (in northern China) and sweetened. Commercial production grew in the 20th century: the Hong Kong company Vitasoy, founded in 1940, popularised packaged soy milk across Asia, and from the 1980s it spread widely in Western supermarkets alongside other plant milks.

Production and nutrition

Traditional production soaks dried beans, grinds them with water, and cooks the resulting slurry; industrial processes use dehulled beans, controlled grinding and ultra-high-temperature treatment for shelf stability. Unsweetened soy milk provides protein comparable to cow's milk (about 3.3 g per 100 ml) with less saturated fat and no cholesterol or lactose. Commercial versions are commonly fortified with calcium and vitamins A, D and B12. Whole soy foods contain isoflavones; scientific consensus reviews have generally found moderate consumption safe and potentially beneficial, while regulatory debates in the EU and elsewhere have restricted use of the word "milk" for plant drinks on packaging.

Uses

Soy milk is drunk on its own, used in coffee and tea, poured on cereals, and used in cooking and baking. In the tofu process it is curdled with nigari or calcium sulfate; when heated, a protein-rich skin (yuba) forms on its surface.
