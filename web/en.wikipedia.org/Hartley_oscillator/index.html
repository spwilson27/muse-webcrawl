Hartley oscillator

The Hartley oscillator is an electronic oscillator circuit in which the oscillation frequency is determined by a tuned circuit consisting of capacitors and inductors, that is, an LC oscillator. The circuit was invented in 1915 by American engineer Ralph Hartley. The distinguishing feature of the Hartley circuit is that the tuned circuit consists of a single capacitor in parallel with two inductors in series (or a single tapped inductor), and the feedback signal needed for oscillation is taken from a tap connection or "voltage divider" at the centre of the two inductors.

Operation

The Hartley oscillator can be implemented with a triode, a field-effect transistor, or a bipolar transistor as the active device. The tank circuit comprises a capacitor C in parallel with two series inductors L1 and L2 (or a single coil with a tap), with the tap connected to the cathode, source, or emitter. The approximate frequency of oscillation is determined by the total inductance and capacitance:

f = 1 / (2π √(LTC)), where LT = L1 + L2 + 2M (M being the mutual inductance between the coils).

The ratio of the two inductances sets the feedback fraction; broadly, the fraction of output voltage fed back is L2/(L1 + L2), and the loop gain condition must be satisfied for sustained oscillation (the Barkhausen criterion).

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages of the Hartley oscillator include ease of tuning with a single variable capacitor, a relatively constant output amplitude over the tuning range, and a simple circuit. Its disadvantages include a harmonic-rich output waveform if the tank circuit's Q is low, and difficulty of construction at very high frequencies where the tapped inductor becomes impractical. It is closely related to the Colpitts oscillator, which uses a capacitive divider instead of an inductive one.

Applications

Hartley oscillators were historically used in radio transmitters and receivers and remain common in hobbyist radio circuits, signal generators, and local-oscillator applications in the RF range.
