Nicolas Léonard Sadi Carnot (1796–1832) was a French military engineer and physicist, often described as the "father of thermodynamics". In his single book, Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire (1824), he laid the foundations of the science of heat engines and of the second law of thermodynamics.

Life

Carnot was the son of Lazare Carnot, the mathematician and "Organiser of Victory" of the Revolutionary Wars. He studied at the École Polytechnique in Paris and became an officer in the army's engineering corps, though his military career was unfulfilling and he spent much of his time on scientific study in Paris. He died in a cholera epidemic in 1832, aged only 36, and most of his papers were burned as a precaution against infection.

Reflections on the Motive Power of Fire

Published in 1824 and largely ignored at first, the Reflections asked a fundamental question: what limits the efficiency of a heat engine? Carnot modelled an ideal, reversible cycle — the Carnot cycle of isothermal and adiabatic expansion and compression — and proved Carnot's theorem: no engine operating between two given temperatures can be more efficient than a reversible engine, and all reversible engines between the same temperatures are equally efficient. Although he reasoned within the caloric theory of heat, his surviving notes show he later accepted that heat is a form of motion and estimated the mechanical equivalent of heat.

Legacy

Carnot's work was recovered by Émile Clapeyron in 1834 and decisively used by William Thomson (Lord Kelvin) and Rudolf Clausius in formulating the absolute temperature scale and the second law of thermodynamics, including the concept of entropy. The Carnot efficiency, 1 − Tcold/Thot, remains the theoretical ceiling for any heat engine, from steam turbines to power stations.

Note on the name

He should not be confused with his nephew, also named Sadi Carnot (1837–1894), who became President of the French Third Republic and was assassinated in office; the nephew was named after the physicist.
