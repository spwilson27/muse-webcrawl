The Pacific Electric Railway Company, nicknamed the Red Cars for its bright red electric streetcars, was a privately owned mass transit system in Southern California. Consisting of electrically powered streetcars, light rail, and buses, it was the largest electric railway system in the world in the 1920s, linking cities in Los Angeles and Orange Counties, and the Inland Empire.

History

The system was established in 1901 by railroad magnate Henry E. Huntington and banker Isaias W. Hellman. Huntington's real estate ventures were closely tied to the railway: Red Car lines were extended into suburbs he developed, and land values along the routes rose sharply. By the mid-1920s the network operated over 1,150 miles of track, reaching as far as San Bernardino, Redlands, Santa Ana, Newport Beach, and San Pedro.

Pacific Electric was sold to Southern Pacific Railroad in 1911, although Huntington retained the Los Angeles Railway (the "Yellow Cars") streetcar system within the city of Los Angeles. Passenger ridership peaked during the Second World War, then declined rapidly as automobiles and freeways transformed Southern California. The last Red Car passenger line, the Long Beach Line, ceased operation in April 1961, and freight operations continued until 1965.

Legacy

Several former Pacific Electric routes have been revived as modern light rail lines, including portions of the Los Angeles Metro A Line (the former Long Beach Line) and the E Line. The Red Cars remain a cultural touchstone, famously depicted in the 1988 film Who Framed Roger Rabbit and in popular accounts of the General Motors streetcar conspiracy.
