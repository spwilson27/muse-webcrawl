Rime ice

Rime ice is a type of ice deposit that forms when supercooled water droplets in fog or cloud freeze on contact with a surface whose temperature is below freezing. The droplets freeze so rapidly that air is trapped between them, giving rime its characteristic white, opaque, feathery or granular appearance.

Rime ice accumulating on trees exposed to freezing fog.

Types

Hard rime is a dense, comb-like deposit formed in strong winds with relatively large supercooled droplets; it adheres tenaciously and grows into the wind. Soft rime is a lighter, feathery, needle-like deposit formed in light winds at very low temperatures. Rime is often confused with hoar frost, which forms by deposition of water vapour on clear calm nights rather than from fog droplets.

Effects

Rime accumulation adds weight and wind resistance to trees, power lines, radio masts and wind turbines, and can bring structures down in severe mountain weather. On aircraft, in-flight icing related to supercooled droplets is a serious hazard addressed by de-icing and anti-icing systems; on ships, freezing spray can destabilise vessels in high latitudes. Rime is common on exposed summits, including weather stations such as Mount Washington Observatory, famous for extreme rime build-up.
