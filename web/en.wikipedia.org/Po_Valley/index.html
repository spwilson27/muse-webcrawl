Po Valley

The intensively farmed plain of the Po Valley

The Po Valley (Italian: Pianura Padana or Val Padana) is a major geographical feature of Northern Italy. It comprises the drainage basin of the Po River and its tributaries, extending about 650 km from the Western Alps to the Adriatic Sea and covering some 46,000 km². The plain is bounded by the Alps to the north and west, the Apennines to the south, and opens to the Adriatic at the Po Delta.

Geography and climate

The valley is the filled remnant of an arm of the Adriatic that was progressively infilled by sediment carried from the Alps and Apennines. It is Italy's largest plain, generally divided into the higher, gravelly alta pianura near the mountain fronts and the low, clayey bassa pianura, where the line of fontanili (spring-fed streams) marks the transition and has been exploited for irrigation since the Middle Ages. The climate is humid subtropical to continental, with hot, humid summers and cold, foggy winters; temperature inversions trap fog (nebbia) and, notoriously, air pollution, making the valley one of Europe's most polluted areas despite its wealth.

Economy

The Po Valley is the economic heartland of Italy, home to roughly a third of the country's population and a large share of its industry and agriculture. Cities of the plain include Turin, Milan, Brescia, Verona, Padua, Bologna, Parma, Modena and Ferrara. Milan and Turin anchor automotive, fashion, finance and manufacturing industries. Agriculture is highly intensive and irrigated: the valley produces rice (around half of Europe's crop, centred on Vercelli and the Lomellina), maize, wheat, and fodder for the dairy herds behind Parmigiano-Reggiano and Grana Padano cheeses, as well as cured hams such as Prosciutto di Parma. Historically the plain's canals — including the Navigli of Milan, partly designed with input from Leonardo da Vinci — served both irrigation and transport.

History

Known in Roman times as Cisalpine Gaul, the valley was latinised after Rome's conquest in the 3rd–2nd centuries BC and crossed by roads such as the Via Emilia. In the Middle Ages and Renaissance its wealthy city-states — Milan, Mantua, Ferrara, Venice's mainland empire — were among the richest and most contested territories in Europe. In modern Italy the contrast between the industrialised north centred on the Po Valley and the poorer south (the Questione Meridionale) has shaped national politics, including the rise of northern regionalist movements.
