Baháʼí Faith

The Baháʼí Faith is a monotheistic religion founded in the 19th century by Baháʼu'lláh (1817–1892) in Persia (Iran). It teaches the essential worth of all religions, the unity of all peoples, and the progressive revelation of God's will through successive messengers, who include Abraham, Moses, Jesus, Muhammad, the Báb, and Baháʼu'lláh. Adherents, numbering several million, are found in most countries of the world.

Type: Monotheistic religion
Founder: Baháʼu'lláh (1817–1892)
Founded: 1863, Baghdad
World centre: Haifa and Acre, Israel
Adherents: Several million

Teachings

Central principles include the oneness of God, the unity of humanity, the equality of women and men, the harmony of science and religion, universal education, and the elimination of extremes of wealth and poverty. Baháʼís abstain from alcohol, observe a nineteen-day fast, and pray daily; the faith has no clergy, being administered by elected local and national Spiritual Assemblies and, at the world level, the Universal House of Justice in Haifa.

History

The faith grew out of Bábism, the movement founded by the Báb, who in 1844 declared himself a messenger preparing the way for a greater figure to come. Baháʼu'lláh, exiled from Persia to Baghdad, Adrianople, and finally the prison city of Acre in Ottoman Palestine, announced his claim in 1863. Baháʼís have faced sustained persecution in Iran, where the faith originated. The Baháʼí World Centre in Haifa, with the Shrine of the Báb and its terraced gardens, is the religion's spiritual and administrative heart.
