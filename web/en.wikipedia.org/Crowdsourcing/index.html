Crowdsourcing

Crowdsourcing obtains work, ideas or content from a large, often online community.
Details
Term coined2006, Jeff Howe in Wired
Related conceptsCrowdfunding, citizen science, open innovation, collective intelligence
Major platformsAmazon Mechanical Turk, Wikipedia, Kaggle, InnoCentive

Crowdsourcing involves a large group of dispersed participants contributing or producing goods or services — including ideas, votes, micro-tasks and finances — for payment or as volunteers. Contemporary crowdsourcing often involves dividing work between participants to achieve a cumulative result, or using an open call to attract people with diverse skills. The word was coined in 2006 by Jeff Howe as a blend of "crowd" and "outsourcing".

Historical examples

Open-call problem solving long predates the internet: the British Longitude Prize (1714), the compilation of the Oxford English Dictionary from volunteer-submitted quotation slips, and the 19th-century French government prize that produced margarine all mobilised public contributions. Early internet examples included the SETI@home distributed computing project (1999) and the collaborative encyclopedia Wikipedia (2001).

Forms

Common forms include crowdfunding (e.g. Kickstarter), citizen science (e.g. Galaxy Zoo, Foldit), microtasking through platforms such as Amazon Mechanical Turk, idea competitions and innovation contests (InnoCentive, Kaggle), and crowdsourced mapping (OpenStreetMap). Businesses use it for design contests, product testing and content moderation, while governments use it for consultation and crisis mapping.

Criticism

Critics raise concerns about low or unpaid labour ("digital sweatshops"), uneven quality control, intellectual-property disputes, and the displacement of professional work. Scholars also note participation is typically highly skewed, with a small minority of contributors doing most of the work.
