The Battle of Columbus was an attack on the small border town of Columbus, New Mexico, in the early hours of March 9, 1916, by some 500 mounted raiders led by the Mexican revolutionary general Francisco "Pancho" Villa. It was the last major attack by a foreign armed force on the continental United States until the Second World War era, and it provoked the U.S. Punitive Expedition into Mexico.

The raid

Villa's men crossed the border before dawn, expecting to loot arms, money and supplies from the town and the adjacent Camp Furlong, garrisoned by elements of the 13th Cavalry. Surprised American troops and armed townspeople organized a defense; machine guns enfiladed the main street, and the raiders withdrew toward Mexico with cavalry in pursuit, leaving dozens dead and parts of Columbus in flames. Eighteen Americans — soldiers and civilians — were killed.

Motives and aftermath

Villa's reasons remain debated: grievances over U.S. recognition of the Carranza government, revenge against an arms dealer, and the need for supplies all feature in accounts. In response, President Woodrow Wilson dispatched Brigadier General John J. Pershing with some 10,000 troops on the "Punitive Expedition" deep into Chihuahua. Pershing never caught Villa, but the expedition mobilized the National Guard, tested motorized transport and aircraft, and provided experience later used in the First World War.
