Taxation in the United States

Taxation in the United States is a complex system of taxes imposed at the federal, state, and local levels. The largest sources of federal revenue are the individual income tax and payroll taxes, followed by the corporate income tax. State and local governments levy their own income, sales, and property taxes.

Federal taxation

The federal government taxes income on a progressive basis, with marginal rates rising across income brackets. Payroll taxes — Social Security and Medicare — are levied under the Federal Insurance Contributions Act (FICA) on wages. The Internal Revenue Service (IRS), a bureau of the Department of the Treasury, administers the federal tax code, which is set out principally in Title 26 of the United States Code.

History

Before the income tax, the federal government relied mainly on tariffs and excise taxes. A temporary income tax was levied during the Civil War. The permanent modern income tax dates from the Sixteenth Amendment to the Constitution, ratified in 1913, which authorized Congress to tax incomes "from whatever source derived" without apportionment among the states. The Revenue Act of 1913 followed.

State and local taxes

States differ widely: some levy no broad-based income tax, while others tax income at rates above ten percent. Most states impose a general sales tax, and local governments depend heavily on property taxes to fund schools and services.
