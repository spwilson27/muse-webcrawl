Almoravid dynasty

The Almoravid dynasty (Arabic: al-Murābiţūn) was a Berber Muslim dynasty centered in the territory of present-day Morocco. It established an empire in the 11th century that stretched over the western Maghreb and al-Andalus (Muslim Iberia), at its height extending from the Senegal River to the Ebro. Founded by Abdallah ibn Yasin and the Sanhaja tribes of the Sahara, the Almoravids founded Marrakesh in about 1070 and made it their capital.

Rise

The movement began as a religious reform campaign among the veiled Sanhaja nomads of the western Sahara, inspired by Maliki Islam. Under Yusuf ibn Tashfin, the Almoravids crossed into Iberia in 1086 and defeated Alfonso VI of Castile at the Battle of Sagrajas (Zallaqa), halting Christian expansion and annexing the taifa kingdoms of al-Andalus one by one.

Decline

In the twelfth century the Almoravids weakened under pressure from the Almohads, a rival Berber reform movement from the Atlas Mountains, and from renewed Christian offensives in Iberia. Marrakesh fell to the Almohads in 1147, ending the dynasty.

Legacy

Almoravid rule unified the western Maghreb and al-Andalus under one state for the first time and left enduring monuments, including the Koutoubia Mosque's predecessor, the Koubba in Marrakesh, and the Great Mosque of Tlemcen. Their name survives in the Spanish word morabetino and in the gold coins, maravedís, that circulated widely in Christian Iberia.
