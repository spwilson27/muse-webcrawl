Offshore bank

An offshore bank is a bank regulated under an international banking licence (often called an offshore licence), which usually prohibits the bank from conducting business with citizens of its jurisdiction of incorporation. The term implies that most account holders are not resident in the country where the bank is physically located, and the bank's services are typically provided in an offshore financial centre.

Characteristics

Offshore banks are commonly associated with low or zero taxation, financial privacy, ease of access to deposits in major currencies, and protection against local political or financial instability. Historically, the Channel Islands and the Isle of Man were among the first offshore banking centres; others include the Cayman Islands, Switzerland, Singapore, Hong Kong, Panama, and the Bahamas.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages cited include tax efficiency, currency diversification, estate planning, and asset protection. Disadvantages include greater perceived risk, distance and expense of conducting business, possible lack of deposit insurance, and — since the 2000s — increasing regulatory scrutiny. Following international pressure, frameworks such as the U.S. Foreign Account Tax Compliance Act (FATCA) and the OECD Common Reporting Standard have sharply reduced bank secrecy by requiring automatic exchange of account information with account holders' home tax authorities.

Regulation

Many jurisdictions distinguish "Class A" (full domestic) and "Class B" (offshore-only) banking licences. Offshore banks are generally barred from certain domestic activities in their licensing jurisdiction, and reputable centres impose licensing, capital, and anti-money-laundering requirements.
