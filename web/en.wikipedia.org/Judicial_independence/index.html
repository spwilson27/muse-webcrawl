Judicial independence

Judicial independence is the concept that the judiciary should be institutionally separated from the other branches of government — the executive and the legislature — so that judges can decide cases impartially, on the basis of the facts and the law, without improper influence, pressure, threats, or interference. It is a foundational element of the rule of law and of the separation of powers, and a safeguard of human rights and democratic government.

Dimensions

Judicial independence has two main dimensions. Institutional independence concerns the judiciary as a branch: security of judicial tenure (typically appointment until a fixed retirement age, removable only for cause through a demanding process), protection of salaries from reduction, independent administration of court budgets and assignments, and merit-based appointment processes insulated from partisan control. Decisional (personal) independence concerns the individual judge, who must be free to rule against the government, powerful litigants, or public opinion without fear of retaliation. Judicial independence is complemented by judicial accountability through appellate review, published reasons, and codes of conduct.

Historical development

In England, the Act of Settlement 1701 gave senior judges tenure during good behaviour, removable only upon an address by both Houses of Parliament. Montesquieu's The Spirit of the Laws (1748) theorised the separation of judicial power, influencing the United States Constitution, whose Article III grants federal judges life tenure and undiminishable salaries. International standards include the UN Basic Principles on the Independence of the Judiciary (1985) and the Bangalore Principles of Judicial Conduct (2002).

Threats and contemporary debates

Threats include court packing, forced retirements, disciplinary proceedings used against independent judges, budgetary pressure, and political attacks on courts. Judicial reforms in countries such as Poland, Hungary, Turkey, and Venezuela have drawn international criticism for undermining independence, while elsewhere debate centres on elected judges, campaign funding, and the proper limits of judicial review.
