Gauss's law

In physics and electromagnetism, Gauss's law, also known as Gauss's flux theorem, is a law relating the distribution of electric charge to the resulting electric field. It states that the total electric flux through any closed surface is proportional to the total electric charge enclosed by that surface. The law was first formulated by Joseph-Louis Lagrange in 1773 and developed by Carl Friedrich Gauss in 1835, though it was published only after his death. It forms one of Maxwell's equations.

Statement

In integral form, the law states:

∮ E ⋅ dA = Qenc / ε0

where the left side is the electric flux through a closed surface (called a Gaussian surface), Qenc is the charge enclosed, and ε0 is the permittivity of free space. In differential form the law reads ∇ ⋅ E = ρ / ε0, relating the divergence of the electric field at each point to the local charge density ρ.

Applications and relations

Gauss's law is especially useful for computing electric fields when the charge distribution has a high degree of symmetry — spherical, cylindrical, or planar — because a Gaussian surface can then be chosen on which the field is constant. It is equivalent to Coulomb's law for static charges and holds generally for moving charges as well. An analogous law exists for magnetism (no magnetic monopoles, so magnetic flux through a closed surface is zero) and for gravity, where the flux of the gravitational field is proportional to the enclosed mass.
