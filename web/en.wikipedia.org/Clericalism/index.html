Clericalism

Clericalism is the application of the formal, structured leadership of an organised religion — especially a priesthood or clergy — to matters beyond the church itself, in particular to political and public life. It denotes the influence of religious authorities over secular government, law, education, and social policy, and is historically associated above all with the Roman Catholic Church, though the term is also applied to other traditions. Its opposite is anticlericalism.

History

The term became prominent in nineteenth-century European politics, when conflicts between liberal states and the Catholic Church over schools, marriage law, and church property defined public debate in France, Italy, Germany, Spain, and Belgium. In France, anticlerical governments of the Third Republic culminated in the 1905 law on the separation of church and state. In Germany, Bismarck's Kulturkampf attempted to subordinate the Catholic Church to the state and was resisted by the Centre Party.

Clericalism within the Church

In a second, internal sense, clericalism describes an attitude within a church that exalts the status and authority of clergy over the laity. Pope Francis repeatedly criticised clericalism in this sense, calling it a distortion of ministry and linking it to failures in addressing abuse. Reformers since the Second Vatican Council have used the term for hierarchical attitudes that resist lay participation.

Modern usage

Today the label is applied in debates over the political role of religious institutions in many countries, including the influence of the Islamic clergy in Iran, Orthodox churches in Eastern Europe, and Catholic bishops in Latin America and the Philippines. Scholars note that the term is often pejorative and context-dependent, used by critics of religious involvement in politics rather than by its proponents.
