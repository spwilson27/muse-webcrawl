Search warrant

A search warrant is a court order, issued by a judge or magistrate, that authorises law enforcement officers to search a specific location for evidence of a crime and to seize any evidence found. In most common-law jurisdictions, a warrant is required for a search to be lawful, subject to limited exceptions.

Requirements

To obtain a search warrant, officers normally must submit a sworn affidavit establishing probable cause to believe that evidence of a crime will be found at the place to be searched. The warrant must particularly describe the place to be searched and the persons or things to be seized. In the United States these requirements derive from the Fourth Amendment to the Constitution.

Execution

Warrants usually must be executed within a set number of days and often during daytime hours, unless a judge authorises night-time service. Officers generally must knock and announce their presence before entering, though "no-knock" warrants may be authorised where announcement would be dangerous or futile. A copy of the warrant and a receipt for seized property are typically left with the occupant.

Consequences of unlawful searches

Evidence obtained through an unconstitutional search may be suppressed at trial under the exclusionary rule, and unlawfully seized evidence can lead to the exclusion of further evidence derived from it ("fruit of the poisonous tree").
