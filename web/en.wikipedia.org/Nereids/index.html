Nereids

In Greek mythology, the Nereids are sea nymphs — the fifty daughters of the ancient sea god Nereus and the Oceanid Doris. They dwell with their father in the depths of the Aegean Sea and are generally benevolent toward sailors, helping them through storms; they should not be confused with the dangerous Sirens or the freshwater Naiads.

In myth

Hesiod's Theogony names the fifty Nereids, including Amphitrite, who became the wife of Poseidon; Thetis, the mother of Achilles; Galatea, loved by the Cyclops Polyphemus; Psamathe; and Thalia. The Nereids appear throughout Greek literature: they escort the dead Patroclus's mourning, bring Achilles his new armour in the Iliad, and in later sources ride hippocamps and dolphins in Poseidon's retinue. Cassiopeia's boast that she outshone the Nereids provoked Poseidon's flood and the sea monster sent against Ethiopia in the story of Andromeda.

Worship and legacy

The Nereids received cult at various coastal sites and ports, often alongside Poseidon. In modern Greek folklore the name survives as neraides, fairy-like water spirits of springs and seas. The name has been given to Neptune's third-largest moon, Nereid (discovered in 1949), and to a family of marine worms, the Nereididae.
