Hassler Whitney (March 23, 1907 – May 10, 1989) was an American mathematician. He was one of the founders of singularity theory and did foundational work in manifolds, embeddings, immersions, characteristic classes, and geometric integration theory.

Life and career

Whitney was born in New York City; his grandfather was the philologist and Sanskrit scholar William Dwight Whitney, and his great-uncle Josiah Whitney was the geologist for whom Mount Whitney is named. He studied physics and music at Yale, receiving bachelor's degrees in both, then turned to mathematics, earning a Ph.D. from Harvard in 1932 under George David Birkhoff with a dissertation on graph coloring. He spent most of his career at Harvard, becoming professor in 1935, before moving in 1952 to the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, where he remained until retirement in 1977. An accomplished mountaineer, he made first ascents in the Alps and elsewhere.

Work

Whitney began with graph theory — his 1931 theorem relating line graphs, and the notion of 2-isomorphism — and in 1935 introduced matroids as an abstraction unifying linear independence in linear algebra and acyclicity in graphs. In differential topology he proved the Whitney embedding theorem (1936), showing that every smooth n-manifold can be embedded in ℝ2n, and the companion immersion theorem. He discovered the Whitney umbrella, a singular surface in singularity theory, and developed the theory of characteristic classes jointly associated with Eduard Stiefel (the Stiefel–Whitney classes) while building toward cohomology theory. Later work concerned the extension of functions (the Whitney extension theorem), differentiable manifolds' foundations, and, in his last decades, research on mathematics education, where he championed the teaching of children through meaningful applications.

Honors

Whitney received the National Medal of Science in 1976, the Wolf Prize in Mathematics in 1982 (shared with Mark Krein), and the Leroy P. Steele Prize of the American Mathematical Society in 1985. He was president of the National Science Foundation's mathematics advisory activities and served on numerous national committees.
