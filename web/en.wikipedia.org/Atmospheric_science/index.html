Atmospheric science


Satellite view of Earth's weather systems

Atmospheric science is the study of the Earth's atmosphere and the processes that govern weather and climate. Its main areas include meteorology (the study of weather and forecasting), climatology (long-term patterns and climate change), atmospheric physics and dynamics, and atmospheric chemistry. The field draws on physics, chemistry, mathematics and computing, and is closely related to oceanography within the Earth sciences.

Branches

Meteorology focuses on short-term phenomena, from thunderstorms and tornadoes to hurricanes and fronts, and on numerical weather prediction, in which supercomputers solve the equations of fluid motion on a three-dimensional grid. Climatology examines averages and variability over decades to millennia, including natural archives such as ice cores and the human-caused warming of the climate system. Atmospheric chemistry studies composition and reactions, from ozone depletion to air pollution, while aeronomy concerns the upper atmosphere.

Observations and tools

Modern atmospheric science rests on a global observing system: surface stations, weather balloons (radiosondes), Doppler radar networks, aircraft, buoys, and fleets of geostationary and polar-orbiting satellites. These data feed global models run by centres such as the European Centre for Medium-Range Weather Forecasts (ECMWF) and NOAA, whose forecasts underpin aviation, agriculture, disaster warning and military planning.

History

The field has roots in Aristotle's Meteorologica and in 17th- and 18th-century instrument-making (barometers and thermometers). The telegraph made synoptic weather maps possible in the 19th century; the Bergen School in Norway developed the polar-front theory of cyclones after the First World War. Lewis Fry Richardson's 1922 attempt at numerical forecasting, Jule Charney's and John von Neumann's first successful computer forecast in 1950, and Edward Lorenz's discovery of deterministic chaos in the 1960s shaped the modern discipline.
