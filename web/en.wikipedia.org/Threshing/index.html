Threshing

Threshing or thrashing is the process of loosening the edible part of grain (or other crop) from the straw to which it is attached. It is the step in grain preparation after reaping and before winnowing, which separates the grain from the chaff. Threshing does not remove the bran from the grain.

History

For millennia, threshing was performed by hand using a flail, an implement consisting of two sticks joined by a short chain or leather strap. Grain was spread on a hard threshing floor and beaten to release the kernels. In other traditions, animals were driven over the grain to tread it out, or a threshing board or sledge studded with flints or metal was dragged across the crop. These methods were extremely labour-intensive: threshing was one of the most time-consuming tasks of the agricultural year.

Mechanisation

The Scottish millwright Andrew Meikle is credited with inventing the threshing machine around 1786, which beat the grain with a rotating drum. Horse-powered and later steam-powered threshing machines spread through Britain and North America in the nineteenth century, often operated by travelling crews during the harvest season. In the twentieth century, the combine harvester merged reaping, threshing and winnowing into a single machine, making separate threshing largely obsolete in industrialised agriculture. Small-scale and traditional threshing continues in many parts of the world, and pedal-powered and small motorised threshers are used for crops such as rice.

Festivals and culture

Threshing was traditionally associated with communal labour and harvest celebrations. Threshing bees in North America brought neighbours together to complete the work, accompanied by large communal meals.
