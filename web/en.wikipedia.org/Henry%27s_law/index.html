Henry's law

In physical chemistry, Henry's law is a gas law stating that the amount of dissolved gas in a liquid is directly proportional to the partial pressure of that gas above the liquid, at constant temperature. The proportionality factor is called the Henry's law constant. The law was formulated by English chemist William Henry in 1803.

Formulation

In its common form, p = kH·c, where p is the partial pressure of the solute gas, c is its concentration in solution, and kH is a constant that depends on the gas, the solvent, and the temperature. Several equivalent definitions of the constant (with reciprocal values and different units) are in use, so care is needed when comparing tabulated values. Henry's law is a limiting law: it holds for dilute solutions and for gases that do not react chemically with the solvent.

Applications

The law explains the carbonation of soft drinks — CO₂ dissolves under pressure and escapes when the bottle is opened — and governs gas exchange in the lungs. In diving, Henry's law underlies decompression sickness: nitrogen absorbed in body tissues at pressure can form bubbles if ascent is too rapid. It is also used in environmental science to model the partitioning of pollutants between air and water, and in oceanography to quantify uptake of atmospheric carbon dioxide by the oceans.
