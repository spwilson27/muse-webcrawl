Disability rights movement

The disability rights movement is a global social movement that seeks to secure equal opportunities and equal rights for all people with disabilities. It is made up of organisations of disability activists, also known as disability advocates, around the world working together with similar goals and demands, such as accessibility and safety in architecture, transportation and the physical environment; equal opportunities in independent living, employment equity, education and housing; and freedom from discrimination, abuse, neglect and other rights violations.

A central shift associated with the movement is from the medical model of disability, which locates the problem in the individual's impairment, to the social model of disability, which holds that people are disabled by barriers in society — physical, organisational and attitudinal — rather than by their impairments alone. The slogan "Nothing about us without us" expresses the movement's demand that disabled people participate in decisions affecting them.

History

Organising by disabled people dates to the 19th century — for example associations of blind and deaf people — and grew after the world wars with activism by disabled veterans. The modern movement took shape in the 1960s and 1970s alongside other civil rights struggles. In the United States, the independent living movement began at the University of California, Berkeley, where Ed Roberts and other students with significant disabilities demanded access to education and the community; the first Center for Independent Living was founded in Berkeley in 1972. The 1977 Section 504 sit-ins — in which disabled protesters occupied federal buildings, most famously in San Francisco for 25 days — forced the signing of regulations implementing the Rehabilitation Act of 1973. In Britain, the Union of the Physically Impaired Against Segregation (UPIAS) articulated the social model in 1976.

Landmark achievements

  • Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act (1973, US) — banned disability discrimination by federally funded programmes.
  • Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) — guaranteed free appropriate public education for children with disabilities in the US.
  • Americans with Disabilities Act of 1990 — comprehensive US civil rights law covering employment, public services, public accommodations and telecommunications; its passage was preceded by protests including the 1990 "Capitol Crawl".
  • Disability Discrimination Act 1995 (UK), later consolidated into the Equality Act 2010.
  • UN Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (2006) — the first comprehensive human rights treaty of the 21st century, ratified by the great majority of states, affirming rights to accessibility, independent living, inclusive education and legal capacity.

Issues

Movement priorities include accessible built environments and transport; inclusive education; supported employment and an end to subminimum wages; deinstitutionalisation and the right to live in the community; accessible information and communication technology (such as web accessibility standards); and recognition of the rights of people with intellectual and psychosocial disabilities, including an end to substituted decision-making. Disability activists also engage with debates on prenatal screening, assisted dying, and the representation of disability in media.
