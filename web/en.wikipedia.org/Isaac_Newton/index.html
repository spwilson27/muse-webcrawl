Sir Isaac Newton (4 January 1643 – 31 March 1727) was an English polymath active as a mathematician, physicist, astronomer, alchemist, theologian, and author. He was a key figure in the Scientific Revolution and the Enlightenment that followed. His pioneering book Philosophiæ Naturalis Principia Mathematica (1687), often called the Principia, laid the foundations of classical mechanics. Newton also made seminal contributions to optics, and shares credit with Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz for developing infinitesimal calculus.

Early life

Isaac Newton was born prematurely on Christmas Day 1642 (Old Style) at Woolsthorpe Manor in Woolsthorpe-by-Colsterworth, Lincolnshire. His father, also named Isaac Newton, had died three months before. When Newton was three, his mother Hannah Ayscough remarried and went to live with her new husband, the Reverend Barnabas Smith, leaving her son in the care of his maternal grandmother. Newton disliked his stepfather and maintained enmity towards his mother for marrying him.

From about age twelve to seventeen, Newton was educated at The King's School in Grantham, where he learned Latin and Ancient Greek and acquired a foundation in mathematics. In June 1661 he was admitted to Trinity College, Cambridge, as a sizar — a role in which he performed menial tasks in exchange for financial support. His studies were shaped by the works of modern philosophers such as René Descartes, and astronomers such as Galileo Galilei and Johannes Kepler.

Middle years

Mathematics

Newton's work in mathematics included the generalised binomial theorem, the development of infinitesimal calculus, Newton's identities, Newton's method for approximating roots, and the classification of cubic plane curves. His calculus, which he called the "method of fluxions," was developed in the mid-1660s, during the closure of the university due to the Great Plague. A priority dispute with Leibniz over the invention of calculus embittered Newton's later years and became one of the most famous controversies in the history of science.

Optics

From 1670 to 1672 Newton lectured on optics. His experiments with prisms demonstrated that white light is composed of a spectrum of colours, and he argued that light consists of particles (corpuscles). In 1704 he published Opticks, presenting his corpuscular theory of light and the first systematic account of his optical work. He also built the first practical reflecting telescope, known as the Newtonian telescope, in 1668.

Mechanics and gravitation

In the Principia, Newton formulated the laws of motion and universal gravitation that formed the dominant scientific viewpoint for centuries. He used his mathematical description of gravity to derive Kepler's laws of planetary motion, account for tides, the trajectories of comets, and the precession of the equinoxes. Newton's law of universal gravitation states that every particle attracts every other particle with a force proportional to the product of their masses and inversely proportional to the square of the distance between them. The famous story of the falling apple inspiring his thoughts on gravitation was recounted by Newton himself to acquaintances including William Stukeley.

Later life

In the 1690s Newton moved to London to take up the post of Warden of the Royal Mint, and in 1699 became Master of the Mint, a position he held until his death. He took the role seriously, pursuing counterfeiters with vigour. He was president of the Royal Society from 1703 until his death in 1727, and was knighted by Queen Anne in 1705. Newton died in his sleep in London on 20 March 1727 (Old Style) and was buried in Westminster Abbey.

Theology and alchemy

Newton wrote extensively on theology and biblical chronology, though most of this work remained unpublished in his lifetime; he held unorthodox religious views, privately rejecting Trinitarianism. He also devoted great effort to alchemy, writing more than a million words on the subject, much of it kept secret in his lifetime.

Legacy

Newton is widely recognised as one of the greatest mathematicians and physicists of all time. His laws of motion and theory of gravitation dominated physics until the advent of relativity and quantum mechanics in the early twentieth century. The SI unit of force, the newton, is named after him. Alexander Pope's famous epitaph reads: "Nature and Nature's laws lay hid in night: / God said, Let Newton be! and all was light."
