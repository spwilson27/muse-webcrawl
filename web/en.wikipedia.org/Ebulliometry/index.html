Ebulliometry
Measures: Boiling point elevation
Used for: Molar mass determination
Instrument: Ebulliometer

Ebulliometry is the measurement of the boiling point elevation of a solvent produced by dissolving a non-volatile solute, used to determine the solute's molecular weight. It belongs to the family of colligative-property methods, along with cryoscopy (freezing point depression).

Principle

For a dilute solution, the elevation of the boiling point ΔTb is proportional to the molality of the solute: ΔTb = Kb·b, where Kb is the ebullioscopic constant of the solvent. Measuring the elevation with a sensitive thermometer in an apparatus called an ebulliometer — such as the Cottrell apparatus, which pumps boiling liquid over the thermometer bulb to avoid superheating — allows the molar mass to be calculated.

Applications

Before the advent of mass spectrometry, ebulliometry was a standard technique for estimating the molecular weights of organic compounds and polymers. It requires the solute to be non-volatile and the solution to behave ideally, limiting its accuracy for strongly associating or dissociating solutes.
