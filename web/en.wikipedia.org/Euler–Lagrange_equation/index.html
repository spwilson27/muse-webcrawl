Euler–Lagrange equation

Euler–Lagrange equation
FieldCalculus of variations, classical mechanics
Developed byLeonhard Euler, Joseph-Louis Lagrange (1750s)
Key useDeriving equations of motion from an action principle

The Euler–Lagrange equation is a second-order partial differential equation whose solutions are the functions for which a given functional is stationary. Developed by Euler and Lagrange in the 1750s while solving the tautochrone and minimal-surface problems, it is the central equation of the calculus of variations and of Lagrangian mechanics.

Statement

For a functional S = ∫ L(x, y, y′) dx, stationarity requires ∂L/∂y − d/dx (∂L/∂y′) = 0. In mechanics, with L the Lagrangian (kinetic minus potential energy) and generalised coordinates q, the equations d/dt (∂L/∂q̇) − ∂L/∂q = 0 give the system's equations of motion directly.

Applications

The equation recovers Newton's laws, the geodesic equation, the wave and field equations of physics (via the field-theoretic Euler–Lagrange equations), and the Beltrami identity when L lacks explicit x-dependence. Noether's theorem links symmetries of the action to conserved quantities computed from the same framework.

History

Lagrange, then a teenager, communicated his δ-method to Euler in 1755; Euler presented the younger man's approach to the Berlin Academy and generously shared credit, naming the equation for them both.
