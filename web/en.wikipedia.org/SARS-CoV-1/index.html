SARS-CoV-1

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 1 (SARS-CoV-1), previously known as the SARS coronavirus (SARS-CoV), is a strain of coronavirus that causes severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS), the respiratory illness responsible for the 2002–2004 SARS outbreak.

Discovery and outbreak

The virus was first identified in 2003 during the outbreak that began in Guangdong province, China, in November 2002 and spread to some 30 countries. The World Health Organization, led locally by physician Carlo Urbani — who himself died of the disease — coordinated the international response. The outbreak was contained by July 2003, with 8,096 known cases and 774 deaths.

Virology

SARS-CoV-1 is an enveloped, positive-sense, single-stranded RNA virus of the genus Betacoronavirus. Its genome of roughly 29,700 nucleotides encodes structural proteins including the spike (S) protein, which binds the human ACE2 receptor to enter cells. Genetic studies traced the virus to horseshoe bats, with masked palm civets sold in live-animal markets acting as an intermediate host.

Aftermath

No cases of SARS have been reported since 2004, apart from a small number of laboratory-acquired infections. SARS-CoV-1 is closely related to SARS-CoV-2, the virus that causes COVID-19, and research on the earlier virus — including its spike protein and ACE2 entry route — informed the rapid development of COVID-19 vaccines nearly two decades later.
