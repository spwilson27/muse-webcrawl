Aluminium alloy

Aluminium alloys are alloys in which aluminium is the predominant metal. Typical alloying elements include copper, magnesium, manganese, silicon, tin, nickel and zinc. Aluminium alloys are widely used in engineering structures and components where light weight or corrosion resistance is important, notably in aircraft, automobiles, ships, packaging and construction.

Classification

Alloys are classified into two main categories: casting alloys and wrought alloys, each further divided into heat-treatable and non-heat-treatable types. Wrought alloys are designated by a four-digit system: 1xxx (pure aluminium), 2xxx (copper), 3xxx (manganese), 4xxx (silicon), 5xxx (magnesium), 6xxx (magnesium and silicon), 7xxx (zinc) and 8xxx (other elements).

Properties and uses

Aluminium alloys are about one-third the density of steel, giving excellent strength-to-weight ratios, especially in the heat-treated 2xxx and 7xxx series used in airframes. The 5xxx and 6xxx series offer good corrosion resistance and are used in marine applications, architecture and beverage cans. Cast alloys such as A356 are used for wheels and engine components. Pure aluminium's oxide layer gives alloys inherent corrosion protection, though alloying can reduce it.

History

The first widely used alloy, duralumin, was developed by Alfred Wilm in Germany in the early 1900s and enabled the construction of rigid airships and early all-metal aircraft.
