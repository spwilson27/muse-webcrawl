Papyrology

Papyrology
FieldClassical studies, ancient history
Object of studyManuscripts written on papyrus (and related materials) from the ancient world
Major sourcesOxyrhynchus Papyri, Herculaneum papyri, Nag Hammadi library, Dead Sea Scrolls (parchment/papyrus)
LanguagesChiefly Greek, Egyptian (Demotic, Coptic), Latin, Arabic

Papyrology is the study of manuscripts written on papyrus, the principal writing material of the ancient Mediterranean world. Papyrologists transcribe, translate and interpret literary texts and — in their great majority — everyday documents such as letters, contracts, tax receipts, petitions and census returns, which provide an unrivalled window onto daily life in antiquity, especially in Greco-Roman Egypt.

Discovery

Papyrus survives almost only where conditions are exceptionally dry, above all in the Egyptian desert. Carbonised scrolls were found at Herculaneum in the 1750s. The discipline took off in the late 19th century with mass finds at sites in the Faiyum and, from 1896–97, at Oxyrhynchus, where Bernard Grenfell and Arthur Hunt excavated hundreds of thousands of fragments from ancient rubbish mounds. The Oxyrhynchus Papyri, published in a long-running series of volumes, recovered lost works of authors such as Sappho, Pindar, Menander and the historian Hellenica Oxyrhynchia, along with early Christian texts.

Methods

Papyrologists piece together fragments, decipher cursive hands, restore lacunae (gaps) using formulae and parallels, and date texts by handwriting, content and archaeological context. Imaging techniques — multispectral photography and, for the carbonised Herculaneum scrolls, X-ray phase-contrast tomography and "virtual unwrapping" — have allowed texts to be read without physically opening fragile rolls. Closely related disciplines include epigraphy (inscriptions on stone) and palaeography (the study of handwriting).

Significance

Because the documents are unfiltered survivals of everyday writing, papyrology has transformed knowledge of ancient administration, law, economics, religion and private life, and continues to recover lost literature: newly published fragments have added poems of Sappho and Simonides, and parts of lost tragedies of Euripides and Sophocles.
