Cabaret

Cabaret is a form of theatrical entertainment featuring music, song, dance, recitation, or drama. The audience, often dining or drinking, typically sits at tables. Performances are usually introduced by a master of ceremonies, or MC. The entertainment is often orientated towards adult audiences and of a topical nature.

History

Cabaret originated in France in the 1880s, most famously with Le Chat Noir, opened by Rodolphe Salis in Montmartre, Paris, in 1881. The genre became a hallmark of Berlin culture during the Weimar Republic, when cabarets offered political satire alongside song and dance. Dutch, Polish, British and American cabaret traditions developed during the 20th century, and political cabaret remains a strong tradition in Germany.

In popular culture

The Broadway musical Cabaret (1966) and its 1972 film adaptation, set in Weimar Berlin, are among the most famous works in the form. Famous venues include the Moulin Rouge and the Crazy Horse in Paris. Modern cabaret encompasses burlesque, drag performance and stand-up comedy in intimate settings.
