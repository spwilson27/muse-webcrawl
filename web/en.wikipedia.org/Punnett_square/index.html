Punnett square

Punnett square
Field: genetics
Devised by: Reginald C. Punnett (c. 1905)
Used for: predicting offspring genotypes and phenotypes
Crosses: monohybrid, dihybrid, test cross

The Punnett square is a square diagram used to predict the genotypes and phenotypes of the offspring of a genetic cross. It was devised in the early twentieth century by the British geneticist Reginald Crundall Punnett and remains a standard tool in teaching Mendelian inheritance.

Construction

The gametes produced by one parent are written along the top of the grid and those of the other parent down the side. Each cell combines the allele from its row and column, giving one possible genotype of the offspring. Because each combination is (usually) equally likely, counting the cells yields the expected genotypic and phenotypic ratios.

Monohybrid and dihybrid crosses

A monohybrid cross between two heterozygotes (Aa × Aa) gives a 2×2 square with genotypes AA, Aa, Aa and aa — a 1:2:1 genotypic ratio and, with complete dominance, a 3:1 phenotypic ratio. A dihybrid cross, following two genes at once, requires a 4×4 square and produces the classic 9:3:3:1 phenotypic ratio when the genes assort independently.

Limitations

The diagram assumes independent assortment, random fertilisation and no interaction between genes. It becomes unwieldy for crosses involving many genes and does not directly represent linkage, epistasis or polygenic inheritance, for which other methods such as probability rules or branched diagrams are used.
