Lignite (derived from Latin lignum meaning wood), often referred to as brown coal, is a soft, brown, combustible, sedimentary rock formed from naturally compressed peat. It has a carbon content of around 60–70 percent and is considered the lowest rank of coal because of its relatively low heat content.

Characteristics

Lignite has a high moisture content, sometimes as high as 75 percent, and crumbles on exposure to air, which makes it inefficient to transport. It is mined in large open pits and burned in nearby power stations.

Production and use

Major producers include Germany, Russia, the United States, Australia and Greece. Because lignite has the highest carbon dioxide emissions per unit of energy of any coal, its use is being phased down in many countries.
