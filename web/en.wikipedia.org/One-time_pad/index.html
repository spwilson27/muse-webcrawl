One-time pad

In cryptography, the one-time pad (OTP) is an encryption technique in which a plaintext is combined, character by character, with a secret random key (the "pad") that is at least as long as the message, used only once, and kept completely secret. When these conditions are met, the system is provably unbreakable: the ciphertext gives an adversary no information about the plaintext beyond its length.

How it works

Historically, letters were converted to numbers and added modulo 26 to the key numbers; the modern binary form XORs each bit of the message with a key bit. Decryption repeats the same operation with the same pad. Claude Shannon proved in 1949 that the one-time pad achieves perfect secrecy, formalising work anticipated by Gilbert Vernam (who patented a telegraph cipher in 1919) and Joseph Mauborgne, who insisted the key be truly random and never reused.

The key problem

Perfect secrecy comes at a severe practical price: keys as long as the traffic must be generated with true randomness, delivered securely to both parties in advance, synchronised, and destroyed after a single use. Reuse is catastrophic — when Soviet operators reused pad material during and after the Second World War, American cryptanalysts in the Venona project were able to decrypt thousands of intelligence messages. One-time pads were nevertheless used for the highest-grade traffic, including the Washington–Moscow hotline, and remain a benchmark against which other ciphers are judged.

Modern relevance

Quantum key distribution attempts to solve the pad-distribution problem by generating shared random keys over quantum channels. Stream ciphers imitate the pad with pseudorandom keystreams, but lack its information-theoretic guarantee.
