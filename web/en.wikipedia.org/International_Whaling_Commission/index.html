The International Whaling Commission (IWC) is the international body established under the International Convention for the Regulation of Whaling, signed in Washington, D.C. on 2 December 1946, to provide for the proper conservation of whale stocks and the orderly development of the whaling industry. It is best known for the moratorium on commercial whaling adopted in 1982.

The IWC's remit has shifted from managing whaling to conserving whales.

History

The Convention was negotiated after decades of largely unregulated factory-ship whaling had driven Antarctic blue, fin, and other great-whale populations to collapse. The IWC initially functioned as a forum of whaling nations setting catch quotas, often above sustainable levels; quotas denominated in "blue whale units" encouraged taking the largest species first. As whale stocks fell and public opinion changed, non-whaling and conservation-minded states joined in the 1970s, shifting the body's politics.

The moratorium

In 1982 the Commission voted by the required three-quarters majority to set commercial catch limits to zero from the 1985/86 season. Norway and Iceland have conducted commercial whaling under objection or reservation to the moratorium, and Japan — which for decades took whales under a special-permit "scientific research" provision, a practice the International Court of Justice ruled in 2014 was not genuinely scientific in its Antarctic programme — withdrew from the IWC in 2019 and resumed commercial whaling within its own waters. Aboriginal subsistence whaling by indigenous communities in Alaska, Greenland, Russia, and elsewhere continues under IWC quotas.

Other work

The IWC has established whale sanctuaries in the Indian Ocean (1979) and Southern Ocean (1994), and its Scientific Committee coordinates research on whale populations, bycatch, ship strikes, ocean noise, and whale watching. Debates persist between member states over whether the Commission's primary purpose is conservation or the management of resumed sustainable whaling.
