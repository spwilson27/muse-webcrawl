Maimonides

Moses ben Maimon (1138–1204), commonly known as Maimonides and by the Hebrew acronym Rambam, was a medieval Sephardic Jewish philosopher, rabbi, astronomer, and physician from Córdoba in al-Andalus. One of the most influential Torah scholars of the Middle Ages, he authored the Mishneh Torah, the first comprehensive code of Jewish law, and the Guide for the Perplexed, a landmark attempt to reconcile Aristotelian philosophy with Jewish theology that influenced Jewish, Christian, and Islamic thought.

Maimonides' Mishneh Torah systematised all of Talmudic law in fourteen books.

Life

Born into a distinguished rabbinic family in Córdoba, Maimonides was displaced as a youth when the Almohad dynasty conquered al-Andalus and persecuted Jews and Christians; after years of wandering in Spain and Morocco, the family settled in Fustat (Old Cairo), Egypt, around 1168. There he became head of the Egyptian Jewish community and physician to the court of Saladin's vizier and later to the sultan's family. His famously gruelling daily schedule — court duties by day, communal leadership and writing by night — is described in his own letters. He died in 1204 and, by tradition, was buried in Tiberias.

Works

His Commentary on the Mishnah (written in Judeo-Arabic) introduced the Thirteen Principles of Faith, still printed in Jewish prayer books. The Mishneh Torah (c. 1180), written in clear Mishnaic Hebrew, organised the whole of Jewish law into fourteen books without citing sources or disputes — revolutionary in scope, it drew both admiration and criticism for its boldness. The Guide for the Perplexed (c. 1190), addressed to philosophically trained readers troubled by biblical anthropomorphism, argued that scriptural language about God is equivocal and that reason and revelation, rightly understood, cannot conflict. He also wrote medical treatises (on asthma, poisons, and regimen) that were translated into Latin and used for centuries.

Influence

Maimonides profoundly shaped subsequent Jewish thought and was read by Thomas Aquinas, Albertus Magnus, and later by Spinoza and Leibniz. The saying "From Moses [of the Bible] to Moses [Maimonides] there arose none like Moses" is inscribed on his tombstone. His works sparked the "Maimonidean controversies" of the 13th century between rationalists and traditionalists, and remain central to Jewish education today.
