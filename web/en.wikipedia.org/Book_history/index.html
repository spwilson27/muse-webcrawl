Book history

Book history is the academic discipline that studies the production, circulation and reception of texts — the book understood not merely as a container of words but as a material object and a social force. Drawing on bibliography, library science, literary history and social history, it examines scribes, printers, publishers, booksellers, censors, readers and libraries. The field is associated with scholars such as Lucien Febvre and Henri-Jean Martin, whose L'Apparition du livre (1958, "The Coming of the Book") founded the modern approach in France, and with Robert Darnton's "communications circuit" model.

Before printing

Writing surfaces progressed from clay tablets in Mesopotamia, through papyrus scrolls in Egypt and the Greco-Roman world, to the codex — folded sheets bound at one edge — which Christians spread widely in the first centuries AD. Medieval books were copied by hand, often by monks in scriptoria, on parchment made from animal skins. In China, paper was invented by the 2nd century AD, printing with woodblocks followed by the Tang dynasty, and movable type was developed by Bi Sheng in the 11th century; the Korean Jikji (1377) is the oldest extant book printed with metal movable type.

The printing revolution

Around 1440–1450, Johannes Gutenberg in Mainz combined metal movable type, oil-based ink, a screw press and cheap paper to make printing economically transformative in Europe. Within fifty years presses operated in hundreds of towns and had produced millions of books — more than all the scribes of the previous millennium. Print standardised texts, enabled the rapid spread of the Reformation's pamphlets and of scientific findings, and gradually created mass reading publics.

Industrial and digital eras

Steam presses (1814), machine-made paper, stereotyping and cheap binding turned books into mass commodities in the 19th century; the paperback revolution of the 1930s (Penguin Books) completed the process. Since the late 20th century, digital texts, e-books and print-on-demand have again reshaped publishing, prompting historians to treat the printed codex itself as one phase in a longer history of media — alongside institutions such as the private devotional book and mass prizes like the Booker Prize.
