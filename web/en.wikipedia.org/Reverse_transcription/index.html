Reverse transcription is the transfer of genetic information from RNA to DNA — the reverse of the usual transcription of DNA into RNA. It is catalysed by the enzyme reverse transcriptase and was discovered by Howard Temin and David Baltimore in 1970 in studies of RNA tumour viruses. Reverse transcription is the defining step of the retroviral life cycle: a retrovirus copies its single-stranded RNA genome into double-stranded DNA, which is then integrated into the host cell's genome as a provirus.

Steps of reverse transcription: an RNA template is copied into an RNA–DNA hybrid, the RNA is degraded, and a second DNA strand completes the duplex.

Mechanism

  1. A primer (in retroviruses, a specific host tRNA) anneals to the RNA template.
  2. Reverse transcriptase synthesises a DNA strand complementary to the RNA, forming an RNA–DNA hybrid.
  3. The RNase H activity of the enzyme degrades the RNA strand, with terminal repeats allowing template jumps that complete the molecule.
  4. A second DNA strand is synthesised, yielding double-stranded DNA with long terminal repeats (LTRs) at each end, ready for integration by viral integrase.

Occurrence in nature

Besides retroviruses, reverse transcription occurs in the replication of hepadnaviruses (such as hepatitis B virus), caulimoviruses of plants, retrotransposons in eukaryotic genomes, bacterial retrons, and in telomere maintenance, where telomerase reverse-transcribes a short RNA template to extend chromosome ends.

Laboratory use

Reverse transcription is a foundational laboratory technique. Combined with the polymerase chain reaction it forms RT-PCR, used to detect and quantify RNA (including in diagnostic tests for RNA viruses) and to study gene expression; it is also used to build cDNA libraries and in RNA sequencing workflows, where RNA is first converted to cDNA.
