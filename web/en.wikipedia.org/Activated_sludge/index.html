Activated sludge

The activated sludge process is a type of biological wastewater treatment used worldwide for treating sewage and industrial wastewaters. Developed in England in 1913–1914 by Edward Ardern and William T. Lockett at Manchester, it uses air (or oxygen) and a concentrated mass of microorganisms — the "activated sludge" — to oxidise organic pollutants.

Process

Settled sewage flows into an aeration basin, where air is blown in or surface aerators agitate the liquid, keeping the microbial floc in suspension while bacteria and protozoa consume dissolved organic matter (measured as BOD). The mixed liquor then passes to a secondary clarifier, where the sludge settles; most of it is returned to the aeration basin (return activated sludge) and the excess — waste activated sludge — is removed for sludge treatment such as anaerobic digestion.

Variants

Variants include extended aeration, sequencing batch reactors (SBRs), oxidation ditches, membrane bioreactors (MBRs) that replace the clarifier with membrane filtration, and nutrient-removal configurations that add anoxic and anaerobic zones to remove nitrogen and phosphorus. Process control issues include sludge bulking caused by filamentous bacteria, foaming, and maintaining proper food-to-microorganism ratios and sludge age.
