Cross section (physics)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In physics, the cross section is a measure of the probability that a specific process will take place in a collision of two particles. For example, the Rutherford cross section is a measure of the probability that an alpha particle will be deflected by a given angle during an interaction with an atomic nucleus. Cross sections are expressed as an effective area, conventionally measured in barns (1 barn = 10−28 m²).

Scattering

In scattering experiments, the differential cross section describes how the probability of scattering depends on angle, and integrating it over all angles gives the total cross section. The concept allows experimenters to infer the structure of targets — atoms, nuclei, or nucleons — from the distribution of scattered particles, a method used from Rutherford's gold foil experiment to modern collider physics.

Absorption and reaction

Cross sections are equally important in nuclear and neutron physics, where absorption, fission, and capture cross sections govern reactor behaviour and shielding. In particle physics, measured production cross sections for particular final states are compared with theoretical predictions from quantum field theory, making the cross section one of the central observables of collider experiments.
