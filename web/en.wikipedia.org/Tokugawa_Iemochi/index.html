Tokugawa Iemochi (徳川 家茂; July 17, 1846 – August 29, 1866) was the 14th shōgun of the Tokugawa shogunate of Japan, reigning from 1858 to 1866. He ascended as a child amid the crisis provoked by the arrival of Commodore Perry and the opening of Japan, and his reign was dominated by the struggle between the shogunate and the movement to expel foreigners and restore imperial rule. He died aged only 20 while campaigning against the rebellious Chōshū Domain, and was succeeded by Tokugawa Yoshinobu, the last shōgun.

Early life and succession

Iemochi was born Tokugawa Yoshitomi, the eldest son of Tokugawa Nariyuki, daimyō of Wakayama, one of the gosanke collateral houses. When the 13th shōgun Iesada died in 1858 without an heir, a bitter succession dispute erupted between supporters of the young Yoshitomi and supporters of the older, experienced Tokugawa Yoshinobu of Mito. The tairō Ii Naosuke imposed Yoshitomi, who took the name Iemochi, and purged his opponents in the Ansei Purge — a repression that deepened the shogunate's political crisis.

Reign

To reconcile court and camp, Iemochi married the imperial princess Kazu-no-miya (Princess Chikako) in 1862, a union embodying the policy of kōbu gattai (union of court and shogunate); Princess Kazu famously refused to abandon her imperial customs at the shogunal court. Under pressure from the court, the shogunate promised to expel foreigners by 1863, a deadline it could not meet; foreign powers retaliated with the bombardments of Kagoshima and Shimonoseki. Iemochi travelled to Kyoto in 1863 — the first visit by a shōgun in over two centuries — where he was effectively compelled to submit to court demands.

Chōshū expeditions and death

After Chōshū's defeat in the Forbidden Gate Incident of 1864, the shogunate launched a first punitive expedition, and a second in 1866 after Chōshū rearmed with Western weapons and allied with Satsuma. Iemochi led the campaign in person but fell ill — traditionally said to be of beriberi aggravated by heart trouble — and died at Osaka Castle on August 29, 1866, only weeks into the war, which the shogunate promptly lost. His death, and the succession of Yoshinobu, opened the final chapter of Tokugawa rule, ending with the Meiji Restoration in 1868.
