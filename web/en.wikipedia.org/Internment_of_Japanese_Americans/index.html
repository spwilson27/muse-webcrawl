The internment of Japanese Americans in the United States during World War II was the forced relocation and incarceration of about 120,000 people of Japanese ancestry, most of whom lived on the Pacific Coast. Notably, some 70,000 of those incarcerated were American citizens. The policy was applied in a sweeping manner in the western United States, contrasting with the treatment of German and Italian Americans, who were interned only on an individual basis.

Background

Anti-Japanese sentiment in the United States long predated the war, expressed in measures such as the Immigration Act of 1924, which barred Japanese immigration. Following the attack on Pearl Harbor on 7 December 1941, fears of espionage and sabotage — unsupported by evidence — intensified. On 19 February 1942, President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed Executive Order 9066, authorising the military to designate exclusion zones. General John L. DeWitt, head of the Western Defense Command, oversaw the removal of all persons of Japanese ancestry from the West Coast.

The camps

Evacuees were first held in temporary "assembly centers", often at fairgrounds and racetracks, then moved to ten camps administered by the War Relocation Authority: Manzanar and Tule Lake (California), Poston and Gila River (Arizona), Granada (Colorado), Heart Mountain (Wyoming), Jerome and Rohwer (Arkansas), Minidoka (Idaho), and Topaz (Utah). Families lived in tar-paper barracks behind barbed wire, under armed guard, in harsh desert or swamp conditions. Internees lost homes, businesses, and property estimated in the billions of dollars in today's terms.

Loyalty and military service

Despite incarceration, thousands of Japanese Americans served in the U.S. military. The 442nd Regimental Combat Team, composed largely of Nisei volunteers, became one of the most decorated units in American military history for its size and length of service, fighting in Italy and France. Others resisted the draft from behind barbed wire, and some challenged internment in court. In Korematsu v. United States (1944), the Supreme Court upheld the exclusion order; the conviction of Fred Korematsu was vacated in 1983 after evidence emerged that the government had suppressed reports showing Japanese Americans posed no military threat.

Redress

The last camp closed in 1946. A federal commission concluded in 1983 that internment had been driven by "race prejudice, war hysteria and a failure of political leadership". The Civil Liberties Act of 1988 formally apologised and paid $20,000 to each surviving internee. In 2018, the Supreme Court repudiated the Korematsu decision in dicta, calling it "gravely wrong the day it was decided".
