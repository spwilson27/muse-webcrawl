Conservatism

Conservatism is a cultural, social, and political philosophy and ideology that seeks to promote and preserve traditional institutions, customs, and values. The central tenets of conservatism may vary in relation to the culture and civilization in which it appears. In Western culture, depending on the particular nation, conservatives seek to promote and preserve a range of institutions, such as the nuclear family, organized religion, the military, the nation-state, property rights, rule of law, aristocracy, and monarchy.

The first established use of the term in a political context originated in 1818 with François-René de Chateaubriand during the period of the Bourbon Restoration that sought to roll back the policies of the French Revolution. Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790) is widely regarded as a founding text of modern conservatism.

Beliefs

Conservatives generally emphasize continuity and stability, valuing institutions that have evolved over time and expressing skepticism toward rapid or utopian social change. Many strands stress the importance of tradition as accumulated practical wisdom, respect for established authority, the rule of law, and private property. At the same time, conservatism is not a single fixed doctrine: liberal conservatives accept free markets and many liberal institutions, while traditionalist conservatives place greater weight on religion and social hierarchy.

History

Conservative thought emerged as a self-conscious response to the Enlightenment and the French Revolution. In the nineteenth century, conservative parties defended monarchy, church, and landed interests, but gradually accommodated democracy and social reform. In the twentieth century, conservatism in the English-speaking world became closely associated with free-market economics, anti-communism, and the political movements led by figures such as Margaret Thatcher and Ronald Reagan.

Variants

Major varieties include liberal conservatism, conservative liberalism, fiscal conservatism, green conservatism, national conservatism, neoconservatism, paleoconservatism, social conservatism, and traditionalist conservatism. Their relative strength differs sharply by country and era.

Criticism

Critics argue that conservatism can entrench privilege and resist necessary reform; defenders reply that gradual, tested change protects social order and liberty. The term remains contested and context-dependent across political cultures.
