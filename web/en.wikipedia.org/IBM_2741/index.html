IBM 2741

The IBM 2741 is a printing computer terminal introduced by IBM in 1965. It combined a rugged Selectric typewriter mechanism with electronic communications electronics, allowing it to be used both as an interactive terminal connected to a remote computer and, with the communications circuits switched off, as an ordinary office typewriter.

The 2741 was not compatible with the ASCII code then coming into widespread use; instead it used codes derived from the typeball in use, most commonly the correspondence code. It printed at a maximum rate of about 14.8 characters per second, limited by the speed of the Selectric mechanism, and its lines could accommodate up to 132 characters depending on the carriage fitted.

Development and design

The 2741 was developed at IBM's facilities in Lexington, Kentucky, the home of the Selectric typewriter. Because it was built around the Selectric's interchangeable "golf ball" type element, different typefaces and character sets could be fitted simply by changing the typeball. Terminals could also be equipped with a feature that allowed the typeball to be exchanged between printing correspondence text and printing computer output.

Two main variants were produced: one using IBM's correspondence code, most often paired with System/360 computers, and one using a PTTC code arrangement. The terminal connected over telephone lines or private leased circuits, commonly using IBM line adapters rather than standard modems.

Use with APL

The 2741 became strongly associated with the programming language APL. A special APL typeball provided the distinctive APL character set, with its overstrike characters formed by backspacing and typing a second character over the first. For many programmers of the late 1960s and 1970s, the 2741 was the characteristic APL terminal, used with time-sharing services such as those provided by I. P. Sharp Associates and STSC.

Successors

The 2741 was followed by the faster and quieter IBM 2740 and by later terminals, but the spread of video display terminals in the 1970s gradually displaced printing terminals of its kind. Nevertheless, hard-copy terminals remained in use in some time-sharing and office applications into the 1980s.
