Residency (medicine)

Residency or postgraduate training is a stage of graduate medical education. It refers to a qualified physician (one who holds the degree of MD, DO, MBBS, MBChB or equivalent) who practices medicine, usually in a hospital or clinic, under the direct or indirect supervision of a senior medical clinician registered in that specialty such as an attending physician or consultant. In many jurisdictions, successful completion of such training is a requirement in order to obtain an unrestricted license to practice medicine, and in particular a license to practice a chosen specialty. In general, a resident physician is more commonly referred to as a resident, senior house officer (in Commonwealth countries), or alternatively, a senior resident medical officer or house officer.

Residency training may be followed by fellowship or sub-specialty training. Whereas medical school teaches physicians a wide range of medical knowledge, basic clinical skills, and supervised experience practicing medicine in a variety of fields, medical residency gives in-depth training within a specific branch of medicine.

History

The modern residency system developed in the late 19th century, notably at Johns Hopkins Hospital under the influence of William Osler and William Stewart Halsted. The term "resident" derives from the fact that trainee physicians literally lived in the hospital, being on call around the clock. This live-in tradition persisted in diluted form well into the 20th century and shaped the long working hours that became characteristic of residency training.

Over the 20th century, residency programs became formalized and accredited, with standardized curricula, examinations, and supervision requirements. Duty-hour reforms in the early 21st century, prompted by concerns over patient safety and trainee well-being, progressively limited the length of shifts and total weekly hours in many countries.

Structure and duration

The length of residency varies by specialty. In the United States, family medicine and internal medicine residencies typically last three years, pediatrics three years, emergency medicine three to four years, psychiatry four years, obstetrics and gynecology four years, general surgery five years, and neurosurgery seven years. Before beginning a specialty residency, many trainees complete a one-year internship (a preliminary or transitional year).

Residents progress through postgraduate years (PGY-1, PGY-2, and so on), taking on increasing responsibility as they gain experience. Senior residents may supervise junior residents and medical students, and a chief resident — usually in the final year — takes on additional administrative and teaching duties.

United States

In the United States, residency programs are accredited by the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education (ACGME). Medical graduates apply to programs through the National Resident Matching Program (NRMP), whose "Match" algorithm pairs applicants with programs. International medical graduates must be certified by the Educational Commission for Foreign Medical Graduates (ECFMG) to enter ACGME-accredited training.

Residents are employees of their hospitals and receive a salary, which rises modestly with each postgraduate year, along with benefits. Because residency is required for licensure and board certification, residents have limited bargaining power, a situation that has drawn scrutiny and periodic calls for reform.

Working conditions

Historically, residents worked extremely long hours — often more than 100 hours per week with shifts of 36 hours or more. Following the death of Libby Zion in 1984 and subsequent investigations, New York State adopted the Bell Regulations, and in 2003 the ACGME imposed an 80-hour weekly limit (averaged over four weeks) across the United States, with further restrictions on first-year residents' shift lengths in 2011. Studies of the effects of these reforms on patient outcomes and trainee education have produced mixed findings, and debate continues in other countries as well.
