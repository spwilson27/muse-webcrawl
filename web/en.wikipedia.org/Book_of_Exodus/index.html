The Book of Exodus (from Greek Éxodos, "exit" or "departure"; Hebrew: Shemot, "Names") is the second book of the Bible and the Torah. It tells the story of the Israelites' enslavement in Egypt, their deliverance under the leadership of Moses, the giving of the Law at Mount Sinai, and the construction of the Tabernacle.

Narrative summary

The book opens with the descendants of Jacob multiplying in Egypt, where a new pharaoh, fearing their numbers, enslaves them and orders Hebrew male infants killed. The infant Moses is hidden, rescued by Pharaoh's daughter, and raised in the Egyptian court; after killing an Egyptian overseer he flees to Midian, where God calls to him from the burning bush and commissions him to lead Israel out of Egypt.

When Pharaoh refuses to release the Israelites, God sends the Ten Plagues upon Egypt. After the final plague, the death of the firstborn — commemorated in the festival of Passover — Pharaoh relents, but pursues the Israelites to the Red Sea, where the waters part and the Egyptian army is drowned.

The covenant at Sinai

Three months after leaving Egypt, the Israelites reach Mount Sinai. There God makes a covenant with Israel and gives the Ten Commandments and the body of laws known as the Book of the Covenant. While Moses is on the mountain, the people worship the golden calf; Moses intercedes, the covenant is renewed, and the book concludes with detailed instructions for, and the completion of, the Tabernacle, the portable sanctuary where God's presence dwells.

Composition

Traditional Jewish and Christian belief attributes the book to Moses. Modern scholars generally hold that Exodus reached its final form in the Persian period (5th century BC), combining older traditions — including a northern "Elohist" narrative, Priestly material, and early poetry such as the Song of the Sea (Exodus 15) — though a historical kernel behind the exodus tradition remains debated.
