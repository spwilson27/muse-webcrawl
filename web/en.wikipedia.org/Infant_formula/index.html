Infant formula

Infant formula, also called baby formula, simply formula (American English), baby milk or infant milk (British English), is an ultra-processed food designed and marketed for feeding to babies and infants under 12 months of age, usually for bottle-feeding, prepared as a powder (to be mixed with water) or as a liquid (with or without additional water). Most infant formula is based on cow's milk modified to approximate human breast milk — adjusting whey-to-casein ratios, replacing milk fat with vegetable oils, and adding lactose, vitamins, minerals and often DHA/ARA fatty acids; soy-based and extensively hydrolysed (hypoallergenic) formulas exist for infants with allergies or intolerance.

Health guidance and regulation

The World Health Organization and paediatric bodies recommend exclusive breastfeeding for about the first six months where possible, regarding formula as an acceptable substitute when breastfeeding is not possible or chosen against. Formula composition and marketing are regulated internationally by the Codex Alimentarius and, since 1981, by the WHO International Code of Marketing of Breast-milk Substitutes, adopted after controversies over promotion of formula in low-income countries where unsafe water made bottle feeding dangerous — the controversy that sparked the Nestlé boycott.

History

Commercial formulas began in the 1860s with Justus von Liebig's soluble infant food and Henri Nestlé's "farine lactée" (1867). Modern formulas were standardised in the 20th century. In 2022 a contamination-related shutdown of a major Abbott plant in the United States triggered a nationwide formula shortage.
