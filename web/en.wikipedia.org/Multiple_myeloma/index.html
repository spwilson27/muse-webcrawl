Multiple myeloma


Specialty: Haematology, oncology
Symptoms: Bone pain, fatigue, anaemia, kidney problems, infections
Usual onset: Around age 65–74
Type: Plasma cell cancer

Multiple myeloma (MM), also known as plasma cell myeloma, is a cancer of plasma cells, a type of white blood cell that normally produces antibodies. In myeloma, abnormal plasma cells accumulate in the bone marrow, crowding out healthy blood cells, and produce an abnormal antibody (M protein) that can damage the kidneys and other organs.

Signs and symptoms

Common features are summarized as CRAB: hypercalcaemia, renal failure, anaemia, and bone lesions. Patients often present with bone pain, especially in the spine and ribs, pathological fractures, fatigue, recurrent infections, and weight loss. Myeloma develops from the precursor states monoclonal gammopathy of undetermined significance (MGUS) and smouldering myeloma.

Diagnosis and staging

Diagnosis rests on finding clonal plasma cells in the bone marrow, M protein in blood or urine, and evidence of end-organ damage or biomarkers of malignancy. Skeletal imaging (low-dose whole-body CT, MRI, or PET) reveals lytic bone lesions. The Revised International Staging System uses albumin, beta-2 microglobulin, LDH, and cytogenetics for prognosis.

Treatment

Although generally considered incurable, myeloma is increasingly controllable. Treatment combines proteasome inhibitors (such as bortezomib), immunomodulatory drugs (such as lenalidomide), monoclonal antibodies (such as daratumumab), corticosteroids, and, for eligible patients, autologous stem-cell transplantation. Newer options include CAR T-cell therapies and bispecific antibodies. Median survival has improved markedly with modern regimens.
