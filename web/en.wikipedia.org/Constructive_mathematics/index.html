Constructive mathematics

Constructive mathematics is a branch of mathematics that insists that a mathematical object must be explicitly constructed — or an algorithm given for constructing it — in order to prove that it exists. It rejects the unrestricted law of the excluded middle and proof by contradiction as means of establishing existence.

Principles

In constructive logic, a proof that “there exists an x with property P” must provide a specific x together with a proof that x has property P. A statement A ∨ ¬A is provable only if one can decide which disjunct holds. This discipline gives constructive proofs direct computational content: a proof of existence is effectively a program.

Schools

Major schools include L. E. J. Brouwer's intuitionism, Errett Bishop's constructive analysis, and the Russian constructivist school of A. A. Markov. Type theories such as those underlying proof assistants (Coq, Agda, Lean) embody constructivist ideas and support computer-verified proofs.

Examples

Classically, one can prove there exist irrational numbers a and b with ab rational without saying which; a constructivist instead exhibits explicit witnesses, for example √2 and log29. Constructivists likewise reject the full axiom of choice, while often accepting weaker countable forms.
