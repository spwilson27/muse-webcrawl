Shor's algorithm

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: quantum circuit diagram for Shor's algorithm]

Shor's algorithm is a quantum algorithm for finding the prime factors of an integer. It was developed in 1994 by the mathematician Peter Shor and runs in polynomial time on a quantum computer, whereas the best-known classical factoring algorithms (such as the general number field sieve) run in super-polynomial time. Because widely used public-key cryptosystems such as RSA depend on the practical difficulty of factoring, Shor's algorithm is one of the principal motivations for building large-scale quantum computers and for developing post-quantum cryptography.

How it works

The algorithm reduces factoring a composite number N to finding the period of the function f(x) = ax mod N for a randomly chosen a. The quantum part of the procedure uses quantum superposition and the quantum Fourier transform to extract this period efficiently; the remaining computation (continued fractions and greatest common divisors) is classical. Once the period r is known, the factors gcd(ar/2 − 1, N) and gcd(ar/2 + 1, N) are computed.

Implementation

Experimental demonstrations have factored tiny numbers such as 15 and 21 using a few qubits, often with heavily compiled circuits. Factoring cryptographically relevant integers would require millions of physical qubits with error correction, far beyond present devices, though steady progress continues.
