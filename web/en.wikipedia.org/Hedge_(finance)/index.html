Hedge (finance)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A hedge is an investment position intended to offset potential losses or gains that may be incurred by a companion investment. In simple terms, a hedge is used to reduce any substantial losses or gains suffered by an individual or an organisation. Hedges can be constructed from many types of financial instruments, including stocks, exchange-traded funds, insurance, forward contracts, swaps, options, futures contracts, and derivatives.

How hedging works

Hedging involves taking an offsetting position in a related asset. For example, a wheat farmer who will harvest a crop in three months may sell wheat futures today to lock in a price; if prices fall, the gain on the futures offsets the lower price received for the crop, and if prices rise, the futures loss is covered by the higher sale price. In this way the farmer trades away both downside risk and upside potential in exchange for certainty.

A perfect hedge eliminates all risk in a position, while an imperfect hedge only partially reduces it. The difference between the price of the hedged asset and the hedging instrument is known as basis risk.

Common hedging instruments

  • Futures and forwards — agreements to buy or sell an asset at a set price on a future date.
  • Options — a protective put gives the holder the right to sell at a strike price, insuring against price falls while keeping upside.
  • Swaps — exchanging one set of cash flows (for example, floating interest payments) for another (fixed payments).
  • Diversification and natural hedges — holding assets whose values move in opposite directions, or structuring operations so revenues and costs are denominated in the same currency.

Hedge funds

The term gave its name to the hedge fund, a pooled investment vehicle that originally combined long stock positions with short sales — Alfred Winslow Jones's 1949 fund is generally credited as the first — although modern hedge funds pursue a wide range of strategies that may involve little actual hedging.

Hedge accounting

Under accounting standards such as IFRS 9 and US GAAP, companies that formally designate and document a hedging relationship may defer gains and losses on the hedging instrument so they are recognised in the same period as the hedged item, reducing artificial earnings volatility.
