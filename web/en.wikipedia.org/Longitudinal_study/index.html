Longitudinal study

Longitudinal studies observe the same subjects repeatedly over time

A longitudinal study (or longitudinal survey, or panel study) is a research design that involves repeated observations of the same variables — usually the same people — over periods of time spanning months to decades. It is a type of observational study used extensively in medicine, psychology, sociology, economics and epidemiology, and stands in contrast to a cross-sectional study, which observes different subjects at a single point in time.

Types

  • Panel study — the same individuals are measured at successive waves (e.g., the British Household Panel Survey).
  • Cohort study — a group sharing a defining characteristic, such as a birth year, is followed forward in time; cohort studies may be prospective or retrospective. Famous examples include the Framingham Heart Study (begun 1948), the Nurses' Health Study, and the British birth cohort studies of 1946, 1958 and 1970.
  • Trend (repeated cross-sectional) study — different samples from the same population are measured at each time point.

Advantages and disadvantages

Because the same subjects are followed, longitudinal designs can establish the temporal sequence of events, track individual change and development, and control for unchanging personal characteristics, making them much stronger than cross-sectional data for studying causation, ageing and life-course trajectories. Their drawbacks are cost and duration, practice effects from repeated measurement, and attrition — the loss of participants over time, which can bias results if dropout is non-random.

Examples

Notable longitudinal studies include the Dunedin Multidisciplinary Health and Development Study (New Zealand), which has followed about 1,000 people born in 1972–73 into middle age; the Grant Study of adult development at Harvard; the UK Millennium Cohort Study; and the German Socio-Economic Panel. Long-term animal and ecological longitudinal studies, such as the Yellowstone wolf project, apply the same principle.
