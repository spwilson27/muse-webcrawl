Radical polymerization, or free-radical polymerization, is a method of polymerization in which a polymer forms by the successive addition of free-radical building blocks. It is one of the most widely used industrial processes for making plastics, producing materials such as polystyrene, poly(methyl methacrylate), poly(vinyl chloride) and low-density polyethylene.

Mechanism

The reaction proceeds in three stages. In initiation, an initiator molecule — commonly a peroxide such as benzoyl peroxide, or AIBN — decomposes under heat or light into radicals that add to a monomer's double bond. In propagation, the growing chain-end radical repeatedly adds monomer units, extending the chain by thousands of units within seconds. In termination, two radicals meet and either combine or disproportionate, ending growth. Chain transfer reactions can also stop one chain while starting another, controlling molecular weight.

Features and control

Radical polymerization tolerates many functional groups and can be run in bulk, solution, suspension or emulsion. Its main limitation is poor control over molecular weight distribution and architecture, because termination is fast and statistical. Controlled ("living") radical methods — including ATRP, RAFT and nitroxide-mediated polymerization, developed from the 1980s onward — suppress termination and allow block copolymers and narrow dispersities.
