Quinine

FormulaC20H24N2O2
SourceBark of the cinchona tree
UseAntimalarial
Isolated1820, by Pelletier and Caventou

Quinine is a medication used to treat malaria and babesiosis. It is a naturally occurring alkaloid found in the bark of the cinchona tree, native to the Andes of South America. Quinine was the first effective treatment for malaria and remains on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines, although artemisinin-based combination therapies are now the standard first-line treatment for most malaria.

History

The medicinal properties of cinchona bark were known to the indigenous Quechua peoples of Peru, and Jesuit missionaries brought the bark to Europe in the 17th century, where it became known as "Jesuit's bark" or "Peruvian bark." Quinine was isolated from the bark in 1820 by French researchers Pierre Joseph Pelletier and Joseph Bienaimé Caventou. Dutch and British colonial powers later established cinchona plantations in Java and India, breaking the South American monopoly. The drug played a major role in enabling European colonization of tropical Africa and Asia.

Medical use

Quinine acts against the malaria parasite Plasmodium in the blood stage of its life cycle. It is still used for severe malaria, for malaria resistant to other drugs, and—in some countries—for nocturnal leg cramps, though this use is discouraged because of side effects. Side effects include cinchonism (tinnitus, headache, nausea), hypoglycaemia, and rare but serious blood and cardiac disorders.

Other uses

Quinine is the flavouring agent in tonic water and bitter lemon drinks, giving them their characteristic bitter taste. It fluoresces brightly under ultraviolet light, a property often used in demonstrations. Quinine also served as a model compound in the development of stereochemistry and was an early target of total synthesis; the 1944 Woodward–Doering formal synthesis was a landmark in organic chemistry.
