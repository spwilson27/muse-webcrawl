The Keeling Curve is a graph of the accumulation of carbon dioxide in the Earth's atmosphere based on continuous measurements taken at the Mauna Loa Observatory on the island of Hawaii from 1958 to the present day. The curve is named for the scientist Charles David Keeling, who started the monitoring programme and supervised it until his death in 2005.

Measurements

Keeling began measurements in March 1958 using a carefully calibrated infrared gas analyser, funded partly through the International Geophysical Year. He found a value of about 315 parts per million (ppm) and, crucially, a regular seasonal oscillation of about 6 ppm — the "breathing" of Northern Hemisphere vegetation, with CO₂ drawn down in spring and summer and released in autumn and winter — superimposed on a steady year-on-year rise.

Significance

The Keeling Curve provided the first unambiguous evidence that atmospheric CO₂ was rapidly increasing, and it demonstrated that the increase matched fossil-fuel combustion. Seasonal and isotopic analyses reinforced the attribution. The record continued after Keeling's death under his son Ralph Keeling at the Scripps Institution of Oceanography, in parallel with NOAA measurements. Daily readings crossed 400 ppm for the first time in May 2013, and annual averages have since climbed past 420 ppm.

Recognition

The curve has become an icon of climate science. In 2015 the Mauna Loa record was designated a National Historic Chemical Landmark by the American Chemical Society.
