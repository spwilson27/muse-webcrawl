Digital single-lens reflex camera

A typical DSLR camera body with lens attached

A digital single-lens reflex camera (digital SLR or DSLR) is a digital camera that combines the optics and mechanisms of a single-lens reflex camera with a digital imaging sensor, as opposed to photographic film. The reflex design scheme is the primary difference between a DSLR and other digital cameras; in the reflex design, light travels through the lens and then to a mirror that alternates to send the image to either the viewfinder or the image sensor.

Design

Like SLRs for film, DSLRs use interchangeable lenses with a proprietary lens mount. A movable mechanical mirror system is switched down to direct light from the lens over a focusing screen via a condenser lens and a pentaprism or pentamirror to an optical viewfinder. Most DSLRs also feature live preview, allowing the display screen to be used as a viewfinder in the same way as on a compact camera.

History

The first commercial DSLR was launched in 1991 by Kodak with the Kodak DCS series. Early DSLRs were expensive and aimed at professional photojournalists. Through the 2000s, prices fell and DSLRs became the dominant camera type among professionals and enthusiasts, with major systems from Canon, Nikon, Pentax, and others. From the 2010s onward, mirrorless interchangeable-lens cameras increasingly displaced DSLRs, and by the early 2020s major manufacturers had largely ceased developing new DSLR models.

Features

DSLRs are known for large image sensors (commonly APS-C or full-frame), fast phase-detection autofocus through dedicated sensors, optical viewfinders with no lag, long battery life, and extensive ranges of lenses and accessories. Many models also record high-definition video, a capability popularized by the Canon EOS 5D Mark II in 2008.
