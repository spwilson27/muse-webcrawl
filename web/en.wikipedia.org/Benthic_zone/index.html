Benthic zone

The benthic zone is the ecological region at the lowest level of a body of water such as an ocean, lake, or river, including the sediment surface and some sub-surface layers. Organisms living in this zone are called benthos and include many invertebrates — such as sea anemones, sponges, corals, sea stars, worms, crabs, and clams — as well as bottom-feeding fish.

Zones and habitats

The benthic zone begins at the shoreline (the intertidal or littoral zone) and extends downward along the continental shelf and slope to the abyssal depths. Pressure increases and light decreases with depth; below the photic zone, benthic communities depend largely on organic matter raining down from above ("marine snow").

Ecology

Benthic organisms play a central role in nutrient cycling, bioturbation of sediments, and as food for demersal fish. Microbial communities in the sediments drive decomposition and chemical exchange with the water column. Deep-sea benthic habitats near hydrothermal vents and cold seeps support chemosynthetic communities independent of sunlight.

Human impact

Bottom trawling, dredging, pollution, and ocean acidification are major pressures on benthic ecosystems, which are slow to recover because many benthic species are long-lived and slow-growing.
