Qin Er Shi (Chinese: 秦二世; 229–207 BCE), personal name Ying Huhai, was the second emperor of the Qin dynasty of China, reigning from 210 to 207 BCE. His brief and disastrous reign, dominated by the eunuch Zhao Gao, saw the outbreak of rebellions that destroyed China's first imperial dynasty within four years of its founder's death.

Accession

Huhai was a younger son of Qin Shi Huang, the First Emperor. When the First Emperor died suddenly on tour in 210 BCE, the eunuch Zhao Gao and the chancellor Li Si concealed the death, forged an edict ordering the heir apparent Fusu to commit suicide, and installed the pliable Huhai as emperor. Fusu and the general Meng Tian obeyed the forged order and died.

Reign

Qin Er Shi withdrew from court business and left government to Zhao Gao, who purged rivals: Li Si was executed by waist chop in 208 BCE, and princes, princesses, and ministers were killed in large numbers. Harsh laws and vast corvée projects — including the Epang Palace and the First Emperor's mausoleum — continued. In 209 BCE the Dazexiang uprising of Chen Sheng and Wu Guang ignited rebellions across the empire; the warlord Xiang Yu annihilated the main Qin army at Julu, and Liu Bang advanced on the capital.

Fearing blame, Zhao Gao staged the famous "calling a deer a horse" test of courtiers' loyalty, then, as rebels approached Xianyang in 207 BCE, had Qin Er Shi forced to commit suicide. Zhao Gao installed Ziying as king (not emperor) of Qin; weeks later Ziying surrendered to Liu Bang and the dynasty ended.

Legacy

In Chinese historiography Qin Er Shi is the archetype of the incompetent, manipulated ruler whose weakness undid his father's achievement; the Qin collapse became a lasting lesson on the costs of tyranny and succession failure, summarized in Jia Yi's essay "The Faults of Qin."
