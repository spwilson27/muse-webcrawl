Sea lice are marine ectoparasites (external parasites) that feed on the mucus, epidermal tissue and blood of host marine fish. They are members of the copepod family Caligidae, comprising some 559 species in 37 genera. The most discussed species is the salmon louse, Lepeophtheirus salmonis, a specialist parasite of salmonid fish found naturally in the northern hemisphere, alongside the more generalist Caligus elongatus and a range of tropical Caligus species that affect a wide variety of fish.

Life cycle

Sea lice have a direct life cycle of planktonic nauplius larvae, a free-swimming infective copepodid stage that must find and attach to a host, several attached chalimus stages anchored to the fish, and mobile pre-adult and adult stages that can move over the host's surface and between hosts. Under warm conditions the whole cycle may take only a few weeks, and a single female, carrying paired egg strings of hundreds of eggs, can produce many thousands of offspring. Development is strongly temperature dependent.

Effects and aquaculture

In the wild, lice burdens on salmon are usually low and cause little harm. In salmon farming, however, densely stocked net-pens can support exploding louse populations. Heavy infestations erode skin and fins, cause open lesions, stress and secondary infection, and can kill the fish; lice from farms can also spill back onto migrating wild juvenile salmon, a central controversy in the industry in Norway, Scotland, Ireland, Canada and Chile. The annual cost of sea lice to global salmon farming is estimated at well over US$1 billion.

Control

Control has relied on chemotherapeutants such as emamectin benzoate (in-feed) and hydrogen peroxide or azamethiphos baths, against all of which resistance has evolved. Growing responses include "cleaner fish" — lumpfish and wrasse stocked to eat lice — mechanical and thermal delousing machines, freshwater baths, laser systems, snorkel cages and lice skirts, selective breeding for resistant salmon, and a growing research effort on vaccines.
