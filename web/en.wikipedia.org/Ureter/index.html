The ureters are tubes made of smooth muscle that propel urine from the kidneys to the urinary bladder. In a human adult, the ureters are usually 20–30 cm (8–12 in) long and around 3–4 mm (0.12–0.16 in) in diameter. The ureter is lined by urothelial cells, a type of transitional epithelium, and has an additional smooth muscle layer that assists with peristalsis.

Structure

Each ureter arises from the renal pelvis at the ureteropelvic junction, descends behind the peritoneum along the psoas major muscle, crosses the pelvic brim near the bifurcation of the common iliac arteries, and enters the bladder obliquely through its wall at the ureterovesical junction. This oblique entry acts as a one-way valve preventing backflow (reflux) of urine during bladder contraction. The ureter is constricted at three points: the ureteropelvic junction, the pelvic brim, and the bladder entry — sites where kidney stones most commonly lodge.

Function

Waves of peristalsis in the ureteral smooth muscle, occurring several times per minute, propel small boluses of urine from the kidneys to the bladder regardless of body position. The kidneys produce urine continuously, and this muscular action maintains flow against gravity when standing.

Clinical significance

Obstruction of the ureter by a kidney stone (ureterolithiasis) causes severe flank pain known as renal colic and can lead to hydronephrosis. Other conditions include vesicoureteral reflux, congenital duplications of the ureter, strictures, and transitional cell carcinoma. Imaging techniques such as intravenous pyelography, CT urography, and ultrasound are used to evaluate the ureters, and ureteroscopy allows direct visualization and treatment.
