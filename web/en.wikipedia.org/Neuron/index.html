A neuron, neurone, or nerve cell is an excitable cell that fires electric signals called action potentials across a neural network. Neurons are the main components of the nervous system — in humans, the brain contains about 86 billion neurons, supported by a comparable number of glial cells. They communicate with other cells via synapses, specialised connections that commonly use minute amounts of chemical neurotransmitters to pass the electric signal from the presynaptic neuron to the target cell through the synaptic gap.

Structure

A typical neuron consists of a cell body (soma) containing the nucleus; dendrites, branching processes that receive signals; and a single axon, often long and insulated by a myelin sheath in vertebrates, which carries the action potential to axon terminals. Neurons are classified by structure (multipolar, bipolar, unipolar) and by function (sensory neurons, motor neurons and interneurons), as well as by the neurotransmitter they release, such as glutamate, GABA, dopamine or serotonin.

Signalling

Neurons maintain a resting membrane potential of about −70 mV through ion pumps and channels. When excitatory input depolarises the axon hillock past threshold, voltage-gated sodium channels open and an all-or-none action potential propagates down the axon. At chemical synapses the signal causes vesicles to release neurotransmitter into the synaptic cleft; electrical synapses (gap junctions) transmit current directly.

History

The neuron doctrine — that the nervous system consists of discrete individual cells — was established by Santiago Ramón y Cajal's microscopic studies using Camillo Golgi's stain, and the two shared the 1906 Nobel Prize despite Golgi's defence of the rival reticular theory. The term "neuron" was coined by Heinrich Wilhelm Waldeyer in 1891, and "synapse" by Charles Sherrington in 1897.
