Metaxas Line

The Metaxas Line was a chain of fortifications built along the Greek–Bulgarian border in the 1930s, named after Ioannis Metaxas, the Greek prime minister and effective dictator of the period. Constructed between 1936 and 1941 at great expense, the line comprised 21 fortress complexes of tunnels, bunkers, machine-gun and artillery positions carved into the mountains of eastern Macedonia and western Thrace, from Mount Belasica to near Komotini.

Design

Unlike the French Maginot Line, the Metaxas Line consisted largely of underground works connected by tunnels, with only observation and firing positions at the surface, making the forts highly resistant to bombardment. The defences were designed primarily against an attack by Bulgaria.

Battle of the Metaxas Line

During the German invasion of Greece in April 1941, German forces attacked the line in the Battle of Greece. Although several forts held out heroically and some never surrendered until the general armistice, German troops outflanked the line by advancing through Yugoslavia and taking Thessaloniki, forcing the Greek Army of Eastern Macedonia to capitulate on 9 April 1941. Several fortresses, such as Roupel and Istibey, repelled repeated assaults and were praised even by the attackers.

Legacy

Many of the forts survive as museums and memorials, including Fort Roupel and Fort Lisse.
