Chromatophore

Chromatophores are cells that produce colour, of which many types are pigmentary cells in cold-blooded animals and some microorganisms. In animals, they generate skin and eye colour through their pigments and, in some species, enable rapid colour change used for camouflage, signalling and thermoregulation.

Types

Chromatophores are classified by the colour of their pigment under white light: melanophores contain black or brown melanin; xanthophores contain yellow pigments; erythrophores contain reddish pigments; leucophores reflect and scatter white light; and iridophores contain reflective platelets that produce iridescent greens, blues and silvers through structural colouration. In cephalopods such as octopuses, squid and cuttlefish, chromatophores are complex organs: a pigment-filled sac is surrounded by radial muscles that expand or contract the sac under direct neural control, producing some of the fastest colour changes in the animal kingdom.

Colour change

Physiological colour change involves the movement of pigment within the cell or changes in cell shape, and may be controlled hormonally (as in many amphibians and fish) or neurally (as in cephalopods). Slower morphological colour change, involving altered pigment production, occurs over days to weeks. Fish and amphibians use these mechanisms to blend with their backgrounds, while chameleons use nanocrystals in iridophores to shift their reflected hue for display.

Research

Chromatophores are studied in developmental biology — zebrafish stripe patterns are a model system — and inspire soft robotics, adaptive camouflage and display technologies.
