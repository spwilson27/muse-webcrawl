Andrew Michael Spence (born 7 November 1943) is an American economist and recipient of the 2001 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences, which he shared with George Akerlof and Joseph Stiglitz "for their analyses of markets with asymmetric information".

Signalling theory

Spence's central contribution came in his 1973 paper "Job Market Signaling". Where employers cannot directly observe a worker's productivity before hiring, education can function as a signal: because obtaining credentials is less costly (in effort) for more able workers, employers rationally use education to screen applicants even if the education itself adds little to productivity. The insight — that informed parties can credibly convey information through costly actions — founded modern signalling theory, with applications from corporate finance (dividend signalling) to advertising and insurance. His book Market Signaling (1974) developed the theory in detail.

Career

A Rhodes Scholar at Oxford, Spence took his PhD at Harvard, where he taught and won the John Bates Clark Medal in 1981 for the most significant contribution by an economist under forty. He became dean of Harvard's Faculty of Arts and Sciences (1984–1990) and then dean of the Stanford Graduate School of Business (1990–1999), later teaching at New York University's Stern School of Business. He chaired the independent Commission on Growth and Development, whose 2008 Growth Report examined how developing countries sustain rapid growth, and wrote the popular book The Next Convergence (2011) on the global economy's shifting structure.

Other work

Spence has written widely on industrial organisation, monopolistic competition, the economics of developing countries, digital technology and the distributional effects of globalisation, and is a frequent commentator on global economic policy.
