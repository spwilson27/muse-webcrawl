Heat wave

A heat wave (or heatwave) is a prolonged period of excessively hot weather relative to a region's normal climate, often accompanied by high humidity. Definitions vary: the World Meteorological Organization suggests five or more consecutive days with maximum temperatures at least 5 °C above the average maximum, while national services use local thresholds tied to health impacts. Heat waves are among the deadliest weather hazards and are becoming more frequent, longer and more intense with climate change.

Causes

Heat waves usually form when a strong ridge of high pressure stalls over a region: sinking air warms adiabatically, skies clear, and soils dry out, which further amplifies heating. The pattern colloquially called a "heat dome" can persist for weeks when the jet stream locks it in place. Urban heat islands add several degrees in cities, and warm nights — which prevent the body from recovering — are a major driver of mortality.

Impacts

Excess deaths in major events number in the tens of thousands: the 2003 European heat wave is associated with roughly 70,000 excess deaths, and Russia's 2010 event with about 55,000, alongside wildfires and crop losses. The June 2021 Pacific Northwest heat dome set Canada's national record of 49.6 °C at Lytton, British Columbia, which burned in a wildfire days later. Heat waves strain electricity grids, buckle roads and rails, reduce labour productivity, warm rivers beyond ecological limits and worsen droughts and air pollution.

Response and adaptation

Forecast-based heat–health warning systems, cooling centres, urban greening and reflective surfaces reduce harm; France's post-2003 heat plan is credited with lowering mortality in later events. The elderly, outdoor workers, infants and people with chronic illness are most at risk. Scientists using attribution methods have found that many recent extreme heat events would have been virtually impossible without human-caused warming.
