Red River Rebellion

The Red River Rebellion (also called the Red River Resistance) was a sequence of events in 1869–1870 in the Red River Colony, in what is now Manitoba, Canada. The Métis, led by Louis Riel, resisted the transfer of Rupert's Land from the Hudson's Bay Company to the Dominion of Canada without consultation.

Course of events

When Canadian surveyors arrived in 1869, the Métis under Riel formed a provisional government, seized Upper Fort Garry, and drew up a List of Rights. Negotiations with Ottawa produced the Manitoba Act of 1870, creating the province of Manitoba with guarantees for Métis land, language, and denominational schools. However, the execution of Thomas Scott, an Orangeman prisoner, by Riel's government provoked outrage in Ontario, and a military expedition under Colonel Garnet Wolseley was dispatched; Riel fled to the United States before its arrival.

Legacy

The resistance secured Manitoba's entry into Confederation on terms protecting French-language and Catholic rights, but broken land promises drove many Métis westward, setting the stage for the North-West Rebellion of 1885. Riel is now widely commemorated in Manitoba, where a February holiday bears his name.
