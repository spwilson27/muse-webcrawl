Washington's Farewell Address

Washington's Farewell Address is a letter written by President George Washington as he retired from public life at the end of his second term in 1796. Published on 19 September 1796 in the Philadelphia newspaper Claypoole's American Daily Advertiser, it was never delivered as a speech. Drafted with substantial assistance from Alexander Hamilton, building on an earlier draft by James Madison from 1792, the address announced Washington's decision not to seek a third term and offered his valedictory advice to the nation.

Contents

Washington urged Americans to prize national unity above regional and partisan loyalties, warned against the "baneful effects of the spirit of party", and counselled vigilance against sectionalism. In foreign policy he advised neutrality and commercial engagement with all nations while avoiding "permanent alliances" and excessive partiality or enmity toward any foreign power — counsel often summarised, though not in his words, as avoiding "entangling alliances". He also stressed public credit, moderation in taxation, religion and morality as supports of political prosperity, and respect for the Constitution.

Influence

The address shaped American foreign policy for more than a century and underpinned the precedent of the two-term presidency, later codified in the Twenty-second Amendment. Since 1862 the United States Senate has read the address aloud each year around Washington's Birthday, a tradition that continues today.
