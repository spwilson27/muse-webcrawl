Phylodynamics

Phylodynamics is the study of how epidemiological, immunological, and evolutionary processes together shape the phylogenies (genealogies) of pathogens such as viruses. The term was introduced in 2004 by Bryan Grenfell and colleagues. Because RNA viruses evolve rapidly, their genetic sequences accumulate changes on the same timescale as their spread, letting researchers reconstruct transmission history from sequence data.

Methods and applications

Phylodynamic methods use coalescent theory and birth–death models fitted to time-stamped phylogenies to estimate quantities such as the basic reproduction number, epidemic growth rates, the date of a pathogen's origin, and the effects of selection and population structure. Applications include tracing the origins and spread of HIV, influenza, Ebola, dengue, and SARS-CoV-2, and informing vaccine strain selection.
