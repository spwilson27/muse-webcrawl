Beach nourishment

Beach nourishment, also called beach replenishment or beach renourishment, is a coastal management technique in which sand or sediment is added to an eroding beach to widen it and restore its protective and recreational functions. It is the principal "soft engineering" alternative to hard structures such as seawalls and groynes, and is practised on eroding coastlines worldwide, from Florida and the Netherlands to Australia's Gold Coast.

A dredger rainbow-casting sand onto a beach.

Method

Sand is usually dredged from offshore deposits or navigation channels and pumped ashore as a slurry through pipelines, then spread by bulldozers to a designed profile; alternatively it may be dumped close offshore (nearshore nourishment) or trucked in. Because waves quickly redistribute the added material to an equilibrium profile, projects typically place considerably more sand than the visible dry-beach gain. Nourishment is not permanent: beaches continue to erode, and projects must be repeated at intervals of a few years to a decade or more, depending on local wave energy and storm frequency.

Purposes and benefits

A wider beach dissipates wave energy, protecting dunes, property and infrastructure behind it from storm damage, floods and erosion, and preserves the amenity and tourism value of the shoreline. Unlike hard engineering, nourishment adds sediment to the coastal system rather than merely shifting erosion to neighbouring shores, and it retains a natural-looking beach.

Criticisms and environmental effects

Costs are high and recurring, and rise with the scarcity of suitable offshore sand. Dredging and placement can disturb marine habitats and bury intertidal organisms; imported sand that differs in grain size or colour may perform poorly or alter beach ecology, affecting nesting sea turtles and shorebirds. Critics also argue that nourishment encourages continued development in hazardous coastal zones. Against this, managers point to the very large storm-protection and tourism benefits, and to techniques such as large-scale "mega-nourishments" — for example the Dutch Sand Motor, a single huge deposit designed to feed adjacent beaches naturally over decades.
