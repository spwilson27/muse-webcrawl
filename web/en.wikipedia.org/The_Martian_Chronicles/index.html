The Martian Chronicles is a 1950 science fiction book by American writer Ray Bradbury. Structured as a "fix-up" of interconnected short stories framed by brief vignettes, it chronicles humanity's colonization of Mars, the conflict with the native Martians, and the eventual abandonment of the planet as Earth is consumed by nuclear war.

Contents

The book opens with "Rocket Summer" and early doomed expeditions whose crews encounter telepathic Martians; in "The Earth Men" and "The Third Expedition" Martians destroy human crews using illusions drawn from their memories. Disease carried by later expeditions wipes out most Martians, after which settlers remake Mars in the image of small-town America. Celebrated stories include "—And the Moon Be Still as Bright," in which a captain defends dead Martian culture against his own crew; "The Martian," a tale of grief and shape-shifting; and "There Will Come Soft Rains," depicting an automated house persisting after humanity's extinction (later moved into some editions). The chronicle closes with "The Million-Year Picnic," in which a family flees a ruined Earth and becomes the new Martians.

Themes

Bradbury used Mars to examine American frontier myths, colonialism, racism, censorship, and nuclear fear; the melancholy, lyrical tone reflects his Midwestern upbringing and unease with technological progress. Though its Mars of canals and breathable air was already scientifically dated at publication, the book is considered a classic of the genre.

Adaptations

The work has been adapted as a 1980 television miniseries starring Rock Hudson, radio dramas, stage plays, and a BBC Radio 4 production. Later editions shifted the story dates forward by 31 years to preserve their futurity.
