C4 carbon fixation

C4 carbon fixation
Kranz anatomy: large bundle-sheath cells around veins in maize leaf
ProcessPhotosynthesis
Key enzymePEP carboxylase; RuBisCO (in bundle sheath)
ExamplesMaize, sugarcane, sorghum, many tropical grasses

C4 carbon fixation or the Hatch–Slack pathway is one of three known photosynthetic processes of carbon fixation in plants. It owes its name to the fact that the first product of carbon fixation is a four-carbon compound (oxaloacetate), as opposed to the three-carbon product of the more common C3 pathway. C4 fixation is an elaboration of C3 that spatially separates the initial CO2 capture from the Calvin cycle, thereby concentrating CO2 around the enzyme RuBisCO and suppressing wasteful photorespiration.

Mechanism

In mesophyll cells, the enzyme PEP carboxylase — which has no oxygenase activity — fixes bicarbonate into oxaloacetate, converted to malate or aspartate. This four-carbon acid is transported to bundle-sheath cells surrounding the leaf veins, where it is decarboxylated, releasing CO2 at concentrations up to ten times atmospheric. The Calvin cycle then proceeds in the bundle sheath, bathed in concentrated CO2. This leaf structure is called Kranz anatomy.

Ecology and agriculture

The pathway evolved independently more than 60 times, mostly in grasses, and flourishes in hot, dry, high-light environments. Although C4 species are only about 3% of plant species, they account for roughly a quarter of terrestrial primary productivity. Maize, sugarcane and sorghum are among the world's most important crops; engineering C4 traits into rice is a major goal of crop science.
