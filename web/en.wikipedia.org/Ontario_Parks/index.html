Ontario Parks

Ontario Parks

Agency: Government of Ontario

Overseen by: Ministry of the Environment, Conservation and Parks

System: 340+ provincial parks and 295 conservation reserves

Area protected: over 8 million hectares (about 9% of Ontario)

First park: Algonquin Provincial Park (1893)

Ontario Parks is the branch of the Government of Ontario responsible for the province's system of provincial parks and conservation reserves. It administers more than 340 provincial parks and 295 conservation reserves, covering over 8 million hectares — roughly nine per cent of Ontario's land area and an area larger than the provinces of Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island combined. The system ranges from small day-use sites to vast wilderness parks in the far north, including Polar Bear Provincial Park, Ontario's largest.

History

The system began with Algonquin Provincial Park in 1893, established to protect the headwaters of several rivers and the region's wildlife and forests. Other early parks included Rondeau (1894) and Quetico. Through the twentieth century the system expanded steadily, and the Provincial Parks and Conservation Reserves Act of 2006 gave ecological integrity first priority in park management. Ontario Parks operates as a partly self-funded agency: day-use, camping, and reservation fees fund much of its operation.

Park classes

Provincial parks are classified by primary purpose:

  • Wilderness parks — large, remote areas with minimal facilities.
  • Natural environment parks — protect representative landscapes with recreation opportunities.
  • Recreation parks — oriented to intensive day use and camping, often near cities.
  • Waterway parks — protect river corridors such as the French River.
  • Historical parks — protect cultural heritage sites.
  • Nature reserve parks — protect significant earth or life science features with restricted access.

Use and significance

Popular parks such as Algonquin, Killarney, Pinery, Sandbanks, Bon Echo, and Bruce Peninsula draw millions of visitors a year for camping, canoeing, hiking, swimming, and wildlife viewing. The parks also protect habitat for species at risk, old-growth forest, and representative samples of Ontario's ecosystems, and hold cultural significance for Indigenous peoples whose territories they encompass.
