Image dissector

The image dissector is an early all-electronic television camera tube invented by American inventor Philo T. Farnsworth. Demonstrated in 1927, it was used in the first fully electronic television transmission and formed the core of Farnsworth's television system, a rival to the iconoscope developed by Vladimir Zworykin at RCA.

Operation

Light from a scene is focused onto a photosensitive plate (photocathode), which emits electrons in proportion to the brightness at each point, forming an "electron image". Magnetic deflection coils sweep this electron image across a small aperture; the electrons passing through the aperture at any instant generate a current proportional to the brightness of a single picture element, producing the video signal line by line.

Limitations and legacy

Because the dissector used only the electrons from one picture element at a time and did not store charge, it was very insensitive and required extremely bright lighting, a weakness that led to its displacement by charge-storage tubes such as the iconoscope and later the image orthicon. Image dissectors nonetheless found a second career in scientific instruments, including star trackers on satellites and early weather satellite cameras.
