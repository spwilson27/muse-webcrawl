Far-sightedness

Other namesHyperopia, hypermetropia, long-sightedness
SpecialtyOptometry, ophthalmology
SymptomsNear blur, eyestrain, headaches
TreatmentConvex lenses, contact lenses, refractive surgery

Far-sightedness, also known as long-sightedness, hypermetropia or hyperopia, is a condition of the eye where distant objects are seen clearly but near objects appear blurred. This blur is due to incoming light being focused behind, instead of on, the retina because the eyeball is too short or the lens cannot become sufficiently round.

Causes

Hyperopia commonly results from an eyeball that is too short from front to back, or from a cornea or lens with insufficient focusing power. It is largely hereditary. Young people can often compensate by accommodation, changing the shape of the lens to focus near objects, but this effort may cause eyestrain, headaches, and fatigue. With age, as the lens stiffens, the ability to compensate declines.

Diagnosis and treatment

The condition is diagnosed by an eye examination, often with cycloplegic refraction in children. It is corrected with convex (plus) lenses in glasses or contact lenses, which shift the focal point forward onto the retina. Refractive surgery such as LASIK can also correct moderate hyperopia. Significant uncorrected hyperopia in children can lead to strabismus and amblyopia ("lazy eye").
