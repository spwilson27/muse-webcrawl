Ancient Egyptian architecture

Ancient Egyptian architecture encompasses the building traditions of Egypt from the Predynastic period through Roman rule, a span of some three thousand years. Working in mudbrick for domestic buildings and stone for temples and tombs, Egyptian builders produced some of the most monumental and enduring structures of the ancient world, including the pyramids of Giza and the temple complexes of Karnak and Luxor.

Characteristics

Egyptian architecture is marked by massive sloping walls (batter), pylon gateways flanked by obelisks and statues, hypostyle halls with closely spaced columns, and forms derived from plant motifs such as papyrus and lotus. Decoration combined relief carving, painting, and hieroglyphic inscription in a unified theological program. Because timber was scarce, roof spans were limited, and stone lintels dictated the dense column spacing of temples.

Major monuments

The Old Kingdom saw the evolution from mastaba tombs to Djoser's Step Pyramid and the true pyramids of Khufu, Khafre, and Menkaure. New Kingdom temples added huge pylons and courts over centuries of royal patronage, while rock-cut tombs in the Valley of the Kings and the temples of Abu Simbel show the tradition's range. Domestic architecture, built of sun-dried mudbrick, survives mainly at sites such as Deir el-Medina and Amarna.
