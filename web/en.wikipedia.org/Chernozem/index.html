Chernozem

Chernozem (from Russian: чернозём, chernozyom, meaning "black soil") is a black-colored soil containing a high percentage of humus (4% to 16%) and high percentages of phosphoric acid, phosphorus, and ammonia. Chernozem is very fertile soil and can produce high agricultural yields with its high moisture storage capacity. Chernozems are a Reference Soil Group of the World Reference Base for Soil Resources (WRB).

Distribution

There are two great "chernozem belts" in the world: the Eurasian steppe extending from eastern Croatia along the Danube through Ukraine and southern Russia into Siberia, and the Canadian Prairies and Great Plains of North America, where equivalent soils are classified as Chernozems and Mollisols. Ukraine alone holds a large share of the world's chernozem, and these soils underpin the region's reputation as a "breadbasket," supporting major production of wheat, sunflower, maize, and sugar beet.

Formation and properties

Chernozems form under grassland steppe climates with cold winters and warm summers, where abundant grass roots contribute organic matter deep into the soil profile, and burrowing animals mix the soil. The characteristic profile has a thick, dark A horizon rich in humus overlying a horizon of calcium carbonate accumulation. The scientist Vasily Dokuchaev, founder of modern soil science, studied chernozems in his 1883 work Russian Chernozem and recognized soil as an independent natural body formed by climate, organisms, relief, parent material, and time.

Significance and threats

Because of their fertility and carbon content, chernozems are among the most valuable agricultural soils on Earth and significant stores of soil carbon. They are threatened by erosion, loss of organic matter under intensive cultivation, compaction, and in Ukraine by the effects of war. Ukrainian law has historically restricted the sale of agricultural land partly to protect this national resource; a land market opened in 2021.
