Thermochemistry

Thermochemistry is the study of the heat energy associated with chemical reactions and physical transformations. A reaction may release heat (an exothermic process, with negative enthalpy change, ΔH < 0) or absorb heat (an endothermic process, ΔH > 0). Thermochemistry rests on the first law of thermodynamics — the conservation of energy — and treats heat, work and internal energy changes in chemical systems.

Enthalpy and calorimetry

The heat exchanged at constant pressure equals the change in enthalpy, ΔH. Enthalpy changes are measured with calorimeters: a coffee-cup calorimeter operates at constant pressure, while a bomb calorimeter operates at constant volume and measures the change in internal energy, ΔU. Standard enthalpies of formation, combustion and reaction are tabulated for use in prediction.

Hess's law

Because enthalpy is a state function, the total enthalpy change of a reaction is independent of the path taken. Hess's law (1840) allows the enthalpy of a reaction to be computed from the enthalpies of intermediate steps, and underlies calculations using standard enthalpies of formation: ΔH°reaction = ΣΔH°f(products) − ΣΔH°f(reactants).

Historical development

The field grew out of the calorimetric work of Antoine Lavoisier and Pierre-Simon Laplace in the 1780s, and was formalised in the nineteenth century by Germain Hess, Marcellin Berthelot and Julius Thomsen. Together with entropy and Gibbs free energy, thermochemical quantities determine reaction spontaneity and equilibrium.
