A hussar (from Hungarian huszár) was a member of a class of light cavalry that originated in Hungary in the 15th century. Hussars became famous for their flamboyant uniforms—braided dolman jackets, pelisses slung over the shoulder, busby hats, and curved sabres—and for their daring in reconnaissance, raiding, and pursuit. By the 18th and 19th centuries nearly every European army fielded hussar regiments modelled on the Hungarian original.

Origins

The earliest hussars were Serbian and Hungarian horsemen who served as mercenary scouts and raiders in the wars against the Ottoman Empire under King Matthias Corvinus of Hungary. The name is often derived from husz, Hungarian for "twenty," referring to the levy by which landowners furnished one horseman for every twenty households. Originally lightly equipped raiders, Hungarian hussars evolved into a regular arm of the Habsburg military.

Polish winged hussars

In the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth the hussars (husaria) developed into elite heavy cavalry, famous for the wooden frames of eagle feathers—"wings"—worn on their backs, which produced a terrifying noise in the charge. Armed with long lances, sabres, and pistols, the winged hussars won celebrated victories at Kircholm (1605) and Vienna (1683), where they took part in the great charge that relieved the city.

Hussars in other armies

Frederick the Great greatly expanded Prussia's hussar regiments, using them for scouting and harassment in the Silesian Wars. France, Britain, Russia, Austria, and Sweden all maintained hussar regiments whose ornate uniforms set the standard for military tailoring. Hussars fought throughout the Napoleonic Wars and the 19th century; several regiments were converted to other roles during the First World War, though the name survives in armoured and ceremonial units today, including the British King's Royal Hussars and the Canadian and Danish hussar regiments.
