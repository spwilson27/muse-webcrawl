Italian school of algebraic geometry

In the history of mathematics, the Italian school of algebraic geometry refers to the loose group of mathematicians, active from the later nineteenth century through the first half of the twentieth, who developed the birational geometry of algebraic surfaces and higher varieties by broadly geometrical and intuitive methods. Its leading figures included Guido Castelnuovo, Federigo Enriques, Francesco Severi, and earlier Luigi Cremona and Corrado Segre.

Work

Building on Riemann's ideas and on Clebsch's and Noether's work on plane curves, the school classified algebraic surfaces: Castelnuovo and Enriques organised surfaces by their numerical invariants (geometric genus, arithmetic genus, plurigenera), clarified the theory of linear systems and irregular surfaces, and Severi systematised correspondences and divisors on surfaces. The school's output was immense — Severi alone wrote hundreds of papers — and it created much of the vocabulary and intuition of the subject.

Rigour problems and aftermath

The school's methods were notably informal: arguments appealing to "continuity" or generic position sometimes concealed counterexamples, and some celebrated results later proved wrong or in need of repair. From the 1930s, Oscar Zariski, André Weil and others rebuilt algebraic geometry on commutative algebra and later on schemes, placing the Italian results on firm foundations (and correcting them where necessary). Modern assessments credit the school with visionary geometric insight that the rigorous reconstruction largely vindicated for surfaces, even as some higher-dimensional claims failed.
