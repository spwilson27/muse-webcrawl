Federal holidays in the United States

Federal holidays in the United States are public holidays established by the United States Congress under 5 U.S.C. § 6103. On these days, non-essential federal government offices are closed, and federal employees are paid for the day off. There are currently eleven federal holidays. Private employers are not required to observe them, though most give employees several of them off, and many states recognize the same days as state holidays.

History

The first federal holidays were created in 1870 for federal employees in the District of Columbia, covering New Year's Day, Independence Day, Thanksgiving, and Christmas; the list was extended nationwide and expanded over the following decades. The Uniform Monday Holiday Act of 1968 moved several holidays to Mondays to create three-day weekends. The Martin Luther King Jr. holiday was signed into law in 1983 and first observed as a federal holiday in 1986, though all states did not recognize it until 2000. Juneteenth, commemorating the end of slavery in the United States, became the newest federal holiday in 2021.

Observance

When a fixed-date holiday falls on a weekend, it is typically observed on the nearest weekday: Friday for a Saturday holiday and Monday for a Sunday holiday. Federal holidays also shape school calendars, financial market closures, and the shutdown of mail delivery by the United States Postal Service.
