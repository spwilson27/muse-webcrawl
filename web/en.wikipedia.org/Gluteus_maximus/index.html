Gluteus maximus

The gluteus maximus is the main extensor muscle of the hip and the largest and most superficial of the three gluteal muscles, making up a large part of the shape and appearance of the buttocks. It is a thick, fleshy, quadrilateral muscle whose size in humans is linked to the demands of maintaining an erect posture.

Gluteus maximus
Origin: Ilium, sacrum, coccyx, sacrotuberous ligament
Insertion: Iliotibial tract and femur (gluteal tuberosity)
Nerve: Inferior gluteal nerve (L5–S2)
Action: Extension and lateral rotation of the hip

Structure

The muscle arises from the posterior ilium behind the posterior gluteal line, the dorsal surface of the sacrum and coccyx, and the sacrotuberous ligament. Its fibres run downward and laterally; the larger upper portion inserts into the iliotibial tract of the fascia lata, while the deeper lower portion attaches to the gluteal tuberosity of the femur. It is supplied by the inferior gluteal nerve and the superior and inferior gluteal arteries.

Function

The gluteus maximus extends the flexed thigh — as in rising from a chair, climbing stairs and running — and assists in lateral rotation and abduction or adduction of the hip depending on which fibres contract. Through its insertion into the iliotibial tract it also helps stabilise the knee and the pelvis during walking. It is relatively inactive during quiet standing and slow walking, coming strongly into play with forceful exertion; its enlargement in humans compared with other primates is associated with the mechanics of bipedal running and trunk stabilisation.

Clinical significance

Weakness of the gluteus maximus produces a characteristic lurching gait. Intramuscular injections into the buttock are given in the upper outer quadrant to avoid the sciatic nerve, which passes deep to the muscle. The muscle is a common focus of strength training exercises such as squats, deadlifts and hip thrusts.
