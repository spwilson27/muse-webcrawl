Pyrophoricity

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pyrophoricity
A pyrophoric substance ignites on contact with air.
DefinitionSpontaneous ignition in air at or below 54 °C (130 °F)
ExamplesFinely divided iron, uranium, plutonium, white phosphorus, many organometallic compounds
Hazard classGHS pyrophoric liquids/solids, Category 1

A substance is pyrophoric (from Greek πῦρ pyr, "fire", and φορός phorós, "bearing") if it ignites spontaneously in air at or below 54 °C (129 °F). Pyrophoricity arises when a material's surface area relative to its volume is large and its oxidation is highly exothermic: finely divided metals, metal powders and certain reactive compounds oxidise so rapidly in air that the heat released ignites the material.

Mechanism and examples

Bulk iron rusts slowly, but finely divided iron powder can glow and ignite spontaneously. Pyrophoric materials include many transition-metal and actinide powders (iron, cobalt, uranium, plutonium), alkali metals in divided form, white phosphorus, metal hydrides, and numerous organometallic reagents used in chemistry, such as tert-butyllithium and Grignard reagents. Raney nickel catalyst is pyrophoric when dry. Lead and its compounds in certain finely divided forms generated from organolead manufacture are notorious industrial hazards. The sparking of lighter flints exploits the pyrophoricity of cerium alloys (ferrocerium), which ignite when scratched.

Safety and regulation

Pyrophoric materials must be handled under inert atmospheres (nitrogen or argon), stored under liquid or sealed, and quenched with dry sand or Class D extinguishers rather than water. They are regulated in transport and laboratory safety codes (GHS hazard statements H250, "catches fire spontaneously if exposed to air"). Fires involving pyrophoric nuclear materials, notably plutonium, are a particular concern in weapons and fuel facilities.
