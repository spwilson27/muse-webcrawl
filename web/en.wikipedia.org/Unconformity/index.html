An unconformity is a buried erosional or non-depositional surface separating two rock masses or strata of different ages, indicating that sediment deposition was not continuous. In general, the older layer was exposed to erosion for an interval of time before deposition of the younger, but the term is used to describe any break in the geologic sedimentary record. Unconformities are a major basis for recognizing gaps — sometimes of hundreds of millions of years — in the rock record.

Types

  • Angular unconformity — younger sediments rest upon the eroded surface of tilted or folded older rocks, so the two units meet at an angle.
  • Disconformity — a break between parallel layers of sedimentary rock, representing erosion or non-deposition, often hard to recognize.
  • Nonconformity — sedimentary rock rests on eroded igneous or metamorphic basement rock.
  • Paraconformity — strata are parallel with no obvious erosional surface; the gap is detected only by fossil or dating evidence.

History of recognition

James Hutton famously recognized unconformities at Jedburgh (1787) and Siccar Point (1788) in Scotland, where near-vertical Silurian greywacke is capped by gently dipping Devonian Old Red Sandstone. The immense time gap implied became key evidence for Hutton's theory of uniformitarianism and the great age of the Earth. John Playfair, who accompanied Hutton, wrote that "the mind seemed to grow giddy by looking so far into the abyss of time."

Significance

Regional unconformities bound stratigraphic sequences and record episodes of uplift, sea-level fall, and erosion. The Great Unconformity, visible in the Grand Canyon where Cambrian strata rest on much older Proterozoic basement, represents a gap of over a billion years in places and remains an active subject of research.
