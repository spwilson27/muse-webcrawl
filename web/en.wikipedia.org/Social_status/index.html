Social status

Social status
FieldSociology, anthropology, social psychology
Key theoristsMax Weber, Pierre Bourdieu, Ralph Linton
TypesAscribed status, achieved status, master status
Related conceptsPrestige, social class, status symbols, status inconsistency

Social status is the relative position or standing of an individual or group within a social hierarchy, based on honour, prestige, or esteem rather than purely on wealth or power. Max Weber distinguished status (the social estimation of honour) from class (market position) and party (political power), treating them as three separate dimensions of social stratification. Status is expressed through lifestyles, consumption, occupation, forms of address, and deference rituals.

Ascribed and achieved status

Anthropologist Ralph Linton distinguished ascribed status, assigned at birth or involuntarily later in life—such as sex, ethnicity, caste, or being a parent—from achieved status, earned through effort, such as a profession or educational credential. A master status is a status that dominates a person's social identity in the eyes of others, which may be an achievement (a famous surgeon) or an ascription. When a person's statuses rank inconsistently—for example, high prestige but low income—sociologists speak of status inconsistency, which some research links to political attitudes.

Status symbols and groups

Status is signalled by status symbols: clothing, accents, titles, cars, and other markers that Thorstein Veblen analysed under the rubric of conspicuous consumption. Weber noted that status groups practise social closure, restricting access through endogamy, credentialing, and exclusivity. Pierre Bourdieu reframed many status processes in terms of cultural and symbolic capital—tastes and dispositions that function as markers of distinction.

Status and health

Epidemiological research, notably Michael Marmot's Whitehall studies of British civil servants, found a "status syndrome": even among employed people above poverty, lower occupational status predicts worse health and shorter life, partly through differences in control and social participation. Evolutionary psychologists separately study status-seeking as a fundamental human motivation, distinguishing dominance-based and prestige-based routes to rank.
