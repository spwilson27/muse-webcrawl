Half-life

Symbolt1/2
Related quantityDecay constant λ; mean lifetime τ
Relationt1/2 = ln 2 / λ

Half-life (symbol t1/2) is the time required for a quantity to reduce to half of its initial value. The term is commonly used in nuclear physics to describe how quickly unstable atoms undergo radioactive decay, and more generally for any process following exponential decay — including the elimination of drugs from the body (biological half-life) in pharmacology.

Probabilistic nature

For radioactive decay, half-life describes a statistical behaviour: each nucleus has a fixed probability of decaying per unit time, so a large sample halves over one half-life, but the decay of an individual atom is unpredictable. Half-lives range from fractions of a second for highly unstable isotopes to billions of years — uranium-238 has a half-life of about 4.47 billion years, while carbon-14's half-life of about 5,730 years underpins radiocarbon dating.

Formula

If N0 is the initial quantity, the amount remaining after time t is N(t) = N0 · (1/2)t/t1/2. After each successive half-life, the quantity falls to 1/2, 1/4, 1/8, and so on of the original amount.

History

Ernest Rutherford coined the term "half-life period" in 1907, having shown that the rate of decay of a radioactive substance is constant and characteristic of the element.
