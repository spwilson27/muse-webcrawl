Yorkshire Wolds

The rolling chalk hills of the Yorkshire Wolds

The Yorkshire Wolds are hills in the counties of the East Riding of Yorkshire and North Yorkshire in Northern England. The name also refers to the region. A district of the East Riding, and part of the Yorkshire Wolds, was designated a National Character Area. On 19 December 2024, Natural England announced plans for the Yorkshire Wolds to be designated as an Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty (AONB), part of a process expected to complete in the following years.

They are the northernmost chalk hills in England, forming an arc from west of Hull, curving north and then east towards Flamborough Head. The highest point is Bishop Wilton Wold (Garrowby Hill) at 246 metres (807 ft). The Wolds are cut by numerous steep-sided dry valleys, formed by glacial meltwater, which give the landscape its distinctive rolling character.

Geology and landscape

The Wolds are formed from Cretaceous chalk laid down under a shallow sea. The gentle dip of the strata produces a prominent west-facing scarp slope overlooking the Vale of York and a gentler dip slope descending towards Holderness. The chalk supports thin, well-drained soils suited to arable farming, and large regular fields enclosed in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries dominate the plateau.

History

The area has been settled since prehistoric times and is exceptionally rich in archaeology, including the Neolithic and Bronze Age monuments of Rudston — home to the tallest standing stone in Britain — and the Iron Age burials of the Arras culture. Deserted medieval villages, such as Wharram Percy, are scattered across the Wolds. The landscape was famously painted by the artist David Hockney, who depicted its lanes, fields, and trees in a celebrated series of works in the 2000s.

Settlements

Market towns serving the Wolds include Driffield, often called the capital of the Wolds, together with Pocklington, Market Weighton, and Malton on the northern edge.
