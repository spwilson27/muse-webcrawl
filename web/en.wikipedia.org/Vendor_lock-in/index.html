Vendor lock-in

In economics, vendor lock-in, also known as proprietary lock-in or customer lock-in, makes a customer dependent on a vendor for products, services or technology, unable to use another vendor without substantial switching costs. Switching costs can arise from proprietary file formats and protocols, incompatible hardware accessories, contractual terms, data that cannot easily be exported, and the cost of retraining staff.

Mechanisms and examples

Classic examples include printer manufacturers tying customers to proprietary ink cartridges, software ecosystems built on closed formats and APIs, games consoles, and cloud computing platforms whose services and data-egress fees discourage migration. Lock-in can be a deliberate business strategy: an installed base generates recurring revenue and weakens competition, and it is related to network effects and the razor-and-blades model.

Avoidance

Buyers counter lock-in by adopting open standards and open file formats, requiring data portability, using multi-vendor strategies, and favouring open-source software. Regulators have also intervened, for example through interoperability and data-portability requirements in competition and digital-markets law.
