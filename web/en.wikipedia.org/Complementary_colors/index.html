Complementary colors

ConceptPairs of colors opposite on the color wheel
RYB pairsRed–green, yellow–purple, blue–orange
RGB pairsRed–cyan, green–magenta, blue–yellow
Effect when mixedNeutral gray/brown (pigments); white light (additive)

Complementary colors are pairs of colors which, when combined or mixed, cancel each other out — producing a grayscale result such as white or black in light, or a neutral dark tone in pigment. When placed side by side, complementary pairs create the strongest contrast for those colors and appear more vivid. In most color models, complementary colors lie directly opposite each other on the color wheel.

Color models

The traditional artists' (RYB) model, based on red, yellow, and blue primaries, gives the complements red–green, yellow–violet (purple), and blue–orange — pairs long used in painting and taught in art education. In the additive RGB model used for light and displays, the complements are red–cyan, green–magenta, and blue–yellow, and mixing a color with its complement yields white. In opponent-process theory, the visual system itself is organized around opposing pairs (red/green and blue/yellow), which explains phenomena such as colored afterimages: staring at a color and then looking at white produces a ghost of its complement.

Use in art and design

Painters exploited complementary contrast from the nineteenth century onward; Chevreul's law of simultaneous contrast (1839) systematized how adjacent complements intensify each other. Van Gogh's juxtaposition of blue and orange and the pointillists' optical mixtures are famous examples. Complementary (and near-complementary "split-complementary") schemes remain staples of graphic design, branding, cinematography — the popular "teal and orange" palette — and sports uniforms, where opposite hues maximize distinguishability.

Shadows and perception

Because of the way color vision works, shadows cast in colored light often appear tinted with the complement of the light source — a bluish cast in shadows under warm sunlight, famously observed by Goethe and used by the Impressionists.
