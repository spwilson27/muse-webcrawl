The Kushan Empire was a syncretic empire formed by the Yuezhi people in Central Asia and northern India, flourishing from the first to the fourth century CE. At its height under the emperor Kanishka the Great (reigned c. 127–150 CE), it stretched from Tajikistan and the Oxus valley across Afghanistan and northern India to the Ganges plain, controlling key sections of the Silk Road trade between China, India, and the Roman world.

Origins

The Yuezhi, a confederation of nomadic peoples displaced westward from the borders of China, settled in Bactria in the second century BCE. Kujula Kadphises united the Yuezhi principalities and founded the empire around 30 CE, expanding into the Kabul valley and Gandhara. His successors Vima Takto and Vima Kadphises extended Kushan power into northern India.

Kanishka and the empire's height

Kanishka, the empire's most famous ruler, made Purushapura (modern Peshawar) his capital and Mathura a second imperial centre. A major patron of Mahayana Buddhism, he is traditionally said to have convened a Buddhist council in Kashmir. Under Kanishka the empire's coinage — including abundant gold coins — displayed a remarkable pantheon of Greek, Iranian, Hindu, and Buddhist deities, reflecting its position at a crossroads of cultures. Kushan-era art of Gandhara famously fused Hellenistic technique with Buddhist subject matter, and Kushan patronage helped transmit Buddhism along the Silk Road to China.

Decline

From the mid-third century the empire contracted under pressure from the rising Sasanian Empire in the west and the Gupta Empire in India, fragmenting into smaller Kushan principalities that survived into the fourth and fifth centuries.
