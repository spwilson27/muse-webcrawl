Kopet Dag

The Kopet Dag foothills near Ashgabat, Turkmenistan.

The Kopet Dag (Turkmen: Köpetdag; Persian: کپه‌داغ, also Kopet Dagh) is a mountain range on the border between Iran and Turkmenistan, running for about 650 kilometres (400 mi) from near the Caspian Sea in the northwest to the Hari River in the southeast, where it continues into Afghanistan. Its name derives from Persian and Turkic words for "many hills". The highest peak entirely in Iran is Mount Quchan at about 3,191 metres; the highest summit in Turkmenistan, Mount Rizeh, rises to 2,940 metres southwest of Ashgabat.

Geology and landscape

The range formed during the Alpine orogeny and consists mainly of folded Mesozoic and Cenozoic sedimentary rocks — limestones, sandstones and marls — cut by fault lines that make the region strongly seismic: the devastating 1948 Ashgabat earthquake occurred in its foothills. The northern slopes facing Turkmenistan are steep and dissected by deep gorges, while the Iranian side descends more gently to the plateau. Juniper woodland, pistachio scrub and mountain steppe clothe the slopes, and the range is a recognised centre of plant endemism.

Human history

The foothills of the Kopet Dag were among the world's earliest farming landscapes: the Neolithic site of Jeitun, occupied from about 6000 BCE, and the Bronze Age cities of Namazga-Tepe and Altyn-Depe testify to early agriculture based on runoff from the mountains. The Parthian capital of Nisa stood in the foothills near modern Ashgabat, which today dominates the Turkmen side of the range; the Kopet Dag also shelters the Nokhurli people of Nokhur and the Baharden underground lake at Kow Ata.
