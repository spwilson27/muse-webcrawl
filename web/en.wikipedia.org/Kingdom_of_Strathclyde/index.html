Kingdom of Strathclyde

Dumbarton Rock, the stronghold known as Alt Clut, early capital of the kingdom.

The Kingdom of Strathclyde (Welsh: Ystrad Clud; Latin: Cumbria) was an early medieval Brittonic kingdom centred on the valley of the River Clyde in what is now south-western Scotland and north-western England. It emerged after the end of Roman rule in Britain, around the 5th century, and survived, with fluctuating borders, until it was absorbed into the Kingdom of Scotland in the 11th century.

History

The kingdom's first royal seat was the fortress of Alt Clut — "the Rock of the Clyde", modern Dumbarton — one of the most powerful strongholds of the Hen Ogledd ("Old North"), the Brittonic-speaking lands of northern Britain. Its kings claimed descent from the legendary figure Ceretic Guletic, a contemporary of Saint Patrick, who is said to have written to a ruler of Alt Clut rebuking him for slave-raiding. In 642 King Eugein I won the Battle of Strathcarron against Dál Riata, and his son's line produced many of the kingdom's rulers.

In 870 the Viking leaders Amlaíb Conung and Ívarr besieged and captured Alt Clut after a four-month blockade, an event recorded in Irish annals as the destruction of the fortress. Thereafter the kingdom's centre shifted upriver to Govan and Partick, and it became known by the name Strathclyde, "the valley of the Clyde".

Decline and absorption

During the 10th century Strathclyde expanded southwards into modern Cumbria. It was repeatedly drawn into the politics of the rising Kingdom of Alba: after the Battle of Brunanburh (937), in which its king Owain fought alongside the Scots against Æthelstan of England, the kingdom was progressively subordinated to Scottish overlordship. The last known king, Owain ap Dyfnwal, died at the Battle of Clontarf in 1014 according to some traditions; by 1093 the former kingdom had been fully incorporated into Scotland, its territory later forming the sheriffdoms of Lanark, Dumfries and the Lennox. The kingdom's language, Cumbric, a Brittonic tongue closely related to Welsh, died out by about the 12th century.
