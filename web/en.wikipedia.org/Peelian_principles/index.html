Peelian principles

The Peelian principles are a set of nine principles of law enforcement associated with Sir Robert Peel, whose name is linked to the founding of London's Metropolitan Police in 1829. They describe an ethical philosophy of policing by consent, in which the police derive their authority from public approval rather than force.

The principles

In the traditional formulation, the principles hold that: the basic mission of the police is to prevent crime and disorder; their ability to perform their duties depends on public approval; they must secure the willing cooperation of the public; the degree of cooperation diminishes in proportion to the necessity of physical force; they should seek public favour not by pandering but by demonstrating impartial service to the law; physical force should be used only when persuasion and warning have failed, and then only the minimum necessary; the police should maintain a relationship with the public that realises the idea that the police are the public and the public are the police; they should direct their action strictly toward their functions and refrain from usurping the powers of the judiciary; and the test of police efficiency is the absence of crime and disorder, not the visible evidence of police action in dealing with them.

Origins and influence

Although attributed to Peel, the nine principles were codified largely by twentieth-century police historians from the general instructions issued to the new force's officers in 1829, drafted under Peel's direction by commissioners Charles Rowan and Richard Mayne. The principles remain a foundational statement of the British model of policing and have influenced police philosophy throughout the Commonwealth and beyond.
