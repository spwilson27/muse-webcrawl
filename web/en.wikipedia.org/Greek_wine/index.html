Greek wine

Greece is one of the world's oldest wine-producing regions.

Greek wine is produced in one of the oldest wine-making regions of the world: Greece has cultivated vines and made wine continuously for some 6,500 years. In antiquity, Greek wine was traded throughout the Mediterranean and praised across the Roman world; today Greece is known for distinctive indigenous grape varieties and appellations such as Santorini, Naoussa and Nemea.

History

Viticulture spread from the Near East to Crete and the Aegean in the Bronze Age. In classical Greece wine was central to the symposium and to the cult of Dionysus; famous ancient wines came from Chios, Lesbos, Thasos and Rhodes. Under Rome, and later Byzantium and Ottoman rule, production continued, with the resin-flavoured retsina tradition persisting from ancient practice of sealing amphorae with pine resin. The modern industry was reshaped after Greek independence, survived phylloxera in the 20th century, and underwent a quality revolution from the 1960s–70s with appellation laws and estate bottling.

Grape varieties

Leading indigenous white varieties include Assyrtiko (notably on volcanic Santorini), Moschofilero, Malagousia, Robola and Savatiano. Principal red varieties include Agiorgitiko (Nemea), Xinomavro (Naoussa and Amyndeo), Mavrodaphne and Limnio. International varieties such as Cabernet Sauvignon and Chardonnay are also widely planted.

Classification

Greek wine law, harmonised with the European Union system, divides wines into PDO wines (formerly Appellation of Superior Quality and Appellation of Controlled Origin, the latter reserved for sweet wines such as the muscats of Samos and Lemnos and Mavrodaphne of Patras) and PGI (regional) wines, alongside table wines.
