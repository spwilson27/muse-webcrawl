Deconstructivism is a postmodern architectural movement that appeared in the 1980s. It gives the impression of fragmentation of the constructed building, commonly characterised by an absence of obvious harmony, continuity, or symmetry. Its name is a portmanteau of the French philosopher Jacques Derrida's "deconstruction" and the Russian constructivism movement in art and architecture of the 1920s, from which many of its formal motifs derive.

History

Two events in 1982 are often cited as marking its emergence: the Parc de la Villette design competition in Paris, won by Bernard Tschumi, and Derrida's involvement with Peter Eisenman's design entry for the same park. The movement gained wide attention with the Museum of Modern Art's 1988 exhibition "Deconstructivist Architecture", curated by Philip Johnson and Mark Wigley, which featured Hadid, Gehry, Koolhaas, Libeskind, Tschumi, Coop Himmelb(l)au and Eisenman. Landmark built works followed: Gehry's Guggenheim Museum Bilbao (1997), Libeskind's Jewish Museum Berlin (2001), and Hadid's Vitra Fire Station (1994) and MAXXI in Rome (2010).

Characteristics

Deconstructivist buildings manipulate the surface or skin of a structure, use non-rectilinear shapes that distort and dislocate elements such as walls and enclosures, and favour controlled chaos over rational order. The finished visual appearance is characterised by unpredictability and stimulated, stimulated disorder. Computer-aided design, and later digital fabrication, made such complex geometries buildable at scale.

Criticism

Critics have charged deconstructivism with formalism — privileging striking form over social purpose or functional fitness — and with producing expensive, difficult buildings. Defenders reply that it questions architecture's basic assumptions about stability, function and the grid, and that its fragmentation reflects the discontinuities of contemporary life.
