Colossal head

The Olmec colossal heads are stone representations of human heads sculpted from large basalt boulders by the Olmec civilisation of ancient Mesoamerica. At least seventeen monumental examples have been unearthed in the Gulf Coast region of Mexico, principally at the sites of San Lorenzo, La Venta, Tres Zapotes and Rancho la Cobata. They date to the Preclassic (Formative) period, roughly 1200–400 BCE.

Description

The heads range from about 1.5 to 3.4 metres (5 to 11 feet) in height and weigh between 6 and 50 tonnes. Each depicts a mature male with fleshy cheeks, a flat nose and slightly crossed eyes, wearing a distinctive close-fitting headdress, often compared to a helmet. No two heads are alike, and archaeologists generally interpret them as portraits of individual Olmec rulers.

Production and transport

The basalt was quarried in the Sierra de los Tuxtlas mountains and transported up to 100 kilometres (62 miles), probably floated on rafts down rivers and dragged overland by large workforces. The San Lorenzo heads, the earliest and finest group, were later mutilated and buried in lines, possibly as part of ritual decommissioning or dynastic upheaval.
