Tetraethyllead

Tetraethyllead (commonly styled tetraethyl lead), abbreviated TEL, is an organolead compound with the formula Pb(C2H5)4. It is a fuel additive, first used in the 1920s as a patented octane rating booster that allowed engine compression to be raised substantially. This in turn increased vehicle performance and fuel economy.

History

TEL was discovered as an antiknock agent in 1921 by Thomas Midgley Jr., working for Charles Kettering at General Motors Research. Leaded petrol went on sale in 1923 under the brand "Ethyl". Its toxicity was evident early: in 1924 dozens of workers at TEL production plants suffered lead poisoning, several dying — the "loony gas" scandal — yet production continued, and TEL became near-universal in petrol worldwide by the mid-twentieth century.

Phase-out

From the 1970s, evidence of widespread environmental lead contamination and harm to children's cognitive development, confirmed by geochemist Clair Patterson's work on environmental lead, drove regulation. Catalytic converters, poisoned by lead, also required unleaded fuel. The United States banned TEL in road-vehicle fuel effective 1996, the European Union followed in 2000, and the last country, Algeria, ended sales of leaded petrol in July 2021 — a milestone announced by the UN Environment Programme. TEL remains in use in some aviation gasoline (avgas) for piston-engine aircraft.

Toxicity

TEL is acutely toxic, readily absorbed through the skin, and converted in the body to triethyllead and inorganic lead. Chronic low-level exposure from exhaust fumes raised average blood lead levels across entire urban populations during the leaded-petrol era.
