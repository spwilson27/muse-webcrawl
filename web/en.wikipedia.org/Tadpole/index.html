Tadpole

A tadpole is the larval stage in the biological life cycle of an amphibian, particularly of a frog or toad. Tadpoles are typically fully aquatic, breathing through gills and swimming with a long tail, before undergoing metamorphosis into the adult form. In many languages, tadpoles of frogs are also called pollywogs (or polliwogs).

Development

Tadpoles hatch from eggs laid in water, often in gelatinous masses called frogspawn. The newly hatched larva has a rounded body, external gills (soon covered by a fold of skin forming an operculum), and a muscular tail for swimming. Most tadpoles are herbivorous, grazing on algae and detritus with small rasping mouthparts, though some species are carnivorous or even cannibalistic. As development proceeds, the hind limbs appear first, followed by the forelimbs; the tail is gradually resorbed, the gills are replaced by lungs, and the gut remodels for an adult carnivorous diet.

Metamorphosis

Metamorphosis is controlled by thyroid hormones and may take from a few weeks in desert species to more than a year in some frogs. Some species, such as many poison dart frogs, care for their tadpoles, transporting them to small pools of water held in plants. The axolotl and some other salamanders famously may remain in a larval-like state (neoteny) without undergoing full metamorphosis.

Ecology

Tadpoles are an important food source for fish, birds, and aquatic invertebrates, and in grazing they strongly influence algal communities in ponds and streams. Because their permeable skin makes them sensitive to pollution and disease—including the chytrid fungus implicated in global amphibian declines—tadpoles are often used as bioindicators of freshwater ecosystem health.
