Discalced Carmelites

A Carmelite monastery of the Discalced reform in Spain.

The Discalced Carmelites (Latin: Ordo Carmelitarum Discalceatorum, postnominal initials OCD) are a Catholic mendicant religious order that arose in 16th-century Spain as a reform movement within the Carmelite Order, initiated by Saint Teresa of Ávila and Saint John of the Cross. The name "discalced" (barefoot, or wearing sandals instead of shoes) signifies the austerity of the reform, expressed in poverty, enclosure for the nuns, and a life centred on contemplative prayer.

History

Teresa of Ávila founded the first convent of the reform, San José in Ávila, in 1562, and with John of the Cross extended the reform to friars from 1568. After initial conflict with the unreformed ("Calced") Carmelites, the Discalced were erected as a separate province in 1580 and as an independent order in 1593. The order spread rapidly through Europe and, as a missionary order, to Persia, India, and the Americas.

Spirituality

The order's spirituality is expressed in the classic works of its founders: Teresa's Interior Castle and Way of Perfection, and John's Ascent of Mount Carmel, Dark Night of the Soul, and Spiritual Canticle, which describe the soul's journey to union with God. Later prominent members include Thérèse of Lisieux (Story of a Soul) and Edith Stein (Teresa Benedicta of the Cross), executed at Auschwitz in 1942. The order today comprises friars, enclosed nuns, and the Secular Order of Discalced Carmelites (OCDS) for laypeople, numbering tens of thousands worldwide.
