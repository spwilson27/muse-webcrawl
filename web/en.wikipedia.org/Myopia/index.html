Myopia


Labelled image: light rays focus in front of the retina in myopia

Myopia, also known as near-sightedness and short-sightedness, is an eye disease where light focuses in front of, instead of on, the retina. As a result, distant objects appear blurry while near objects appear normal. Other symptoms may include headaches and eye strain. Severe near-sightedness increases the risk of retinal detachment, cataracts, and glaucoma.

Causes

The underlying mechanism involves the length of the eyeball growing too long or, less commonly, the lens becoming too strong. It is a type of refractive error. Risk factors include doing work that involves focusing on close objects, greater time spent indoors, urbanization, and a family history of the condition. It is also associated with a high socioeconomic status and higher levels of education.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by an eye examination, typically using a retinoscope and a series of test lenses. Myopia is corrected with eyeglasses, contact lenses, or refractive surgery such as LASIK. Glasses are the simplest and safest method of correction. Methods to slow progression in children include low-dose atropine eye drops, multifocal lenses, and increased time outdoors.

Epidemiology

Myopia is the most common eye problem worldwide, affecting an estimated 1.5 to 2 billion people. Rates vary greatly between regions, reaching 70–90% among some East Asian populations, and have risen sharply in recent decades — a trend often attributed to environmental change rather than genetics alone. High myopia (typically −6 dioptres or worse) carries significantly greater risks of sight-threatening complications.
