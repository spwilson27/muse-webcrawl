Grass-fed beef

Grass-fed beef is beef produced from cattle that eat only grass and other forage throughout their lives, with the exception of milk consumed before weaning. It is distinguished from conventional beef, in which cattle typically graze early in life but are "finished" in feedlots on grain-based rations for the final months before slaughter. The terms grass-fed, grass-finished emphasise that no grain finishing occurred. Grass-fed beef is usually leaner than grain-fed beef, with a somewhat different fatty-acid profile and flavour.

Production

Grass-fed cattle remain on pasture or are fed harvested forage (hay, silage) for their entire post-weaning lives, and typically grow more slowly than grain-finished animals, taking longer to reach slaughter weight. Production suits regions with abundant pasture; Argentina, Uruguay, New Zealand, and Australia have long traditions of predominantly grass-fed beef, while the United States market is mostly grain-finished with a growing grass-fed niche sold at a price premium.

Nutrition and characteristics

Compared with grain-fed beef, grass-fed beef generally contains less total fat and fewer calories, somewhat higher proportions of omega-3 fatty acids and conjugated linoleic acid (CLA), and more vitamin E and beta-carotene — the latter giving its fat a more yellow colour. Its flavour is often described as more mineral, earthy, or "gamey", and because it is leaner it cooks faster and is more prone to drying out. The magnitude and significance of the nutritional differences are debated, since grass-fed beef is eaten as part of varied diets and the absolute differences are modest.

Labelling and environment

Labelling rules vary: in the United States, the USDA withdrew its official grass-fed marketing claim standard in 2016, and third-party certifications (such as the American Grassfed Association's) now verify that animals were fed only forage and raised without confinement in feedlots. Environmental comparisons are contested: grass-fed systems avoid feed-crop inputs and can support well-managed pasture ecosystems, but slower growth and longer lives generally mean more land use and more methane emitted per kilogram of beef than feedlot finishing.
