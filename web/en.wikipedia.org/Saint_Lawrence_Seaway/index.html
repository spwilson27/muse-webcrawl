Saint Lawrence Seaway

The Saint Lawrence Seaway (French: la Voie Maritime du Saint-Laurent) is a system of locks, canals, and channels in Canada and the United States that permits oceangoing vessels to travel from the Atlantic Ocean to the Great Lakes of North America, as far inland as Lake Superior. Legally, the system extends from Montreal to Lake Erie, including the Welland Canal.

The St. Lawrence River portion of the seaway is not a continuous canal; rather, it consists of several stretches of navigable channels within the river, a number of locks, and canals along the banks of the St. Lawrence River to bypass rapids and dams. A number of the locks are managed by the St. Lawrence Seaway Management Corporation in Canada, and others in the United States by the Great Lakes St. Lawrence Seaway Development Corporation.

History

The Saint Lawrence Seaway was preceded by a number of other canals. In 1871, locks on the Saint Lawrence allowed transit of vessels 186 ft (57 m) long, 44 ft 6 in (13.6 m) wide, and 9 ft (2.7 m) deep. The Welland Canal at that time allowed transit of vessels 142 ft (43 m) long, 26 ft (7.9 m) wide, and 10 ft (3.0 m) deep, but was generally too small to allow passage of larger oceangoing ships.

Construction of the modern seaway began in 1954, and it was opened to deep draft ocean navigation on 25 April 1959. The official dedication took place on 26 June 1959, attended by Queen Elizabeth II and President Dwight D. Eisenhower. The project was one of the largest engineering undertakings of its era, employing some 22,000 workers and flooding a number of villages along the Ontario shore, whose residents were relocated to new communities known as the Lost Villages.

Locks and navigation

There are seven locks in the Saint Lawrence River portion of the seaway, and eight on the Welland Canal. The locks are 766 ft (233.5 m) long, 80 ft (24.4 m) wide, and 30 ft (9.14 m) deep over the sill. The maximum allowed vessel dimensions are 740 ft (225.6 m) long and 78 ft (23.8 m) wide; vessels of this size are known as Seawaymax ships. A typical transit of the whole system takes around eight to ten days.

Commerce and impact

The seaway handles mainly bulk cargoes such as iron ore, coal, grain, and stone, and is a vital artery for the industrial heartlands of both countries. However, the waterway also facilitated the spread of invasive species such as the zebra mussel and the sea lamprey into the Great Lakes, an environmental cost weighed against its economic benefits.
