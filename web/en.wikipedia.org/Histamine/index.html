Histamine

Histamine is an organic nitrogenous compound involved in local immune responses (as a type of local hormone), as well as regulating physiological functions in the gut and acting as a neurotransmitter for the brain, spinal cord, and uterus. Since histamine was discovered in 1910, it has been considered a local hormone lacking the feedback regulation of classical hormones; however, recently histamine has been recognized as a central neurotransmitter.

FormulaC5H9N3
Molar mass111.15 g·mol−1
BiosynthesisDecarboxylation of histidine by histidine decarboxylase
ReceptorsH1, H2, H3, H4

Storage and release

Histamine is stored mainly in granules of mast cells and basophils. It is released during allergic and inflammatory responses when IgE antibodies on the cell surface bind allergen, triggering degranulation. Released histamine causes vasodilation, increased vascular permeability, itching, and bronchoconstriction.

Roles in the body

In the stomach, histamine released from enterochromaffin-like cells acts on H2 receptors of parietal cells to stimulate gastric acid secretion — the basis of H2 blocker drugs such as cimetidine and famotidine. In the central nervous system, histaminergic neurons of the tuberomammillary nucleus promote wakefulness, which is why first-generation H1 antihistamines cause drowsiness.

Clinical significance

Antihistamines are widely used for allergies, urticaria and motion sickness. Massive histamine release contributes to anaphylaxis, treated with epinephrine. Histamine intolerance and scombroid fish poisoning result from excessive ingestion or impaired degradation of histamine.
