William Van Alen

William Van Alen (August 10, 1883 – May 24, 1954) was an American architect best known for designing the Chrysler Building in New York City, one of the most celebrated examples of Art Deco architecture. His career was marked by both spectacular success with the Chrysler Building and a bitter professional falling-out with its sponsor, Walter Chrysler, which effectively ended his major commissions.

Early life and education

Van Alen was born in Brooklyn, New York. He studied at the Pratt Institute and worked as a draftsman in the office of Clarence True while attending evening classes. In 1908 he won the Paris Prize of the Society of Beaux-Arts Architects, which allowed him to study in Paris at the Atelier Laloux, exposing him to European modernist thinking that would later influence his skyscraper designs.

Career

Returning to New York in 1911, Van Alen formed a partnership with H. Craig Severance. The firm designed several commercial buildings, including the Bainbridge Building. The partnership dissolved acrimoniously in 1924, and the two men became rivals — a rivalry that culminated in the famous "race for the sky" of 1929–1930, when Severance's Manhattan Company Building (40 Wall Street) and Van Alen's Chrysler Building competed to become the tallest building in the world.

The Chrysler Building

Commissioned by automobile magnate Walter Chrysler, the Chrysler Building rose at 405 Lexington Avenue. Van Alen's design featured a distinctive terraced crown of stainless steel arches and triangular windows, automobile-inspired ornament including radiator-cap gargoyles, and a spire (the "vertex") secretly assembled inside the crown and hoisted into place in October 1929, bringing the building to 1,046 feet (319 m) and briefly making it the tallest structure in the world. The building is widely regarded as a masterpiece of Art Deco design.

Later life

Van Alen sued Chrysler over unpaid fees; the dispute was settled, but his reputation suffered, and he received no further major commissions. He taught sculpture classes and lived quietly until his death in 1954. In 1994 the Van Alen Institute, an organization for architecture and design in New York, was named (renamed) in his honor.
