Valentinus (c. AD 100 – c. 160/180) was an early Christian Gnostic theologian, founder of the Valentinian school, the most successful and sophisticated form of Gnostic Christianity. Clement of Alexandria recorded that Valentinus claimed to have been taught by Theudas, a disciple of Paul the Apostle.

Life

Born in the Nile Delta, Valentinus was educated in Alexandria and preached in Rome between about 136 and 165, where he was reportedly considered for the office of bishop. His followers included Heracleon and Ptolemy, whose writings shaped the western Valentinian tradition, while the Gospel of Truth found at Nag Hammadi is often attributed to Valentinus himself.

Teachings

Valentinian cosmology describes the Pleroma, or fullness, of thirty divine emanations (Aeons) produced in pairs by the ultimate Father, Bythos. The fall of the youngest Aeon, Sophia, produces the Demiurge and the material world; salvation comes through gnosis — knowledge of one's divine spark — mediated by Christ. Humanity is divided into hylics, psychics and pneumatics, a scheme later writers linked to Valentinus's sacramental and ethical teachings.

Legacy

Valentinian communities flourished across the Roman world for centuries despite condemnation by Irenaeus and other heresiologists, and Valentinian ideas strongly influenced modern reconstructions of early Christian diversity after the Nag Hammadi discoveries.
