Stainless steel

Stainless steel is ubiquitous in cutlery, cookware, architecture and industry.

Stainless steel, also known as inox, is a family of iron-based alloys containing a minimum of about 10.5% chromium, which gives them resistance to corrosion and staining far beyond that of ordinary steel. The chromium reacts with oxygen to form an invisible, self-healing passive film of chromium oxide on the surface; if the film is damaged it reforms in the presence of oxygen.

History

The corrosion resistance of iron-chromium alloys was noted in the 19th century, but practical stainless steels emerged in 1912–1913, when Harry Brearley in Sheffield, England, seeking an erosion-resistant alloy for gun barrels, developed a 12.8% chromium steel and recognised its resistance to vinegar and lemon juice — leading to its use for cutlery. Parallel work in Germany (Krupp) produced austenitic grades with nickel. The metal was marketed as "stainless steel" and rapidly adopted for tableware, surgical instruments and chemical plant.

Types

Stainless steels are classified by microstructure: austenitic (the most common family, including grades 304 and 316; non-magnetic, highly formable and weldable), ferritic (magnetic, moderate corrosion resistance), martensitic (hardenable, used for knives and tools), duplex (mixed austenitic-ferritic, high strength), and precipitation-hardening grades. Additions of nickel stabilise the austenitic structure; molybdenum (as in 316) improves resistance to chlorides and seawater.

Properties and uses

Stainless steels combine corrosion resistance, strength, heat resistance, hygiene (an easily cleaned, non-porous surface) and full recyclability. Applications include cutlery and cookware, surgical instruments and implants, food and chemical processing equipment, architecture (such as the cladding of the Chrysler Building), rail vehicles, and piping. World production exceeds 50 million tonnes per year, with China the largest producer.
