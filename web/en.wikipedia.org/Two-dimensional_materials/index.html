Two-dimensional materials

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Two-dimensional materials (2D materials) are crystalline materials consisting of a single layer, or a few layers, of atoms. Because electrons in them are confined to a plane, their properties often differ dramatically from those of the corresponding three-dimensional bulk crystals.

Graphene and the 2D boom

The field began with the isolation of graphene — a single layer of carbon atoms in a honeycomb lattice — by Andre Geim and Konstantin Novoselov at the University of Manchester in 2004, work recognised by the 2010 Nobel Prize in Physics. Graphene proved unexpectedly stable and showed exceptional electrical conductivity, mechanical strength and thermal conductivity.

Families of 2D materials

  • Elemental sheets (Xenes): silicene, phosphorene (black phosphorus), borophene, stanene.
  • Transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs): MoS2, WSe2 and related semiconductors, which become direct-bandgap and strongly light-emitting as monolayers.
  • Hexagonal boron nitride (h-BN): an atomically smooth insulator used as a substrate and encapsulant.
  • MXenes: two-dimensional transition-metal carbides and nitrides, used in energy storage and shielding.
  • Layered oxides, perovskites and magnetic materials such as CrI3.

Heterostructures and applications

Stacking different monolayers, held together by van der Waals forces, creates artificial "van der Waals heterostructures" with designed properties; twisting two graphene layers to a "magic angle" near 1.1° produces superconductivity, discovered in 2018. Promised applications include flexible electronics, sensors, membranes for water filtration, batteries and quantum devices, though large-scale manufacture remains a challenge.
