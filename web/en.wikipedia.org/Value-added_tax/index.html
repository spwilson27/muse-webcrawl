Value-added tax

[Image placeholder: VAT collection diagram]

A value-added tax (VAT), known in some countries as a goods and services tax (GST), is a form of indirect consumption tax assessed incrementally on the value added at each stage of production and distribution. Under the standard invoice-credit method, a business charges VAT on its sales (output tax) but receives credit for VAT paid on its purchases (input tax), remitting only the difference to the government; the final consumer, who cannot claim credit, bears the full tax burden.

History and adoption

The concept was developed in the early 20th century, with proposals by Wilhelm von Siemens in Germany and Thomas S. Adams in the United States. France introduced the first modern VAT in 1954 under the direction of Maurice Lauré. The European Economic Community adopted VAT as its harmonized consumption tax beginning in the late 1960s, and the system spread worldwide; by the 2020s more than 160 countries operated a VAT or GST, including all OECD members except the United States, which relies on retail sales taxes at the state level.

Operation

Rates vary widely: standard rates in the European Union must be at least 15 percent, with Hungary's 27 percent among the world's highest. Many jurisdictions apply reduced rates or zero-rating to necessities such as food and medicine, and exempt services such as healthcare, education, and financial services. Exports are typically zero-rated so that goods leave the country free of VAT, while imports are taxed on entry—the destination principle. Businesses must register, file periodic returns, and maintain invoices that create a paper trail aiding enforcement, though the system has been vulnerable to carousel (missing trader) fraud within the EU.

Economic analysis

Economists generally regard VAT as an efficient revenue raiser because it taxes consumption broadly with limited distortion of production decisions. Criticisms include its administrative and compliance burden—especially for small businesses—and its regressivity, since lower-income households spend a larger share of income on consumption; governments often respond with reduced rates on essentials or compensating transfers.

Comparison with sales tax

Unlike a retail sales tax, which is collected once at the final sale, VAT is collected fractionally at every stage, reducing the incentive for retailers to evade and creating self-enforcing cross-reporting between businesses.
