Appropriations bill (United States)

An appropriations bill in the United States is a federal law that authorises the expenditure of government funds. Under the Appropriations Clause of the Constitution (Article I, Section 9, Clause 7), money may be drawn from the Treasury only "in consequence of appropriations made by law", giving Congress control of federal spending.

Process

The President submits a budget request, after which the House and Senate Committees on Appropriations divide funding among twelve subcommittees, each producing an annual appropriations bill covering areas such as defense, agriculture, energy and water, and homeland security. The bills must pass both chambers and be signed by the President before the fiscal year begins on 1 October. Because individual measures often stall, bills are frequently combined into an omnibus bill, or funding is extended temporarily through a continuing resolution.

Consequences of delay

If appropriations lapse, the Antideficiency Act prohibits agencies from spending, producing a government shutdown in which non-essential federal operations are suspended until funding is restored.
