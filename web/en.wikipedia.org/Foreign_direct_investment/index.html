Foreign direct investment

A foreign direct investment (FDI) is a purchase of an interest in a company by a company or an investor located outside its borders. More precisely, it is an investment in the form of a controlling ownership in a business in one country by an entity based in another country, distinguished from foreign portfolio investment by the element of control. By convention, ownership of 10% or more of voting stock is treated as FDI. Global FDI flows were about US$1.3 trillion in 2023, according to UNCTAD.

Greenfield investment is a major form of FDI.

Forms

FDI takes three principal forms: greenfield investment (building new operations abroad), cross-border mergers and acquisitions, and the expansion of existing affiliates through reinvested earnings and intra-company loans. Investments may be horizontal (replicating home production abroad), vertical (relocating stages of a supply chain), or conglomerate. John Dunning's eclectic (OLI) paradigm explains FDI through ownership, location, and internalisation advantages.

Effects and policy

Host countries generally seek FDI for capital, technology transfer, employment, and export access, offering tax incentives and special economic zones; critics point to profit repatriation, crowding-out of local firms, and regulatory chill. Many states screen inbound investment for national-security concerns (e.g. CFIUS in the United States), and bilateral investment treaties govern investor protection and investor–state dispute settlement.
