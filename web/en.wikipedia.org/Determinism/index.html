Determinism

Determinism is the philosophical view that all events in the universe, including human decisions and actions, are causally inevitable. Deterministic theories throughout the history of philosophy have developed from diverse motives and conceptual considerations. The opposite of determinism is indeterminism, which holds that some events are not fully determined by prior causes. The question of whether determinism is true, and its compatibility with free will, has been debated since antiquity.

Laplace's demon

The classic formulation comes from Pierre-Simon Laplace (1814): an intellect which at a certain moment knew all forces in nature and the positions of all things "would embrace in a single formula the movements of the greatest bodies of the universe and those of the tiniest atom; for such an intellect nothing would be uncertain and the future just like the past would be present before its eyes." This thought experiment assumed Newtonian mechanics described a clockwork universe.

Varieties

Causal determinism holds that every event is necessitated by antecedent events and the laws of nature. Other varieties include theological determinism (all events are preordained by a deity), logical determinism (the truth of future propositions settles what happens), biological and environmental determinism about human behaviour, and nomological determinism, the strongest common form. "Hard determinists" conclude free will is an illusion; "soft determinists" or compatibilists — a tradition running from Hume through much contemporary philosophy — hold that freedom consists in acting from one's own desires rather than being unconstrained by causation.

Determinism and modern physics

Quantum mechanics complicates the classical picture: on standard interpretations, individual measurement outcomes are irreducibly probabilistic, though the Schrödinger equation itself evolves deterministically, and interpretations such as Bohmian mechanics and the many-worlds interpretation restore determinism in different forms. Chaos theory shows that even fully deterministic systems can be practically unpredictable, because tiny measurement errors grow exponentially — determinism does not imply predictability.

Free will debate

Philosophers such as Peter van Inwagen, with his "consequence argument", contend that if determinism is true, our acts are consequences of remote events beyond our control. Responses dispute whether such control is required for moral responsibility. Experimental neuroscience, notably Benjamin Libet's readiness-potential studies, is sometimes invoked in the debate, though its interpretation is contested.
