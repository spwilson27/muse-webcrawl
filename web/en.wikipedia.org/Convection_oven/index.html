Convection oven

A convection oven (also known as a fan-assisted oven or simply a fan oven) is an oven that uses a fan to circulate hot air around food as it cooks. By moving air continuously over the food's surface, a convection oven transfers heat more efficiently than a conventional radiant oven, in which air circulates only by natural convection.

Operation

In a conventional oven, the air layer immediately surrounding the food acts as an insulator, slowing heat transfer. The fan in a convection oven strips away this boundary layer, producing faster and more even cooking, more uniform browning, and effective cooking on several racks at once. Because heat transfer is more efficient, recipes written for conventional ovens are usually adapted by reducing the temperature by about 15–20 °C (25–30 °F), shortening the cooking time, or both.

Some designs, called true or European convection ovens, place an additional heating element around the fan so that the air is heated before it is blown into the cavity, further improving temperature uniformity.

History

Commercial fan-assisted ovens were developed in the mid-twentieth century for professional kitchens, where even baking across large trays was essential. Countertop and domestic models became common from the 1970s and 1980s onward, and convection settings are now standard on most domestic ranges. Smaller countertop appliances such as the air fryer apply the same principle at higher fan speeds in a compact chamber.

Advantages and limitations

Advocates cite faster preheating, reduced energy use, even results for baking and roasting, and superior crisping of foods such as roasted vegetables and poultry skin. Delicate baked goods — soufflés, custards and some cakes — may cook unevenly or have their surfaces dried or displaced by the moving air, and some bakers prefer conventional heat for them.
