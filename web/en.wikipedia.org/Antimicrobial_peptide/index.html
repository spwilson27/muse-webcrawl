Antimicrobial peptide

Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs), also called host defence peptides, are part of the innate immune response found among all classes of life. Fundamental differences exist between prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells that may represent targets for antimicrobial peptides. These peptides are potent, broad spectrum antibiotics which demonstrate potential as novel therapeutic agents.

Structure and activity

Most antimicrobial peptides are short (12–50 amino acids), positively charged, and amphipathic, often folding into alpha-helices or beta-sheets. They act primarily by interacting with negatively charged bacterial membranes, forming pores or otherwise disrupting membrane integrity. Well-known families include the defensins and cathelicidins in vertebrates, cecropins in insects, and magainins in frog skin.

Functions

In addition to direct killing of bacteria, fungi, and some viruses, many AMPs modulate the immune system, promote wound healing, and neutralize bacterial endotoxin. Because their target is the membrane rather than a specific enzyme, resistance develops more slowly than to conventional antibiotics, though it does occur.
