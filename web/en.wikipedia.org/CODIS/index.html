CODIS

The Combined DNA Index System (CODIS) is the United States national DNA database program administered by the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI). It blends forensic science and computer technology into a system that links local, state, and national indexes of DNA profiles, enabling crime laboratories to exchange and compare DNA evidence electronically and to match crime-scene samples against convicted offenders and arrestees.

Structure

CODIS operates at three tiers: the Local DNA Index System (LDIS), the State DNA Index System (SDIS), and the National DNA Index System (NDIS), established under the DNA Identification Act of 1994. Profiles historically used 13 core short tandem repeat (STR) loci, expanded to 20 core loci in 2017 to increase discriminating power and international compatibility. Indexes include convicted offenders, arrestees (where state law permits), forensic casework samples, unidentified remains, and missing persons' relatives.

Use and impact

A CODIS "hit" can identify a suspect in a case with no other leads, link serial crimes across jurisdictions, and exonerate the wrongly convicted. The system has aided hundreds of thousands of investigations. High-profile uses include identifying the Golden State Killer's relatives through related genetic genealogy methods — a related but distinct technique — and processing backlogs of sexual assault kits.

Privacy and criticism

Civil liberties groups have raised concerns about the expansion of DNA collection to arrestees not convicted of crimes, the retention of profiles, and racial disparities in database composition. The U.S. Supreme Court upheld arrestee DNA sampling in Maryland v. King (2013), comparing it to fingerprinting.
