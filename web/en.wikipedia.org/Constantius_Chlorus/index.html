Constantius Chlorus (c. 250 – 25 July 306), also called Constantius I, was a Roman emperor who rose through the ranks of the army to become Caesar under Diocletian in 293 and Augustus of the West in 305. He was the father of Constantine the Great.

Early life and career

Constantius was born in Illyricum, probably in Dardania, of relatively humble origins. He served as a bodyguard and senior officer under the emperors Aurelian, Probus and Carus, and governed Dalmatia before being elevated during the formation of the Tetrarchy.

Caesar of the West

On 1 March 293 Diocletian appointed Constantius as Caesar to Maximian. To seal the alliance he divorced Helena, the mother of Constantine, and married Theodora, Maximian's stepdaughter. His first great task was the recovery of Britain from the usurper Carausius and his successor Allectus; after careful preparation he invaded in 296 and restored the province to imperial rule.

Augustus and death

When Diocletian and Maximian abdicated on 1 May 305, Constantius became the senior Augustus in the West. Campaigning against the Picts in northern Britain, he died at Eboracum (York) on 25 July 306, whereupon his troops proclaimed his son Constantine emperor.

Legacy

Constantius acquired the nickname "Chlorus" ("the Pale") from later Byzantine historians. Christian writers such as Eusebius remembered him as tolerant toward Christians during the Diocletianic persecution, and his dynastic line, the Constantinian dynasty, ruled the empire for much of the fourth century.
