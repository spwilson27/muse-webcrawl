Henry Clay Frick (1849–1919) was an American industrialist, financier and art patron. He founded H. C. Frick & Company, which dominated the production of coke — the fuel essential to steelmaking — and as chairman of the Carnegie Steel Company he was Andrew Carnegie's business partner and a central figure in the creation of U.S. Steel. He is also remembered for his hard-line role in the Homestead Strike of 1892 and for the art collection that became New York's Frick Collection.

Coke and Carnegie Steel

Starting in the 1870s in Pennsylvania's Connellsville region, Frick built an empire of coal mines and beehive coke ovens, borrowing aggressively and buying out competitors. Carnegie acquired a controlling interest in Frick's company, and in 1889 Frick became chairman of Carnegie Steel, driving its efficiency while Carnegie travelled. Their relationship later broke down bitterly; after a dispute over the company's valuation, Frick was bought out and the feud became notorious.

Homestead and the assassination attempt

During the 1892 Homestead Strike, Frick, determined to break the Amalgamated Association of Iron and Steel Workers, locked out the workforce and brought in 300 Pinkerton detectives; the ensuing gun battle killed workers and detectives and led to the state militia occupying the works. Days later the anarchist Alexander Berkman shot and stabbed Frick in his Pittsburgh office; Frick survived, returned to work within a week, and the strike ultimately collapsed — a turning point that set back American steel unionisation for decades.

Art collection and legacy

Frick amassed an outstanding collection of Old Master paintings and decorative art, housed in his Fifth Avenue mansion in New York, which under his will became the Frick Collection, opened to the public in 1935. The collection includes works by Rembrandt, Vermeer, Turner, Whistler and Goya. Frick also endowed the Frick Art Reference Library. In Pittsburgh, his legacy includes the Frick Building, Frick Park and his Clayton estate. He died in 1919, one of the wealthiest Americans of his era.
