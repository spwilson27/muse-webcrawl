Maize (Zea mays), also known as corn in North American English, is a tall annual cereal grass domesticated by indigenous peoples in southern Mexico about 9,000 years ago from the wild grass teosinte. It is one of the most widely grown cereal crops in the world and a staple food for much of humanity and livestock.

The plant typically grows 2–3 metres tall, with a single sturdy stalk bearing long leaves and separate male and female flowers: the tassel at the top and the ears, which develop from modified lateral shoots and are enclosed in husks. The kernels on an ear are the seeds of the plant.

History

Maize was domesticated from teosinte (Zea mays subsp. parviglumis) in the Balsas River valley of south-central Mexico. From there it spread throughout the Americas, becoming a cornerstone of the diets of peoples including the Maya, Aztecs, and the cultures of the North American Southwest and Eastern Woodlands. After European contact it spread worldwide, becoming a major crop in Africa, Asia, and Europe.

Cultivation

Maize is grown on every continent except Antarctica. The United States is the largest producer, followed by China and Brazil. Major types include dent corn (the bulk of field production, used for feed and ethanol), sweet corn (eaten as a vegetable), flint corn, flour corn, and popcorn. Modern production relies heavily on hybrid seed and, in many countries, genetically modified varieties resistant to insects or herbicides.

Uses

Only a minority of the world crop is eaten directly by people, typically as cornmeal, masa for tortillas and tamales, polenta, or sweet corn. The majority is used as livestock feed and for industrial products such as ethanol fuel, corn starch, corn syrup (including high-fructose corn syrup), and biodegradable plastics.
