Storm chasing

Chasers observing a supercell on the Great Plains

Storm chasing is broadly defined as the deliberate pursuit of any severe weather phenomenon, regardless of motive, but most commonly for curiosity, adventure, scientific investigation of a storm, and/or for photography or video, but also for news coverage. A person who chases storms is known as a storm chaser or simply a chaser.

History

Organized storm observation began in the United States in the 1950s with pioneers such as David Hoadley and researchers at the University of Oklahoma. The National Severe Storms Laboratory and field campaigns such as VORTEX in the 1990s and VORTEX2 in 2009–2010 advanced understanding of tornadogenesis, using mobile radar and instrumented probes.

Practice

Chasers use forecasts, radar data, and satellite imagery to position near supercell thunderstorms, mainly in the Great Plains' "Tornado Alley". Motives range from scientific research and spotter reporting to commercial tour guiding, media gathering, and recreation.

Hazards

Chasing is inherently dangerous: hazards include tornadoes, flash flooding, lightning, hail, and traffic accidents. Several chasers were killed in the 2013 El Reno tornado, the first known deaths of storm researchers in a tornado, prompting renewed emphasis on safety.
