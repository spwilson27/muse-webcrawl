Pan-Arabism is a political ideology advocating the unification of the Arab peoples — the Arabic-speaking countries of North Africa and Western Asia — into a single nation or state. Rooted in the idea that Arabs constitute one nation bound by a common language, history and culture, it was the dominant force in Arab politics from the 1940s to the late 1960s.

Origins

Pan-Arab ideas grew out of the Nahda (Arab cultural revival) of the nineteenth century and the Arab Revolt against the Ottoman Empire during the First World War. Thinkers such as Sati' al-Husri grounded Arab nationhood primarily in language, while Michel Aflaq, a co-founder of the Ba'ath Party (1947), combined Arab unity with socialism. The creation of Israel in 1948 and the Palestinian Nakba gave the cause an urgent focus.

Ascendancy and decline

Pan-Arabism reached its zenith under Egypt's president Gamal Abdel Nasser, whose defiance of Britain and France in the Suez Crisis of 1956 made him a hero across the Arab world. In 1958 Egypt and Syria merged as the United Arab Republic, but the union dissolved in 1961 amid Syrian resentment of Egyptian dominance. The Arab defeat in the Six-Day War of 1967 badly discredited the ideology, and after Nasser's death in 1970 state particularism, oil wealth and the rise of Islamism eclipsed it. Several other union schemes — among Libya, Egypt, Syria and Iraq — were proclaimed but never realised.

Legacy

Although a single Arab state never emerged, pan-Arab sentiment survives in institutions such as the Arab League, in shared media and popular culture, and in the pan-Arab colours (black, white, green and red) borne by many Arab flags.
