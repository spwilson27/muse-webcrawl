War of the Second Coalition

Date29 November 1798 – 25 March 1802
LocationEurope, the Mediterranean, the Middle East, the Caribbean
CombatantsFrance vs. the Second Coalition: Austria, Russia (1798–99), Great Britain, the Ottoman Empire, Naples, Portugal, and others
ResultFrench victory; Treaties of Lunéville (1801) and Amiens (1802)

The War of the Second Coalition (1798–1802) was the second war of the European powers against Revolutionary France. Formed while Napoleon Bonaparte's army was committed to the Egyptian campaign, the coalition—principally Austria, Russia, and Great Britain—initially rolled back many of France's gains in Italy and Germany. Bonaparte's return to France, his seizure of power in the coup of 18 Brumaire (November 1799), and the victories of Marengo and Hohenlinden in 1800 reversed the tide, and the war ended with the Treaties of Lunéville and Amiens.

Formation of the coalition

France's expansion after the War of the First Coalition—sister republics in Holland, Switzerland, and Italy, and the invasion of Egypt in 1798—alarmed the other powers. Britain's victory at the Battle of the Nile (1 August 1798) stranded the French army in Egypt and encouraged Austria and Russia to join Britain, Naples, Portugal, and the Ottoman Empire in a new coalition. Fighting began in late 1798.

Course of the war

In 1799, coalition forces under the Archduke Charles in Germany and Alexander Suvorov in Italy won repeated victories: Suvorov's campaigns drove the French from most of Italy, while in Egypt and Syria Bonaparte's forces won at Aboukir on land after failing at Acre. The Anglo-Russian invasion of Holland failed, however, and Suvorov's gruelling crossing of the Swiss Alps—after the Second Battle of Zurich had destroyed the allied plan there—became legendary. Tsar Paul I, angered by his allies, withdrew Russia from the coalition.

Bonaparte left Egypt, returned to France, and overthrew the Directory in November 1799, becoming First Consul. In 1800 he crossed the Alps and narrowly defeated the Austrians at Marengo (14 June), while Moreau's decisive victory at Hohenlinden (3 December) opened the road to Vienna. Austria accepted the Treaty of Lunéville (9 February 1801), confirming French control of the left bank of the Rhine and of northern Italy. Britain, now alone, negotiated the Treaty of Amiens (25 March 1802), producing a brief general peace in Europe that lasted until 1803.

Significance

The war confirmed France's dominance on land, destroyed the Directory and brought Napoleon to power, and demonstrated both the strength and the fragility of coalition warfare against him—a pattern that would repeat until his final defeat in 1815.
