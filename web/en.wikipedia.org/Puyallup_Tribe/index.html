Puyallup Tribe

The Puyallup Tribe of Indians is a federally recognized tribe of Puyallup people, a Coast Salish group whose homeland centers on the Puyallup River and Commencement Bay in present-day Pierce County, Washington. The tribe's reservation, established by the Medicine Creek Treaty of 1854, includes land in Tacoma, Fife, and Puyallup and is one of the most urban Native American reservations in the United States. The name derives from a Lushootseed word often interpreted as "generous people."

History

Under the Treaty of Medicine Creek, the Puyallup ceded most of their territory in exchange for a reservation and reserved fishing rights. The reservation was later broken up by allotment, and by the mid-twentieth century the tribe retained little land. Puyallup members were prominent in the Northwest "fish wars": the 1974 Boldt decision (United States v. Washington) affirmed treaty rights to half the harvestable salmon. The Puyallup Tribe of Indians Settlement Act of 1989 resolved land claims in Tacoma with a package of cash, land, and infrastructure funding worth about $162 million, one of the largest settlements of its era.

Government and enterprises

The tribe is governed by an elected tribal council under its constitution. With several thousand enrolled members, it is one of Pierce County's largest employers. Its enterprises include the Emerald Queen Casinos in Tacoma and Fife, marine terminals partnerships with the Port of Tacoma, a salmon hatchery and fisheries program, the Chief Leschi Schools, and health services through the Puyallup Tribal Health Authority. The tribe is active in restoring the Puyallup River watershed and Commencement Bay habitat.

Culture

The Puyallup speak a dialect of Lushootseed (txʷəlšucid); revitalization programs, including immersion education, work to sustain the language. Salmon, especially Chinook returning to the Puyallup River, remain culturally central.
