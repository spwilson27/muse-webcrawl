The Malagasy peoples are the native Austronesian ethnic groups of Madagascar, comprising roughly eighteen recognised groups including the Merina, Betsileo, Betsimisaraka, Sakalava, and Antaisaka. Unusually for Africa, their origins are twofold: seafarers from Southeast Asia who crossed the Indian Ocean, and Bantu-speaking settlers from mainland East Africa.

Origins

Genetic and linguistic evidence shows the Malagasy descend from Austronesian voyagers — close kin to the Ma'anyan people of southern Borneo — who reached Madagascar in the first millennium AD, mixing with Bantu migrants from the African mainland. The Malagasy language is Austronesian, most closely related to languages of Borneo, though it carries Bantu, Arabic, and later French influences. This dual heritage is visible in Malagasy culture, which blends Southeast Asian rice-terrace agriculture and outrigger canoes with African cattle herding.

Culture

Malagasy society is organised around kinship and reverence for ancestors (razana). Among the best-known customs is famadihana, the "turning of the bones", in which families exhume the remains of ancestors, rewrap them in fresh silk shrouds, and celebrate with music and feasting. Rice is the centre of the diet and of cultural life, traditionally grown in flooded paddies and terraces. Society was historically stratified into nobles (andriana), free people (hova), and descendants of slaves (andevo).
