Moshav

A moshav (Hebrew: מושב, plural moshavim, lit. "settlement, village") is a type of Israeli town or settlement incorporating a cooperative farmers' village that comprises several individual farms. The moshav was designed by the Zionists during the Second Aliyah (1904–1914) as a modified alternative to the more communal kibbutz: residents farm their own plots individually, while purchasing, marketing, and services are organised cooperatively.

Types

The most common type is the moshav ovdim (workers' moshav), in which each family farms its own equal-sized holding, land is nationally owned and leased, and hired labour was historically discouraged. The moshav shitufi (collective moshav) combines features of the kibbutz with the moshav: production and marketing are collective, but consumption and housing are private. Many moshavim have in recent decades taken on a more suburban character, with residents working outside agriculture.

History

The first moshav ovdim, Nahalal, was founded in 1921 in the Jezreel Valley and famously designed by architect Richard Kauffmann in concentric circles, with public buildings at the centre and farms radiating outward. Moshavim became numerous after 1948, when many new immigrants were settled in them; today there are more than 400 moshavim in Israel.
