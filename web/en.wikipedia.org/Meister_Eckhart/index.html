Meister Eckhart

Eckhart von Hochheim OP (c. 1260 – c. 1328), commonly known as Meister Eckhart, the German word for "master", was a German Catholic theologian, philosopher and mystic, born near Gotha in the Landgraviate of Thuringia (now central Germany) in the Holy Roman Empire.

Life

Eckhart joined the Dominican Order as a youth, studied and taught in Paris, and held senior offices including prior of Erfurt, vicar of Thuringia, and vicar general of Bohemia. He preached extensively in Strasbourg and Cologne, especially to religious women and lay audiences, in both Latin and the German vernacular.

Teaching

Eckhart's thought centres on the "birth of the Word" in the soul and the "ground" (grunt) of the soul, where God and the soul are one. He taught radical detachment (Abgeschiedenheit) and releasement (Gelassenheit): letting go of self-will and created things so that God may act in the soul. His daring formulations about the Godhead beyond "God" drew suspicion of heresy.

Trial and condemnation

Accused late in life, Eckhart defended his orthodoxy before the Archbishop of Cologne and appealed to the Pope; he died before the case concluded. In 1329 Pope John XXII issued the bull In agro dominico condemning a list of propositions drawn from his works, some as heretical and others as suspect, while acknowledging Eckhart had professed the Catholic faith at the end of his life.

Legacy

Eckhart influenced later German mysticism (Tauler, Suso), Nicholas of Cusa, and, in modern times, writers, philosophers, and students of comparative mysticism worldwide.
