Universal basic income

A universal basic income pays a regular cash amount to every individual, with no means test or work requirement.

Universal basic income (UBI) is a social welfare proposal in which all citizens of a given population regularly receive a minimum income in the form of an unconditional transfer payment, without a means test or work requirement. The payment is intended to cover basic living costs and to be received in addition to any other income earned.

Proponents argue that a basic income would reduce poverty and income inequality, simplify welfare bureaucracies, improve health outcomes, and give workers greater bargaining power. Critics question its affordability, warn that it could discourage work, and argue that targeted welfare achieves poverty relief more efficiently. Variants include the negative income tax, guaranteed minimum income, and partial basic income.

History

The idea has roots in early modern thinkers such as Thomas More and Thomas Paine, whose Agrarian Justice (1797) proposed a citizen's dividend. In the 20th century, economists including Milton Friedman advocated a negative income tax. Martin Luther King Jr. endorsed a guaranteed income in Where Do We Go from Here: Chaos or Community? (1967). The Alaska Permanent Fund Dividend, paid annually to Alaska residents since 1982 from oil revenues, is frequently cited as a real-world partial example.

Pilot programmes and experiments

Numerous pilots have tested basic income schemes, including experiments in Manitoba, Canada (the Mincome experiment of the 1970s), Namibia, India, Kenya (run by the charity GiveDirectly), and Finland's 2017–2018 basic income experiment, in which 2,000 unemployed people received €560 per month. Results generally showed improved wellbeing and little reduction in employment.

Funding

Proposed funding mechanisms include general taxation, carbon taxes, land value taxes, sovereign wealth funds, and the consolidation of existing welfare programmes. The gross cost of a UBI is high, but supporters note that much of it is offset by taxes paid back by higher earners and by replacing other transfers.
