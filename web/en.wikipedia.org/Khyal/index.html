Khyal (also spelled khayal; from Urdu/Persian khayāl, "imagination" or "thought") is a major form of Hindustani classical music in the Indian subcontinent. It is the most prominent genre of North Indian classical vocal music, known for its emphasis on improvisation and melodic exploration within the framework of a raga.

History

The origins of khyal are traditionally attributed to the court of Muhammad Shah of Delhi in the early 18th century, where the musicians Sadarang (Naimat Khan) and Adarang are credited with composing khyal bandishes and popularising the form. Some scholars trace influences further back to the qawwali tradition and to the court of Husain Shah Sharqi of Jaunpur in the 15th century. Khyal gradually displaced the older, more austere dhrupad as the dominant concert form during the 19th and 20th centuries.

Characteristics

A khyal performance is based on a short composition called a bandish, usually consisting of two parts: the sthayi and the antara. Two main forms are distinguished:

  • Bada khyal (vilambit khyal) – a slow, expansive rendition in a leisurely tempo, allowing elaborate melodic development.
  • Chhota khyal (drut khyal) – a fast-tempo rendering, often concluding the performance.

Improvisation in khyal includes alap-like exposition, bol-baant (rhythmic play with the lyric syllables), sargam passages, and rapid taans (virtuosic runs). The singer is accompanied by the tabla (rhythm), a melodic accompanist on harmonium or sarangi, and the tanpura drone.

Gharanas

Khyal is taught and performed within stylistic schools known as gharanas, including the Gwalior, Agra, Jaipur-Atrauli, Kirana, Rampur-Sahaswan, and Patiala gharanas, each with distinctive approaches to voice production, tempo, and improvisation.
