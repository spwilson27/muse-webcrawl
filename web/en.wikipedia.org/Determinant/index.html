Determinant

In mathematics, the determinant is a scalar-valued function of the entries of a square matrix. It characterizes some properties of the matrix and of the linear map represented by it: in particular, the determinant is zero if and only if the matrix is singular, i.e. not invertible. Geometrically, the absolute value of the determinant gives the factor by which the linear transformation scales areas (in two dimensions) or volumes (in three dimensions), and its sign records whether the transformation preserves or reverses orientation.

Definition

For a 2×2 matrix with entries a, b, c, d, the determinant is ad − bc. For larger matrices it can be computed by cofactor (Laplace) expansion along any row or column, or by the Leibniz formula: a signed sum over all permutations of the matrix's entries. Equivalently, the determinant is the unique multilinear alternating function of the columns that takes the value 1 on the identity matrix.

Properties

The determinant of a product equals the product of the determinants, det(AB) = det(A)·det(B); the determinant of the transpose equals the determinant of the matrix; and swapping two rows changes its sign. Row reduction to triangular form makes computation efficient, since the determinant of a triangular matrix is the product of its diagonal entries. The determinant also appears in Cramer's rule for solving linear systems, in the formula for eigenvalues via the characteristic polynomial, in the change-of-variables formula for multiple integrals (through the Jacobian determinant), and in the theory of differential equations via the Wronskian.

History

Determinants arose in the study of systems of linear equations: Seki Takakazu in Japan and Leibniz in Europe developed expressions in the 1680s–1690s, Cramer published his rule in 1750, and Cauchy gave the theory its modern shape in 1812, introducing the word "determinant" in its present sense (Gauss had used it for a different object in 1801).
