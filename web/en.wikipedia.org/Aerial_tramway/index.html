Aerial tramway

Also known asCable car, téléphérique, Seilbahn
TypeCable transport
ConfigurationTwo cabins shuttling on stationary track ropes, hauled by a moving rope
Notable examplesRoosevelt Island Tramway (New York), Palm Springs Aerial Tramway, Peak 2 Peak Gondola (related type)

An aerial tramway, also called a sky tram, cable car or aerial tram, is a type of aerial lift in which one or two passenger cabins are suspended from one or more stationary support cables (track ropes) and are pulled by a separate haulage cable. Because the two cabins are attached to opposite ends of the haulage loop, they move in reciprocating fashion: as one ascends, the other descends, and they pass each other midway. This distinguishes aerial tramways from gondola lifts, in which many smaller cabins circulate continuously.

Aerial tramways are built where terrain is too steep or broken for roads or railways — to reach mountain summits, cross gorges, or connect districts of hilly cities — and individual spans can be very long. Cabins commonly carry from about 15 to over 100 passengers; the Palm Springs Aerial Tramway in California climbs some 1,800 metres using rotating-floor cars, among the largest in the world, while the Roosevelt Island Tramway in New York City is a rare example used as urban public transport.

Safety and operation

Tramways require towers or stations anchoring the track ropes, powerful drives with auxiliary and evacuation engines, and strict inspection regimes. Notable accidents, such as the 1976 Cavalese disaster in Italy, led to strengthened international safety standards for passenger ropeways.
