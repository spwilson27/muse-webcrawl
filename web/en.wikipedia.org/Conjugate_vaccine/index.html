Conjugate vaccine

A conjugate vaccine is a type of subunit vaccine which combines a weak antigen with a strong antigen as a carrier so that the immune system has a stronger response to the weak antigen. Most conjugate vaccines target the polysaccharide capsule of encapsulated bacteria.

Mechanism

Bacterial capsular polysaccharides are T-cell-independent antigens: they provoke weak, short-lived antibody responses and are poorly immunogenic in infants, whose immune systems respond badly to them. By chemically linking (conjugating) the polysaccharide to a carrier protein — such as a diphtheria or tetanus toxoid, or CRM197, a non-toxic diphtheria toxin variant — the antigen is presented in a T-cell-dependent manner, generating memory B cells, higher-affinity antibodies and effective protection in young children.

Examples

  • Haemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) — introduced in the late 1980s, it virtually eliminated invasive Hib disease, including meningitis, in countries using it.
  • Pneumococcal conjugate vaccines (PCV7, PCV10, PCV13, PCV15, PCV20) — protect against Streptococcus pneumoniae.
  • Meningococcal conjugate vaccines — against serogroups A, C, W and Y of Neisseria meningitidis; the MenAfriVac group A vaccine transformed meningitis epidemiology in the African meningitis belt.
  • Typhoid conjugate vaccine — Vi polysaccharide linked to a carrier, recommended by the WHO for endemic regions.

Impact

Conjugate vaccines also reduce nasopharyngeal carriage of target bacteria, producing herd immunity that protects unvaccinated people. Their success is considered one of the major advances in vaccinology.
