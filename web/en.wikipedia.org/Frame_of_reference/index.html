Frame of reference

Frame of reference
FieldPhysics (kinematics, relativity)
TypesInertial, non-inertial
Key conceptObserver-dependent measurements

In physics, a frame of reference (or reference frame) is a set of coordinates and clocks used to measure positions and times of events, relative to an observer. Measurements such as velocity and kinetic energy generally depend on the frame chosen, while the laws of physics take their simplest form in inertial frames.

Inertial frames

An inertial frame of reference is one in which Newton's first law holds: an object with no net force moves at constant velocity. Any frame moving at constant velocity relative to an inertial frame is also inertial. In special relativity all inertial frames are equivalent and measurements between them are related by the Lorentz transformations.

Non-inertial frames

Accelerating or rotating frames are non-inertial. To keep Newton's laws usable in them, fictitious forces such as the centrifugal force and Coriolis force are introduced. Earth's surface is an approximately inertial frame for many purposes, though its rotation matters for weather and long-range projectiles.

Philosophical aspects

The choice of frame is a convention of description, but the equivalence of inertial frames (Galilean and later Einsteinian relativity) was a key step away from notions of absolute space and time.
