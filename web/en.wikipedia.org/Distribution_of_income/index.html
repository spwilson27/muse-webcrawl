Distribution of income

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The distribution of income is how a country's total gross domestic product is distributed amongst its population. It has long been a central concern of economic theory and policy, reflected in Thomas Piketty's observation that the debate hinges on "the way in which the actors understand what is at stake". Economists distinguish the functional distribution of income — the shares going to wages, profits, rent and interest — from the personal or size distribution among individuals and households.

Measurement

The distribution is commonly visualised with the Lorenz curve, which plots the cumulative share of income against the cumulative share of the population, and summarised by the Gini coefficient, which ranges from 0 (perfect equality) to 1 (maximal inequality). Other measures include income shares of deciles or percentiles, the Palma ratio, and poverty rates. Inequality within most countries fell during the mid-20th century and has risen since about 1980, while global inequality between countries has declined as incomes in large Asian economies have grown.

Determinants and policy

Determinants of the distribution include education and skills, technology, globalisation, labour-market institutions such as minimum wages and unions, inheritance, and tax and transfer systems that redistribute income. Simon Kuznets hypothesised an inverted-U relationship between inequality and economic development, an idea that remains debated. Policies affecting the distribution range from progressive taxation and social insurance to public education and competition policy.
