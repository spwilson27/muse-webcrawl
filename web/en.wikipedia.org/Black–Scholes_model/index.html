The Black–Scholes model (Black–Scholes–Merton model) is a mathematical model of a financial market containing derivative investment instruments. From its partial differential equation, the Black–Scholes equation, one derives the Black–Scholes formula, which gives a theoretical estimate of the price of European-style options.

The formula

For a call option on a non-dividend-paying stock, the formula is C = S·N(d₁) − K·e^(−rT)·N(d₂), where S is the spot price, K the strike price, r the risk-free interest rate, T the time to maturity, and N the cumulative distribution function of the standard normal distribution; d₁ and d₂ depend additionally on the asset's volatility σ.

Assumptions and impact

The model assumes frictionless markets, continuous trading, constant volatility and interest rates, and log-normally distributed returns. Though these assumptions do not hold exactly — real markets show a "volatility smile" — the model became the standard language of options markets, and Scholes and Merton received the 1997 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for the work (Black had died in 1995).
