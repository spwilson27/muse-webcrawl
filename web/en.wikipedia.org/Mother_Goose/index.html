Mother Goose

Mother Goose is a character who appears in fairy tales and nursery rhymes, usually depicted as an elderly country woman or as a goose wearing a bonnet. She is the traditional purported author of collections of tales and rhymes for children in both French and English literature.

Origins

The figure's literary origins are commonly traced to Charles Perrault's 1697 collection Histoires ou contes du temps passé, subtitled Les Contes de ma mère l'Oye ("Tales of My Mother Goose"), which included "Sleeping Beauty", "Cinderella", and "Puss in Boots". The name may echo older folk associations of geese with storytelling and elderly tellers of tales.

In English, John Newbery's Mother Goose's Melody (c. 1765) helped establish Mother Goose as the emblematic source of nursery rhymes such as "Humpty Dumpty", "Jack and Jill", and "Baa, Baa, Black Sheep". Over the 19th and 20th centuries, illustrated Mother Goose collections by artists such as Kate Greenaway and Arthur Rackham became staples of children's publishing.

Cultural presence

Mother Goose remains a fixture of early-childhood culture; in Boston's Public Garden a statue commemorates her, and a grave in the Granary Burying Ground is popularly (if dubiously) identified with a "Mary Goose" said to be the original.
