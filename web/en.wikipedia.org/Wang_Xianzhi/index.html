Wang Xianzhi

Wang Xianzhi (Chinese: 王獻之; 344–386), courtesy name Zijing, was a renowned Chinese calligrapher of the Eastern Jin dynasty, celebrated as one of the greatest masters of the running script (xingshu). He was the youngest son of the celebrated calligrapher Wang Xizhi, often called the Sage of Calligraphy.

Life

Wang Xianzhi was born into the prestigious Wang clan of Langya. A child prodigy, he studied calligraphy under his father, who — according to a famous anecdote — once seized the boy’s brush from behind and failed to pull it from his grip, remarking that the child would one day be famous. Wang Xianzhi served as an official at the Eastern Jin court and held posts including that of a palace attendant and provincial administrator.

Calligraphy

Whereas his father synthesised the traditions of earlier script styles, Wang Xianzhi is credited with a freer, more fluid manner, writing whole passages in a single continuous breath of linked strokes — a technique later called the “one-stroke” method (yibi shu). His style emphasised momentum, cursive ligatures and personal expression, and it strongly influenced the development of running and cursive script in later dynasties.

Works and legacy

None of Wang Xianzhi’s original works survive; his manner is known through tracing copies and stone engravings. The best known is the Mid-Autumn Letter (Zhongqiu Tie), preserved in a tracing copy and held by the Palace Museum in Beijing, where it is grouped with Wang Xizhi’s Timely Clearing After Snowfall and Wang Xun’s Letter to Boyuan as the “Three Rarities”. Tang-dynasty critics such as Zhang Huaiguan ranked father and son together as the twin pinnacles of the art.
