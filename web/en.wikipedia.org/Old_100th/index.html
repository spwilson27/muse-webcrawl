The Old 100th is a hymn tune in long meter, first published in the Genevan Psalter of 1551 and traditionally attributed to the French composer Loys Bourgeois. It takes its name from its association with Psalm 100, and it is among the most widely sung tunes in Christian worship, used for William Kethe's paraphrase "All People That on Earth Do Dwell" and for Thomas Ken's Doxology, "Praise God, from Whom All Blessings Flow".

Origins

The tune appeared in the 1551 edition of the Genevan Psalter, the collection of metrical psalms produced for Calvinist worship under John Calvin's direction, where it was set to Psalm 134 before becoming attached to Psalm 100. Bourgeois, music editor of the psalter, is credited with adapting or composing many of its melodies, possibly drawing on earlier secular chansons. When the psalter tradition spread to England and Scotland, the tune became known as the "Old 100th" to distinguish it from a newer setting (the "New 100th").

Use and adaptations

Kethe's "All People That on Earth Do Dwell" (1561), written while he was a Marian exile in Geneva, cemented the pairing. Ralph Vaughan Williams composed a celebrated arrangement of the tune for the coronation of Elizabeth II in 1953, adding trumpet fanfares. Because the Doxology is sung to the Old 100th in many Protestant traditions, the melody is instantly recognisable across denominations and languages, and its long-meter form lets it serve for many other texts.
