CFTR

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

CFTR (cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator) is a protein in humans encoded by the CFTR gene on chromosome 7. It functions as a channel that transports chloride and bicarbonate ions across the membranes of epithelial cells in the lungs, pancreas, digestive tract, sweat glands and other tissues. Mutations in the gene cause cystic fibrosis, the most common fatal genetic disease among people of European ancestry.

Function

CFTR belongs to the ABC transporter family but acts as an ion channel gated by ATP binding and hydrolysis and regulated by phosphorylation. By moving chloride ions out of cells, it controls the movement of water into secretions; when it fails, mucus in the airways and ducts becomes thick and sticky, leading to chronic lung infections and pancreatic insufficiency.

Mutations

More than 2,000 CFTR variants have been identified. The most common, ΔF508 (deletion of phenylalanine at position 508), accounts for about 70% of cystic fibrosis alleles; the misfolded protein is mostly degraded before reaching the cell surface. Mutations are grouped into classes according to whether they abolish production, impair processing, gating, conduction or stability.

Therapeutics

Understanding CFTR has led to modulator drugs that target the defective protein itself: the potentiator ivacaftor, and corrector combinations such as ivacaftor/lumacaftor and the triple therapy elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor (Trikafta/Kaftrio), which has transformed the prognosis for most people with cystic fibrosis. CFTR also matters in other conditions: heterozygous carriers may be protected against cholera toxin-induced diarrhoea, while excessive CFTR activation mediates secretory diarrhoeas.
