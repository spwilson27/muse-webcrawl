Opium Wars

DateFirst: 1839–1842; Second: 1856–1860
LocationChina
ResultBritish (and later Anglo-French) victory; Treaties of Nanking and Tientsin; cession of Hong Kong

The Opium Wars were two conflicts between China and Western powers in the mid-19th century: the First Opium War (1839–1842) between Britain and Qing-dynasty China, and the Second Opium War (1856–1860), fought by Britain and France against China. The wars stemmed from trade disputes, above all Britain's export of opium from India to China and China's efforts to suppress the trade.

Background

British merchants, seeking to reverse a trade imbalance caused by demand for Chinese tea, silk, and porcelain, smuggled increasing quantities of opium into China. Widespread addiction alarmed the Qing government, which in 1839 appointed Commissioner Lin Zexu to suppress the trade; he confiscated and destroyed large stocks of opium at Humen, provoking war.

First Opium War

Superior British naval power brought decisive victories along the Chinese coast and the Yangtze River. The Treaty of Nanking (1842) opened five treaty ports to British trade, ceded Hong Kong Island to Britain, and imposed an indemnity — the first of the "unequal treaties".

Second Opium War

Frustrated by restrictions on trade and diplomacy, Britain and France went to war again in 1856. The resulting Treaties of Tientsin (1858) and Convention of Peking (1860) opened more ports, legalized the opium trade, permitted foreign envoys in Beijing, and ceded Kowloon to Britain. Anglo-French forces occupied the capital and looted and burned the Old Summer Palace (Yuanmingyuan).

Legacy

The Opium Wars are remembered in China as the beginning of the "century of humiliation", a period of foreign domination that profoundly shaped modern Chinese nationalism.
