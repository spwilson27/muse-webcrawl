Chronicle of a Summer

Chronicle of a Summer (French: Chronique d'un été) is a 1961 French documentary film directed by anthropologist Jean Rouch and sociologist Edgar Morin. It is widely regarded as the founding work of cinéma vérité — a term coined by Morin as a translation of Dziga Vertov's Kino-Pravda ("film-truth") — and as a pioneering experiment in participatory and reflexive documentary filmmaking.

Content

Filmed in Paris and Saint-Tropez during the summer of 1960, the film begins with a conversation between Rouch, Morin and their collaborator Marceline Loridan over whether it is possible to act sincerely in front of a camera. The participants — students, factory workers, immigrants, a former deportee (Marceline herself, whose tattooed camp number becomes the subject of a famous sequence), and the Italian land surveyor Angelo — are asked a simple question: "Are you happy?" Their answers open discussions of work, alienation, racism, the Algerian War and the Congo crisis. In the final sequence the participants watch a rough cut of the film and debate how "truthful" each of them seemed; Rouch and Morin then walk through the Musée de l'Homme reflecting on the experiment — an early instance of filmmakers building their own uncertainty into the finished work.

Technique and influence

The film exploited the new lightweight Éclair Cameflex and Nagra sync-sound equipment, freeing the camera to walk with subjects and record spontaneous conversation — technology that transformed documentary worldwide. Its self-reflexive structure, long takes of unstructured talk, and willingness to let subjects talk back to the film helped define cinéma vérité and influenced both the French New Wave and American Direct Cinema debates (in which some American filmmakers claimed their own, contrasting ideal of unobtrusive "fly-on-the-wall" observation). It won the Critics' Prize at Cannes in 1961 and remains a fixture of documentary histories and syllabi.
