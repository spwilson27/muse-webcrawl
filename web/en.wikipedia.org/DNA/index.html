Deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) is a polymer composed of two polynucleotide chains that coil around each other to form a double helix. The polymer carries genetic instructions for the development, functioning, growth and reproduction of all known organisms and many viruses. DNA and ribonucleic acid (RNA) are nucleic acids.

Structure

Each strand of DNA is built from nucleotides, each consisting of a deoxyribose sugar, a phosphate group and one of four nitrogenous bases: adenine (A), guanine (G), cytosine (C) and thymine (T). The two strands run in opposite directions (antiparallel) and are held together by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases — A pairs with T, and G pairs with C — a rule known as Chargaff's rules in its empirical form. The most common form, B-DNA, is a right-handed helix making a complete turn every 10.5 base pairs.

History

DNA was first isolated by the Swiss physician Friedrich Miescher in 1869, who called it "nuclein." The experiments of Oswald Avery and colleagues (1944) and of Alfred Hershey and Martha Chase (1952) demonstrated that DNA is the carrier of genetic information. In 1953, James Watson and Francis Crick, drawing on X-ray diffraction data produced by Rosalind Franklin and Raymond Gosling and on the model-building of Maurice Wilkins, deduced the double-helical structure of DNA.

Replication and expression

Before a cell divides, its DNA is copied by DNA replication, in which enzymes such as DNA polymerase synthesize a new strand complementary to each template strand. Genetic information is expressed through transcription into RNA and translation into proteins, as described by the central dogma of molecular biology. Sequences of three bases, codons, specify individual amino acids in the genetic code.

Genome organization

In eukaryotes, DNA is packaged with proteins into chromosomes within the cell nucleus; the complete set is the genome. The human genome contains about 3 billion base pairs and roughly 20,000 protein-coding genes. DNA also occurs in mitochondria and chloroplasts. In prokaryotes it is typically a single circular chromosome in the cytoplasm.

Uses

DNA analysis underpins genetic testing, forensic identification, ancestry research, biotechnology and the Human Genome Project's legacy of genomic medicine.
