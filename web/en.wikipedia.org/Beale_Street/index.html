Beale Street

Beale Street is a street in Downtown Memphis, Tennessee, famous as a historic center of African American commerce and music—especially the blues. Running about 1.8 miles (2.9 km) from the Mississippi River to East Street, its clubs and theatres made Memphis a cradle of blues, soul, and rock and roll. In 1977 the U.S. Congress declared Beale Street the "Home of the Blues," and it was designated a National Historic Landmark District.

History

Beale Street was created in 1841 by entrepreneur and developer Robertson Topp, who named it for a little-known Mexican–American War hero, Edward Fitzgerald Beale. Its western end initially served merchants trading along the river. In the 1860s many African American musicians began performing there; the Young Men's Christian Association band and, most famously, W. C. Handy's orchestra turned the street into a musical hub. Handy's "Memphis Blues" (1909) and "Beale Street Blues" (1916) carried its name worldwide.

Heyday and decline

In the early 20th century, businessman Robert Church developed the street as a Black business district with venues such as the Palace and Daisy theatres, Church Park, and the Grand Opera House; performers including Louis Armstrong, Muddy Waters, Howlin' Wolf, B. B. King, and Rufus Thomas played its clubs. The district declined in the 1960s and 1970s amid urban renewal and closures, with many buildings demolished or left derelict.

Revival

Beginning in the 1980s, redevelopment restored Beale Street as an entertainment district of clubs, restaurants, and festivals. Landmarks include the Orpheum Theatre, B. B. King's Blues Club, the Memphis Rock 'n' Soul Museum, and a statue of Elvis Presley. The street hosts the Beale Street Music Festival as part of Memphis in May.
