Satin

Satin is a weave construction that produces a characteristically glossy, smooth surface. In a satin weave, each warp thread floats over several weft threads (or vice versa in the related sateen), with binding points spaced irregularly so that few interruptions disturb the face of the cloth. The long floats reflect light evenly, giving satin its lustre; the reverse side is dull.

History and materials

Originally satin was woven from silk in China and reached Europe through the medieval port of Zaytun (Quanzhou), from which the name is often said to derive. Silk satin became a luxury fabric for gowns, linings, and ecclesiastical vestments. Modern satins are also made from polyester, nylon, acetate, and other filament yarns; sateen applies the same float structure to cotton, showing a softer sheen.

Properties and uses

Because floats snag easily, satin is less hard-wearing than plain weave of the same yarn. It drapes fluidly, which suits evening dress, lingerie, wedding gowns, ribbons, upholstery, and bedding. Common varieties include charmeuse, duchess satin, and slipper satin; the number of threads floated (four or more) defines the repeat.
