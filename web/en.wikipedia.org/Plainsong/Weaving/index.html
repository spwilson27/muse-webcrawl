Weaving

Weaving is a method of textile production in which two distinct sets of yarns — the warp, running lengthwise, and the weft (or filling), running crosswise — are interlaced at right angles to form fabric. Weaving is performed on a loom and is, with knitting, one of the two principal ways of making cloth.

Process

Weaving proceeds in four repeating motions: shedding, lifting some warp threads to open a V-shaped gap (the shed); picking, passing the weft through the shed; beating-up, pushing the new pick into the fell of the cloth with a reed; and taking-up and letting-off, winding finished cloth on and releasing fresh warp. The order in which warp threads rise determines the weave structure: plain weave, twill, and satin are the three basic weaves, from which innumerable derivatives and pattern weaves are built.

History

Woven textiles survive from Neolithic sites; linen weaving was perfected in ancient Egypt, silk in China, and cotton in India and the Americas. Weaving guilds shaped medieval towns, and its mechanization — the flying shuttle, the power loom, the Jacquard head — stood at the centre of the Industrial Revolution in Lancashire, New England, and later South and East Asia, which remain major weaving regions today.
