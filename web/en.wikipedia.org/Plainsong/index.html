Plainsong

Plainsong or plainchant is a body of chants used in the liturgies of the Western Church. When referring to the term plainsong, it is those sacred songs of the Roman Catholic Church, the Anglican Church, and of other historic Christian liturgies, sung in unison, that are classed together. Plainsong is monophonic — a single melodic line, sung by all voices together, without harmony or accompaniment — and is traditionally sung in free rhythm following the words rather than a strict metre.

Gregorian chant

The best-known plainsong tradition is Gregorian chant, the chant of the Roman Rite, traditionally attributed to Pope Gregory I (c. 540–604) but in fact codified in the Frankish kingdoms during the 8th and 9th centuries. Other Western traditions include Ambrosian chant (Milan), Old Roman chant, Mozarabic chant, and Gallican chant, of which the Ambrosian alone survives in regular use.

Notation and modes

Plainsong melodies are organised in the system of eight church modes. Early chant was transmitted orally; from the 9th century, neumes — signs written above the text — indicated melodic gestures, later placed on a four-line staff with square notation, as standardised by Guido d'Arezzo's innovations in the 11th century. Standard modern editions derive from the work of the monks of Solesmes Abbey in the 19th and 20th centuries.

Repertory and use

The repertory includes chants for the Mass (the Proper, which changes daily, and the Ordinary, whose texts are fixed: Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus, Agnus Dei), the Divine Office (psalms, antiphons, hymns), sequences, and processional chants. After the Second Vatican Council, Gregorian chant retained "pride of place" in Roman Catholic liturgy by decree, though vernacular hymnody largely displaced it in practice; Anglican chant and plainsong settings continue in cathedral worship, and recordings of chant have periodically reached a wide popular audience — famously the 1994 album Chant by the Benedictine Monks of Santo Domingo de Silos.
