Loom

A loom is a device used to weave cloth by interlacing warp threads (held lengthwise under tension) with weft threads (inserted crosswise). The principal parts are the warp beam, heddles that raise and lower selected warp threads to open a shed, a shuttle or other device that carries the weft through the shed, and a reed that beats the weft into place; the shed sequence determines whether the cloth is plain weave, twill, or satin.

History

Weaving is Neolithic in origin; horizontal ground looms appear in ancient Egyptian art, and warp-weighted looms spread across Europe. Key innovations included the drawloom for patterned silks in China and the medieval world; John Kay's flying shuttle (1733), which doubled hand-loom output; Edmund Cartwright's power loom (1785), a driver of the Industrial Revolution; Joseph-Marie Jacquard's punched-card pattern control (1804), a forerunner of programmable machines; and twentieth-century shuttleless looms — rapier, projectile, air-jet, and water-jet — that raised speeds enormously.

Types

Looms range from simple frame, backstrap, and inkle looms used by hobbyists and traditional weavers, through treadle floor looms for handwoven cloth such as tartan, to industrial machines weaving metres of cloth per minute. Looms for carpets, tape, and technical textiles follow the same shed-and-pick principle at different scales.
