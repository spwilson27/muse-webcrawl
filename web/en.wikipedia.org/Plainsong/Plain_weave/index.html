Plain weave

Plain weave is the simplest and most common weave structure in textile production. Each weft yarn passes alternately over and under successive warp yarns, and each succeeding row reverses the order, so that the two sets of threads interlock as tightly as possible. The result is a firm, stable fabric with the same appearance on both sides.

Properties

Because plain weave has the maximum number of interlacings per unit area of any basic weave, it produces the firmest and often the strongest cloth for a given yarn, although it also tears and wrinkles more readily than longer-float weaves such as twill or satin. It is the basis of sheer fabrics like voile and lawn as well as heavy ones like canvas and duck, depending on the yarns used.

Examples

Well-known plain-weave fabrics include muslin, cambric, percale, seersucker (made with slack-tension warp yarns), taffeta, and crêpe. In industry it is also used for filtration cloth, cheesecloth, and medical gauze.

Variants

Grouping threads yields basket weave; using threads of contrasting colour yields small-scale checks and stripes; and using warp and weft of differing thicknesses produces ribbed effects such as grosgrain and poplin.
