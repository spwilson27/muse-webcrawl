Octave

In music, an octave (Latin octavus) is the interval between one musical pitch and another with exactly double (or half) its frequency. The 2:1 frequency ratio makes the octave the most consonant interval after the unison; notes an octave apart sound so similar that they share a name, a phenomenon called octave equivalence.

Notation and theory

In the diatonic scale the octave spans eight letter names (C to the next C), hence the name. Scientific pitch notation labels octaves numerically, with middle C as C4 and the A above it A4, the concert pitch reference at 440 Hz. In equal temperament the octave is divided into twelve equal semitones, each a frequency ratio of the twelfth root of two.

Perception and use

Octave equivalence is found in nearly all musical cultures and appears even in the songs of some animals. Men and women singing together naturally sing in octaves. In chant and organum, doubling a melody at the octave was among the earliest forms of polyphony; piano keyboards span just over seven octaves, and the typical human hearing range covers about ten.
