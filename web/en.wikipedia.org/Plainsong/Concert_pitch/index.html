Concert pitch

Concert pitch is the pitch reference to which a group of musical instruments is tuned for a performance. The modern standard, fixed by international agreement in the twentieth century, sets A above middle C (A4) at 440 hertz. The term also distinguishes an instrument's sounding pitch from its written pitch for transposing instruments such as the B♭ clarinet.

History

Pitch was once purely local: organ and town standards across Europe varied by as much as a major third. The invention of the tuning fork by John Shore in 1711 gave performers a portable reference, but standards crept upward through the nineteenth century as makers sought brilliance, straining singers' voices. France legislated the diapason normal at A = 435 Hz in 1859; a British standard of A = 439 followed, and an international conference in 1939 adopted A = 440, confirmed by the International Organization for Standardization in 1955 and 1975 (ISO 16).

Variations today

Many major orchestras tune slightly sharp — A = 441 to 443 Hz in parts of Europe — while period-instrument ensembles tune to historical standards such as A = 415 Hz for Baroque music, roughly a semitone below modern pitch. Choirs singing plainsong traditionally take pitch relative to the chant's range rather than from a fixed standard.
