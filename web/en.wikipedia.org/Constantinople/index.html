Constantinople was the capital city of the Roman Empire (330–395), the Byzantine Empire (395–1204 and 1261–1453), the Crusader Latin Empire (1204–1261), and the Ottoman Empire (1453–1922). Founded by the Roman emperor Constantine the Great on the site of the Greek city of Byzantium, it stood at the crossroads of Europe and Asia, commanding the straits between the Black Sea and the Mediterranean.

History

Foundation and early centuries

Constantine dedicated his new capital, officially New Rome but soon called Constantinople ("Constantine's City"), on 11 May 330. Enlarged by successive emperors, it acquired monumental fora, baths, palaces and churches. Under Theodosius II the triple Theodosian Walls made it nearly impregnable, and under Justinian I the rebuilt Hagia Sophia (537) crowned the city with the largest cathedral in the world.

Middle Byzantine period

Constantinople survived sieges by Persians and Avars (626), Arabs (674–678 and 717–718), Bulgars and Rus', protected by its walls, its navy and Greek fire. It was the largest and richest city in Christian Europe, a centre of trade, learning and Orthodox Christianity — and the repository of Roman law and classical literature. In 1204, Crusaders of the Fourth Crusade sacked the city and established the Latin Empire; Michael VIII Palaiologos recovered it in 1261, but the empire never regained its strength.

Fall and Ottoman era

On 29 May 1453 the city fell to Sultan Mehmed II after a siege of 53 days; the last emperor, Constantine XI, died in the fighting. Renamed in common usage Istanbul (officially adopted in 1930), the city remained the Ottoman capital, adorned with mosques by Sinan and the Topkapı Palace, until the empire's collapse. Ankara became the capital of the Turkish Republic in 1923.

Legacy

For over a thousand years Constantinople preserved Roman law, Greek literature and Orthodox theology, and its fall is conventionally treated as the end of the Middle Ages. Its site, straddling the Bosphorus, remains Turkey's largest city as modern Istanbul.
