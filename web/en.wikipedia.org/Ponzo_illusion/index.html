Ponzo illusion

The Ponzo illusion is a geometrical-optical illusion first demonstrated by the Italian psychologist Mario Ponzo (1882–1960) in 1911. Two identical lines drawn across a pair of converging lines — like railway tracks receding into the distance — appear to be of different lengths: the line nearer the point of convergence looks longer, although the two are exactly the same size.

The two horizontal bars are identical in length.

Explanation

The leading explanation invokes misapplied size constancy: the visual system reads the converging lines as perspective cues to depth, interprets the upper line as farther away, and, because both lines cast the same retinal image, unconsciously scales the "farther" one up — concluding it must be larger. A rival low-level account attributes the effect to framing and contrast with the surrounding converging lines. The Ponzo illusion has been demonstrated in non-human animals, including primates, birds, and even insects, and is considered a relative of the moon illusion, in which the horizon moon appears larger than the zenith moon.

Variations

The illusion appears in touch and audition analogues as well as vision, and modified versions using real three-dimensional scenes show that the effect persists, though sometimes reduced, when genuine depth cues are present.
