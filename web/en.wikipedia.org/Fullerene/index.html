A fullerene is an allotrope of carbon whose molecules consist of carbon atoms connected by single and double bonds to form a closed or partially closed mesh, with fused rings of five to seven atoms. The best-known fullerene is buckminsterfullerene (C60), a molecule of 60 carbon atoms arranged as a truncated icosahedron — the shape of a football (soccer ball) — with 12 pentagonal and 20 hexagonal faces. Because of their resemblance to Buckminster Fuller's geodesic domes, the molecules were named after him, and C60 is nicknamed the "buckyball".

Discovery

Fullerenes were discovered in 1985 by Harold Kroto, Robert Curl and Richard Smalley at Rice University, who vaporised graphite with a laser and detected an unusually stable C60 cluster. They proposed the cage structure, confirmed when bulk quantities were produced in 1990 by Krätschmer and Huffman using arc discharge between graphite electrodes. Kroto, Curl and Smalley received the 1996 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.

Types

Besides spherical buckyballs (C60, C70 and larger cages), the fullerene family includes cylindrical carbon nanotubes, sometimes called buckytubes. Fullerenes occur naturally in trace amounts in soot and have been detected in interstellar space; C60 has been identified in planetary nebulae and meteorites.

Properties and applications

C60 forms a dark, crystalline solid that dissolves in aromatic solvents to give magenta solutions. The cage can encapsulate atoms or small molecules (endohedral fullerenes, written M@C60), accept electrons, and be chemically functionalised. Alkali-doped C60 becomes superconducting at low temperatures. Research applications span materials science, electronics, lubricants and drug delivery.
