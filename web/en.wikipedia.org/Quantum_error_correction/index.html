Quantum error correction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Quantum error correction (QEC) is a collection of techniques used in quantum computing to protect quantum information from errors due to decoherence and other quantum noise. Because quantum states cannot be copied (the no-cloning theorem) and measurement disturbs them, classical redundancy methods cannot be applied directly; QEC instead spreads logical information across entangled states of many physical qubits.

Principles

A quantum error-correcting code encodes a logical qubit into a subspace of a larger Hilbert space. Errors move the state out of the code space, and syndrome measurements of stabilizer operators reveal which error occurred without revealing the encoded data, allowing it to be reversed. The first codes were proposed in 1995 by Peter Shor (the nine-qubit Shor code) and Andrew Steane (the seven-qubit Steane code).

Fault tolerance

The threshold theorem states that if the physical error rate is below a certain threshold, arbitrarily long quantum computations can be carried out reliably, with overhead growing only polylogarithmically. Topological codes, above all the surface code, are the leading approach in current hardware because they require only nearest-neighbour interactions and have relatively high thresholds, around 1%.

Challenges

Practical QEC demands many physical qubits per logical qubit — often hundreds to thousands — which is a principal driver of the scale targets of superconducting, trapped-ion, and neutral-atom quantum processors.
