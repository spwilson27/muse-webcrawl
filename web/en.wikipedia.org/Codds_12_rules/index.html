Codds 12 rules

Codd's twelve rules are a set of thirteen rules (numbered zero to twelve) proposed by Edgar F. Codd, the inventor of the relational model of data, as a standard for determining whether a database management system qualifies as a relational database management system (RDBMS). Codd published the rules in 1985 in a pair of articles in Computerworld, partly in response to vendors marketing non-relational products as "relational".

The rules

Rule 0, the foundation rule, states that a system advertised as an RDBMS must be able to manage databases entirely through its relational capabilities. The remaining rules include: the information rule (all information is represented in exactly one way, as values in tables); the guaranteed access rule (every datum is accessible by table name, primary key value, and column name); systematic treatment of null values; a dynamic online catalog based on the relational model; a comprehensive data sublanguage; view updating; high-level insert, update, and delete; physical data independence; logical data independence; integrity independence; distribution independence; and the nonsubversion rule, which forbids low-level interfaces that bypass relational integrity constraints.

Significance

Few if any commercial systems have ever satisfied all of the rules perfectly, and the rules are best understood as an idealized yardstick rather than a practical specification. Nevertheless, they were highly influential in shaping the expectations placed on relational products and in defining the terms of debate about what "relational" means. Codd later expanded his requirements into a much larger set of features in his 1990 book The Relational Model for Database Management: Version 2.
