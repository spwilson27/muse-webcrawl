Smooth pursuit

FieldNeuroscience, ophthalmology
TypeConjugate eye movement
Typical speed rangeUp to about 30°/s (human)
Contrast withSaccades

Smooth pursuit describes a type of eye movement in which the eyes remain fixated on a moving object, matching its velocity so that its image stays on the fovea. It is one of the two ways the visual system can voluntarily shift gaze, the other being saccadic eye movements.

Mechanism

Pursuit is driven by visual motion signals from the retina, processed in areas MT and MST of the visual cortex and relayed through the pons and cerebellum — particularly the flocculus — to the oculomotor nuclei. Because processing takes time, the system works predictively: after a latency of roughly 100 milliseconds, the eye accelerates to match target speed, and pursuit gain (eye velocity divided by target velocity) is normally close to 1 for targets moving below about 30 degrees per second.

When pursuit fails

If a target moves too fast, or pursuit gain falls, the image slips off the fovea and the system inserts small "catch-up" saccades; pursuit and saccades then alternate in a pattern called optokinetic nystagmus when a whole visual field moves. Smooth pursuit cannot be produced voluntarily in the absence of a moving target — attempting it, most people's eyes move in small saccades instead.

Clinical significance

Impaired smooth pursuit is a well-replicated finding in schizophrenia and is studied as a possible endophenotype of the disorder. Pursuit performance also declines with age, fatigue, alcohol and certain medications, and abnormal pursuit can help localise lesions in the brainstem and cerebellum.
