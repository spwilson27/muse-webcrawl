English Restoration

The English Restoration, or simply the Restoration, began in 1660 when the English, Scottish, and Irish monarchies were restored under King Charles II. It followed the Interregnum — the period of republican Commonwealth rule under Oliver Cromwell and his successors that began with the execution of Charles I in 1649. The term also describes Charles II's reign and the cultural era associated with it.

Background

After Cromwell's death in 1658, his son Richard proved unable to govern, and the army and Parliament fell into disorder. In 1660 General George Monck marched from Scotland, secured London, and engineered free elections. The Convention Parliament invited Charles II to return; his Declaration of Breda promised pardons, religious toleration within limits, and settlement of army pay and land disputes. Charles landed at Dover in May 1660 and entered London on his thirtieth birthday, 29 May.

The Restoration settlement

The monarchy, the House of Lords, and the Anglican Church were re-established. The Cavalier Parliament (1661–1679) enacted the Clarendon Code, a series of laws reasserting Anglican supremacy and penalising dissenters, and passed the Licensing Act controlling the press. Leading regicides were executed. Charles's reign went on to see the Second and Third Anglo-Dutch Wars, the Great Plague of 1665, the Great Fire of London in 1666, the secret Treaty of Dover with France, and the Popish Plot and Exclusion Crisis of 1678–1681, which gave rise to the Whig and Tory parties.

Culture

The Restoration reopened the theatres closed under Puritan rule, and introduced actresses to the English stage for the first time. Restoration comedy — witty, sexually frank plays by writers such as William Wycherley, William Congreve, and Aphra Behn, one of the first professional women writers — epitomises the era's reaction against Puritan restraint. The Royal Society was founded in 1660, and the period saw major work by Isaac Newton, Robert Boyle, and Christopher Wren, whose St Paul's Cathedral rose after the Great Fire.
