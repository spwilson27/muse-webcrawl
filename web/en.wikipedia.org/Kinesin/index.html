A kinesin is a protein belonging to a superfamily of motor proteins found in all eukaryotic cells. Kinesins move along microtubule filaments, powered by the hydrolysis of ATP, carrying cargo such as vesicles, organelles, and chromosomes, and they play essential roles in cell division.

Structure

The prototypical kinesin-1 is a tetramer of two heavy chains and two light chains. Each heavy chain has a globular motor domain (the "head") that binds microtubules and ATP, a flexible neck linker, and a long coiled-coil stalk ending in a tail that, with the light chains, attaches to cargo. Humans have about 45 kinesin genes, grouped into around 14 families with specialised functions.

Mechanism of movement

Kinesin-1 walks processively toward the plus end of a microtubule in 8-nanometre steps — the spacing of tubulin dimers — using a hand-over-hand mechanism: the two heads alternate, with ATP binding, hydrolysis, and product release coordinated so that one head stays attached while the other swings forward. A single motor can take hundreds of steps before detaching, transporting cargo over long distances in axons and other cells.

Functions

Kinesins haul membrane organelles and secretory vesicles, position the endoplasmic reticulum and Golgi apparatus, and slide microtubules. During mitosis, kinesin families organise the spindle, separate spindle poles, align chromosomes at the metaphase plate, and drive chromosome movement. Defects in kinesin genes are linked to neurological disorders such as hereditary spastic paraplegia and Charcot–Marie–Tooth disease, and mitotic kinesins are targets in cancer drug research.
