Workers' self-management

Workers' self-management, or autogestion, is a form of workplace organisation in which the workers of an enterprise collectively make decisions about its operations — what to produce, how work is organised, and how surpluses are distributed — in place of owners or appointed managers. It is a central idea in libertarian socialism, anarcho-syndicalism and some forms of market socialism, and is related to the worker cooperative, though self-management can also occur in enterprises owned by the state or by municipalities.

History

Experiments in self-management appeared during the Paris Commune of 1871, in factory committees during the Russian Revolution, and on a large scale in revolutionary Spain in 1936–37. The best-known national system operated in Yugoslavia from the 1950s, where socially owned enterprises were formally governed by elected workers' councils, though in practice the ruling party retained much control. Self-managed firms also emerged in Algeria after independence, in Peru under military reformers, and in Argentina after the 2001 crisis, where workers occupied and restarted bankrupt factories as empresas recuperadas.

Arguments and criticisms

Supporters argue self-management realises workplace democracy, reduces alienation and aligns incentives. Critics raise questions about access to capital, willingness to invest earnings rather than distribute them, decision speed, and behaviour of labour-managed firms in markets. Empirical studies of cooperatives suggest comparable productivity to conventional firms, with greater employment stability.
