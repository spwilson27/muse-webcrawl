Language revival

Language revitalization, also called language revival or reversing language shift, is an attempt to halt or reverse the decline of a language, or to revive an extinct one. Such efforts involve governments, linguists, and members of the speech community itself, and may include language planning, education programs, documentation, and media production.

Background

Of the roughly 7,000 languages spoken in the world, a large proportion are endangered: UNESCO estimates that a language disappears roughly every two weeks as its last native speakers die. Language loss is driven by globalization, urbanization, assimilation policies, and the prestige of dominant languages. Revitalization seeks to increase the number of speakers, broaden the domains in which the language is used, and restore intergenerational transmission.

Notable examples

The most famous case is the revival of Hebrew, which ceased to be an everyday spoken language in antiquity and was restored as a vernacular in the late 19th and 20th centuries through the efforts of Eliezer Ben-Yehuda and the Zionist movement. Other well-known programs include the revitalization of Irish, Welsh, Basque, Catalan, Hawaiian (through the Pūnana Leo "language nest" preschools), Māori (Te Kōhanga Reo), and numerous Indigenous languages of North America and Australia, such as Wampanoag (Wôpanâak), which was revived from written records after having no native speakers for over a century.

Methods

Common methods include immersion schooling, master-apprentice programs pairing elders with learners, documentation and archiving of recordings, development of orthographies and teaching materials, and use of digital media and apps. Joshua Fishman's Graded Intergenerational Disruption Scale (GIDS) is frequently used to assess a language's vitality and prioritize interventions.
