Multi-link suspension

Multi-link suspension is a type of independent vehicle suspension design which uses three or more lateral arms, and one or more longitudinal arms, to connect a wheel to the car body. Unlike simpler designs such as the MacPherson strut or double wishbone, a multi-link layout separates the arms that control camber, toe and longitudinal location, allowing engineers to tune ride comfort and handling largely independently of one another.

Design

A typical multi-link axle uses four or five links per wheel. Because the links can be arranged with considerable geometric freedom, the designer can control wheel alignment changes through the suspension's travel, reduce unwanted toe change ("bump steer"), and isolate road noise by mounting the links to a subframe through rubber bushings. The design was popularised on production cars by Mercedes-Benz in the 1980s (notably the 190E's five-link rear axle) and is now common on the rear — and increasingly the front — axles of premium and performance cars.

Advantages and disadvantages

Advantages include excellent ride/handling balance, good isolation, and compact packaging vertically. Disadvantages are greater cost, weight and complexity, more bushings and ball joints to wear, and more difficult wheel alignment service compared with simpler suspensions.
