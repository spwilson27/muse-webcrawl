Umbilical hernia

An umbilical hernia is a health condition where the abdominal wall behind the navel is damaged in some way, and tissue such as part of the intestine may protrude through this weakened area, producing a bulge or swelling at the umbilicus (navel). It is most common in infants, in whom it results from incomplete closure of the opening in the abdominal wall through which the umbilical cord passed, but it also occurs in adults, particularly in association with obesity, multiple pregnancies, ascites, or prior abdominal surgery.

Signs and symptoms

The hernia typically presents as a soft, painless bulge at the navel that becomes more prominent when intra-abdominal pressure rises — when an infant cries or an adult coughs or strains. In most children the bulge is harmless. Pain, vomiting, tenderness, or discoloration of the bulge may indicate incarceration (the hernia cannot be pushed back) or strangulation (its blood supply is cut off), which are surgical emergencies.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually made by physical examination alone. Imaging, such as ultrasound or CT, is used mainly in adults with obesity or when the diagnosis is uncertain or complications are suspected.

Treatment

Most umbilical hernias in children close spontaneously by four to five years of age, so watchful waiting is standard; surgery is advised if the hernia persists beyond that age, is very large, or becomes incarcerated. In adults, repair is usually surgical — either open or laparoscopic — and may involve suturing the defect or reinforcing it with mesh, particularly for larger hernias, to reduce recurrence.

Epidemiology

Umbilical hernias occur in an estimated 10–20% of newborns, with higher rates in premature infants and in infants of African descent. In adults they are more common in women and in people with conditions that raise intra-abdominal pressure.
