The Fugger family was a historically prominent group of European bankers, members of the Augsburg patriciate, originally a family of weavers and merchants. Active in international trade from the 14th century, the Fuggers became the pre-eminent financiers of early modern Europe, bankrolling emperors and popes and effectively controlling much of Europe's copper and silver production. Within a few generations their wealth rivalled that of the Medici, and Jakob Fugger "the Rich" is often described as one of the wealthiest individuals of all time relative to his era.

Rise

The family fortune began with Hans Fugger, a weaver who settled in Augsburg in 1367, and grew through the textile trade of his sons. Jakob Fugger the Elder expanded into commerce and mining in Tyrol and Hungary. His son Jakob II, "the Rich" (1459–1525), transformed the firm into a pan-European bank: he lent heavily to the Habsburgs, financed Maximilian I, and in 1519 provided the enormous loans that secured the election of Charles V as Holy Roman Emperor — an investment repaid with mining rights, offices and monopolies.

Activities

The Fuggers operated a network of factors and counting houses from Venice and Rome to Antwerp, Lisbon and Seville. They financed the papal sale of indulgences (provoking Martin Luther's protest), the first Portuguese voyages to India, Albrecht of Mainz's purchase of his archbishopric, and Habsburg wars. In Augsburg, Jakob founded the Fuggerei in 1516–1521, the world's oldest social housing complex still in use, where needy Catholic citizens live for a nominal annual rent.

Decline

Under Jakob's nephew Anton Fugger the firm reached its greatest extent, but repeated Spanish royal bankruptcies, the shifting of trade to the Atlantic, and war losses eroded the fortune in the later 16th century. The family's mercantile empire wound down over the following century, though Fugger lines persisted as landed nobility — counts and later princes of the Empire — and survive in Augsburg to this day.
