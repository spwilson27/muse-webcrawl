Japanese New Year

Japanese New Year (Shōgatsu)
Japanese正月 (Shōgatsu); 元日 (Ganjitsu, 1 January)
Observed1–3 January (festivities from late December)
TypeNational holiday, cultural festival

The Japanese New Year (Japanese: 正月, Shōgatsu) is the most important holiday in Japan's calendar. Since 1873 the country has officially followed the Gregorian calendar, celebrating New Year on 1 January (元日, Ganjitsu), with 1–3 January a general holiday period when most businesses close and families gather at home.

Preparations

The year ends with thorough housecleaning (ōsōji) to welcome the toshigami, the deity of the new year. Homes are decorated with kadomatsu (pine and bamboo arrangements) at the gate and shimekazari ropes above doors, and with kagami mochi — stacked rice cakes topped with a bitter orange. New Year greeting cards (nengajō) are mailed in vast numbers to arrive precisely on 1 January.

New Year's Eve and Day

On Ōmisoka (31 December), families eat toshikoshi soba — long buckwheat noodles symbolising longevity — and watch the NHK song contest Kōhaku Uta Gassen. At midnight Buddhist temples across Japan ring their bells 108 times (joya no kane), one for each earthly desire in Buddhist teaching. Many people make their first shrine or temple visit of the year (hatsumōde) in the first days of January, drawing crowds in the millions at major shrines such as Meiji Jingū in Tokyo and Fushimi Inari in Kyoto. Children receive money gifts called otoshidama in decorated envelopes.

Food

The festive cuisine, prepared in advance so no cooking is needed over the holiday, is osechi-ryōri: colourful small dishes packed in lacquered boxes, each with a symbolic meaning — black soybeans for health, herring roe for fertility, sweet rolled omelette for wealth. A spiced sake (otoso) is drunk to ward off illness, and zōni soup with mochi varies by region.
