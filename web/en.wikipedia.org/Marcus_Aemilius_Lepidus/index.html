Marcus Aemilius Lepidus

Marcus Aemilius Lepidus
Born: c. 89 BC
Died: late 13 or early 12 BC, Circeii
Occupation: Roman statesman
Offices: Consul (46, 42 BC), triumvir, Pontifex Maximus

Marcus Aemilius Lepidus (c. 89 BC – late 13/early 12 BC) was a Roman Patrician and statesman who formed the Second Triumvirate alongside Octavian and Mark Antony in 43 BC. Ancient sources often portray him as the weakest of the three triumvirs.

Early career

Lepidus came from the distinguished gens Aemilia; his father, also Marcus Aemilius Lepidus, led a failed revolt against the Senate in 77 BC. The younger Lepidus supported Julius Caesar, served as praetor in 49 BC, governed Nearer Spain, and was consul in 46 BC. As Caesar's master of horse he was the dictator's senior subordinate in Rome.

The Triumvirate

After Caesar's assassination, Lepidus — commanding legions in Gaul and Spain — allied with Antony. In November 43 BC the three men met near Bononia and constituted the Triumvirate by law, dividing the Roman world: Lepidus took Spain and later Africa. He also became Pontifex Maximus, succeeding Caesar. His role in the proscriptions that followed was significant, though later propaganda shifted blame among the three.

Downfall

After the defeat of the assassins at Philippi, Lepidus was progressively marginalised. In 36 BC, intervening in Sicily against Sextus Pompey, he tried to assert control over the island; Octavian won over his legions and stripped him of his triumviral power, though he was allowed to keep the office of Pontifex Maximus and live in comfortable internal exile at Circeii until his death.
