Ideasthesia

Ideasthesia (from Greek idea and aisthesis, "sensing concepts") is a neuropsychological phenomenon in which activations of concepts (inducers) evoke perception-like experiences ( concurrents). The term was introduced by psychologist Danko Nikolić to capture cases — most famously synesthesia — where the triggering stimulus is not a simple sensory input but a concept: for example, a grapheme–color synesthete experiences color not from the visual shape of a letter but from its conceptual identity as that letter.

Relation to synesthesia

Research on ideasthesia suggests that most phenomena classified as synesthesia are in fact ideasthesic: experiments show that the same visual shape evokes different colors when interpreted as different letters, and that experiences depend on learned meaning rather than raw sensation. This has led to the proposal that ideasthesia underlies ordinary cognition as well — for instance, the association of shapes with sounds in the bouba/kiki effect.

Implications

Because concept–sensation links are learned through culture and experience, ideasthesia provides a framework for studying how meaning shapes perception, and has been invoked in discussions of art, metaphor, and the development of writing systems.
