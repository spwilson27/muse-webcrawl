Streaking (microbiology)

In microbiology, streaking is a technique used to isolate a pure strain of a microorganism (often bacteria) from a single species. The sample — usually taken from a mixed culture or a specimen — is progressively thinned out over the surface of a solid agar medium in a Petri dish. The main principle is that by streaking, the concentration of organisms decreases successively until individual cells are deposited far enough apart on the agar surface that, after incubation, they grow into separated, genetically identical colonies.

Technique

A sterile inoculating loop is used to pick up a small amount of sample and spread it in a zig-zag pattern over one quadrant of the agar plate. The loop is sterilized (typically by flaming and cooling), then dragged from the first quadrant into a second, and likewise into third and fourth quadrants — the common "four-quadrant streak" or "T-streak" and "three-sector" variants. After incubation, often overnight at 37 °C for human pathogens, isolated colonies appear in the later quadrants where cells were most diluted.

Streaking is a form of the more general principle of dilution used in the pour plate and spread plate methods. Variants include the continuous streak, radiant streak, and zig-zag streak.

Applications

Isolated colonies allow microbiologists to identify organisms, test antibiotic susceptibility, and obtain pure cultures for further study. Streak plates are a routine first step in clinical microbiology for specimens such as urine, throat swabs, and wound cultures.
