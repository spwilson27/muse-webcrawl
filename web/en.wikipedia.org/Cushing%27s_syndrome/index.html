Cushing's syndrome

Cushing's syndrome is a collection of signs and symptoms due to prolonged exposure to glucocorticoids such as cortisol. It is named after the American neurosurgeon Harvey Cushing, who described the condition in 1932.

Signs and symptoms

Common features include rapid weight gain with central obesity, a rounded "moon" face, fatty deposits between the shoulders ("buffalo hump"), purple or pink stretch marks (striae), thinning skin that bruises easily, muscle weakness, high blood pressure, elevated blood sugar or diabetes, mood changes and, in women, irregular menstruation and increased hair growth.

Causes

The most common cause overall is the long-term use of glucocorticoid medication (exogenous Cushing's syndrome). Endogenous causes include Cushing's disease — a pituitary adenoma secreting excess ACTH — adrenal tumours secreting cortisol, and ectopic ACTH secretion by tumours elsewhere in the body.

Diagnosis and treatment

Screening tests include the overnight dexamethasone suppression test, 24-hour urinary free cortisol and late-night salivary cortisol, followed by ACTH measurement and imaging to locate the source. Treatment is directed at the cause: surgery to remove a causative tumour is preferred where possible, together with dose reduction of steroid medication and drugs that lower cortisol production.
