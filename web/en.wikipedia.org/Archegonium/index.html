Archegonium

An archegonium (plural: archegonia) is a multicellular structure that produces and contains the ovum or female gamete in certain plants. It is the female reproductive organ of bryophytes (mosses, liverworts, and hornworts), ferns and fern allies, and most gymnosperms. The corresponding male organ is the antheridium.

Structure

The archegonium is flask-shaped, consisting of a long slender neck of canal cells and a swollen base, the venter, which encloses a single large egg cell and a ventral canal cell. In bryophytes the archegonia are borne on the gametophyte generation, often in clusters protected by modified leaves called perichaetia.

Fertilization

At maturity the canal cells of the neck disintegrate, forming a passage filled with mucilage and chemical attractants. Motile sperm, released from antheridia and requiring a film of water to swim, travel down the neck to fertilize the egg. The resulting zygote develops into the embryo sporophyte, which remains attached to and dependent upon the gametophyte in bryophytes.

Evolutionary significance

Land plants that retain archegonia are sometimes grouped as archegoniates. In the course of seed plant evolution the archegonium was reduced: it persists in most gymnosperms but is absent in flowering plants, where the female gametophyte is reduced to the embryo sac within the ovule.
