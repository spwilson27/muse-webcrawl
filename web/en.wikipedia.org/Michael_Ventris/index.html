Michael Ventris

Michael George Francis Ventris, OBE (12 July 1922 – 6 September 1956) was an English architect, classicist and philologist who deciphered Linear B, the ancient Greek script used by the Mycenaean civilisation. Working with John Chadwick, he demonstrated in 1952 that Linear B recorded an early form of Greek, overturning the prevailing view that it represented a non-Greek language.

Early life

Ventris was born in Wheathampstead, Hertfordshire, into a military family; his father was a lieutenant colonel in the Indian Army. As a child he lived for several years in Switzerland, where he picked up French and Swiss German and showed an early gift for languages. Educated at Stowe School, he heard Sir Arthur Evans, the excavator of Knossos, lecture in 1936 and became fascinated by the undeciphered Cretan scripts. He published his first article on the subject at eighteen.

During the Second World War Ventris served in the Royal Air Force as a navigator on bombing raids over Germany. After the war he completed his training as an architect, while continuing to study Linear B as an amateur pursuit in his spare time.

Decipherment of Linear B

Linear B tablets had been excavated at Knossos on Crete and later at Pylos on the Greek mainland. Building on the grid method pioneered by the American scholar Alice Kober, Ventris arranged the signs into a syllabic grid and, in 1952, realised that certain sign groups corresponded to Cretan place names such as Amnisos and Knossos. Substituting the resulting sound values revealed recognisably Greek vocabulary and inflections. On 1 July 1952 he announced the breakthrough in a BBC radio broadcast.

Working with the Cambridge philologist John Chadwick, Ventris published the decipherment in the Journal of Hellenic Studies in 1953. Their joint work, Documents in Mycenaean Greek, appeared in 1956 and established the foundations of Mycenaean studies.

Death and legacy

Ventris was killed in a car accident near Hatfield on 6 September 1956, aged only 34, weeks before the publication of Documents in Mycenaean Greek. He had been appointed an Officer of the Order of the British Empire (OBE) in 1955 for services to Mycenaean paleography. His decipherment transformed the study of the Bronze Age Aegean, pushing the recorded history of the Greek language back by several centuries.
