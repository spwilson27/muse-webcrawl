IS–LM model

The IS–LM model (investment–savings / liquidity preference–money supply), also known as the Hicks–Hansen model, is a macroeconomic model that shows the relationship between interest rates and real output in the goods and services market (IS curve) and the money market (LM curve). Their intersection represents a general equilibrium in which both markets clear simultaneously.

History

John Hicks introduced the framework in his 1937 paper "Mr. Keynes and the 'Classics'", as an interpretation of Keynes's General Theory (1936). Alvin Hansen later popularized it, and it became the workhorse of the neoclassical synthesis taught for decades after the Second World War.

The curves

The IS curve slopes downward: higher interest rates reduce investment and thus aggregate demand and income. The LM curve slopes upward: higher income raises the demand for money, which, with a fixed money supply, requires a higher interest rate to maintain money-market equilibrium. Fiscal expansion shifts IS right; monetary expansion shifts LM right (or down), lowering interest rates and raising output.

Criticism and successors

Critics, including some Keynesians, objected that the model distorts Keynes's ideas about uncertainty and expectations, and modern macroeconomics largely replaced it with models in which central banks set interest rates directly, such as the IS–PC–MP model. It nevertheless remains widely taught as an introduction to short-run macroeconomic policy analysis.
