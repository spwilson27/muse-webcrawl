Incongruity theory

Incongruity theory is a theory of humour which proposes that laughter arises from the perception of something incongruous — a violation of our mental patterns and expectations. It is, along with relief theory and superiority theory, one of the three classical accounts of humour.

History

Elements of the idea appear in Aristotle and were developed by Kant, who described laughter as an affection arising from the sudden transformation of a strained expectation into nothing, and by Schopenhauer, who located the ludicrous in the mismatch between a concept and the reality subsumed under it.

Mechanism

On this view a joke sets up an expectation and then violates it; the hearer resolves or savours the incongruity. Modern versions, such as incongruity-resolution theory, hold that the punch line must also make a kind of sense in hindsight.

Criticism

Critics note that not all incongruity is funny — surprises can also frighten or confuse — so the theory is often combined with ideas about benign violation and play.
