Coriolis force

In physics, the Coriolis force is an inertial (fictitious) force that acts on objects in motion within a rotating frame of reference. It deflects moving objects to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to the left in the Southern Hemisphere, perpendicular to the direction of motion and to the axis of rotation. The effect is named for French scientist Gaspard-Gustave de Coriolis, who described it in 1835.

Meteorology

The Coriolis effect is central to large-scale weather: it causes air masses and ocean currents to curve, drives the rotation of cyclones (counterclockwise in the Northern Hemisphere, clockwise in the Southern), and shapes trade winds and jet streams. It is negligible for small-scale phenomena such as draining water in sinks, contrary to popular myth.

Other applications

The effect must be corrected for in long-range artillery, ballistic missiles, and satellite trajectories. It also underlies the precession of the Foucault pendulum, which provided vivid evidence of Earth's rotation.
