The Outer Hebrides (Scottish Gaelic: Na h-Eileanan Siar, "the Western Isles") are an island chain off the west coast of mainland Scotland, forming a 210-kilometre arc from Lewis and Harris in the north to Barra in the south. Separated from the Inner Hebrides by the Minch, the islands constitute one of Scotland's 32 council areas and one of its last strongholds of the Scottish Gaelic language.

Geography and history

More than 70 named islands make up the group, of which about 15 are inhabited. The east coasts are rocky and indented with sea lochs, while the west coasts feature fertile machair — low-lying sandy grassland — and long white beaches. Settled since the Mesolithic, the islands were ruled by Norse settlers from the 9th century, passed to Scotland by the Treaty of Perth in 1266, and later formed part of the MacLeod, Mackenzie, and other clan territories. Landmarks include the Neolithic Callanish Stones on Lewis and the medieval Kisimul Castle on Barra.

Culture and economy

A majority of residents speak Gaelic, the highest proportion anywhere in Scotland, and the islands have a distinctive religious profile: the northern islands (Lewis, Harris, North Uist) are predominantly Presbyterian, while the southern islands (Benbecula, South Uist, Barra) are largely Roman Catholic. The economy rests on crofting, fishing, Harris Tweed weaving, fish farming, public services, and a growing tourism sector. A chain of causeways and ferries links the islands, with Stornoway the largest settlement.
