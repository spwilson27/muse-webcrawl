Reasonable person

In English law the reasonable person was famously pictured as "the man on the Clapham omnibus".

In law, a reasonable person, reasonable man, or the man on the Clapham omnibus is a hypothetical person whose behaviour provides a legal standard — especially in negligence law — against which the conduct of actual persons is measured. The reasonable person is an ordinary member of society: neither a paragon of caution nor unusually careless, but someone who exercises average care, skill and judgment under the circumstances.

Negligence

The doctrine took shape in the nineteenth-century English case Vaughan v. Menlove (1837), which rejected a purely subjective standard, and the figure was given enduring form in Blyth v. Birmingham Waterworks Co. (1856), which defined negligence as failing to do what a reasonable man would do, or doing what a prudent and reasonable man would not. Justice Greer coined the "man on the Clapham omnibus" phrase in Hall v. Brooklands Auto-Racing Club (1933). In the United States, Judge Learned Hand's formula in United States v. Carroll Towing Co. (1947) expressed the standard economically: a person is negligent where the burden of adequate precautions is less than the probability of harm multiplied by its gravity.

Characteristics and adjustments

The standard is objective, but it is applied with reference to the actor's situation: children are judged by the standard of a reasonable child of similar age and experience, while professionals are held to the standard of a reasonable member of their profession. Physical disabilities may be taken into account; mental characteristics generally are not. The reasonable person is deemed to know common facts and widely known risks, but not specialist knowledge unless held out as possessing it.

Other uses

Reasonable-person standards recur across the law: the reasonable person provoked in criminal law, the reasonable belief of an officer in self-defence cases, the reasonable reader of a publication in defamation, and the "reasonable observer" in constitutional tests. Feminist and other critics have questioned whether the "reasonable man" standard embeds a male or otherwise partial perspective, prompting reforms such as the "reasonable woman" standard in some sexual-harassment jurisprudence.
