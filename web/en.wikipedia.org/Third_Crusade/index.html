Third Crusade

LocationLevant, Anatolia, Cyprus, Sicily

The Third Crusade (1189–1192), also known as the Kings' Crusade, was an attempt by three European monarchs — Frederick I Barbarossa of the Holy Roman Empire, Richard I of England, and Philip II of France — to reconquer the Holy Land following Saladin's capture of Jerusalem in 1187 after the Battle of Hattin.

Background

In 1187 Saladin destroyed the Crusader field army at Hattin and took Jerusalem, Tyre alone holding out. Pope Gregory VIII issued the bull Audita tremendi calling for a new crusade, and the three kings took the cross.

The German crusade

Frederick Barbarossa led the largest army overland through Anatolia, defeating the Seljuks at Iconium, but drowned on 10 June 1190 while crossing the Saleph River (Göksu) in Cilicia. His army largely dispersed, though a remnant under his son Frederick of Swabia reached Acre.

Richard and Philip

Philip and Richard travelled by sea, quarrelled in Sicily, and arrived at the siege of Acre, which fell in July 1191. En route Richard conquered Cyprus from Isaac Komnenos. Philip returned to France, leaving Richard in effective command. Richard defeated Saladin at Arsuf (September 1191) and Jaffa (1192), twice advanced within sight of Jerusalem but judged it indefensible to hold, and recovered the coastal strip from Tyre to Jaffa.

Treaty of Jaffa

The crusade concluded with the Treaty of Jaffa (2 September 1192): the coast remained in Crusader hands, Ascalon's fortifications were demolished, and unarmed Christian pilgrims were granted access to Jerusalem. Richard sailed home in October 1192 and was captured near Vienna by Duke Leopold of Austria, then held for ransom by Emperor Henry VI.
