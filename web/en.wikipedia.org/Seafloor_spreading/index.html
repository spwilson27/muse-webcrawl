Seafloor spreading

Seafloor spreading is a process of plate tectonics in which new oceanic crust is formed at mid-ocean ridges and gradually moves away from them, so that the ocean floor spreads apart and is eventually recycled into the mantle at subduction zones. The theory, developed in the early 1960s, provided the mechanism missing from continental drift and became a cornerstone of the plate tectonic revolution.

History

The hypothesis was articulated by Harry Hess in a widely circulated 1960 manuscript (published 1962 as "History of Ocean Basins"), building on post-war surveys that mapped mid-ocean ridges and their central rift valleys. In 1963 Frederick Vine and Drummond Matthews (and independently Lawrence Morley) proposed that the stripes of alternating magnetic polarity recorded in oceanic crust either side of ridges were a tape-recording of Earth's magnetic field reversals as new crust cooled — the Vine–Matthews–Morley hypothesis. The symmetry and datability of these magnetic anomalies gave spreading rates directly and proved decisive evidence. Deep-sea drilling by the ship Glomar Challenger from 1968 confirmed that crustal age increases with distance from ridges exactly as predicted.

Mechanism and rates

At a spreading centre, upwelling mantle partially melts to form basaltic magma, which erupts or intrudes to build new oceanic lithosphere. The fresh crust moves aside as more material is added, cools and subsides with age, and grows a thickening blanket of deep-sea sediment. Spreading rates range from slow (about 1–2 cm per year, as at parts of the Mid-Atlantic Ridge) to fast (over 15 cm per year on the East Pacific Rise). Because crust is destroyed at subduction zones as fast as it is created, no part of today's ocean floor is older than about 200 million years (Jurassic), in striking contrast to continental rocks billions of years old.

Related features

Spreading ridges host hydrothermal vent systems, including black smokers, where heated seawater circulates through the young crust. Fracture zones and transform faults offset the ridge axis and preserve a record of past plate motions.
