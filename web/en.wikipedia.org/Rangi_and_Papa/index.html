Rangi and Papa

In Māori mythology, Rangi (Ranginui, the sky father) and Papa (Papatūānuku, the earth mother) are the primordial parents from whose embrace the world and the gods were born. Their story is the central creation narrative (whakapapa cosmogony) of the Māori of New Zealand, told with variations among different iwi (tribes).

The separation

In the beginning, Rangi and Papa clung together in darkness (Te Pō), and their many children — the atua, including Tāne (forests), Tangaroa (sea), Tāwhirimātea (winds and storms), Rongo (cultivated food), Haumia-tiketike (wild food) and Tūmatauenga (humanity and war) — were confined in the cramped darkness between them. The children debated killing or separating their parents; in most versions Tāne eventually forced them apart, pushing Rangi upward with his feet while standing on Papa, letting light (Te Ao Mārama) into the world.

Aftermath

Tāwhirimātea, who had opposed the separation, attacked his brothers with storms. Rangi's tears for Papa became rain and dew, and Papa's sighs rose as mist — signs of their continuing love and grief. Papa was later turned over (in some accounts by her children) so she would not see Rangi. The separation narrative explains the structure of the cosmos, the weather, and the domains of the gods, and is woven into Māori concepts of whakapapa (genealogy), kaitiakitanga (guardianship) and the kinship of people with the natural world.

Variations

Traditions differ between tribes: in some southern versions the sky father is Raki, and the roles and generations above Rangi and Papa vary, with sequences of nothingness and night (Te Kore, Te Pō) preceding them. The figures remain prominent in Māori art, carving, and contemporary cultural and environmental discourse in New Zealand.
