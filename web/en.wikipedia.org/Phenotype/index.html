Phenotype

An organism's phenotype is the set of its observable characteristics or traits — its morphology, development, biochemical and physiological properties, behaviour, and products of behaviour. The phenotype results from the expression of the organism's genetic code, its genotype, together with the influence of environmental factors and the interactions between the two. The genotype–phenotype distinction was proposed by the Danish botanist Wilhelm Johannsen in 1911.

Variation and plasticity

Some phenotypes are discrete and largely genotype-determined, such as blood group or the ability to taste phenylthiocarbamide; others, like height, are continuous and polygenic. Phenotypic plasticity is the capacity of a single genotype to produce different phenotypes in different environments — for example, genetically identical water fleas growing defensive helmets in the presence of predators. Richard Dawkins proposed the "extended phenotype" (1982), treating artefacts such as beaver dams and bird nests as phenotypic effects of genes.

Importance

Natural selection acts directly on phenotypes, and evolutionary change is measured in phenotypic terms, making the concept foundational to genetics, medicine (where the phenome is studied in phenomics), and the modern evolutionary synthesis.
