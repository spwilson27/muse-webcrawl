Gig economy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The gig economy is a labour market characterised by the prevalence of short-term contracts or freelance work, as opposed to permanent jobs. The term "gig" is a slang word borrowed from the music industry, where musicians are hired for single performances. In its modern usage, the gig economy usually refers to work mediated by digital platforms — ride-hailing and food delivery services such as Uber, Lyft, Deliveroo, and DoorDash, and task marketplaces such as TaskRabbit, Fiverr, and Upwork — which match workers with customers on a per-job basis using apps and algorithmic management.

Characteristics

Gig workers are typically classified by platforms as independent contractors rather than employees. They choose when to log in, supply much of their own equipment (vehicles, phones), and are paid per task rather than by the hour, but their work is often directed and priced by algorithms that set fares, monitor ratings, and can deactivate workers without a traditional dismissal process. Estimates of participation vary widely: surveys in the United States and Europe suggest that a substantial minority of adults have earned money through platform work, though for most it supplements rather than replaces a main income.

Arguments and criticism

Supporters argue that platform work offers flexibility, low barriers to entry, and income opportunities that suit students, carers, and people between jobs. Critics contend that the model shifts risk and costs onto workers, who lack minimum-wage guarantees, sick pay, pensions, and protection against unfair dismissal, and that "flexibility" is often one-sided, since peak-hour incentives and ratings pressure workers into long or unsocial hours. Economists also debate the gig economy's effect on productivity, tax collection, and the erosion of the mid-20th-century employment relationship.

Legal status and regulation

The classification of gig workers has been contested in courts and legislatures worldwide. In February 2021, the UK Supreme Court ruled that Uber drivers are "workers" entitled to minimum wage and holiday pay. California's Assembly Bill 5 (2019) sought to reclassify many platform workers as employees, but voters then approved Proposition 22 (2020), a platform-backed measure exempting app-based drivers while granting some benefits. The European Union adopted a Platform Work Directive in 2024 establishing a rebuttable presumption of employment for platform workers. Debates over a durable legal category for gig work continue in most major economies.
