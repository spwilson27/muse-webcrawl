John Henry Poynting

John Henry Poynting FRS (9 September 1852 – 30 March 1914) was an English physicist who developed the Poynting vector, which describes the direction and magnitude of electromagnetic energy flow. The Poynting vector appears in Poynting's theorem, an energy-conservation statement for electromagnetic fields derived in his 1884 paper "On the Transfer of Energy in the Electromagnetic Field".

Life and career

Poynting was born in Monton, Lancashire, the son of a Unitarian minister. He was educated at Owens College, Manchester, and at Trinity College, Cambridge, where he was a fellow student of J. J. Thomson. In 1880 he became the first professor of physics at Mason Science College, Birmingham (later the University of Birmingham), a post he held until his death. He was also a notable experimentalist: with Philip Barbour he made precise measurements of the mean density of the Earth and of Newton's gravitational constant using a torsion balance.

Scientific work

Poynting's theorem states that the rate of energy transfer per unit area (the Poynting vector S = E × H) accounts for the flow of electromagnetic energy. He also gave his name to the Poynting effect in elasticity, the lengthening of a wire under torsion, and to the Poynting–Robertson effect, the drag force exerted on small particles by solar radiation, which causes dust grains to spiral slowly into the Sun. A crater on Mars and a crater on the Moon are named after him.
