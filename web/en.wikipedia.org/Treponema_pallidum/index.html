Treponema pallidum

DomainBacteria
PhylumSpirochaetota
GenusTreponema
SpeciesT. pallidum

Treponema pallidum, formerly known as Spirochaeta pallida, is a microaerophilic, Gram-negative spirochaete bacterium with subspecies that cause the diseases syphilis, bejel, and yaws. It is transmitted only among humans. It is a helically coiled microorganism usually 6–15 μm long and 0.1–0.2 μm wide. T. pallidum's lack of either tricarboxylic acid cycle or oxidative phosphorylation results in minimal metabolic activity. The treponemes have a cytoplasmic and an outer membrane. Using light microscopy, treponemes are visible only by using dark-field illumination. T. pallidum consists of three subspecies, each of which has its own associated diseases.

Subspecies

The subspecies are: T. pallidum subsp. pallidum, which causes syphilis; T. pallidum subsp. endemicum, which causes bejel or endemic syphilis; and T. pallidum subsp. pertenue, which causes yaws. The yaws-causing subspecies has been argued to have evolved from the bejel-causing subspecies based on their phylogenetic proximity.

Clinical significance

T. pallidum subsp. pallidum is a spiral-shaped, Gram-negative, highly mobile bacterium. It is a sexually transmitted pathogen that causes the disease syphilis. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, the number of cases of syphilis has been increasing, especially among men. Congenital syphilis, passed from mother to unborn baby, is particularly dangerous.

Laboratory diagnosis

Because the organism is too thin to be seen under ordinary light microscopy, dark-field microscopy of lesion exudate is a classical diagnostic method. Diagnosis more commonly relies on serologic tests, including nontreponemal tests such as the VDRL and rapid plasma reagin tests, and treponemal tests such as FTA-ABS.
