Milk substitute

A milk substitute is any substance that resembles milk and can be used in the same way, typically as a beverage, in cooking, or as an ingredient in processed foods. Milk substitutes are used by people who avoid dairy milk because of lactose intolerance, milk allergy, veganism, religious practice, taste preference or environmental concern.

The term covers two broad families: plant milks, made by extracting liquid from plant material such as seeds, grains, nuts and legumes; and laboratory-developed alternatives, including precision-fermentation products that produce milk proteins such as casein and whey without animals.

Plant milks

Plant-based milk substitutes have a long history in many cuisines. Common varieties include:

  • Soy milk — originating in China, one of the oldest and most nutritionally complete plant milks, with protein content comparable to cow's milk.
  • Almond milk — used in medieval European cooking and now one of the most popular plant milks in North America.
  • Oat milk — developed in Sweden in the 1990s; valued for its creamy texture and popularity in coffee drinks.
  • Coconut milk — a staple of Southeast Asian and tropical cuisines, traditionally extracted from grated coconut flesh.
  • Rice milk, cashew milk, hemp milk, pea milk and others, each with distinct flavour and nutritional profiles.

Most commercial plant milks are fortified with calcium, vitamin D and vitamin B12 to approximate the nutrient content of dairy milk, and many contain added emulsifiers and sweeteners. Their protein content is often lower than cow's milk, with soy and pea milk being notable exceptions.

Infant formula and medical substitutes

A distinct category is infant formula, a manufactured substitute for human breast milk used when breastfeeding is not possible or chosen. Formulas may be based on modified cow's milk, soy protein, or extensively hydrolysed proteins for infants with allergies. These products are tightly regulated in most countries.

Labelling disputes

The use of the word "milk" for plant-based products has been contested by dairy industries. In the European Union, Court of Justice rulings since 2017 generally reserve "milk" and related terms for animal products, with exceptions for traditional uses such as coconut milk. In the United States, the Food and Drug Administration's 2023 draft guidance allows the term "milk" for plant-based alternatives while recommending voluntary nutrient disclosures.

Environmental impact

Life-cycle studies generally find that plant milks produce lower greenhouse-gas emissions and use less land and water than dairy milk, though the footprint varies by crop: almond production is notably water-intensive in some regions, while oat and soy milks tend to score lowest overall.
