Sir Thomas Francis Wade (25 August 1818 – 31 July 1895) was a British diplomat and sinologist who served for many years in China. He is best known for developing the Wade system of romanisation of Mandarin Chinese, later modified by Herbert Giles into the Wade–Giles system that remained the standard transcription in the English-speaking world for much of the twentieth century.

Career in China

Wade was born in London, the son of a soldier, and was educated at Harrow and Trinity College, Cambridge. He went to China in 1842 as an interpreter with British forces at the close of the First Opium War, and served in the colonial administration of Hong Kong before joining the diplomatic service. He worked at the British legation in Peking (Beijing) for many years, becoming Chinese Secretary and then, in 1871, British Minister to China. He negotiated the Chefoo Convention of 1876 following the Margary affair, securing additional treaty ports and formal recognition of British consular rights.

Scholarship

Wade devoted much of his life to the study of Chinese. His Yü-yen Tzŭ-erh Chi (1867), a progressive course in spoken Mandarin, laid the groundwork for his romanisation, which standardised the transcription of Chinese sounds — including the use of aspiration marks — and was later refined by Herbert Allen Giles in his Chinese–English dictionary of 1892. After retiring from the diplomatic service in 1883, Wade became the first Professor of Chinese at the University of Cambridge in 1888, a position he held until his death. He bequeathed his library of Chinese books to the university.

Legacy

The Wade–Giles system produced familiar spellings such as "Peking", "Mao Tse-tung" and "Tao Te Ching". It has since been largely superseded by the Hanyu Pinyin system developed in the People's Republic of China, but remains in use for some names and in older scholarship.
