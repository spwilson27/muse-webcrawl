Severe combined immunodeficiency

Other namesSCID; "bubble boy disease"
SpecialtyImmunology, medical genetics
SymptomsRecurrent severe infections in infancy, failure to thrive
CausesGenetic defects in T-cell (and often B-cell/NK-cell) development
Commonest formX-linked SCID (IL2RG mutation, ~50% of cases)
TreatmentHaematopoietic stem cell transplant; gene therapy; enzyme replacement (ADA-SCID)

Severe combined immunodeficiency (SCID) is a group of rare, life-threatening genetic disorders characterised by profoundly defective development and function of T lymphocytes, and often B cells and natural killer cells, leaving affected infants almost completely unable to fight infection. It is popularly known as "bubble boy disease" after David Vetter, an American boy with SCID who lived in a sterile plastic isolator and died in 1984 aged 12 following a bone-marrow transplant.

Causes and types

SCID results from mutations in many different genes essential to lymphocyte development. The commonest is X-linked SCID, caused by mutations in IL2RG, encoding the common gamma chain of several interleukin receptors. Other forms include adenosine deaminase deficiency (ADA-SCID), defects in RAG1/RAG2 recombination genes, JAK3 deficiency, and Artemis (DCLRE1C) deficiency. Classification is often by which lymphocyte lineages are absent (T−B+ or T−B−, with or without NK cells).

Presentation and diagnosis

Infants typically present in the first months of life with recurrent, severe or opportunistic infections (such as Pneumocystis pneumonia), chronic diarrhoea, oral thrush, skin rashes and failure to thrive. Live vaccines (BCG, rotavirus) can cause fatal infections. Newborn screening using dried blood-spot measurement of T-cell receptor excision circles (TRECs), adopted across the United States and several other countries, now detects SCID before symptoms appear.

Treatment

The definitive treatment is early haematopoietic stem cell transplantation, ideally from a matched sibling before infections occur, with survival above 90% when performed in the first months of life. Gene therapy — inserting a correct gene copy into the patient's own stem cells — has cured ADA-SCID (licensed as Strimvelis in 2016) and X-linked SCID, though an early X-SCID trial caused leukaemia in some children due to insertional mutagenesis, a setback that spurred development of safer lentiviral vectors. Enzyme replacement therapy (PEG-ADA) is used as a bridge for ADA-SCID.
