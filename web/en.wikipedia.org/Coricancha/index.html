Coricancha

Coricancha (Quechua: Qurikancha, "golden enclosure"; also spelled Koricancha or Qorikancha) was the most important temple in the Inca Empire, dedicated principally to the sun god Inti. It stood in the Inca capital, Cusco, in present-day Peru. According to Spanish chroniclers its interior walls were once covered with sheets of solid gold, and its courtyard contained life-size sculptures of animals and plants in gold and silver.

History

A temple existed on the site earlier, but the structure known today was built under the Inca Pachacuti in the fifteenth century as part of his rebuilding of Cusco. Within the enclosure were shrines not only to Inti but also to the moon (Mama Killa), Venus, the Pleiades, and the weather deities of thunder and rainbow, each with its own chamber plated in the appropriate metal. Mummies of past rulers were kept and venerated in the temple, and the great gold image of the sun, the Punchao, held the ashes of the hearts of dead Incas.

Spanish conquest and after

After the Spanish conquest, the temple's gold was looted — much of it forming part of the ransom gathered for Atahualpa at Cajamarca — and the site was granted to the Dominican Order, which built the Convent of Santo Domingo directly on top of the Inca foundations. Earthquakes in 1650 and 1950 destroyed much of the colonial church but revealed and vindicated the precision of the underlying Inca masonry, whose precisely fitted ashlar walls survived intact.

Today

The site now houses the Museo del Convento de Santo Domingo – Coricancha. Its best-known feature, the curved western wall of the Temple of the Sun, is a masterpiece of Inca stonework and a symbol of Cusco.
