Longwall mining

Longwall face with powered supports and shearer

Longwall mining is a form of underground coal mining where a long wall of coal is mined in a single slice (typically 0.6–6.0 metres thick). The longwall panel (the block of coal being mined) is typically 3–4 km long and 250–400 metres wide. It is a highly mechanised and productive method that accounts for a large share of the world's underground coal production.

Method

A longwall face is equipped with a shearer (or plough) that traverses the seam, cutting coal that falls onto an armoured face conveyor. The face is protected by a line of self-advancing hydraulic powered supports. As mining advances, the supports move forward and the roof behind them is allowed to collapse into the worked-out area (the goaf or gob). Development roadways (gate roads) flank the panel for access, ventilation and coal transport.

History and issues

The method originated in England in the late 17th century (initially in Shropshire) using hand methods, and was transformed in the 20th century by mechanised cutting and powered supports, spreading widely in Europe, the United States, Australia and China. Longwall mining recovers a high proportion of the seam but causes predictable surface subsidence above the panel, which can damage buildings, waterways and ecosystems, and the method requires careful management of ventilation and methane.
