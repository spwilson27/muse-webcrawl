Up from Slavery is the 1901 autobiography of the African-American educator Booker T. Washington (1856–1915). The book describes his personal experience of working to rise up from the position of a slave child during the Civil War, the difficulties he overcame to get an education at the Hampton Institute, and his work establishing vocational schools — most notably the Tuskegee Institute in Alabama — to help black people and other disadvantaged minorities learn useful, marketable skills and work to pull themselves up, as a race, by the bootstraps.

Contents

Washington recounts his birth into slavery in Franklin County, Virginia, his family's emancipation, and his work as a child in the salt furnaces and coal mines of West Virginia, where he taught himself to read. He describes his journey in 1872 to Hampton Normal and Agricultural Institute in Virginia, working as a janitor to pay his way, and his later work teaching in West Virginia and at Hampton, under its founder Samuel Chapman Armstrong.

The central chapters describe the founding of Tuskegee Institute in 1881, beginning in a shanty and an old church, and its growth through student labour, brickmaking and fundraising tours. Washington explains his philosophy of industrial education, self-reliance and economic advancement, and reproduces the argument of his famous 1895 Atlanta Exposition speech, in which he urged black Americans to focus on economic progress and famously declared that "in all things that are purely social we can be as separate as the fingers, yet one as the hand in all things essential to mutual progress."

Reception and legacy

The book was a bestseller, widely translated, and remained the most popular of Washington's writings; it is regarded as a classic of African-American literature and an influential example of the slave narrative tradition adapted to the post-emancipation era. Washington's programme of accommodation and gradual economic advancement was later criticised by W. E. B. Du Bois and other black leaders, who demanded full civil and political rights, but the autobiography remains a central document of the era.
