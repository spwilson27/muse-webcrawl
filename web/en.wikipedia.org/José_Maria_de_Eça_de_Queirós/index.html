José Maria de Eça de Queirós

Born: November 25, 1845, Póvoa de Varzim, Portugal
Died: August 16, 1900 (aged 54), Paris, France
Occupation: Novelist, journalist, diplomat
Notable works: The Crime of Father Amaro; The Maias; Cousin Bazilio

José Maria de Eça de Queirós (25 November 1845 – 16 August 1900) is generally considered to be the greatest Portuguese writer in the realist style. Zola believed him to be far greater than Flaubert. His novels, above all The Maias (1888), are masterpieces of ironic social observation, dissecting the provincialism, clerical influence, and political stagnation of nineteenth-century Portugal.

Early life

Eça was born in Póvoa de Varzim, the son of a magistrate; his parents married years after his birth, and he was raised largely by relatives — an experience of genteel illegitimacy that shadowed his writing. He read law at the University of Coimbra, where he came under the influence of the rebellious "Generation of 70," and in 1871 joined Antero de Quental and others in the Democratic Casino Conferences, which sought to modernize Portuguese culture and were suppressed by the government.

Diplomatic career and writing

Eça entered the consular service, serving in Havana, Newcastle upon Tyne, Bristol, and finally Paris. Abroad, with the journalist Ramalho Ortigão, he wrote the satirical periodical As Farpas ("The Barbs"). His first major novel, The Crime of Father Amaro (1875), attacked the corruption of the provincial clergy; Cousin Bazilio (1878), a story of adultery in Lisbon, drew comparisons with Madame Bovary.

The Maias and later work

His masterpiece, The Maias (1888), traces the decline of an aristocratic family over three generations, interweaving a tragic incest plot with a panoramic comedy of Lisbon society. Later works such as The Illustrious House of Ramires (1900) and the posthumous The City and the Mountains show a softening of his satire into a more sympathetic, though still ironic, view of Portuguese life.

Legacy

Eça died in Paris in 1900. His prose style — precise, musical, and devastatingly ironic — set the standard for modern Portuguese fiction, and his novels are canonical in Portugal and Brazil, widely translated and repeatedly adapted for film and television.
