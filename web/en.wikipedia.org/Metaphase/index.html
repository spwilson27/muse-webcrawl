Metaphase (from the Greek words for "after" and "stage") is a stage of mitosis in the eukaryotic cell cycle in which chromosomes are at their second-most condensed and coiled stage. The chromosomes, carrying genetic information, align in the equator of the cell — the metaphase plate — before being separated into each daughter cell.

Events

During metaphase, the two centrosomes at opposite poles pull the chromosomes through the spindle fibres attached to their kinetochores, and the chromosomes come under bipolar tension. Their characteristic X-shaped appearance, with sister chromatids joined at the centromere, is most recognisable at this stage; chromosomes are easiest to study and count (karyotyping) in metaphase.

Checkpoint

The metaphase checkpoint, or spindle assembly checkpoint, ensures that all chromosomes are properly attached to the spindle before anaphase begins. The anaphase-promoting complex (APC/C) is held inactive until every kinetochore is correctly attached, preventing premature sister-chromatid separation and chromosome mis-segregation. Cells arrested in metaphase by spindle poisons such as colchicine are used to prepare karyotypes.
