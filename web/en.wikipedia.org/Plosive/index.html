Plosive

In phonetics, a plosive, also known as a stop consonant or simply a stop, is a pulmonic consonant in which the vocal tract is blocked so that all airflow ceases. The occlusion may be made with the tongue blade ([t], [d]), tongue body ([k], [ɡ]), lips ([p], [b]), or glottis ([ʔ]). It contrasts with continuants, which do not block airflow, and nasals, which block airflow in the oral cavity but allow it to escape through the nose.

Terminology

The terms "stop", "oral stop" and "plosive" are sometimes used interchangeably. Aero-dynamic studies by Peter Ladefoged and Ian Maddieson distinguish "stops" from "plosives": a stop involves a total blockage of airflow but not necessarily a release or burst, whereas a plosive specifically involves both closure and a release burst of air. The International Phonetic Association uses the term "plosive" for pulmonic oral stops.

Production

A plosive is produced in three phases: approach (closure of the articulators), hold (a period of maintained occlusion during which pressure builds up behind the closure), and release (a sudden burst of air as the closure is opened). Voiced plosives are produced with vibration of the vocal folds during the hold phase.

Common plosives

The six most common plosives in the world's languages are the voiceless bilabial [p], alveolar [t], and velar [k], together with their voiced counterparts [b], [d], and [ɡ]. Nearly every language has at least one voiceless plosive, and most have a full series.
