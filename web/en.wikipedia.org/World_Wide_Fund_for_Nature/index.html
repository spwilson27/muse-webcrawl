The World Wide Fund for Nature (WWF) is a Swiss-based international non-governmental organization founded in 1961 that works in the field of wilderness preservation and the reduction of human impact on the environment. It was formerly named the World Wildlife Fund, which remains its official name in Canada and the United States. WWF is the world's largest conservation organization, with over 5 million supporters worldwide, working in more than 100 countries and supporting around 3,000 conservation and environmental projects. It has invested over $1 billion in more than 12,000 conservation initiatives since 1995.

History

WWF was founded on 29 April 1961 by a group including biologist Sir Julian Huxley, ornithologist Guy Mountfort, and painter-naturalist Peter Scott, who designed its famous logo based on Chi-Chi, the giant panda then at London Zoo. Prince Bernhard of the Netherlands became its first president. Early work funded species protection and helped create organisations such as the IUCN's Red List process and conservation projects in the Galápagos and East Africa. In 1986 the organisation adopted the name World Wide Fund for Nature to reflect its broader mission, except in North America, where it remains World Wildlife Fund after a trademark dispute was settled in 2001.

Programme

WWF works on conserving priority places (such as the Amazon, the Congo Basin, the Coral Triangle, and the Arctic), protecting threatened species including tigers, elephants, rhinos, and giant pandas, and addressing systemic drivers: climate change, food systems, freshwater, forests, and oceans. It promotes market-based tools such as the Forest Stewardship Council (which it co-founded in 1993) and the Marine Stewardship Council, campaigns such as Earth Hour (begun in Sydney in 2007), and publishes the biennial Living Planet Report, which documents steep declines in monitored wildlife populations since 1970.

Funding and criticism

WWF receives funding from individuals, governments, aid agencies, and corporations — corporate partnerships that have drawn criticism over "greenwashing", as have its relations with extractive industries and, in reports published from 2019, human-rights concerns involving anti-poaching rangers it funded in some parks. WWF responded with independent reviews and safeguarding reforms.
