Shot peening

Shot peening is a cold working process used to produce a compressive residual stress layer and modify the mechanical properties of metals and composites. It consists of striking a surface with shot (round metallic, glass, or ceramic particles) with enough force to generate plastic deformation.

Shot peening is used on gear parts, camshafts, turbine blades, landing gear, and other components subject to cyclic fatigue. It is similar to sandblasting, except that it operates by the mechanism of plasticity rather than abrasion: each particle functions as a ball-peen hammer. In practice, this means that less material is removed by the process, and less dust is created.

Mechanism

When a shot pellet strikes a metal surface, it creates a small indentation in the surface texture. Beneath the indentation, the material that has been compressed attempts to restore its original shape, producing a hemisphere of cold-worked material highly stressed in compression. Overlapping indentations produce a uniform layer of residual compressive stress. Residual compressive stresses in a component have been shown to benefit its fatigue strength, resistance to stress-corrosion cracking, and resistance to fretting fatigue.

Process details

The intensity of the peening process is measured with Almen strips: thin strips of spring steel that are peened on one side and the resulting curvature measured. Shot peening may be performed with air pressure machines, which propel the shot with compressed air, or centrifugal blast wheels, which throw the shot outward from a rapidly rotating wheel.

Applications

The process is widely used in the aerospace and automotive industries to extend the fatigue life of turbine blades, springs, gears, shafts, and welded structures. A related process, laser peening, uses pulses of laser energy to generate deeper compressive residual stresses.
