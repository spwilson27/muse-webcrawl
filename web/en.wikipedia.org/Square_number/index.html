In mathematics, a square number or perfect square is an integer that is the square of an integer; in other words, it is the product of some integer with itself. For example, 9 is a square number, since it equals 32 and can be written as 3 × 3. The name comes from geometry: n2 equals the area of a square with side length n.

Properties

  • The nth square number is the sum of the first n odd numbers: n2 = 1 + 3 + 5 + ... + (2n − 1). Geometrically, each square is built from the previous by adding an L-shaped border (a gnomon).
  • Squares modulo 4 can only be 0 or 1; the last digit of a square in base 10 can only be 0, 1, 4, 5, 6 or 9.
  • A positive integer is a square if and only if every exponent in its prime factorisation is even.
  • Lagrange's four-square theorem states that every natural number is the sum of at most four squares.
  • The sum of the first n squares is n(n+1)(2n+1)/6 (the square pyramidal numbers).

Examples and uses

Square numbers appear throughout mathematics: in the Pythagorean theorem, in quadratic equations and forms, in the classification of conic sections, and in number theory (quadratic residues — the squares modulo a prime p — play a central role). Numbers that are both square and triangular, and results such as Fermat's theorem on sums of two squares, are classical topics.
