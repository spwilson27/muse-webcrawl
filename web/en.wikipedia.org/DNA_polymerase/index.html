DNA polymerase

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A DNA polymerase is a member of a family of enzymes that catalyse the synthesis of DNA molecules from nucleoside triphosphates, the molecular precursors of DNA. These enzymes are essential for DNA replication and usually work in groups to create two identical DNA duplexes from a single original DNA duplex.

Mechanism

DNA polymerases add nucleotides to the 3′-hydroxyl group of the growing strand, reading the template in the 3′→5′ direction so that synthesis proceeds 5′→3′. They cannot start synthesis de novo and require a primer with a free 3′-OH. Many polymerases have 3′→5′ exonuclease "proofreading" activity that removes misincorporated bases, greatly increasing fidelity. A phosphodiester bond is formed as pyrophosphate is released from each incoming nucleotide.

Families

Prokaryotic polymerases include Pol I (gap filling and primer removal), Pol II (repair), and Pol III (the main replicative enzyme) in bacteria such as E. coli. Eukaryotes use Pol α (priming), Pol ε (leading strand), and Pol δ (lagging strand and repair), among at least 15 DNA polymerases, several specialised for translesion synthesis or repair. Thermostable polymerases such as Taq polymerase, isolated from Thermus aquaticus, are the basis of the polymerase chain reaction (PCR).
