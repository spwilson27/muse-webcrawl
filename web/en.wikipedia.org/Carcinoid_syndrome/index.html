Carcinoid syndrome

Carcinoid syndrome is a paraneoplastic syndrome caused by the systemic release of compounds, chiefly serotonin, secreted by certain neuroendocrine tumours (historically called carcinoid tumours). Its characteristic features are episodic flushing of the face and upper body, diarrhoea, bronchospasm and, over time, fibrotic damage to the heart valves known as carcinoid heart disease.

[Image: Schematic of a midgut neuroendocrine tumour with liver metastases, the usual source of carcinoid syndrome]

Cause

The syndrome usually appears when a serotonin-secreting tumour of the midgut has metastasised to the liver, allowing vasoactive substances to escape hepatic inactivation and reach the systemic circulation. Tumours outside the portal drainage area, such as bronchial or ovarian carcinoids, can cause the syndrome without liver metastases. Besides serotonin, the tumours may release histamine, prostaglandins, tachykinins and kallikrein.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is supported by finding raised urinary levels of 5-hydroxyindoleacetic acid (5-HIAA), the main metabolite of serotonin. Treatment includes somatostatin analogues such as octreotide, which suppress hormone release and control symptoms; surgical resection or debulking of tumour; liver-directed therapies; and, for refractory diarrhoea, the serotonin synthesis inhibitor telotristat ethyl. Carcinoid crisis — a life-threatening surge of mediators — can be precipitated by anaesthesia, surgery or tumour manipulation and is treated with intravenous octreotide.
