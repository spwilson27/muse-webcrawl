The M1 Garand is a semi-automatic rifle that was the service rifle of the United States during World War II and the Korean War. Designed by Canadian-American engineer John Cantius Garand at Springfield Armory, it was formally adopted in 1936 as the "U.S. Rifle, Caliber .30, M1" and replaced the bolt-action M1903 Springfield. General George S. Patton famously called it "the greatest battle implement ever devised."

The Garand gave American infantry a decisive firepower advantage: a trained soldier could fire 40–50 aimed rounds per minute, far more than opponents armed with bolt-action rifles. Chambered for the .30-06 Springfield cartridge, it loads with a distinctive eight-round en bloc clip; when the last round is fired, the empty clip is ejected with a characteristic metallic ping and the bolt locks open.

History

Garand began work on semi-automatic designs after the First World War, competing against the Pedersen rifle and other designs. After trials in the 1920s and early 1930s — including a period in which the Army considered the smaller .276 Pedersen cartridge before General Douglas MacArthur insisted on .30-06 commonality — the Garand was adopted on 9 January 1936. Production ramped up slowly before 1941, then reached mass scale during the war, with Springfield Armory and Winchester producing over 4 million rifles by 1945. Production resumed during the Korean War at Springfield, Harrington & Richardson, and International Harvester, bringing total output to roughly 5.4 million.

Design details

The M1 is gas-operated: a long-stroke piston, driven by gas tapped near the muzzle, acts on the operating rod to cycle a rotating bolt with two locking lugs. Ammunition feeds from an internal magazine loaded by pressing an en bloc clip of eight cartridges down into the receiver; pressing the clip home past the follower was known to cause "Garand thumb" if done carelessly. Sights consist of an aperture rear sight adjustable for windage and elevation and a protected front post. The rifle weighs about 4.4 kg (9.5 lb) unloaded and is 1,107 mm (43.5 in) long with a 610 mm (24 in) barrel.

Service and successors

The Garand served as the standard US infantry rifle through World War II (in every theatre) and Korea. It was replaced in 1957–59 by the selective-fire M14, itself derived from the Garand's action. The Italian firm Beretta produced licensed versions (the BM59 family) in 7.62×51mm NATO, and many allied nations received Garands as military aid. Sniper variants (M1C and M1D) served into the Vietnam era. Surplus rifles sold through the Civilian Marksmanship Program remain extremely popular with collectors and competitive shooters, and the Garand is still used by US military drill teams and honour guards.
