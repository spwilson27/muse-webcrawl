Uranium is a chemical element with the symbol U and atomic number 92. It is a silvery-grey metal in the actinide series of the periodic table. A uranium atom has 92 protons and 92 electrons, of which 6 are valence electrons. Uranium is weakly radioactive because all naturally occurring isotopes of uranium are unstable; the most common are uranium-238 (99.3% abundance) and uranium-235 (0.7%). Uranium decays slowly by emitting an alpha particle, with half-lives ranging from 159,200 years (uranium-233) to 4.4 billion years (uranium-238), making it useful for dating the age of the Earth.

Occurrence

Uranium is more abundant in the Earth's crust than antimony, tin, cadmium or mercury, occurring at an average concentration of about 2–4 parts per million, mainly in the minerals uraninite (pitchblende), carnotite and autunite. Major producing countries include Kazakhstan, Canada, Namibia and Australia. In seawater it is present at very low concentrations of about 3 parts per billion.

History

The German chemist Martin Heinrich Klaproth identified uranium as an element in 1789 in pitchblende ore and named it after the recently discovered planet Uranus; the metal itself was first isolated by Eugène-Melchior Péligot in 1841. Henri Becquerel discovered uranium's radioactivity in 1896. In 1938 Otto Hahn and Fritz Strassmann demonstrated nuclear fission in uranium, interpreted by Lise Meitner and Otto Frisch, which led to the development of nuclear reactors and nuclear weapons during the Second World War.

Applications

The principal use of uranium is as fuel in nuclear power reactors, which supply about a tenth of the world's electricity; the fissile isotope uranium-235 must usually be enriched from its natural abundance. Uranium is also used in nuclear weapons, in depleted uranium munitions and armour, and historically as a colorant in glass and ceramic glazes (uranium glass). Its compounds have applications in chemistry and photography.

Hazards

Uranium is both a heavy-metal chemical toxin — particularly damaging to the kidneys — and a radiological hazard. Exposure is regulated by workplace and environmental standards, and the mining and milling of uranium ores require controls on dust, radon and tailings.
