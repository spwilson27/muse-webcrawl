Synchronous rotation

In astronomy, synchronous rotation or tidal locking of a natural satellite or other orbiting body occurs when its rotation period matches its orbital period, so that the same hemisphere always faces the body it orbits. The best-known example is Earth's Moon, which rotates once per orbit (about 27.3 days) and therefore presents (nearly) the same face to Earth at all times.

Mechanism

Tidal locking results from gravitational interaction between two bodies. The gravitational pull of the primary raises tidal bulges on the satellite; if the satellite initially rotates faster than it orbits, friction inside it drags the bulge ahead of the line of centres, and the primary's pull on the offset bulge exerts a torque that slows the rotation (while also pushing the satellite's orbit outward). The process continues until rotation and revolution are synchronised, a state of minimum energy for the given angular momentum. The same dissipation originally locked most of the large moons of the Solar System to their planets.

Examples

All four Galilean moons of Jupiter, most moons of Saturn including Titan, and Pluto and Charon (which are doubly tidally locked, each always showing the same face to the other) exhibit synchronous rotation. Mercury occupies a related state: it is in a 3:2 spin–orbit resonance, rotating three times for every two orbits. Many close-in exoplanets are presumed to be tidally locked to their stars, with permanent daysides and nightsides that strongly influence their climates and potential habitability.

Effects

Because the Moon's orbit is elliptical, its rotation rate and orbital rate match only on average, producing the wobble known as libration, which lets observers on Earth see about 59% of the lunar surface over time. Tidal locking also affects stellar binaries, many of which synchronise their rotations, and it can circularise orbits.
