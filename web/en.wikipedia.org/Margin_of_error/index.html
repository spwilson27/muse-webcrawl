Margin of error

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Confidence interval diagram illustrating margin of error]

The margin of error is a statistic expressing the amount of random sampling error in the results of a survey. It is typically the radius of a confidence interval for a reported percentage, so that the true value for the whole population is expected to lie within the reported percentage plus or minus the margin of error, with a stated level of confidence (commonly 95%).

Calculation

For a simple random sample of size n from a large population, the maximum margin of error at 95% confidence is approximately 0.98 / √n (when the proportion is near 50%, where variability is greatest). Thus a sample of 1,000 gives a margin of about ±3.1 percentage points, and 400 gives about ±4.9 points. Larger samples yield smaller margins, with diminishing returns.

Interpretation and misuse

The margin of error accounts only for random sampling error, not systematic errors such as non-response bias, question wording, or coverage problems. It is commonly misused in media reporting: differences between two candidates within the margin are not necessarily a "statistical tie", and the margin for a subgroup (e.g. one region) is larger than for the full sample.

Related concepts

The margin of error is related to, but distinct from, the standard error of the estimate; it is usually the critical value (z-score) multiplied by the standard error.
