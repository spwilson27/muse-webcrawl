Epictetus

Epictetus (Greek: Ἐπίκτητος, Epíktētos; c. AD 50 – c. 135) was a Greek Stoic philosopher. Born into slavery at Hierapolis in Phrygia, he was brought to Rome as a slave of Epaphroditus, a wealthy freedman secretary of Nero. He studied Stoicism under Musonius Rufus, was freed, and began teaching; when the emperor Domitian banished philosophers from Rome in AD 93, Epictetus withdrew to Nicopolis in Greece, where he founded a school attended in later years by the future emperor's circle and recorded by his pupil Arrian.

Teachings

Epictetus wrote nothing himself; Arrian's notes survive as the Discourses and the condensed Enchiridion ("Handbook"). His central doctrine is the dichotomy of control: some things are up to us — our judgments, impulses and desires — and some are not — the body, property, reputation, external events. Freedom and happiness consist in confining one's will to what is up to us and accepting the rest with equanimity. "If you want things to be good," he taught, "want them to be as they are."

Influence

The Enchiridion was adapted as a manual of Christian discipline in the Middle Ages, and Epictetus deeply influenced Marcus Aurelius, early modern moralists, and modern cognitive-behavioural therapy, which credits his insight that people are disturbed not by things themselves but by their views of things.
