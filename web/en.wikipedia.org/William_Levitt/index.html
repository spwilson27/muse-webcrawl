William Jaird Levitt (February 11, 1907 – January 28, 1994) was an American real-estate developer and housing pioneer. As president of Levitt & Sons, he is widely credited with creating the modern American suburb through the mass production of affordable houses, most famously at Levittown, New York.

Career

Levitt worked in his father's firm before serving in the U.S. Navy Seabees during the Second World War, where he learned techniques of rapid, standardised construction. After the war, facing a severe housing shortage among returning veterans, Levitt & Sons bought farmland on Long Island and applied assembly-line methods — crews moving from slab to slab performing single tasks, with pre-cut components delivered on site — to build over 17,000 houses in the development that became Levittown between 1947 and 1951. Houses initially sold for $7,990 and could be built at a rate of more than 30 a day at peak.

Levittowns and legacy

Further Levittowns followed in Pennsylvania and New Jersey, and the company later built in France and elsewhere. Levitt sold the company to ITT in 1968. His developments were criticised for monotony and for their original racially restrictive sales policies, which excluded Black buyers and helped entrench suburban segregation; Levitt defended the practice as a business decision. Nonetheless, his methods transformed American housing, home ownership and suburban culture, and he appeared on the cover of Time magazine in 1950.
