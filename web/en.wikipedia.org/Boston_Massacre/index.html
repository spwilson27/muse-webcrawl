Boston Massacre

The Boston Massacre was a confrontation in Boston on March 5, 1770, in which British Army soldiers shot and killed several people while being harassed by a mob. The incident was a significant event leading up to the American Revolutionary War.

Events

Tensions had grown in Boston because of the presence of British troops enforcing unpopular trade laws. On the evening of March 5, a crowd gathered outside the Custom House on King Street, taunting a lone sentry. Reinforcements under Captain Thomas Preston arrived, and amid the confusion the soldiers fired into the crowd. Three men — Crispus Attucks, Samuel Gray, and James Caldwell — died immediately, and two more died later of their wounds.

Aftermath

The soldiers were tried for murder; they were defended by John Adams, who argued they had acted in self-defense. Most were acquitted, and two were convicted of manslaughter. Patriots such as Samuel Adams publicized the event as a massacre, and Paul Revere's dramatic engraving of the shooting became one of the most effective pieces of anti-British propaganda of the era. March 5 was commemorated annually in Boston until it was replaced by Independence Day celebrations.
