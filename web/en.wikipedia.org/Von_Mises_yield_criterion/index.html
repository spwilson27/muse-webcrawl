Von Mises yield criterion

The von Mises yield criterion (also known as the maximum distortion energy criterion) suggests that yielding of a ductile material begins when the second deviatoric stress invariant J2 reaches a critical value. It is named after Richard von Mises, who proposed it in 1913; related ideas had earlier been suggested by Maxwell and Huber.

In terms of the principal stresses σ1, σ2, σ3, the criterion states that yielding occurs when the von Mises equivalent stress

σv = √( ½[(σ1−σ2)² + (σ2−σ3)² + (σ3−σ1)²] )

reaches the yield strength of the material in uniaxial tension. Geometrically, the yield surface is a circular cylinder in principal stress space whose axis is the hydrostatic line σ1 = σ2 = σ3, reflecting the experimental finding that hydrostatic pressure alone does not cause yielding in metals.

Comparison and use

The von Mises criterion generally fits experimental data for ductile metals better than the older Tresca criterion, which predicts yielding based on maximum shear stress; the two agree in uniaxial and pure-shear limits but differ by up to about 15% in some stress states. Von Mises stress is computed routinely in finite element analysis as a scalar measure for assessing whether a design will yield.
