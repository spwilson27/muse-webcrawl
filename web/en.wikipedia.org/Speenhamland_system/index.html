Speenhamland system

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


The Pelican Inn, Speenhamland, where the system was decided

The Speenhamland system was a form of outdoor relief intended to mitigate rural poverty in England at the end of the 18th century and during the early 19th century. It was implemented by magistrates at a meeting held on 6 May 1795 at the Pelican Inn in the village of Speenhamland, near Newbury in Berkshire.

Operation

Under the system, workers' wages were supplemented from the poor rates according to a scale tied to the price of bread and the size of the worker's family: when the gallon loaf of bread cost one shilling, every poor and industrious man would receive a minimum weekly income whether earned or made up by the parish. The scale rose as bread prices rose. Similar allowance systems spread widely across the rural south of England during the Napoleonic Wars.

Assessment

Contemporary critics, including Thomas Malthus, argued that Speenhamland depressed wages, encouraged population growth and burdened ratepayers; the Royal Commission of 1832 blamed it for pauperisation and it was swept away by the Poor Law Amendment Act 1834. Karl Polanyi famously discussed it in The Great Transformation (1944). Later economic historians, notably Mark Blaug and Daniel Baugh, have questioned the traditional account, arguing that the system's effects were exaggerated and that it largely operated in areas already suffering structural unemployment and high bread prices.
