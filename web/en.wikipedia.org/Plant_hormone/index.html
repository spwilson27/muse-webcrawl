Plant hormone

Plant hormones (or phytohormones) are signal molecules, produced within plants, that occur in extremely low concentrations. Plant hormones control all aspects of plant growth and development, including embryogenesis, organ size, pathogen defence, stress tolerance, and reproductive development. Unlike in animals, where hormones are produced in specialised glands, each plant cell is capable of producing hormones.

The main classes

The five classic classes of plant hormones are:

  • Auxins — principally indole-3-acetic acid (IAA), promoting cell elongation, apical dominance, root initiation, and tropic bending; the first plant hormone discovered, through the experiments of Charles and Francis Darwin and Frits Went.
  • Gibberellins — promoting stem elongation, seed germination, and flowering; best known from the "foolish seedling" disease of rice and from the semi-dwarf cereals of the Green Revolution.
  • Cytokinins — promoting cell division, shoot formation, and delaying leaf senescence.
  • Abscisic acid (ABA) — a stress hormone that induces seed dormancy and closes stomata during drought.
  • Ethylene — a gaseous hormone regulating fruit ripening, leaf abscission, and responses to wounding and flooding.

More recently recognised signalling compounds include brassinosteroids, jasmonates, salicylic acid, strigolactones, and various peptide hormones, all interacting in complex networks.

Uses

Synthetic plant growth regulators are widely used in agriculture and horticulture: auxin analogues as rooting powders and herbicides (such as 2,4-D), gibberellins to enlarge seedless grapes, ethylene (or ethephon) to synchronise fruit ripening, and cytokinins in tissue culture.
