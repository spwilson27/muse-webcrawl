The Pakistan Movement was a political movement in British India in the first half of the twentieth century that aimed to create an independent Muslim state in the north-western and eastern Muslim-majority regions of the subcontinent. Led principally by the All-India Muslim League under Muhammad Ali Jinnah, it culminated in the creation of Pakistan on 14 August 1947 as part of the Partition of India.

Origins

The movement's intellectual roots lie in the reformist thought of Sir Syed Ahmad Khan and the Aligarh Movement, which promoted modern education among Indian Muslims and argued that Hindus and Muslims formed distinct political communities — an idea later formalised as the two-nation theory. The Muslim League was founded in Dhaka in 1906 to safeguard Muslim political interests; separate electorates for Muslims were conceded in 1909. In his Allahabad Address of 1930, the philosopher-poet Muhammad Iqbal proposed a consolidated Muslim state in north-western India.

The demand for Pakistan

The word "Pakistan" was coined by Choudhry Rahmat Ali in his 1933 pamphlet Now or Never. The turning point came with the Lahore Resolution of March 1940, in which the Muslim League demanded that Muslim-majority areas be grouped into "independent states". After the failure of the Cabinet Mission Plan of 1946 and escalating communal violence, partition became unavoidable; the Indian Independence Act of July 1947 created the two dominions of India and Pakistan.

Consequences

Partition displaced an estimated 10–20 million people along religious lines and was accompanied by mass violence in which several hundred thousand to over a million people died. Pakistan emerged in two wings — West Pakistan and East Bengal (later East Pakistan) — separated by more than 1,600 km of Indian territory; the eastern wing seceded in 1971 to become Bangladesh.
