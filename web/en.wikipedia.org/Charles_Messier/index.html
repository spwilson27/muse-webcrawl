Charles Messier (26 June 1730 – 12 April 1817) was a French astronomer best known for publishing the Messier catalogue, an astronomical list of more than one hundred nebulae, star clusters, and galaxies. An avid comet hunter — he discovered thirteen comets — Messier compiled the catalogue to avoid mistaking these fixed fuzzy objects for comets, and it remains a standard reference for amateur astronomers today.

Life and career

Messier's interest in astronomy was sparked by the great six-tailed comet of 1744 and a solar eclipse visible from his native Lorraine in 1748. He obtained a post with Joseph-Nicolas Delisle, astronomer of the French Navy in Paris, and in 1758 recovered Halley's Comet — though Delisle's delay in announcing it denied him credit. While searching for the comet he recorded the Crab Nebula, the first entry (M1) in his catalogue. Working from the Hôtel de Cluny observatory, Messier observed comets, transits, and eclipses, was elected to the French Academy of Sciences in 1770, and received patronage from Louis XV, who called him his "comet ferret."

The Messier catalogue

The first edition (1771) listed 45 objects; the final version published in 1781 contained 103, with later researchers extending it to 110. The list includes some of the most studied objects in the sky: the Andromeda Galaxy (M31), the Orion Nebula (M42), the Pleiades (M45), the Ring Nebula (M57), and the Whirlpool Galaxy (M51). Ironic to Messier's purpose, the catalogue of objects that were not comets entirely eclipsed his comet hunting in posterity. Amateur astronomers still observe all the Messier objects in single-night "Messier marathons."

Legacy

The lunar crater Messier and asteroid 7359 Messier are named in his honor. Messier died in Paris in 1817, having outlived the French Revolution and the imperial honors of Napoleon.
