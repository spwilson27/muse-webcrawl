The Goliath birdeater (Theraphosa blondi) is a spider belonging to the tarantula family Theraphosidae. Found in northern South America, it is among the largest spiders in the world by mass and body length — rivalled in leg span by the giant huntsman spider. Specimens can reach a body length of about 13 cm (5.1 in), a leg span of up to 30 cm (12 in), and a mass of around 175 g (6.2 oz).

Habitat and behaviour

The Goliath birdeater lives in marshy or swampy areas of the rainforests of Suriname, Guyana, French Guiana, northern Brazil, and southern Venezuela. It is a terrestrial, nocturnal predator that shelters in burrows, often taking over abandoned rodent burrows or digging its own, lined with silk.

Diet

Despite its name, the Goliath birdeater rarely preys on birds — the name comes from an 18th-century engraving by Maria Sibylla Merian showing a tarantula eating a hummingbird. Its diet consists mainly of large invertebrates such as earthworms, crickets, beetles, and other spiders, supplemented by small vertebrates including frogs, lizards, rodents, and snakes. Like other tarantulas, it subdues prey with venom delivered by fangs up to about 2 cm (0.8 in) long; its venom is relatively mild to humans, comparable to a wasp sting.

Defences

When threatened, T. blondi may rear up and bare its fangs, stridulate — producing a hissing sound by rubbing bristles together that can be heard several metres away — and kick urticating hairs (setae) from its abdomen, which irritate skin and mucous membranes.
