The phalanx (Greek: φάλαγξ) was a rectangular mass military formation, usually composed entirely of heavy infantry armed with spears, pikes, shields or similar pole weapons. Developed in ancient Greece, it dominated Mediterranean warfare for centuries and reached its apex under Philip II of Macedon and Alexander the Great.

Greek hoplite phalanx

In the classical Greek phalanx, citizen-soldiers called hoplites stood shoulder to shoulder, typically eight ranks deep, each man's large round shield (aspis) covering his left side and his neighbour's right. Battles were decided by the othismos — a mass shoving and spear-fighting contest — in which cohesion was everything. Spartan hoplites, the most professional, drilled to advance in step to the sound of pipes.

Macedonian phalanx

Philip II reformed the phalanx around the sarissa, a pike some 4–6 metres long wielded with both hands; the first five ranks' points projected beyond the front, creating a dense hedge of spearheads. Combined with elite cavalry (the Companions) and light troops in the "hammer and anvil" tactics perfected by Alexander, the Macedonian phalanx conquered the Persian Empire. Under the Hellenistic successor kingdoms the formation grew deeper and less flexible, and it was ultimately outmanoeuvred by the more articulated Roman legion, as at Cynoscephalae (197 BC) and Pydna (168 BC).

Legacy

Similar pike blocks revived in late medieval Europe with Swiss pikemen and German Landsknechts, before firearms and field artillery displaced massed pike formations.
