Détournement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A détournement (French for "rerouting" or "hijacking") is a technique developed in the 1950s by the Letterist International, and later adopted by the Situationist International (SI), in which pre-existing artistic elements are integrated into a new construction to create a new ensemble with a different, typically subversive, meaning. It is the "integration of present or past artistic productions into a superior construction of a milieu".

Definition and method

Guy Debord and Gil Wolman set out the method in their 1956 "A User's Guide to Détournement": any elements, no matter where they are taken from, can be reused in new combinations — advertising slogans reversed, comic strips re-lettered, classic paintings altered, films re-edited. Unlike plagiarism or parody, détournement is meant to turn the spectacle's own expressions against it; it is paired with its opposite, récupération, the process by which radical ideas are absorbed back into mainstream culture.

Examples and legacy

Situationist works include Asger Jorn's altered thrift-shop paintings, Debord's films, and the détourné comics of the May 1968 uprising in Paris. The technique anticipates culture jamming, adbusting, sampling in hip-hop, appropriation art and modern internet remix and meme culture.
