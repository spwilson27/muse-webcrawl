New Keynesian economics

New Keynesian economics is a school of macroeconomics that strives to provide microeconomic foundations for Keynesian economics. It developed partly as a response to criticisms of Keynesian macroeconomics by adherents of new classical macroeconomics, in particular the Lucas critique. Two main assumptions define the New Keynesian approach: like the new classical approach, households and firms are assumed to have rational expectations; but unlike the new classical approach, prices and wages are "sticky" — they do not adjust instantly to changes in economic conditions.

Sticky prices and wages

Because prices cannot adjust immediately, changes in the money supply can affect real output and employment, which explains how monetary policy can be non-neutral in the short run. Models of stickiness include the Calvo model of staggered price-setting, menu costs, and efficiency wage and implicit-contract theories of wage rigidity. Firms are modelled as monopolistic competitors that set their own prices.

The New Keynesian Phillips curve and DSGE models

The New Keynesian Phillips curve relates current inflation to expected future inflation and real marginal cost. Together with a forward-looking IS curve and a monetary policy rule such as the Taylor rule, it forms the canonical three-equation model. Dynamic stochastic general equilibrium (DSGE) models built on these foundations became the standard workhorse of central banks for policy analysis in the 1990s and 2000s.

Policy implications and criticism

New Keynesian models support activist monetary policy to stabilize output and inflation, and assign fiscal policy a role especially at the zero lower bound on interest rates. Critics include post-Keynesians, who dispute the rational-expectations and equilibrium foundations, and economists who argue the models failed to anticipate the 2008 financial crisis because they lacked a meaningful financial sector.
