Insanity defense

The insanity defense, also known as the mental disorder defense, is an affirmative defense in criminal trials by which a defendant argues that, because of a severe mental disease or defect at the time of the offence, they should not be held criminally responsible. A successful plea typically results in a verdict of "not guilty by reason of insanity" and commitment to a psychiatric facility rather than acquittal and release.

The insanity defense turns on the defendant's mental state at the time of the offence.

Legal tests

The classic common-law standard is the M'Naghten rule (1843), from the case of Daniel M'Naghten: a defendant is excused if a defect of reason from disease of the mind meant they did not know the nature and quality of the act, or did not know it was wrong. Some jurisdictions added an "irresistible impulse" limb; the American Law Institute's Model Penal Code (1962) adopted a broader test of lacking substantial capacity to appreciate the criminality of the act or to conform conduct to the law. After John Hinckley Jr. was found not guilty by reason of insanity for the 1981 attempted assassination of President Ronald Reagan, the US federal Insanity Defense Reform Act of 1984 narrowed the federal test and shifted the burden of proof to the defendant.

Use and controversy

The defense is raised in fewer than 1% of felony cases in the United States and succeeds in only a fraction of those, contrary to public perception. It is distinct from competency to stand trial, which concerns the defendant's present ability to participate in their defense, and from diminished capacity/mens rea arguments. Successful defendants are often confined in secure psychiatric hospitals for periods as long as or longer than a prison sentence would have been.
