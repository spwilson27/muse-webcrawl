The Irish diaspora consists of Irish emigrants and their descendants who live outside the island of Ireland. An estimated 70 to 80 million people worldwide claim some Irish ancestry — more than ten times the population of Ireland itself — with the largest communities in the United States (where over 30 million identify as Irish American), the United Kingdom, Canada, Australia and Argentina.

History

Irish emigration is centuries old — from early medieval missionaries and the "Wild Geese" soldiers who served continental European armies after 1691 — but its defining event was the Great Famine (1845–1852), when roughly a million people died and a million more emigrated, many in "coffin ships" to North America. Ireland's population fell from about 8.2 million in 1841 to about 4.4 million by 1911 and, uniquely in Europe, continued to fall for decades. Emigration remained a structural feature of Irish life through the twentieth century — to Britain in the 1950s, to the US, Europe and Australia in the 1980s — before reversing during the Celtic Tiger boom, then resuming after the 2008 financial crisis.

Culture and influence

The diaspora has profoundly shaped the politics and culture of the receiving countries: Irish Americans became a dominant force in urban politics, the labour movement and the Catholic Church in the United States, producing a president in John F. Kennedy. Diaspora organisations supported Irish nationalism, from the Fenians to funding during the Northern Ireland conflict, and remittances long sustained families at home. Saint Patrick's Day is celebrated on a grand scale in cities such as New York, Boston, Chicago and Sydney, and Ireland has institutionalised the connection through initiatives such as The Gathering in 2013 and a Minister of State for the Diaspora.
