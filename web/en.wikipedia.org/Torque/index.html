In physics and mechanics, torque is the rotational analogue of linear force. It describes the tendency of a force to change the rotational motion of an object around an axis, fulcrum, or pivot. Just as a linear force is a push or a pull, torque may be thought of as a twist applied to an object. It is also referred to as the moment of force, or simply moment.

Definition

Torque is defined as the vector cross product of the position vector from the axis of rotation to the point of application of the force, and the force vector:

τ = r × F

Its magnitude is τ = rF sin θ, where r is the length of the lever arm, F the magnitude of the force, and θ the angle between the force and the lever arm. A force applied perpendicular to a longer lever arm produces a larger torque — the principle behind wrenches and door handles. The SI unit of torque is the newton-metre (N·m), dimensionally equal to the joule, though torque is not energy and the units are kept distinct.

Relation to angular momentum and power

Net torque on a rigid body equals the rate of change of its angular momentum, τ = Iα for a fixed axis, where I is the moment of inertia and α the angular acceleration — the rotational form of Newton's second law. The power delivered by a torque acting on a rotating shaft is P = τω, where ω is the angular velocity; this relation governs how engine torque and rotational speed trade off in vehicle performance.

Applications

Torque specifications are central to engineering: internal combustion engines and electric motors are rated by their torque curves, bolts are tightened with torque wrenches to achieve correct clamping force, and the concept is fundamental to gyroscopes, drivetrains, and robotics.
