Poster

A poster is a large printed (or, increasingly, digital) sheet designed to be displayed in a public place to promote an idea, product, event, or message. Posters combine text and graphics to attract attention at a distance and communicate quickly; they are used for advertising, propaganda, protest, education, entertainment, and decoration.

History

Printed public notices date to the earliest days of printing, but the artistic poster emerged in the late 19th century with advances in colour lithography. In Paris, Jules Chéret pioneered the large colour lithographic poster from the 1860s–70s, and artists such as Henri de Toulouse-Lautrec, Alphonse Mucha, and Théophile Steinlen made the poster a celebrated art form during the Belle Époque. The poster became a principal medium of public communication in the two world wars — famous examples include Alfred Leete's "Lord Kitchener Wants You" (1914), James Montgomery Flagg's "I Want You" Uncle Sam (1917), and the "Keep Calm and Carry On" design of 1939.

Types and uses

Common types include advertising posters, film posters, travel posters, propaganda posters, political campaign posters, event and concert posters, educational and public-health posters, and academic conference posters (poster sessions). In the mid-20th century, Swiss/International Typographic Style designers such as Josef Müller-Brockmann brought grid-based modernist design to poster art, while Polish poster school artists developed a distinctive expressive style.

Production and collecting

Posters have historically been printed by lithography, screen printing, letterpress, and offset printing; large-format digital printing is now common. Vintage posters are widely collected, and major museums hold poster collections as works of graphic design.
