Quadragesimo anno (Latin for "In the fortieth year") is an encyclical letter of Pope Pius XI, issued on 15 May 1931, forty years after Pope Leo XIII's encyclical Rerum novarum (1891). Written in the depths of the Great Depression, it develops Catholic social teaching on labour, capital, and the organization of society, and is famous for articulating the principle of subsidiarity as a cornerstone of social philosophy.

Contents

The encyclical reaffirms the teaching of Rerum novarum on the right to private property, the just wage, and the right of workers to form associations, while condemning both unrestrained capitalism — in which, Pius wrote, "immense power and despotic economic domination" had become concentrated in few hands — and communism, which it rejects for its class struggle and denial of private property. It calls for the reconstruction of the social order through vocational groups and cooperation between classes rather than conflict.

Subsidiarity

Paragraph 79 states the principle of subsidiarity: "Just as it is gravely wrong to take from individuals what they can accomplish by their own initiative and industry and give it to the community, so also it is an injustice and at the same time a grave evil and disturbance of right order to assign to a greater and higher association what lesser and subordinate organizations can do." The principle became influential in Catholic social thought and in the political theory of federalism, and was later incorporated into the law of the European Union.

Influence

Quadragesimo anno influenced corporatist experiments in some European states in the 1930s, though interpretations varied and the encyclical itself guarded against state domination. It remains a foundational document of modern Catholic social teaching, cited by later encyclicals such as John Paul II's Centesimus annus (1991), issued on its sixtieth anniversary. The German Jesuit Oswald von Nell-Breuning was the principal drafter.
