Baburnama

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Baburnama (Chagatai Turkish: Bāburnāma, "Book of Babur"; Persian: Bāburnāma) is the memoirs of Zahīr ud-Dīn Muhammad Babur (1483–1530), founder of the Mughal Empire and a great-great-great-grandson of Timur. It is an autobiographical work, originally written in the Chagatai language, known to Babur as Türki ("Turkish"), and is widely regarded as the first true autobiography in Islamic literature.

Contents

The memoirs recount Babur's life from his accession as ruler of Fergana at age twelve, through his repeated attempts to hold Samarkand, his conquest of Kabul, and his invasion of India culminating in the First Battle of Panipat (1526) and the Battle of Khanwa (1527). Alongside campaign narrative, Babur writes with unusual frankness about his feelings, his drinking and opium use, poetry, the flora and fauna of Hindustan, geography, and the people he met, making the work a remarkable source for the history and natural history of Central and South Asia in the early sixteenth century.

Translation and legacy

During the reign of Babur's grandson Akbar, the work was translated into Persian — the court language — by Abdul Rahim Khan-i-Khanan and illustrated by imperial atelier artists; several celebrated illustrated manuscripts survive. Standard English translations include those of John Leyden and William Erskine (1826) and Annette Beveridge (1922), with a later translation by Wheeler Thackston. Historians value the Baburnama both as literature and as a primary record of the founding of Mughal rule in India.
