Satire X

Satire X is the tenth of the sixteen satires of the Roman poet Juvenal, written in the late 1st or early 2nd century AD. It is among the most celebrated of Juvenal's works and the source of the famous phrase panem et circenses — "bread and circuses".

A medieval manuscript of Juvenal's Satires

Content

The poem examines the things people pray for — wealth, power, eloquence, military glory, long life and beauty — and argues that each is a snare: riches bring danger, power invites downfall, and old age is a catalogue of losses. Juvenal illustrates his argument with historical examples, including the fall of Sejanus, the deaths of Cicero and Demosthenes, and the ruin of Hannibal, Alexander the Great and Xerxes.

The satire culminates in the advice that humans should leave outcomes to the gods and pray instead for mens sana in corpore sano — "a sound mind in a sound body" — together with a spirit strong enough to endure labour and free from fear of death.

Legacy

Satire X was imitated by Samuel Johnson in The Vanity of Human Wishes (1749), often considered one of the finest poems of the 18th century. The "bread and circuses" passage remains a standard reference in discussions of political distraction and public apathy.
