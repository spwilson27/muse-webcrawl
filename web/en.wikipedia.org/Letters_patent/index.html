Letters patent

Letters patent (always in the plural) are a type of legal instrument in the form of a published written order issued by a monarch, president or other head of state, generally granting an office, right, monopoly, title or status to a person or corporation. Letters patent can be used for the creation of corporations, government offices, the granting of city status, the creation of coats of arms, and the appointment of officials. The word "patent" in this sense derives from the Latin litterae patentes, "open letters", because the document was sealed but left open for all to read — as opposed to letters close, sealed shut for the addressee alone.

Historical usage

In medieval England, letters patent were a standard means by which the Crown granted rights and privileges, recorded on the Patent Rolls in the National Archives from 1201 onward. Notable grants include those authorising overseas exploration and colonisation — for example, the letters patent issued by Henry VII to John Cabot in 1496 and by Elizabeth I to Sir Humphrey Gilbert and Walter Raleigh in the 1570s–80s — and charters founding colonies and trading companies. The modern patent for an invention descends from this practice: letters patent granted an inventor a temporary monopoly.

Modern usage

In the United Kingdom, letters patent remain the formal instrument for many royal acts: creating peers, appointing senior judges, bishops and officials, granting royal assent to some measures, conferring city status, and issuing new standards of royal styles and titles (such as the letters patent of 2012 concerning the titles of royal children). They are issued under the Great Seal of the Realm and published in the London Gazette. Commonwealth realms and other states use letters patent similarly — in Canada, for example, the Letters Patent, 1947 define the powers of the governor general, and in Australia letters patent establish royal commissions.

Form

A letters patent typically begins with the sovereign's full style and titles, addresses "all to whom these presents shall come", recites the reasons for the grant, and sets out the operative grant before concluding with the witness clause and seal. Modern texts are printed rather than engrossed in calligraphy, though ceremonial examples are still produced for some grants.
