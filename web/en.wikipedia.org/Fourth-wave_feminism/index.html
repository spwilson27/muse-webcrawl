Fourth-wave feminism

Fourth-wave activism combines street protest with social-media mobilisation.

Fourth-wave feminism is a feminist movement that began around 2012 and is characterised by a focus on the empowerment of women, the use of internet tools, and intersectionality. The fourth wave seeks to combat sexual harassment, violence against women, body shaming, and sexism in media representation, often using social media to make these issues visible and to challenge misogyny in digital spaces.

Commentators place its beginning around 2012–2013, with projects such as Laura Bates's Everyday Sexism Project and campaigns against online harassment. The movement reached global prominence with the #MeToo movement in 2017.

Background

Earlier waves pursued suffrage (first wave), legal and workplace equality (second wave), and individualism and diversity (third wave). By the early 2010s, writers such as Kira Cochrane and Jessica Valenti described a renewed movement shaped by the internet's reach and by high-profile sexual-violence cases, including the 2012 Delhi gang rape protests.

Characteristics

  • Digital activism — hashtags and viral testimony as organising tools
  • Intersectionality — attention to how gender interacts with race, class, sexuality and disability, drawing on Kimberlé Crenshaw's framework
  • Call-out culture — public challenge of sexist behaviour and institutions
  • Focus on harassment — street, workplace and online harassment as central grievances

Major campaigns

  • Everyday Sexism Project (2012) — crowdsourced catalogue of everyday sexism
  • #MeToo (2017) — founded by Tarana Burke in 2006; viral after allegations against Harvey Weinstein, leading to global reckoning over sexual abuse
  • Women's March (2017) — among the largest single-day protests in U.S. history
  • Time's Up (2018) — legal defence fund and industry initiative
  • Regional movements such as Ni una menos in Latin America and feminicide protests worldwide

Criticism and debate

Debates include whether social-media activism translates into structural change, tensions between liberal, radical and intersectional strands, inclusivity toward trans women and sex workers, and disputes over the "wave" periodisation itself, which some scholars argue obscures continuity of feminist work.
