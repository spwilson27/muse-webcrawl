Prussian Homage

Prussian Homage by Jan Matejko (1882), depicting the ceremony of 10 April 1525.

The Prussian Homage (Polish: Hołd pruski) was a ceremony held on 10 April 1525 in the market square of Kraków, in which Albert, Duke of Prussia — the former Grand Master of the Teutonic Order — paid feudal homage to King Sigismund I the Old of Poland. The event followed the secularisation of the Teutonic Order's Prussian lands and the Treaty of Kraków, ending the Polish–Teutonic War of 1519–1521. By its terms the newly created Duchy of Prussia became a fief of the Polish Crown, the first Protestant state in Europe, with Albert as its hereditary duke.

Background

Albert of Hohenzollern, Grand Master of the Teutonic Order from 1511, had refused to swear allegiance to the Polish king as required by the Second Peace of Thorn (1466). War broke out in 1519 and ended in a four-year truce. Influenced by Martin Luther's advice, Albert dissolved the Order's state, converted to Lutheranism, and negotiated the secularisation of the territory. Under the Treaty of Kraków of 8 April 1525, the Duchy of Prussia was established as a Polish fief, to descend in Albert's male line and, failing that, in the lines of his brothers, before reverting to Poland.

The ceremony

Two days after the treaty, Albert knelt before Sigismund I in Kraków's Main Market Square, received the ducal banner bearing a black eagle with the letter "S" (for Sigismundus) on its breast, and swore fealty. The moment was immortalised nearly 360 years later in the monumental 1882 painting Prussian Homage by Jan Matejko, one of the most famous works of Polish historical art, now in the National Museum in Kraków.

Significance

The homage symbolised both the decline of the Teutonic Order and the high point of Polish power in the Baltic. Through dynastic inheritance the duchy later passed to the Brandenburg electors (1618), forming Brandenburg-Prussia; the Treaty of Wehlau (1657) released the dukes from Polish suzerainty, and in 1701 Frederick I crowned himself King in Prussia — a trajectory leading eventually to the Prussian-led unification of Germany and the partitions of Poland. For this reason the 1525 homage has carried a double resonance in Polish memory: a triumph of the Jagiellonian monarchy and the first step toward the rise of Prussia.
