International Ice Patrol

The International Ice Patrol is an organization with the purpose of monitoring the presence of icebergs in the northern Atlantic Ocean and reporting their movements for safety purposes. It is operated by the United States Coast Guard but is funded by the 13 nations that signed the treaty creating it in 1914. It was established in the aftermath of the sinking of the RMS Titanic on 15 April 1912, in which an iceberg claimed more than 1,500 lives.

Mission and operations

The Ice Patrol monitors iceberg danger near the Grand Banks of Newfoundland, particularly along the transatlantic shipping lanes, and broadcasts the southeastern, southern and southwestern limits of all known ice twice daily during the ice season, which typically runs from February to July. Information is gathered largely by reconnaissance flights of Coast Guard HC-130 aircraft operating from Newfoundland, supplemented by satellite imagery, ship reports and drift modelling.

Each year, on average, more than 500 icebergs drift south past 48°N into the shipping lanes in the area surveyed. No ship that has heeded the Ice Patrol's published ice limits has been lost to an iceberg collision in the patrol area since the service began.

History

Immediately after the Titanic disaster, the United States Navy and then the Revenue Cutter Service patrolled the area. The first International Convention for the Safety of Life at Sea (SOLAS) in 1914 formalised the arrangement, mandating that the patrol continue and providing for cost-sharing among the signatory maritime nations. The Coast Guard assumed the duty and has conducted it since, with a wartime interruption.

Every year, on or near the anniversary of the Titanic sinking, the Ice Patrol participates in a memorial ceremony over the wreck site, dropping a wreath from an aircraft in remembrance of the victims.
