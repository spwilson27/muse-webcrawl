Fire tornado

A fire whirl during a wildfire.

A fire tornado, also known as a fire whirl or fire devil, is a whirlwind induced by a fire and often (at least partially) composed of flame or ash. These start with a whirl of wind, often made visible by smoke, and may occur when intense rising heat and turbulent wind conditions combine to form whirling eddies of air.

Formation

Fire whirls form when hot air rising from a fire is rotated by converging winds or by the fire's own convection column, and the rotation is stretched vertically, intensifying the vortex much as in a tornado. Most fire whirls are small — a few metres wide and tens of metres tall, lasting only a few minutes. Large wildfires, however, can generate fire-generated thunderstorms (pyrocumulonimbus) and true fire tornadoes with much greater strength.

Notable examples

A catastrophic fire whirl swept through Tokyo following the 1923 Great Kantō earthquake, killing tens of thousands of people who had fled to an open area in Honjo. During the Second World War, firestorms in Hamburg and Dresden produced intense whirls. In 2018, during the Carr Fire in California, a vortex with winds estimated at over 230 km/h (equivalent to an EF3 tornado) destroyed structures in Redding and is regarded as one of the strongest documented fire tornadoes. A fire tornado was also documented during the 2003 Canberra bushfires in Australia.

Hazards

Fire tornadoes can uproot trees, strip roofs, spread burning debris, and rapidly change a fire's direction and intensity, posing an extreme danger to firefighters. They are a factor in the erratic behaviour of large wildfires and firestorms.
