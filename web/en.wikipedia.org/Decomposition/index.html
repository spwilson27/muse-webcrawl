Decomposition

Decomposition is the process by which dead organic substances are broken down into simpler organic or inorganic matter such as carbon dioxide, water, simple sugars, and mineral salts. It is part of the nutrient cycle and is essential for recycling the finite matter that occupies physical space in the biosphere. Bodies of living organisms begin to decompose soon after death. Animals, such as worms, also help decompose organic material; organisms engaged in this process are called decomposers or detritivores.

Stages of decomposition

For animal remains, decomposition is commonly divided into stages: fresh, bloat, active decay, advanced decay, and dry or skeletal remains. Autolysis — the self-digestion of cells by their own enzymes — and putrefaction, driven by anaerobic bacteria, begin the process, producing gases (such as hydrogen sulfide, methane, cadaverine, and putrescine) and the characteristic odours of decay. Scavengers and insects, especially blow flies and carrion beetles, are major agents in the later stages.

Plant decomposition

Plant litter decomposes through leaching of soluble compounds, fragmentation by detritivores, and microbial attack on cellulose and lignin, principally by fungi. One product is humus, the stable organic fraction of soil that improves its structure and fertility.

Factors affecting decomposition

The rate of decomposition depends on temperature, moisture, soil pH, oxygen, and the chemical composition (such as the carbon-to-nitrogen ratio and lignin content) of the material. Cold, dry, or anaerobic environments greatly slow decay — preserving bodies as mummies or in peat bogs — whereas warm, moist, aerobic conditions accelerate it.

Applications

Understanding decomposition is used in forensic science (estimating the post-mortem interval), composting and waste management, agriculture, and archaeology.
