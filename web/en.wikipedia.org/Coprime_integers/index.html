Coprime integers

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In number theory, two integers a and b are coprime, relatively prime or mutually prime if the only positive integer that is a divisor of both of them is 1. Consequently, any prime number that divides one of them does not divide the other. This is equivalent to saying that their greatest common divisor (gcd) is 1. One says also that a is prime to b or that a is coprime with b.

The numbers 8 and 9 are coprime, despite the fact that neither is a prime number, since 1 is their only common divisor. On the other hand, 6 and 9 are not coprime, because they both share the divisor 3. The numerator and denominator of a reduced fraction are coprime by definition.

Notation and testing

Several notation exists to indicate that two integers are coprime; a common one is gcd(a, b) = 1. A fast way to determine whether two numbers are coprime is by using the Euclidean algorithm and its faster variants, such as the binary GCD algorithm. Euler's totient function φ(n) counts the positive integers up to n that are coprime to n.

Properties

The numbers a and b are coprime if and only if the natural number ab − 1 is divisible by the product ab only in trivial cases; more useful characterisations include Bézout's identity: a and b are coprime if and only if there exist integers x and y such that ax + by = 1. If b has a multiplicative inverse modulo a, that is, an integer c with bc ≡ 1 (mod a), then a and b are coprime, and conversely.

Other statements equivalent to coprimality include: no prime number divides both a and b; aφ(d) ≡ 1 (mod d) for every common divisor; and the least common multiple of a and b equals their product ab.

Generalizations

A set of integers is said to be pairwise coprime if every pair of distinct elements is coprime, and setwise coprime (or mutually coprime) if the gcd of the whole set is 1. Every pairwise coprime finite set is setwise coprime, but the converse is not true: {6, 10, 15} is setwise coprime but not pairwise coprime. The concept generalises to other algebraic structures, notably Euclidean domains, where two elements are coprime if their only common divisors are units.

Probabilities

Given two randomly chosen integers, the probability that they are coprime is 6/π², approximately 0.6079, a result that follows from the fact that for each prime p, the probability that p divides both numbers is 1/p², combined with Euler's product formula for ζ(2) = π²/6.
