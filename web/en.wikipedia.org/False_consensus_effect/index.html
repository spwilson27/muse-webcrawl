False consensus effect

In psychology, the false consensus effect, also known as consensus bias, is a pervasive cognitive bias that causes people to "see their own behavioral choices and judgments as relatively common and appropriate to existing circumstances". In other words, they assume that their personal qualities, characteristics, beliefs, and actions are relatively widespread through the general population.

Origin

The effect was named and systematically studied by social psychologist Lee Ross and his colleagues at Stanford in 1977. In their experiments, participants who agreed to wear a large sandwich-board sign advertising a restaurant estimated that a much higher percentage of their peers would also agree than did participants who refused; each group saw its own choice as the more common one.

Explanations

Several mechanisms may contribute: people mainly associate with others who are similar to themselves (selective exposure), making their own views seem more common than they are; their own opinion is the most salient and accessible example; and projecting one's own preferences onto others serves motivational goals such as maintaining self-esteem. The bias is related to the availability heuristic and to egocentrism in social judgment.

Implications

The false consensus effect can contribute to overconfidence in one's opinions, misunderstanding in negotiation, and the assumption in marketing or politics that one's preferred product or candidate is more popular than it is. It is considered one of the biases that shape attribution, alongside the fundamental attribution error. Its opposite pattern, in which people underestimate the commonality of their abilities or desirable traits, is sometimes called the false uniqueness effect.
