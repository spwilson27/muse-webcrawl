Deforestation in Indonesia

Deforestation in Indonesia
Forest coverIndonesia retains roughly half its land area as forest, down from about 84% in 1900
Main driversOil palm plantations, pulpwood plantations, logging, mining, smallholder agriculture, fire
Key regionsSumatra, Kalimantan (Borneo), Papua, Sulawesi
PolicyPermanent moratorium on new primary-forest and peatland clearing (made permanent 2019)

Deforestation in Indonesia involves the long-term removal of forests over much of the country and has had major ecological and social impacts. Indonesia is home to some of the world's most biodiverse rainforests and the third-largest area of tropical forest, but between 1950 and the 2010s it lost tens of millions of hectares, with particularly rapid clearing from the 1980s onward.

Causes

The principal driver is conversion to plantations, above all oil palm — Indonesia is the world's largest producer of palm oil — and pulpwood estates supplying the paper industry. Logging, both legal and illegal, mining concessions, infrastructure and shifting cultivation also contribute. Drainage of carbon-rich peatlands for plantations makes the land highly flammable; the resulting fires, notably in 1997–98 and 2015, produced severe regional haze and very large greenhouse-gas emissions.

Impacts

Forest loss threatens endemic species including the Sumatran tiger, orangutans, and the Sumatran rhinoceros, and undermines the livelihoods of indigenous and forest-dependent communities. Because of deforestation and peat decomposition, Indonesia is among the world's largest greenhouse-gas emitters in high-deforestation years.

Policy responses

Under President Susilo Bambang Yudhoyono, Indonesia signed a US$1 billion partnership with Norway in 2010 and imposed a moratorium on new clearing licences in primary forest and peatland; President Joko Widodo made the moratorium permanent in 2019 and announced a separate halt to new oil-palm permits (2018–2021). Annual primary-forest loss fell markedly from 2017 onward, a trend attributed to policy, corporate "no deforestation" commitments (NDPE), wet weather and low palm-oil prices, though clearing pressures persist in Papua and for mining and food-estate projects.
