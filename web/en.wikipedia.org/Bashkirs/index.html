Bashkirs


Bashkirs in traditional dress.

The Bashkirs (Bashkir: Башҡорттар, Başqorttar) are a Kipchak Turkic ethnic group, indigenous to Bashkortostan and the historical region of Badzhgard, stretching from the left bank of the Volga to the Tobol River and from the syrts of the Southern Urals northward. Most Bashkirs speak the Bashkir language and live principally in the Russian republic of Bashkortostan, where they number about 1.6 million, with additional populations across Russia and in Central Asia.

History

Bashkir tribes appear in medieval sources, including the account of Ahmad ibn Fadlan, who encountered them in 921–922 on his journey to Volga Bulgaria. Islam spread among the Bashkirs from the 10th century onward. Their lands were incorporated into the Golden Horde, and after its decline they were divided among the khanates of Kazan, Siberia, and the Nogai Horde. In the 16th century the Bashkirs accepted Russian suzerainty, in return for recognition of their patrimonial land rights, but repeatedly rose in revolt against colonization and taxation, most famously in the Bashkir rebellions of the 17th and 18th centuries and in support of Pugachev's Rebellion, in which the Bashkir hero Salawat Yulayev became a national symbol.

Culture

Traditionally semi-nomadic herders celebrated for horsemanship, beekeeping (including wild-hive bort beekeeping), and honey, Bashkirs today are predominantly Sunni Muslims. Cultural symbols include the kurai (a long end-blown flute made from a reed), the epic Ural-batyr, and the Sabantuy festival.
