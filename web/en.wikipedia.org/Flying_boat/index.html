Flying boat

A flying boat is a type of fixed-wing seaplane with a hull that allows it to land on and take off from water. Unlike floatplanes, whose fuselage is supported above the water by floats, a flying boat's fuselage itself is a watertight, boat-like hull that provides buoyancy. Small stabilizing floats or sponsons near the wingtips keep the aircraft upright on the water.

History

Glenn Curtiss pioneered practical flying boats in the 1910s, and during the First World War they served in maritime patrol roles. The interwar years were their golden age: large flying boats such as the Dornier Do X, the Short Empire, and the Boeing 314 Clipper carried passengers on long overwater routes where no airfields existed, offering spacious, ship-like luxury. During the Second World War, types such as the Consolidated PBY Catalina and Short Sunderland hunted submarines and rescued downed airmen.

Decline and modern use

After 1945, the worldwide network of land runways built during the war, plus faster land-based airliners, made flying boats obsolete for most transport. They survive in niches: Canadair water bombers fight forest fires by scooping water from lakes, and a few types serve remote coastal and island communities.

Design

The hull bottom is shaped with a planing surface and a distinct "step" that breaks the water's suction during takeoff. Hulls sacrifice aerodynamic cleanliness for seaworthiness, so flying boats are slower and thirstier than comparable landplanes.
