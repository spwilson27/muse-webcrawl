Klinefelter syndrome

Klinefelter syndrome (KS), also known as 47,XXY or XXY syndrome, is a genetic condition in which a male is born with an extra copy of the X chromosome. The primary features are infertility and small, poorly functioning testicles. It is the most common sex-chromosome aneuploidy in humans, occurring in roughly 1 in 500 to 1 in 1,000 live male births; many cases remain undiagnosed.

Signs and symptoms

Boys with KS may have weak muscles, delayed speech and a quiet, shy temperament. At puberty, low testosterone leads to reduced facial and body hair, small testes, increased height with long limbs, and sometimes breast development (gynecomastia). Adults are typically infertile due to impaired sperm production, and there are increased risks of osteoporosis, type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome and breast cancer relative to other men. Intelligence is usually in the normal range, though language-based learning difficulties are common.

Cause and diagnosis

The extra X chromosome arises from nondisjunction during meiosis in either parent; maternal age slightly increases risk. Variants include mosaicism (46,XY/47,XXY) and, more rarely, 48,XXXY or 49,XXXXY, which are associated with more marked effects. Diagnosis is by chromosome analysis (karyotype), possible prenatally or at any age; many cases are found during infertility workups.

History and management

The condition was described by the American endocrinologist Harry Klinefelter in 1942; the chromosomal basis was identified in 1959 by Patricia Jacobs and John Strong. Management centres on testosterone replacement from puberty onward, monitoring of bone and metabolic health, speech and educational support in childhood, and, increasingly, surgical sperm retrieval combined with ICSI, which can allow biological fatherhood in some men.
