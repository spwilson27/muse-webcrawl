The hippopotamus (Hippopotamus amphibius), or common hippopotamus, is a large semiaquatic mammal native to sub-Saharan Africa. One of only two living species in the family Hippopotamidae (the other being the much smaller pygmy hippopotamus), it is among the largest land mammals, after elephants and some rhinoceroses. Despite its name — Greek for "river horse" — its closest living relatives are cetaceans: whales and dolphins.

Biology

Hippos have barrel-shaped bodies, nearly hairless grey-purple skin, short legs and huge mouths whose canine teeth can exceed 50 cm. Eyes, ears and nostrils sit high on the skull, letting the animal stay almost fully submerged. A glandular secretion acts as a natural sunscreen. Adults typically weigh 1,500 kg or more, yet can gallop at about 30 km/h over short distances.

Behaviour and ecology

Groups ("bloats") of 10–30 spend the day in rivers and lakes to keep cool, emerging at dusk to graze on grasses, sometimes walking several kilometres in a night. Hippos are highly territorial in water and are considered among Africa's most dangerous animals to humans. Their dung fertilises aquatic food webs, though in excess it can deoxygenate water during dry-season die-offs.

Conservation

The IUCN lists the common hippopotamus as Vulnerable, threatened by habitat loss and poaching for meat and ivory (from its teeth). A famous introduced population in Colombia descends from animals kept by Pablo Escobar. See also pygmy hippopotamus.
