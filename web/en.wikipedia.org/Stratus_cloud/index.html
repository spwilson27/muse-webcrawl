Stratus cloud

Stratus clouds are low-level clouds characterised by horizontal layering with a uniform base, forming flat, featureless sheets that often cover the whole sky. Their name comes from the Latin stratus, meaning a layer or blanket. Stratus forms in stable air when moist air is cooled to its dew point, typically by gentle lifting, advection over a colder surface, or radiative cooling.

Characteristics

Stratus clouds form at altitudes below about 2,000 metres (6,600 ft). They may produce light drizzle or snow grains but rarely heavy precipitation. When stratus forms at ground level it is called fog; a fog layer that lifts is often described as raised stratus. The genus is subdivided into species including nebulosus (featureless and veil-like) and fractus ( ragged shreds), and varieties such as opacus and translucidus.

Related forms

Higher sheet clouds share the layered structure: altostratus at middle levels and cirrostratus at high levels. When a stratus layer thickens and produces steady precipitation, it may merge into nimbostratus. Stratus is an important indicator of a stable atmosphere and can persist for days under a temperature inversion, contributing to poor air quality in valleys and cities.
