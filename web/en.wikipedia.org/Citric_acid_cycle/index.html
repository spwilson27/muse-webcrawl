Citric acid cycle

The citric acid cycle — also known as the Krebs cycle, after Hans Krebs, or the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle — is a series of biochemical reactions that release stored energy through the oxidation of acetyl-CoA derived from carbohydrates, fats and proteins. The cycle is used by all aerobic organisms to generate energy-rich electron carriers (NADH and FADH2) that feed the electron transport chain, and it also provides precursors for amino acids and other biosyntheses. In eukaryotes it occurs in the mitochondrial matrix; in prokaryotes, in the cytosol.


The eight steps of the citric acid cycle, in which acetyl-CoA is oxidised to two molecules of CO2.

Overview

Acetyl-CoA, produced from pyruvate (from glycolysis), from fatty-acid β-oxidation, or from amino-acid breakdown, enters the cycle by condensing with oxaloacetate to form citrate. Through eight enzyme-catalysed steps, citrate is progressively oxidised and rearranged, releasing two molecules of CO2 and regenerating oxaloacetate. Each turn yields 3 NADH, 1 FADH2, and 1 GTP (or ATP). Because glycolysis produces two acetyl-CoA per glucose, the cycle turns twice per glucose molecule.

History

The cycle was elucidated principally by Hans Adolf Krebs and William Johnson in 1937 at the University of Sheffield, building on work by Albert Szent-Györgyi on dicarboxylic acids in respiration. Krebs shared the 1953 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for the discovery (with Fritz Lipmann, recognised for coenzyme A).

Regulation and significance

Key control points are citrate synthase, isocitrate dehydrogenase (activated by ADP, inhibited by ATP and NADH) and the α-ketoglutarate dehydrogenase complex. Beyond energy, the cycle is the hub of metabolism: its intermediates are withdrawn for synthesis of amino acids, porphyrins (from succinyl-CoA, giving haem), fatty acids (from citrate) and glucose (from oxaloacetate), and must be replenished by anaplerotic reactions.
