Nicolas Boileau-Despréaux (November 1, 1636 – March 13, 1711), often known simply as Boileau, was a French poet and critic who was the chief arbiter of French classical taste in the age of Louis XIV. His verse treatise L'Art poétique (1674) codified the rules of French classicism and strongly influenced English Augustan writers such as Alexander Pope.

Boileau first gained fame with his Satires (from 1666), modelled on Horace and Juvenal. He championed his friends Molière, Racine and La Fontaine, defended the 'Ancients' in the Quarrel of the Ancients and the Moderns, and translated Longinus's On the Sublime into French in 1674, a landmark for the aesthetics of the sublime.

Legacy

Boileau was appointed historiographer to Louis XIV in 1677 and elected to the Académie française in 1684. His couplets established clarity, reason and decorum as the standards of French verse, and his epigrammatic lines—such as 'whatever is well conceived is clearly said'—became proverbial. Pope's Essay on Criticism is deeply indebted to him.
