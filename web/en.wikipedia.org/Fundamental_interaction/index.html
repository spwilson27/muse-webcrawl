Fundamental interaction

In physics, the fundamental interactions (or fundamental forces) are the interactions that cannot be reduced to more basic ones. There are four confirmed fundamental interactions: gravity, electromagnetism, the weak interaction, and the strong interaction. All known physical phenomena — from the structure of atoms to the motions of galaxies — can in principle be explained in terms of these four and the elementary particles they act upon.

The strong interaction binds quarks into protons and neutrons and holds atomic nuclei together; it is described by quantum chromodynamics (QCD). The electromagnetic interaction, described by quantum electrodynamics (QED), governs chemistry, light, and electricity. The weak interaction is responsible for beta decay and other flavour-changing processes and is essential to nuclear fusion in stars. Gravity, by far the weakest, dominates at astronomical scales because it is always attractive and acts on all mass–energy; it is described classically by general relativity and has resisted quantisation.

Unification

The Standard Model of particle physics unifies electromagnetism and the weak interaction into the electroweak interaction at high energies, and describes the strong interaction alongside them as a gauge theory. Grand unified theories attempt to merge the strong force with the electroweak force, while theories such as string theory aim at a "theory of everything" including gravity. No fifth fundamental force has been confirmed, although various anomalies and dark-matter-related hypotheses continue to be investigated; the Higgs interaction is sometimes informally counted separately because it gives particles their masses.
