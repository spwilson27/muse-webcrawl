Mississippian culture

The Mississippian culture was a Native American civilization that flourished in what is now the Midwestern, Eastern, and Southeastern United States from approximately 800 AD until 1600 AD, varying regionally. It is known for building large earthen platform mounds and, in some places, great towns and chiefdoms centred on intensive maize agriculture.

Characteristics

Mississippian societies adopted maize-based agriculture (supplemented by beans and squash), settled in towns with plazas and palisades, and built flat-topped earthen mounds supporting temples, elite residences, and charnel houses. A shared religious-artistic tradition, the Southeastern Ceremonial Complex (SECC), spread motifs such as the falcon dancer, the hand-and-eye symbol, and chunkey stones across the region. Political organization centred on ranked chiefdoms ruled by hereditary elites.

Major centres

The largest centre was Cahokia, near present-day St. Louis, whose Monks Mound is the largest prehistoric earthwork in the Americas north of Mexico; at its peak around 1100 AD, Cahokia may have held 10,000–20,000 people. Other major sites include Moundville (Alabama), Etowah and Ocmulgee (Georgia), Spiro (Oklahoma), and Angel Mounds (Indiana). Hernando de Soto's expedition of 1539–1543 encountered powerful Mississippian chiefdoms such as Cofitachequi and Coosa; disease and social disruption in the following century transformed these societies into the historic tribes—including the Choctaw, Chickasaw, Muscogee, Cherokee, and Natchez—known from the colonial era.

Decline

Many Mississippian centres were declining before European contact, through environmental stress, warfare, and political fragmentation, and epidemics introduced from the Old World accelerated the collapse. Descendant peoples preserve Mississippian traditions in oral history, language, and ceremonial practice.
