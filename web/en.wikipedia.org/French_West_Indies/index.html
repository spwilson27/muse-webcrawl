French West Indies

The French West Indies or French Antilles (French: Antilles françaises) are the French-controlled territories in the Caribbean's Antilles island chain. Today they comprise the overseas departments of Guadeloupe and Martinique, and the overseas collectivities of Saint Martin and Saint Barthélemy. French and Antillean Creole are the principal languages, and the inhabitants are French citizens; the territories use the euro and send representatives to the French Parliament.

History

France began colonizing the Lesser Antilles in 1625–1635, settling Saint-Christophe (Saint Kitts), Guadeloupe, and Martinique. Sugar plantations worked by enslaved Africans became the economic engine of the colonies, governed under the Code Noir of 1685. Saint-Domingue (present-day Haiti), on the western third of Hispaniola, became France's richest colony before the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804) created the first independent Black republic. Slavery was abolished in the French colonies in 1848 under the Second Republic, following the advocacy of Victor Schœlcher.

Under the departmentalization law of 1946, Guadeloupe and Martinique became overseas departments of France, fully integrated into the Republic. Saint Barthélemy and the French part of Saint Martin, formerly administered from Guadeloupe, voted in 2003 to become separate overseas collectivities, a status effective in 2007.

Geography and culture

The French Antilles lie in the volcanic Lesser Antilles arc; notable features include La Soufrière volcano on Guadeloupe and Mount Pelée on Martinique, whose 1902 eruption destroyed the city of Saint-Pierre, killing some 30,000 people. The culture blends French, African, and Caribbean influences, expressed in Creole languages, zouk music (pioneered by the Guadeloupean band Kassav'), and cuisines based on seafood, plantains, and spices.
