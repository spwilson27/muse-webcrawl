Giovanni Pierluigi da Palestrina (c. 1525 – 2 February 1594) was an Italian composer of sacred music and the best-known sixteenth-century representative of the Roman School of musical composition. His smooth, carefully controlled polyphony became the model of the stile antico and has profoundly influenced the teaching of counterpoint ever since.

Life

Palestrina was born in the town of Palestrina, near Rome, from which his surname derives. He became a choirboy at the basilica of Santa Maria Maggiore in Rome and later its organist and choirmaster. In 1551 he was appointed maestro of the Cappella Giulia at St Peter's, and in 1555 he briefly joined the Sistine Chapel Choir under Pope Julius III. Married, he was dismissed by the stricter Paul IV but later rehired at St John Lateran and Santa Maria Maggiore, returning to the Julian Chapel in 1571, where he remained for the rest of his life. He survived the deaths of his wife, sons and brothers in plague outbreaks of the 1570s and died in Rome in 1594.

Music

Palestrina left hundreds of compositions, including 105 masses, more than 300 motets, offertories, hymns, Magnificats and a small number of secular madrigals. His most famous work is the Missa Papae Marcelli (Pope Marcellus Mass). A persistent legend holds that it was written to persuade the Council of Trent that polyphonic music could set sacred texts intelligibly and thus "saved" church polyphony; historians now treat the story as largely mythical, though the mass remains the emblem of his style.

His counterpoint is characterised by smooth melodic lines, careful preparation and resolution of dissonance, and a balance among voices that pedagogues later codified in Johann Fux's Gradus ad Parnassum (1725) as "species counterpoint".

Legacy

Palestrina's reputation as the "Prince of Music" was cemented in the nineteenth century with the publication of his complete works and has endured in choral repertoires worldwide. The Italian conservatory in Rome and the town of his birth honour his name.
