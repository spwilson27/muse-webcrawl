The Guide for the Perplexed

The Guide was written in Judeo-Arabic and soon translated into Hebrew.

The Guide for the Perplexed (Arabic: Dalālat al-ḥā'irīn; Hebrew: Moreh Nevukhim) is a work of Jewish theology and philosophy by Moses Maimonides (the Rambam), written around 1190 in Judeo-Arabic and addressed to his student Joseph ben Judah. It seeks to reconcile Aristotelian philosophy with the teachings of the Hebrew Bible and rabbinic tradition, arguing that anthropomorphic descriptions of God must be understood allegorically.

Contents

The work is divided into three parts. The first explains biblical terms applied to God through homonyms and negative theology — God can only be described by what He is not. The second treats philosophical questions such as the creation of the world, prophecy and providence. The third discusses the reasons for the commandments, the problem of evil, and the parable of the palace, Maimonides' image of differing degrees of human perfection and knowledge of God.

Influence

The Guide provoked the Maimonidean controversies within Judaism but became foundational for Jewish philosophy; it also influenced Christian scholastics such as Thomas Aquinas and Muslim and European philosophers. Shlomo Pines' 1963 English translation remains standard.
