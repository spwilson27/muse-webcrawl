In the United States Armed Forces, a major general is a two-star general officer in the United States Army, Marine Corps, Air Force, and Space Force. A major general ranks above a brigadier general and below a lieutenant general, with the pay grade O-8; the naval equivalent is rear admiral (upper half). The rank is generally a permanent grade, and major generals typically command divisions of 10,000 to 20,000 soldiers or hold senior staff and deputy command appointments.

History

The rank dates to the Continental Army, in which George Washington appointed major generals in 1775. In the nineteenth century it was for long periods the highest substantive rank in the small Regular Army; Winfield Scott, for example, held it as commanding general. During the Civil War hundreds of officers held the grade in the volunteer forces. The modern statutory framework came with twentieth-century personnel acts; today, United States law caps the number of general officers on active duty, and appointments to major general are made by the President with the advice and consent of the Senate from among brigadier generals with the requisite time in grade.

Role and insignia

Army and Marine Corps major generals command divisions, serve as deputy commanders of corps and combatant commands, and direct major staff directorates; Air Force major generals command numbered air forces and major directorates. Insignia consists of two silver stars on shoulder boards and rank flags. By statute, major generals must retire after five years in grade or 35 years of service, whichever is later, unless promoted or deferred; the mandatory retirement age framework for flag officers generally requires retirement by age 64.

Limits by service

The number of two-star officers is controlled by Title 10 of the United States Code, which sets service-by-service ceilings for general and flag officers on active duty, with a portion of joint-duty billets allocated separately.
