Ontological argument

The ontological argument is a philosophical argument for the existence of God that uses ontology—the study of being—as its basis. Unlike cosmological or teleological arguments, which appeal to observations of the world, ontological arguments proceed a priori, attempting to show that God's existence follows from the very concept or definition of God. If sound, such an argument would demonstrate that denying God's existence is self-contradictory.

The argument was first articulated by the medieval monk and philosopher Anselm of Canterbury in his Proslogion (1077–1078). Versions were later defended by René Descartes, Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, and, in the twentieth century, by Norman Malcolm, Charles Hartshorne, Alvin Plantinga, and Kurt Gödel. It has been criticized by, among others, Gaunilo of Marmoutiers, Thomas Aquinas, Immanuel Kant, and David Hume.

Anselm's argument

Anselm defined God as "that than which nothing greater can be conceived." He reasoned that such a being exists at least in the understanding, for even the fool who denies God understands the phrase. But a being that exists in reality as well as in the understanding is greater than one that exists only in the understanding. Therefore, if God existed only in the understanding, we could conceive of a greater being—one that also exists in reality—which contradicts the definition. Hence God must exist in reality.

In a second formulation in chapter III of the Proslogion, Anselm argued that a being that cannot be conceived not to exist is greater than one that can be conceived not to exist; God, as the greatest conceivable being, therefore exists necessarily.

Contemporary responses

Anselm's contemporary Gaunilo replied with the "perfect island" parody: by the same reasoning, one could prove the existence of a perfect island, since an island that exists would be greater than one that exists only in the mind. Anselm responded that the argument applies only to a being that is maximally great in every respect, not to things that admit of indefinite improvement.

Descartes and Leibniz

Descartes argued in the Meditations that existence is a perfection, and that the idea of a supremely perfect being lacking existence would be a contradiction, just as a triangle lacking three sides would be. Leibniz sought to shore up the argument by proving that the concept of a most perfect being is internally consistent—that all perfections are compatible with one another—without which, he conceded, the argument could not get started.

Kant's criticism

Immanuel Kant advanced the most influential objection: "being" is not a real predicate. A hundred actual thalers, he wrote, do not contain the least bit more than a hundred possible thalers; existence does not add a property to a concept. To say a thing exists is to say the concept is instantiated, not to enlarge the concept itself. If existence is not a predicate, it cannot be part of God's definition from which God's existence could be extracted.

Modern versions

Alvin Plantinga developed a modal version using possible-worlds semantics: if it is possible that a maximally great being exists, and maximal greatness includes necessary existence, then such a being exists in every possible world, including the actual one. Critics reply that the key premise—that such a being is possible—begs the question. Kurt Gödel formalized an ontological proof in modal logic based on axioms about "positive properties"; his proof was later verified by computer scientists using automated theorem provers, though the soundness of its axioms remains disputed.
