A maar lake in the Volcanic Eifel, Germany.

A maar is a broad, low-relief volcanic crater caused by a phreatomagmatic eruption—an explosion that occurs when groundwater comes into contact with hot lava or magma. A maar characteristically fills with water to form a relatively shallow crater lake. The name comes from a Moselle Franconian dialect word used for the volcanic lakes of the Eifel mountains of western Germany, derived from Latin mare, meaning "sea".

Maars range in size from tens of metres to several kilometres across. Most maars have low rims composed of a mixture of loose fragments of volcanic rock and rocks torn from the walls of the diatreme (the vertical volcanic conduit below the crater). After an eruption, the crater may fill with water from rainfall or groundwater, forming a lake; sediments accumulating in maar lakes form valuable, finely layered archives for palaeoclimate research.

Formation

Maars are formed by subsurface phreatomagmatic explosions. When rising magma meets groundwater, the water flashes to steam and expands explosively, fragmenting the magma and the surrounding country rock. Repeated explosions progressively excavate a funnel-shaped crater that cuts below the surrounding ground surface — distinguishing maars from craters that sit atop accumulated eruptive material. The underlying carrot-shaped volcanic pipe is called a diatreme.

Examples

  • The maars of the Volcanic Eifel in Germany, including the Pulvermaar and the Daun maars, are the type examples from which the term derives.
  • Al Wahbah crater in Saudi Arabia, a large maar in the Harrat Rahat volcanic field.
  • Lake Nyos and Lake Monoun in Cameroon, maar lakes known for limnic eruptions of dissolved carbon dioxide in the 1980s.
  • The Kilbourne Hole and Hunt's Hole in New Mexico, United States.
  • Lake Purrumbete and Tower Hill in Victoria, Australia.

Scientific value

Because maar lakes are often deep relative to their area and sheltered from disturbance, their sediments accumulate in annual layers (varves). Cores from lakes such as the Meerfelder Maar in Germany have been used to reconstruct past climates and environments with high precision, and the Laacher See region maars preserve records of late-glacial vegetation change.
