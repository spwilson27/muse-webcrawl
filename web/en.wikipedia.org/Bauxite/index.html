Bauxite

Bauxite
TypeSedimentary rock / aluminium ore
Main mineralsGibbsite, boehmite, diaspore, with haematite and kaolinite
Named afterLes Baux-de-Provence, France

Bauxite is a sedimentary rock with a relatively high aluminium content. It is the world's main source of aluminium and gallium. Bauxite consists mostly of the aluminium minerals gibbsite, boehmite and diaspore, mixed with iron oxides such as haematite and goethite, the clay mineral kaolinite and small amounts of anatase and rutile.

Formation

Bauxite forms by intense lateritic weathering of aluminium-rich rocks in tropical and subtropical climates, in which silica and other soluble material are leached away, leaving aluminium hydroxides and iron oxides concentrated near the surface. Most deposits are young, shallow and mined by open-cast methods.

Production and processing

Australia is the largest producer, followed by Guinea and China. Bauxite is refined into alumina (aluminium oxide) by the Bayer process, and the alumina is then smelted into aluminium metal by the Hall–Héroult process. The alkaline waste from refining, known as red mud, is an environmental concern. Bauxite is also used in cement, abrasives and refractories.

History

The rock was first recognised in 1821 by the French geologist Pierre Berthier near the village of Les Baux-de-Provence in southern France, from which it takes its name.
