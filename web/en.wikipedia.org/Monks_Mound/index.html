Monks Mound

Monks Mound is the largest pre-Columbian earthwork in North America north of Mexico. It stands at the Cahokia Mounds State Historic Site near Collinsville, Illinois, about 13 kilometres (8 miles) northeast of St. Louis, Missouri, across the Mississippi River. Built by the Mississippian culture between roughly 900 and 1200 CE, the mound covers about 14 acres (5.7 hectares), rises some 30 metres (100 feet) in four terraces, and contains an estimated 22 million cubic feet of earth, all of it moved in baskets by hand.

Function and construction

Monks Mound was the centrepiece of Cahokia, the largest city of the Mississippian world, which at its peak around 1100 CE had a population comparable to contemporary London. The mound served as a platform for a great building — interpreted as the residence or temple of Cahokia's paramount chief — measuring about 105 by 48 feet and up to 50 feet tall. Construction occurred in stages: an originally smaller mound was enlarged repeatedly with successive layers of basket-loaded soil and clay. The French Trappist monks who settled nearby in the early 19th century and cultivated a garden on one terrace gave the mound its modern name.

Archaeology and preservation

Excavations on the summit reached a large building floor and evidence of massive posts. The mound's flat summit and terraces have suffered slumping and erosion in modern times, requiring stabilisation work. Cahokia Mounds is preserved as an Illinois state historic site and was designated a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1982. From the summit, visitors can see the St. Louis skyline and the Gateway Arch across the river.
