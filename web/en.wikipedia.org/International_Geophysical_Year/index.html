International Geophysical Year

Dates1 July 1957 – 31 December 1958
Participants67 nations
DisciplinesMeteorology, oceanography, seismology, geomagnetism, aurora, cosmic rays, glaciology, and others
LandmarksLaunch of Sputnik 1 and Explorer 1; discovery of the Van Allen radiation belts; Antarctic Treaty (1959)

The International Geophysical Year (IGY) was an international scientific project that lasted from 1 July 1957 to 31 December 1958. It marked the end of a long period during the Cold War when scientific interchange between East and West had been seriously curtailed, and it involved some 67 countries in coordinated Earth-science research.

The IGY coordinated simultaneous observations from pole to pole.

Origins

The IGY grew out of the earlier International Polar Years (1882–83 and 1932–33). In April 1950 a group of scientists including Lloyd Berkner, Sydney Chapman, and James Van Allen proposed a third, worldwide polar year timed to coincide with a maximum of solar activity in 1957–58. The proposal was taken up by the International Council of Scientific Unions, which broadened it into a global geophysical year, coordinated by a special committee (CSAGI) under Chapman.

Activities

Thousands of scientists staffed stations from the Arctic to Antarctica — the United States established the Amundsen–Scott South Pole Station, and twelve nations operated Antarctic research bases. Programmes studied the aurora and airglow, cosmic rays, geomagnetism, gravity, ionospheric physics, meteorology, oceanography, seismology, and solar activity. World Data Centers were created to archive and share observations, and "World Days" coordinated intensive global measurements. IGY results confirmed key aspects of plate tectonics through mapping of mid-ocean ridges and advanced understanding of the magnetosphere.

The satellite race

Both the United States and the Soviet Union announced plans to launch artificial satellites for the IGY. The USSR's Sputnik 1, launched on 4 October 1957, stunned the world and began the Space Race; the US Explorer 1 followed on 31 January 1958 carrying Van Allen's instrument, which discovered the radiation belts that bear his name. The scientific cooperation of the IGY also paved the way for the Antarctic Treaty of 1959, which reserved the continent for peaceful research.
