Speech or Debate Clause

Speech or Debate Clause
Part of Article I, Section 6, Clause 1 of the U.S. Constitution
Purpose: protect legislative independence

The Speech or Debate Clause is a clause in the United States Constitution (Article I, Section 6, Clause 1) that immunises members of Congress from lawsuits and arrest for any speech or debate in either house of Congress. It provides that Senators and Representatives "shall in all Cases, except Treason, Felony and Breach of the Peace, be privileged from Arrest during their Attendance at the Session of their respective Houses... and for any Speech or Debate in either House, they shall not be questioned in any other Place."

Purpose

The clause is intended to protect the independence and integrity of the legislature by preventing the executive or judiciary from intimidating members through prosecutions based on their legislative acts. It has roots in English parliamentary privilege, notably Article 9 of the Bill of Rights 1689.

Case law

Supreme Court decisions such as Gravel v. United States (1972) extended the protection to legislative aides performing acts that would be privileged if done by a member, while United States v. Brewster (1972) held that the clause does not shield members from prosecution for taking bribes.
