Letter case

Letter case
CasesUppercase (majuscule), lowercase (minuscule)
Origin of termsTypesetters' type cases: capitals kept in the upper case, small letters in the lower
Bicameral scriptsLatin, Greek, Cyrillic, Armenian, Georgian (partly), others

Letter case is the distinction between the larger uppercase (or majuscule) letters and the smaller lowercase (or minuscule) letters in bicameral writing systems such as the Latin, Greek, and Cyrillic alphabets. The terms derive from printing: in the days of movable type, capital letters were stored in the upper of a pair of type cases and the small letters in the lower case.

History

Classical Latin inscriptions were written entirely in capitals. Lowercase forms developed from the cursive hands of late antiquity and the early Middle Ages — uncial, half-uncial, and finally the Carolingian minuscule standardised around 800 AD. For centuries the two sets of forms were used interchangeably; the modern convention — capitals for sentence beginnings and proper names, minuscules elsewhere — crystallised only with early modern printing.

Usage

In English, uppercase letters begin sentences, mark proper nouns, form acronyms, and (in strings of text) conventionally signal emphasis or shouting in digital communication. Style rules differ by language: German capitalises all nouns, while French capitalises days and months only in certain positions.

In computing

Case distinctions pervade computing: many file systems, programming languages, and protocols are case-sensitive, while others (such as DNS names) are not. Named styles of multi-word capitalisation include camel case (camelCase), Pascal case, snake case, and kebab case, and Unicode assigns case mappings and case-folding rules for thousands of characters, with special behaviour in languages such as Turkish, where the uppercase of i is İ.
