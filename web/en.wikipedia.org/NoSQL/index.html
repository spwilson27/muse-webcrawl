NoSQL

NoSQL (originally referring to "non-SQL" or "non-relational") is an approach to database design that focuses on providing a mechanism for storage and retrieval of data that is modeled in means other than the tabular relations used in relational databases. Such databases have existed since the late 1960s, but the name "NoSQL" was only coined in the early 21st century, triggered by the needs of Web 2.0 companies. NoSQL databases are increasingly used in big data and real-time web applications.

Types of NoSQL databases

  • Document stores (e.g. MongoDB, CouchDB) store semi-structured documents such as JSON or BSON.
  • Key–value stores (e.g. Redis, Amazon DynamoDB) associate unique keys with values.
  • Wide-column stores (e.g. Apache Cassandra, HBase) organise data into flexible column families.
  • Graph databases (e.g. Neo4j) store nodes and relationships directly.
  • Search engines and time-series databases are also frequently grouped under the NoSQL umbrella.

Characteristics

Motivations for this approach include simplicity of design, simpler horizontal scaling to clusters of machines (a problem for relational databases), and finer control over availability. NoSQL systems often relax the ACID guarantees of traditional databases in favour of the CAP theorem trade-offs summarised by the BASE model (Basically Available, Soft state, Eventual consistency). Critics note that the lack of a standard query language and weaker consistency can shift complexity into application code.

History

The term was first used by Carlo Strozzi in 1998 for a lightweight open-source relational database that did not expose the standard SQL interface. It was reintroduced in 2009 by Eric Evans of Rackspace at a meetup discussing open-source distributed databases, and the label stuck to the rapidly growing movement surrounding systems built by Google (Bigtable), Amazon (Dynamo) and Facebook (Cassandra).
