Parliamentary sovereignty

Parliamentary sovereignty, also called parliamentary supremacy or legislative supremacy, is a concept in the constitutional law of some parliamentary democracies. It holds that the legislative body has absolute sovereignty and is supreme over all other government institutions, including executive or judicial bodies. It also holds that the legislature may change or repeal any previous legislation and is not bound by the decisions of its predecessors — and cannot bind its successors.

The orthodox doctrine

The classic statement is that of the Victorian jurist A. V. Dicey, who wrote that Parliament has "the right to make or unmake any law whatever; and, further, that no person or body is recognised by the law of England as having a right to override or set aside the legislation of Parliament." Courts must apply Acts of Parliament and cannot strike them down as unconstitutional — in stark contrast to systems with a codified constitution and judicial review, such as the United States or Germany.

History

The doctrine emerged from the struggles between Crown and Parliament in 17th-century England, culminating in the Glorious Revolution of 1688 and the Bill of Rights 1689, which established that the monarch could not suspend or dispense with laws made by Parliament. Over the 18th and 19th centuries the courts consolidated the rule, refusing to question the validity of enrolled Acts.

Modern qualifications

Even in the United Kingdom, the doctrine operates alongside important qualifications: membership of the European Union (1973–2020) required courts to disapply Acts inconsistent with EU law; the Human Rights Act 1998 empowers courts to declare legislation incompatible with the European Convention on Human Rights (though not to strike it down); devolution created legislatures in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland whose powers rest on Westminster statutes; and in cases such as Jackson v Attorney General (2005) senior judges mused, obiter, that there might be extreme circumstances in which courts could qualify sovereignty. The UK Supreme Court's 2019 prorogation judgment showed judicial policing of the executive's use of prerogative power while affirming Parliament's central place.

Comparative perspective

New Zealand's Parliament is likewise generally sovereign within a unitary, uncodified constitutional order. Many other democracies reject the doctrine in favour of constitutional supremacy: in Canada, Australia, India and Germany, courts may invalidate legislation inconsistent with the constitution. In the Netherlands, courts may not review Acts of Parliament against the constitution, but can test them against treaties — a partial analogue.
