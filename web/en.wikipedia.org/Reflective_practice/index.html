Reflective practice

Reflective practice is the ability to reflect on one's actions so as to take a critical stance or attitude towards one's own practice and that of one's peers, engaging in a process of continuous adaptation and learning. It is a core component of professional development in fields such as teaching, nursing, medicine, social work and management.

Origins

The concept is chiefly associated with Donald Schön's The Reflective Practitioner (1983), which challenged the model of "technical rationality" — the straightforward application of research-based theory — and proposed that professionals also rely on knowing-in-action. Schön distinguished reflection-in-action (thinking on one's feet during an activity, reframing problems as they arise) from reflection-on-action (retrospective analysis after the event). Earlier roots lie in John Dewey's philosophy of experience and reflective thought.

Models and methods

Popular frameworks include Gibbs' reflective cycle (description, feelings, evaluation, analysis, conclusion, action plan), Kolb's experiential learning cycle, and Johns' model of structured reflection used in nursing. Common methods include reflective journals and portfolios, critical incident analysis, clinical or peer supervision, and action learning sets. In many health and education professions, documented reflective practice is a formal requirement of registration and revalidation.

Criticism

Critics note that reflection can become ritualised and formulaic when mandated, that models may oversimplify complex practice, and that genuine critical reflection on power, assumptions and organisational constraints ("critical reflection") is rarer than descriptive reflection on technique.
