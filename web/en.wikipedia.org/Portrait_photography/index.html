Portrait photography

Portrait photography, or portraiture, is a genre of photography aimed at capturing the personality, likeness, and mood of a person or group of people. Like portrait painting before it, it typically concentrates on the subject's face and expression, using composition, pose, clothing, setting, and controlled lighting to convey character. Portraits range from formal studio sittings to candid environmental portraits and the ubiquitous smartphone selfie.

History

The daguerreotype of 1839 made portraiture available far beyond the wealthy few who could commission paintings, and commercial portrait studios spread worldwide. Nineteenth-century practitioners such as Nadar in Paris and Julia Margaret Cameron in Britain elevated the portrait to an art form. In the twentieth century, photographers like Yousuf Karsh, Richard Avedon, and Irving Penn defined the modern editorial portrait, while August Sander and Diane Arbus used portraiture to document and probe society. The arrival of cheap film, then digital cameras and camera phones, democratised portraiture entirely: self-portraits taken at arm's length became a dominant vernacular form.

Technique

Portrait photographers traditionally favour short-telephoto lenses (around 85 mm on full-frame cameras) that flatter facial proportions and, at wide apertures, blur the background to isolate the sitter. Lighting patterns — Rembrandt, butterfly, loop, split, and broad or short lighting — shape the face by the placement of the key light relative to the subject and camera. Studio portraiture now commonly uses strobes with softboxes, while environmental portraiture uses location and natural light to place the subject in a meaningful context.
