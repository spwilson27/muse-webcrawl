PageRank

PageRank (PR) is an algorithm used by Google Search to rank web pages in their search engine results. It is named after both the term "web page" and co-founder Larry Page. PageRank is a way of measuring the importance of website pages by counting the number and quality of links to a page to determine a rough estimate of how important the website is. The underlying assumption is that more important websites are likely to receive more links from other websites.

History

PageRank was developed in 1996 by Larry Page and Sergey Brin as part of a research project at Stanford University about a new kind of search engine. The algorithm formed the core of the prototype search engine Page and Brin built in 1998, which became Google. The system built on earlier ideas of citation analysis in academic literature, treating a hyperlink as an endorsement analogous to a citation.

Algorithm

PageRank is a link analysis algorithm that assigns a numerical weighting to each element of a hyperlinked set of documents. A page's PageRank can be understood through the "random surfer" model: the algorithm models a person who randomly clicks links, with a damping factor (typically set around 0.85) representing the probability that the surfer will continue clicking at each step, and a chance of teleporting to a random page. A page's rank is the probability that the random surfer arrives at it. Links from highly ranked pages transmit more rank than links from obscure pages, and the rank a page passes on is divided among its outgoing links.

Public PageRank and later developments

Google formerly displayed a "Toolbar PageRank" score (0–10) to the public, but discontinued updates in 2013 and removed the display entirely in 2016 amid manipulation by search-engine optimisers. Internal versions of link-based ranking remain part of Google's systems alongside hundreds of other signals, though Google has stated that the original PageRank patent has been superseded by newer methods.

Applications

PageRank-like methods — variations on eigenvector centrality — have been applied to bibliometrics, social network analysis, biology, chemistry, neuroscience, and sports ranking, among other fields.
