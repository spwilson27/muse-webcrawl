Hauberk

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A hauberk or byrnie is a shirt of mail armour. The term is conventionally reserved for a shirt reaching at least to mid-thigh and including sleeves. The shorter mail shirt reaching only to about the waist and typically with short sleeves is called a haubergeon.

History

Mail armour was known to the Celts and Romans; the Roman lorica hamata was a mail shirt worn by legionaries and auxiliaries. After the fall of Rome, mail remained the principal body armour of well-equipped warriors across early medieval Europe. The word hauberk derives from the Old French hauberc, itself from the Frankish halsberg, meaning "neck protection".

By the eleventh century, as depicted on the Bayeux Tapestry, the typical hauberk reached to the knees, was slit front and back to allow riding, and had elbow- or wrist-length sleeves. A flap of mail, the ventail, could cover the lower face. By the fourteenth century, plate armour increasingly supplemented and then replaced mail as the primary defence for knights, though hauberks continued to be worn beneath plate and by less wealthy soldiers for centuries.

Construction

A hauberk was constructed from thousands of interlinked iron or steel rings, each ring passing through four neighbours in the standard "four-in-one" weave. Rings were usually closed by riveting, making the garment highly resistant to cuts. A full-length hauberk might contain 20,000 to 30,000 rings and weigh between 9 and 14 kilograms (20–30 lb), with the weight borne largely on the hips by means of a belt.
