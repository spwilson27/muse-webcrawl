Read-write culture

Read-write culture describes a society in which ordinary people both consume and produce media.

Read-write culture is a term popularized by law professor Lawrence Lessig to describe a participatory culture in which the consumers of media are also its producers. Lessig contrasts it with read-only culture, the twentieth-century model in which professionally produced content — records, films, radio and television broadcasts — flowed one way from a small number of creators to a passive mass audience.

History

Lessig developed the idea in books including Free Culture (2004) and especially Remix: Making Art and Commerce Thrive in the Hybrid Economy (2008). He argued that before the industrial era, culture was largely read-write: ordinary people sang folk songs, retold stories, and built on the work of others as a normal part of cultural life. The technologies of mass production in the twentieth century professionalized creativity and turned audiences into consumers.

The rise of the personal computer and the Internet restored read-write practices at scale. Websites such as blogs, wikis, and video-sharing platforms let amateurs publish text, audio and video; fan fiction, remix music, memes and open-source software are all products of a read-write culture. Wikipedia itself is a prominent example, being written collaboratively by its readers.

Read-only culture

In a read-only culture, creative works are produced by a concentrated professional class and distributed through channels that audiences cannot easily answer. Lessig argues this model was an historical exception produced by the economics of broadcast technology, not a natural state of culture.

Legal issues

Read-write practices frequently collide with copyright law, because remixing, sampling and fan works create derivative works that rights holders may control. Lessig and organizations such as Creative Commons have advocated licensing frameworks and copyright reform that give amateur creators legal room to build on existing culture.
