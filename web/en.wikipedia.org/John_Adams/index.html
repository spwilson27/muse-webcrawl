John Adams

John Adams (19 October 1735 [O.S.] – 4 July 1826) was an American statesman, attorney, diplomat and Founding Father who served as the second president of the United States (1797–1801) and the first vice president (1789–1797) under George Washington.

Early life and the Revolution

Adams was born in Braintree (now Quincy), Massachusetts, graduated from Harvard, and became a successful Boston lawyer. He gained notice defending the British soldiers tried after the Boston Massacre of 1770. A delegate to the Continental Congresses, he was a leading advocate of independence, helped draft the Declaration of Independence with Thomas Jefferson, and served as a diplomat in Europe, negotiating Dutch recognition and loans and the 1783 Treaty of Paris that ended the Revolutionary War.

Vice presidency and presidency

Adams served two terms as Washington's vice president, an office he called "the most insignificant… that ever the invention of man contrived". As president he kept the United States out of full-scale war with revolutionary France during the Quasi-War, against the wishes of his own Federalist Party's Hamiltonian wing, but his administration was marred by the Alien and Sedition Acts of 1798, which were used against opposition newspaper editors. Defeated for re-election by Jefferson in 1800, he made the "midnight appointments" of Federalist judges, including Chief Justice John Marshall, before leaving office.

Retirement and legacy

Adams retired to Peacefield in Quincy, writing extensively and reconciling with Jefferson in a famous correspondence from 1812 until their deaths — on the same day, 4 July 1826, the fiftieth anniversary of the Declaration of Independence. His son John Quincy Adams became the sixth president. Long overshadowed by Washington and Jefferson, Adams's reputation rose in the late 20th century, aided by David McCullough's Pulitzer Prize-winning biography (2001).
