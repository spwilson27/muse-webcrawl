Galactorrhea (also spelled galactorrhoea) is the spontaneous flow of milk from the breast, unassociated with childbirth or nursing. It can occur in females and in males, and results most commonly from raised levels of the hormone prolactin. Galactorrhea is reported in approximately 5–32% of women depending on the definition used, and is distinct from physiological lactation and from other forms of nipple discharge.

Causes

The most frequent pathological cause is a prolactin-secreting pituitary adenoma (prolactinoma). Dopamine normally suppresses prolactin release, so drugs that block dopamine receptors — notably antipsychotics such as risperidone, and antiemetics such as metoclopramide — are a common cause. Primary hypothyroidism (through elevated TRH), chronic kidney failure, chest-wall stimulation or lesions, and certain herbal products can also produce galactorrhea. In newborns, transient galactorrhea ("witch's milk") results from maternal oestrogens crossing the placenta.

Diagnosis

Evaluation includes a medication history, pregnancy testing, and measurement of serum prolactin and thyroid function. Markedly elevated prolactin or clinical features such as headache and visual field defects prompt MRI of the pituitary. Associated amenorrhea or infertility in women, and reduced libido or erectile dysfunction in men, reflect prolactin's suppression of the gonadal axis.

Treatment

Treatment is directed at the cause: withdrawing an offending drug where possible, treating hypothyroidism, or giving dopamine agonists such as cabergoline or bromocriptine, which shrink most prolactinomas and restore normal prolactin levels. Surgery is reserved for tumours resistant to medical therapy or threatening vision.
