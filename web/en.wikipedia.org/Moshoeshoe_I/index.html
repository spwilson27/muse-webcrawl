Moshoeshoe I (c. 1786 – 11 March 1870) was the founder and first king of the Basotho nation, the kingdom that became modern Lesotho. Renowned as a diplomat as much as a warrior, he united disparate Sotho-speaking chiefdoms during the upheavals of the early 19th century (the Lifaqane/Mfecane) and defended his mountain kingdom against Boer and British encroachment.

Rise to power

Born the son of a minor chief of the Koena clan, Moshoeshoe (originally named Lepoqo) gained renown through cattle raids and wise counsel. Around 1820 he became chief, and in 1824 he moved his people to the flat-topped mountain stronghold of Thaba Bosiu ("mountain at night"), which was never successfully taken by enemies. From there he absorbed refugees and smaller groups fleeing the wars associated with Shaka's expansion, building a kingdom through diplomacy, generosity, and the redistribution of cattle under the mafisa (cattle-loan) system.

Relations with Europeans

Moshoeshoe welcomed Paris Evangelical Missionary Society missionaries from 1833, who helped him conduct diplomacy and introduced writing. He signed treaties with the British Cape Colony but came into conflict with the Orange Free State over land, losing territory in wars of 1858 and 1865. Facing conquest, he appealed to Britain, and in 1868 his kingdom was declared the British protectorate of Basutoland — a step that preserved Lesotho's existence as a distinct state.

Death and legacy

Moshoeshoe died in 1870 and was buried on Thaba Bosiu. He is remembered in Lesotho as a national founder; the country's highest honour and its capital's institutions commemorate him.
