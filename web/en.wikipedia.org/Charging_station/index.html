Charging station

A charging station, also known as a charge point or electric vehicle supply equipment (EVSE), is a piece of equipment that supplies electrical power for charging plug-in electric vehicles (including electric cars, electric trucks, electric buses, neighborhood electric vehicles, and plug-in hybrids). Charging stations provide connectors that conform to a variety of standards such as CCS (Combined Charging System), CHAdeMO, GB/T, and the North American Charging Standard (NACS).

Charging levels

Level 1 charging uses a standard household outlet (120 V AC in North America) and adds only a few miles of range per hour. Level 2 charging uses 240 V AC and is common at homes, workplaces, and public sites, typically delivering 10–60 miles of range per hour. DC fast charging (sometimes called Level 3) supplies direct current at high power — modern units deliver 50 to 350 kW — and can bring many vehicles to 80% charge in 20 to 40 minutes, making it suitable for highway corridors.

Locations and networks

Charging stations are found at residences, workplaces, parking facilities, shopping centers, fleet depots, and dedicated highway sites. Large networks, such as Tesla's Supercharger network, ChargePoint, EVgo, and Electrify America in North America and Ionity in Europe, operate roaming and payment systems through apps and RFID cards. Public charging is a key part of government strategies to expand electric vehicle adoption.

Standards and technology

Connector standards have consolidated over time: CCS dominates in Europe, CHAdeMO has declined outside Japan, and from 2023 many automakers adopted Tesla's connector, standardized as SAE J3400 (NACS), in North America. Bidirectional charging and vehicle-to-grid (V2G) technology allow suitably equipped vehicles to return power to buildings or the grid. Battery swapping stations, which exchange a depleted battery for a charged one, are an alternative model deployed notably in China.
