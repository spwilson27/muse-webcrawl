Medical examiner

A medical examiner (ME) is a medical doctor, typically a board-certified forensic pathologist, appointed to investigate deaths occurring under specific circumstances, such as sudden, unexpected, violent or suspicious deaths, and to determine the cause and manner of death. In the United States, medical examiner systems coexist with the older coroner system; jurisdictions use one or the other, or a mixture of both.

Role and duties

Medical examiners take jurisdiction over deaths that are sudden and unexpected, result from injury or poisoning, occur in custody, or involve unidentified bodies. Their offices conduct scene investigations, external examinations and autopsies, order toxicology and histology studies, certify the cause and manner of death (natural, accident, suicide, homicide or undetermined), and testify in court. They may also identify the deceased and notify next of kin.

Medical examiner versus coroner

The principal difference is qualification: a medical examiner is a licensed physician, usually with subspecialty training in forensic pathology, and is appointed; a coroner is frequently an elected official who may have no medical training and must rely on pathologists to perform autopsies. Massachusetts adopted the first modern medical examiner system in 1877, and reform advocates have long recommended replacing coroner systems with medical examiner systems.

Training

In the United States, the pathway is medical school, residency in anatomic (and often clinical) pathology, then a fellowship in forensic pathology, followed by board certification. Chief medical examiners head state or large metropolitan offices supervising teams of forensic pathologists, death investigators, toxicologists and forensic anthropologists.
