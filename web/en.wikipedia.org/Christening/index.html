Christening

A christening ceremony, in which an infant is baptised and formally given a name.

A christening is a Christian ceremony in which an infant is baptised and formally given a Christian name. The word derives from the idea of bringing the child to Christ. In traditions that practise infant baptism — including Roman Catholicism, Eastern Orthodoxy, Lutheranism, Anglicanism and many Methodist and Presbyterian churches — the christening is typically the same event as the baptism itself, performed at a font in a church, often during or after a regular service.

During the rite, the parents and godparents make promises on the child's behalf, renouncing evil and professing the Christian faith. The minister or priest pours or sprinkles water over the child's head (or, in some traditions, immerses the child) while pronouncing the baptismal formula, "I baptise you in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit." The child may be anointed with oil or chrism and presented with a lighted candle. Traditionally, the baby wears a white christening gown, sometimes a family heirloom, symbolising purity.

History

Infant baptism developed in the early centuries of Christianity and was widely practised by the third century. The term "christening" became common in Middle English usage and originally referred to the whole baptismal rite, including the naming. Churches that practise believer's baptism, such as Baptists, do not christen infants but may hold a separate ceremony of dedication or thanksgiving for a child.

Social customs

Christenings are often followed by a family gathering or party. Godparents, chosen by the parents, traditionally give gifts such as silver spoons, christening mugs, or bibles. In maritime usage, "christening" also refers to the ceremonial naming and launching of a ship, in which a bottle of champagne is broken against the hull.
