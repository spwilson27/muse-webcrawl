Transmembrane protein

Transmembrane protein
ClassIntegral membrane protein
LocationBiological membrane
ExamplesIon channels, G protein-coupled receptors, transporters

A transmembrane protein is a type of integral membrane protein that spans the entirety of the cell membrane. Many transmembrane proteins function as gateways to permit the transport of specific substances across the membrane. They frequently undergo significant conformational changes to move a substance through the membrane, and are usually highly hydrophobic, aggregating and precipitating in water unless solubilised by detergents or other methods.

Types

Transmembrane proteins are classified by their topology and secondary structure. Alpha-helical proteins, common in the inner membranes of bacteria and the plasma membrane of eukaryotes, cross the membrane as one or more alpha helices whose hydrophobic side chains interact with the lipid bilayer. Beta-barrel proteins, found mainly in the outer membranes of Gram-negative bacteria, mitochondria, and chloroplasts, cross the membrane as a rolled-up beta sheet forming a pore.

Functions

Transmembrane proteins serve as receptors that relay signals across the membrane, as channels and transporters that move ions and molecules, and as enzymes with active sites on either side of the membrane. They are targets of a large fraction of pharmaceutical drugs, notably the G protein-coupled receptors.

Stability and folding

The thermodynamic stability of transmembrane proteins arises from hydrophobic interactions with the lipid tails, hydrogen bonding within helices and sheets, and, in some cases, oligomerisation. Unlike water-soluble proteins, they typically cannot be refolded once denatured.
