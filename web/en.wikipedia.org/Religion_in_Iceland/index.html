Religion in Iceland

Religion in Iceland has been predominantly Christian since the adoption of Christianity as the state religion by the Althing in 999/1000 CE. The Evangelical Lutheran Church of Iceland is the national church, and the constitution guarantees freedom of religion. In recent decades Iceland has become increasingly secular, with church membership declining and regular attendance low by European standards.

History

The first settlers of Iceland (from c. 870 CE) were a mixture of Norse pagans and Christians from the British Isles. The settlement-era faith combined worship of the Norse gods with, increasingly, Christian belief. In 999 or 1000, under pressure from King Olaf Tryggvason of Norway, the Althing accepted the lawspeaker Thorgeir Thorkelsson's arbitration that Icelanders should adopt Christianity, initially with private pagan practice tolerated. The Reformation, imposed by the Danish crown in 1541–1550, made Iceland Lutheran; the last Catholic bishop, Jón Arason, was executed in 1550.

Demographics

Roughly three-fifths of Icelanders are registered members of the Evangelical Lutheran Church of Iceland (Þjóðkirkjan), down from over 90% in the late 20th century. Other registered faiths include the Roman Catholic Church (the largest non-Lutheran body, bolstered by immigration), free Lutheran churches, the Ásatrúarfélagið (the Norse pagan fellowship, recognised since 1973 and among the fastest-growing faith communities), Orthodox churches, Muslim and Buddhist associations, and Zuism (Sumerian neopaganism), which briefly attracted thousands of registrants in 2015 as a protest against the parish tax. Citizens pay a church tax (sóknargjald) directed to their registered faith organisation or, if unaffiliated, to the state.
