Sacramento–San Joaquin River Delta

The Sacramento–San Joaquin River Delta, or California Delta, is a vast inverted river delta and estuary in Northern California formed by the confluence of the Sacramento and San Joaquin rivers just west of Stockton, flowing westward through the Carquinez Strait into San Francisco Bay. Covering about 1,100 square miles (2,800 km²), it is the hub of California's water supply system and one of the most altered estuaries in North America.

Geography and formation

The Delta formed over roughly the last 10,000 years as sea level rose after the last glacial period, slowing the rivers and depositing sediment and peat behind the Carquinez Strait. Before reclamation it was a tidal freshwater marsh and floodplain of tule wetlands, inhabited for millennia by Miwok, Yokuts and other Indigenous peoples. Beginning in the 1850s, largely with Chinese and later Japanese and Filipino labour, farmers enclosed the marsh with levees, creating sunken "islands" of rich peat soil, many now lying 3–8 metres below sea level due to oxidation and subsidence of the peat.

Water supply

The Delta is the switching yard of California water: the federal Central Valley Project and the State Water Project pump water from its southern edge into aqueducts serving San Joaquin Valley farms and some 27 million Californians in Southern California and the Bay Area. Managing the competing demands of water exports, flood protection, and a collapsing ecosystem — including the endangered delta smelt — has made the Delta one of the state's most contested policy arenas, and proposals for conveyance tunnels under or around it have been debated for decades.

Ecology

The estuary historically supported immense runs of Chinook salmon and steelhead and vast waterfowl migrations on the Pacific Flyway. Habitat loss, altered flows, invasive species such as the overbite clam, and water exports have driven steep declines in native fishes, prompting extensive restoration of tidal marsh in recent years.
