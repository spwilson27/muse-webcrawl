Phreatomagmatic eruption

A phreatomagmatic eruption is a volcanic eruption driven by the interaction of magma with water. When hot magma contacts groundwater, seawater, or surface water, the water flashes to steam and expands explosively, fragmenting the magma into fine ash. Such eruptions are among the most violent short-lived types of volcanic activity.

Mechanism

Unlike purely magmatic eruptions powered by dissolved volcanic gases, phreatomagmatic eruptions derive much of their energy from the thermal energy of magma converted to mechanical energy through fuel–coolant interaction. The resulting ash is characteristically fine-grained and blocky, and the eruptions generate fast-moving, wet pyroclastic density currents called base surges.

Landforms and examples

Phreatomagmatic eruptions commonly excavate broad, shallow craters called maars, often ringed by low tuff rings. Examples include the 1963 eruption that created the island of Surtsey off Iceland, the formation of Diamond Head in Hawaii, the maars of the Eifel region in Germany, and phases of the 2022 Hunga Tonga–Hunga Haʻapai eruption, in which seawater interacting with magma contributed to an exceptionally powerful explosion.
