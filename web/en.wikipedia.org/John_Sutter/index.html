John Sutter

Born: Johann August Sutter, 28 February 1803, Kandern, Baden (Germany)
Died: 18 June 1880 (aged 77), Washington, D.C., US
Known for: Founder of Sutter's Fort and New Helvetia; the California Gold Rush began on his land (1848)

John Augustus Sutter (born Johann August Sutter; February 28, 1803 – June 18, 1880) was a Swiss immigrant who became a Mexican and later American citizen, known for establishing Sutter's Fort in the area that would become Sacramento, California. Although most famous for the discovery of gold at his sawmill in 1848 that launched the California Gold Rush, Sutter saw his vast agricultural and commercial empire collapse as prospectors overran his lands.

Early life and arrival in California

Born in Kandern, Baden, to Swiss parents, Sutter left Europe in 1834 to escape debts, abandoning his wife and children, and travelled across America before sailing to Mexican California in 1839. Winning the favour of Governor Juan Bautista Alvarado, he obtained a land grant of some 48,000 acres at the confluence of the Sacramento and American rivers, became a Mexican citizen, and built Sutter's Fort, the centre of his colony of New Helvetia ("New Switzerland"). His fort became a destination for American emigrant parties, including the relief of the ill-fated Donner Party.

Gold discovery and ruin

In January 1848 Sutter's employee James W. Marshall found gold at Sutter's Mill in Coloma, where the two were building a sawmill. Despite Sutter's attempts to keep the discovery secret, news spread and triggered the Gold Rush of 1849. Squatters overran his lands, stole his cattle and crops, and his workforce deserted for the goldfields. The United States courts largely rejected his Mexican land titles, and he spent his remaining decades petitioning Congress for compensation. Congress granted him a modest pension, but he died in Washington, D.C., in 1880 while still pressing his claims.

Legacy

Sutter's Fort is preserved as a California state historic park, and his complex legacy — as pioneer, coloniser, and a man whose enterprises rested heavily on coerced Native American labour — has been the subject of continuing historical reassessment.
