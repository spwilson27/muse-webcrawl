Alençon lace

Alençon lace (French: dentelle d'Alençon) is the finest of the needle laces, produced in the town of Alençon in Normandy, France. It is known for its delicate floral designs worked on a fine net ground and is sometimes called the "Queen of lace."

TypeNeedle lace
OriginAlençon, Normandy, France
Known asPoint d'Alençon; "Queen of lace"

History

Lace manufacture in Alençon is documented from the 16th century. Under Jean-Baptiste Colbert, finance minister to Louis XIV, a royal manufactory was established in 1665 to produce lace in the Venetian style, reducing France's dependence on imported luxury goods. French workers soon developed their own distinctive technique.

Production flourished through the 18th century but declined during the French Revolution and with the rise of machine-made lace in the 19th century. The craft was preserved by the Sisters of Providence and later by dedicated workshops.

Technique

Alençon lace is worked with a needle on a parchment pattern. The design is outlined with a cordonnet — a thread covered in close buttonhole stitches — and the motifs are filled with a variety of decorative filling stitches over a net ground (réseau). Pieces are made in sections by different workers, each specializing in one step, then joined invisibly.

Recognition

In 2010, the craftsmanship of Alençon needle lace-making was inscribed on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
