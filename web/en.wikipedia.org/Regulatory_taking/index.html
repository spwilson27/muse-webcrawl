Regulatory taking

In United States constitutional law, a regulatory taking occurs when governmental regulations limit the use of private property to such a degree that the landowner is effectively deprived of all economically reasonable use of the property, even though the property is not physically appropriated. Under the Takings Clause of the Fifth Amendment, applied to the states through the Fourteenth Amendment, such a taking requires the payment of just compensation.

Doctrine

The modern doctrine traces to Justice Oliver Wendell Holmes's opinion in Pennsylvania Coal Co. v. Mahon (1922), which held that while property may be regulated to a certain extent, "if regulation goes too far it will be recognized as a taking." The Supreme Court's framework was substantially developed in Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City (1978), which set out a balancing test considering the economic impact of the regulation, the extent to which it interferes with distinct investment-backed expectations, and the character of the governmental action.

Categorical takings

Two situations are treated as takings without case-specific inquiry. In Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp. (1982), a permanent physical occupation of property was held to be a taking. In Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council (1992), a regulation depriving land of all economically beneficial use was held to be a categorical taking unless the restriction inhered in background principles of state property or nuisance law. Temporary moratoria were addressed in Tahoe-Sierra Preservation Council v. Tahoe Regional Planning Agency (2002), which rejected a categorical rule for temporary development bans.

Exactions

In Nollan v. California Coastal Commission (1987) and Dolan v. City of Tigard (1994), the Court required an "essential nexus" and "rough proportionality" between conditions imposed on development permits and the impact of the proposed development.
