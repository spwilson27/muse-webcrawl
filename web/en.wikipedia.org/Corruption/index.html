Corruption

Corruption — the abuse of entrusted power for private gain — occurs in government, business and other institutions worldwide.

Corruption is a form of dishonesty or criminal activity undertaken by a person or organisation entrusted with a position of authority, in order to acquire illicit benefits or abuse power for personal gain. The anti-corruption organisation Transparency International defines it as "the abuse of entrusted power for private gain". Corruption may involve activities such as bribery, embezzlement, fraud, extortion, nepotism and cronyism, and can facilitate further criminal enterprise including drug trafficking, money laundering and human trafficking.

Forms

  • Bribery — offering or accepting something of value to influence the actions of an official.
  • Embezzlement — theft of funds or assets by someone entrusted with them.
  • Nepotism and cronyism — favouring relatives or friends in appointments and contracts regardless of merit.
  • State capture — systematic influence by private interests over the formation of laws and rules themselves.
  • Petty corruption — everyday abuse by low- and mid-level officials in dealings with ordinary citizens, contrasted with grand corruption at senior levels.

Causes and effects

Corruption flourishes where discretion is wide, accountability weak, and institutions opaque. Its effects include distorted public spending, reduced investment and economic growth, poorer public services, environmental harm, and erosion of trust in institutions and the rule of law. Because corrupt transactions are secret, measuring corruption is difficult; researchers rely on perception surveys, experience-based reporting and governance indicators.

Measurement

The best-known comparative measure is Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI), published since 1995, which ranks countries by perceived levels of public-sector corruption as assessed by experts and businesspeople. Other instruments include the World Bank's Worldwide Governance Indicators and regional barometer surveys.

Countermeasures

International efforts include the United Nations Convention against Corruption (UNCAC, adopted 2003), the OECD Anti-Bribery Convention, asset-declaration regimes, open procurement, protection for whistleblowers, independent anti-corruption agencies, and investigative journalism. Reforms typically combine transparency, enforcement and institutional design such as meritocratic civil services and judicial independence.
