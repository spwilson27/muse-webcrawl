Seed bank


Seed storage at a gene bank

A seed bank (also seed vault) stores seeds to preserve genetic diversity; hence it is a type of gene bank. There are many reasons to store seeds: to preserve the genetic diversity of plant species in the face of climate change, habitat destruction, and disease, and to provide a backup for agriculture should varieties in current use be lost.

Storage conditions

Seeds are typically cleaned, dried to a moisture content of about 5%, and stored in sealed containers at low temperatures, often around −18 °C (0 °F) for long-term "base" collections. Under these conditions, many orthodox seeds can remain viable for decades or centuries, though viability is periodically tested and samples are regenerated by growing them out.

Major seed banks

The best-known seed bank is the Svalbard Global Seed Vault in Norway, which holds backup copies of seed collections from around the world in a tunnel carved into permafrost. Other major facilities include the Millennium Seed Bank at Wakehurst, United Kingdom, run by the Royal Botanic Gardens, Kew; the Vavilov Institute's collection in Russia; and the United States National Center for Genetic Resources Preservation in Fort Collins, Colorado.

Challenges

Not all seeds tolerate drying and freezing; species with recalcitrant seeds, including many tropical trees, require other conservation methods such as tissue culture or living collections. Seed banks also face risks from war, funding shortfalls, and equipment failure — the ICARDA gene bank in Aleppo, Syria, was evacuated during the Syrian civil war, and its duplicate holdings at Svalbard were withdrawn in 2015 to re-establish the collection in Lebanon and Morocco.
