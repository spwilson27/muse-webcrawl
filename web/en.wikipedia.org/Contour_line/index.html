Contour line

A contour line (also isoline, isopleth or isarithm) of a function of two variables is a curve along which the function has a constant value, so that the curve joins points of equal value. In cartography, a contour line joins points of equal elevation (height) above a given level, such as mean sea level. A map with such lines is a topographic map. The vertical distance between adjacent contour lines is the contour interval.

Topographic contours

Contour lines on a topographic map reveal the shape of the terrain: closely spaced lines indicate steep slopes, widely spaced lines gentle slopes. Heavier "index contours" are drawn at regular intervals and labelled with their elevation. Closed contours with tick marks (hachures) pointing downhill indicate a depression rather than a summit. Contours never cross except at an overhanging cliff, and V-shaped bends point upstream in valleys.

Other isolines

The same idea is used for many quantities: isobars join points of equal atmospheric pressure, isotherms points of equal temperature, isobaths points of equal water depth, and equipotential lines points of equal potential in physics. In mathematics, contour lines are the level sets of a function, and the gradient of the function is everywhere perpendicular to them.

History

Lines of equal value appeared on charts as early as the 17th century — Edmond Halley published a chart of magnetic variation with isogonic lines in 1701 — and contour lines of elevation were in use on land maps by the end of the 18th century, becoming standard on national topographic surveys in the 19th.
