November 2015 Paris attacks

The November 2015 Paris attacks were a series of coordinated Islamist terrorist attacks that took place on Friday, 13 November 2015, in Paris, France, and the city's northern suburb of Saint-Denis. Beginning at 21:16 CET, three suicide bombers struck outside the Stade de France during an international football match. This was followed by several mass shootings at cafés and restaurants and a mass shooting and hostage-taking at the Bataclan theatre, where an American rock band was performing. In total, 130 people were killed and more than 400 were injured. The attacks were the deadliest in France since the Second World War.

The attacks

Attackers wearing suicide vests detonated outside the Stade de France in Saint-Denis during a friendly match between France and Germany attended by President François Hollande, who was evacuated. Elsewhere in Paris, gunmen opened fire with automatic weapons on patrons at restaurants and cafés in the 10th and 11th arrondissements, including Le Petit Cambodge, Le Carillon, La Casa Nostra, Café Bonne Bière and La Belle Équipe. At the Bataclan theatre, three attackers shot concertgoers during a performance by the band Eagles of Death Metal and took hostages; police assaulted the theatre shortly after midnight, killing the attackers. Ninety people died at the Bataclan alone.

Response and aftermath

President Hollande declared a state of emergency across France — the first nationwide emergency since 1961 — and closed the borders. The Islamic State claimed responsibility, describing the attacks as retaliation for French airstrikes in Syria and Iraq. A manhunt led to a major police raid in Saint-Denis on 18 November in which the suspected ringleader, Abdelhamid Abaaoud, was killed. One fugitive, Salah Abdeslam, was captured in Brussels in March 2016; he was later convicted in trials in Belgium and France. The sole surviving member of the attacking cell, Abdeslam received a whole-life sentence in France in June 2022.

International reaction

World leaders condemned the attacks, and landmarks worldwide were lit in the colours of the French tricolour. The attacks profoundly shaped European debates on security, borders and the Schengen Area, and France intensified its military campaign against the Islamic State.
