Sect

A sect is a subgroup of a religious, political, or philosophical belief system, usually emerging as an offshoot of a larger organization. Historically, the term referred to any group that followed a distinctive teaching or leader; in modern usage it often carries a pejorative connotation, suggesting a group perceived as heretical, extreme, or excessively controlling, though sociologists use it as a neutral analytical category.

Etymology

The word derives from the Latin secta, meaning "a way, mode of life," or "a school of thought," from the verb sequi, "to follow." In classical usage it was not necessarily negative: philosophical and medical schools were called sects. Through Christian usage, where secta translated the Greek hairesis (heresy), the term acquired connotations of error and schism.

Sociological usage

In the sociology of religion, the sect forms one pole of the church–sect typology developed by Max Weber and Ernst Troeltsch. A "church" is a large, established, inclusive religious body integrated with society; a "sect" is a smaller, voluntary, exclusive group that separates from a church in protest against perceived compromise or worldliness, demanding high commitment from members. Over generations, sects that survive often institutionalize and become denominations, in the typology developed by H. Richard Niebuhr.

Sociologists such as Bryan Wilson classified sects by their orientation to the world: conversionist, reformist, introversionist, manipulationist, thaumaturgical, revolutionist and utopian types.

Pejorative usage

In everyday language, "sect" is often applied to new religious movements regarded with suspicion, overlapping with the still more pejorative term "cult." Governments in some countries, including France and Russia, have maintained official lists or laws concerning "sects," which has generated controversy over religious freedom. Scholars caution that popular use of the word reflects social disapproval more than any objective property of the group itself.
