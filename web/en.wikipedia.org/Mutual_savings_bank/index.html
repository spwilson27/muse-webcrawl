A mutual savings bank is a financial institution chartered by a state or federal government, with the purpose of maintaining savings of individuals. Unlike stock banks, a mutual savings bank has no stockholders; it is owned by its depositors, and profits beyond reserves are returned to depositors as interest or reinvested. Such banks were historically concentrated in the northeastern United States.

History

The mutual savings bank movement began in Scotland and England in the early nineteenth century as a philanthropic effort to encourage thrift among working people. The first incorporated American savings banks, the Philadelphia Savings Fund Society and the Provident Institution for Savings in Boston, were both established in 1816. By the early twentieth century there were more than 600 mutual savings banks in the United States.

Operations

Mutual savings banks traditionally invested conservatively, first in government securities and mortgages — they were important lenders for home construction — and their deposits are insured by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation. During the late twentieth century, deregulation allowed many mutuals to expand their services and, in some cases, convert to stock ownership ("demutualization"). Fewer than 500 mutual savings banks remain in the United States, most of them small, community-oriented institutions.
