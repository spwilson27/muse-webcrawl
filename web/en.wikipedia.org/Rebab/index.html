The rebab is a type of bowed spike fiddle that spread with Islam from the Middle East across North Africa and into Southeast Asia, where it became a central instrument of Indonesian gamelan. Its name derives from the Arabic rabāb, a family of bowed and plucked lutes ancestral to many spike fiddles; the plucked Afghan rubab is a distinct, lute-type cousin rather than the same instrument.

The Javanese rebab

In Javanese and Sundanese gamelan the rebab is a two-stringed fiddle with a heart- or spade-shaped wooden body covered with a thin membrane, a long neck often richly carved, and a spike at the base on which it rests upright. Held vertically and bowed with a loose-haired bow, it has a soft, breathy tone that seldom sounds alone: doubled by the singer's line, it elaborates and leads the melody (lagu), signalling changes of piece and tempo to the ensemble. Its parts are among the most demanding in the gamelan repertoire, and the rebab player is traditionally regarded as the ensemble's musical leader.

Other forms

The Malay rebab of Kelantan and Patani has three strings and a larger, more ornate body, accompanying the Mak Yong dance-theatre and healing rituals. Simpler rebab types accompany epic singing and folk entertainment from Morocco to Malaysia. Through Islamic trade routes the bowed rabāb family also fed into the medieval European rebec, an ancestor in spirit of later Western fiddles.
