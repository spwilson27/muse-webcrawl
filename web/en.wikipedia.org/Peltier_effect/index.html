Peltier effect


A thermoelectric (Peltier) module

The Peltier effect is the reversible heating or cooling that occurs at the junction of two different conductors or semiconductors when an electric current passes through it. Discovered in 1834 by the French physicist Jean Charles Athanase Peltier, it is one of the three thermoelectric effects, alongside the Seebeck effect (1821) and the Thomson effect (1851). When current flows one way across a junction, heat is absorbed and the junction cools; reverse the current, and heat is released.

Physics

Charge carriers in different materials sit at different average energies; moving across a junction forces them to gain or release energy, exchanging heat with the surroundings. The rate of Peltier heating or cooling is proportional to the current and to the difference in the materials' Peltier coefficients. Lord Kelvin showed that the Peltier and Seebeck coefficients are linked: the Peltier coefficient equals the Seebeck coefficient multiplied by the absolute temperature. In metals the effect is small; it is much larger between p-type and n-type semiconductors such as bismuth telluride.

Applications

A Peltier device (thermoelectric cooler, TEC) chains many such junctions in series between ceramic plates, pumping heat from one face to the other with no moving parts. TECs are used for portable coolers and camping fridges, precise temperature stabilisation of laser diodes and electronics, CPU spot cooling, dehumidifiers, and scientific instruments. Their efficiency is poor compared with vapour-compression refrigeration, so they are favoured where compactness, silence, reliability, or fine control matter more than efficiency.
