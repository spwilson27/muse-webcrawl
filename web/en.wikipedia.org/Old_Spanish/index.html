Old Spanish

Old Spanish
Region: Iberian Peninsula
Era: 10th–15th centuries
Family: Indo-European → Romance
Writing system: Latin

Old Spanish (also called Old Castilian, Spanish: español antiguo) is the stage of the Spanish language spoken and written roughly from the 10th century until the end of the 15th century, before the consonantal reorganisation that gave rise to modern Spanish. It developed from Vulgar Latin in the north-central Iberian Peninsula and gradually spread southwards during the Reconquista.

History

The earliest texts generally considered Old Spanish include the Glosas Emilianenses, marginal glosses from the monastery of San Millán de la Cogolla, and the epic poem Cantar de mio Cid (c. 1200), the earliest preserved work of Spanish literature. King Alfonso X of Castile (r. 1252–1284) promoted Castilian as a language of learning, law and historiography, producing works such as the Siete Partidas.

Phonology

Old Spanish had a richer sibilant system than the modern language, with seven sibilant phonemes including voiced and voiceless pairs. Initial Latin F- generally became h- (Latin farina → harina, "flour"), and the medieval palatal sounds written x and j/g were pronounced [ʃ] and [ʒ] respectively before shifting to the modern velar [x] in the 16th–17th centuries.

Grammar

Old Spanish retained features later lost, including the future subjunctive, a wider use of the verb aver (modern haber) as a possessive "to have," and clitic pronouns that could be interpolated inside verb forms. Word order was freer than in modern Spanish, and the second-person forms vos and tú were used with different levels of deference than today.
