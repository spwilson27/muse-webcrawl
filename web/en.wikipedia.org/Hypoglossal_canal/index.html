Hypoglossal canal

Part of: Occipital bone
System: Skeletal
Transmits: Hypoglossal nerve (CN XII)

The hypoglossal canal is a foramen in the occipital bone of the skull. It is hidden medially and superiorly to each occipital condyle and transmits the hypoglossal nerve, the twelfth cranial nerve, which controls most movements of the tongue.

Structure

The canal is situated in the epiphyseal junction between the basioccipital and jugular process of the occipital bone, near the foramen magnum. It begins on the intracranial surface of the skull and runs laterally and forward, emerging above the occipital condyle. An emissary vein from the transverse sinus, and occasionally a branch of the ascending pharyngeal artery, may also pass through it.

Clinical significance

Fractures of the skull base, tumours, or expansion of the canal can injure the hypoglossal nerve, causing ipsilateral tongue weakness and deviation of the protruded tongue toward the side of the lesion. In paleoanthropology, enlargement of the canal was once proposed, controversially, as evidence for speech capacity in Neanderthals, a claim later studies questioned because canal size varies widely in modern humans and primates.

Variation

In a minority of skulls the canal is divided by a bony spicule, reflecting the nerve's development from multiple rootlets.
