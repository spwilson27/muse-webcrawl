Jure uxoris

Jure uxoris (Latin for "by right of his wife") is a Latin phrase and legal doctrine used to describe a title of nobility or office held by a man because his wife holds the office or title suo jure ("in her own right"). It most commonly applies to a man who became king through marriage to a reigning queen.

Usage

When a woman inherited a crown, fief or peerage in her own right, her husband was frequently recognised as sharing her dignity, ruling jointly or exercising authority in her name. Such a husband is described as king jure uxoris, distinguishing his derivative right from that of a king regnant by inheritance. Examples include Philip I of Castile, husband of Joanna of Castile, and several kings of Jerusalem whose queens were the hereditary sovereigns, such as Guy of Lusignan through Sibylla.

Related concepts

The counterpart phrase jure matris ("by right of his mother") describes a title claimed through one's mother. In British peerage law, husbands of peeresses in their own right historically acquired no automatic title, though some were summoned to Parliament in their wives' baronies during the medieval period. The doctrine contrasts with a prince consort, who holds rank by marriage but does not share sovereignty.
