Thyrotropin-releasing hormone

Thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH), also called thyroliberin or protirelin, is a releasing hormone produced by neurons in the paraventricular nucleus of the hypothalamus. It is a tripeptide (pyroglutamyl-histidyl-prolinamide) that stimulates the anterior pituitary gland to release thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH, thyrotropin) and prolactin. TRH thus stands at the top of the hypothalamic–pituitary–thyroid axis, which regulates thyroid hormone production and metabolic rate.

Synthesis and mechanism

TRH is synthesised as part of a larger precursor protein that is cleaved into multiple copies of the tripeptide. Released into the hypophyseal portal circulation, TRH binds to G-protein-coupled TRH receptors on pituitary thyrotroph cells, triggering phospholipase C signalling and TSH secretion within minutes. TRH release is stimulated by cold exposure and inhibited, through negative feedback, by circulating thyroid hormones (T3 and T4) acting at both pituitary and hypothalamic levels. TRH also occurs widely elsewhere in the brain, spinal cord, and gut, where it may act as a neurotransmitter or neuromodulator affecting arousal, thermoregulation, pain, and autonomic function.

History and clinical use

TRH was the first hypothalamic releasing hormone to be characterised: in 1969 the groups of Roger Guillemin and Andrew Schally independently isolated it from hundreds of thousands of sheep and pig hypothalami and determined its structure, work that contributed to their share of the 1977 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine. Its synthetic form, protirelin, was formerly used in the TRH stimulation test to assess pituitary and thyroid function, though sensitive TSH assays have largely replaced the test. Rare hypothalamic (tertiary) hypothyroidism can result from TRH deficiency or resistance. TRH analogues have been investigated for depression, spinal cord injury, and neurodegenerative disease, with taltirelin approved in Japan for spinocerebellar degeneration.
