Marquetry

Marquetry (also spelled marqueterie) is the art and craft of applying pieces of veneer to furniture or other surfaces to form decorative patterns, designs or pictures. The veneers are usually of wood, but may also include ivory, bone, horn, mother-of-pearl, tortoiseshell, brass and other materials. Unlike intarsia, in which pieces are inlaid into a solid ground, marquetry applies a complete veneer skin over the surface, all pieces at the same level.

Technique

The craftsman cuts each piece to shape — historically with a fret saw, chevalet de marqueterie or knife — assembles the design face down, often using the packet-cutting (Boulle) or window method so that adjacent pieces fit exactly, glues the sheet to the carcass, and finishes the surface. Shading is achieved with naturally coloured woods, dyed veneers, or by scorching pieces in hot sand. Modern practice may use lasers or CNC routers for cutting.

History

Veneer inlay was practised in ancient Egypt and Rome, but marquetry as a fine decorative art flowered in 16th-century Italy (where the related tradition of tarsia produced pictorial panels) and reached its height in 17th- and 18th-century France and the Netherlands. André-Charles Boulle, cabinetmaker to Louis XIV, gave his name to Boulle work — marquetry combining tortoiseshell with brass or pewter. Floral and pictorial marquetry adorned the great French commodes and English and German furniture of the period, and the craft survived in revival and studio furniture to the present day.
