The Michelson interferometer is a precision optical instrument that produces interference fringes by splitting a beam of light into two paths, reflecting each path back on itself, and recombining the beams so that they interfere with one another. Invented by the American physicist Albert A. Michelson in the 1880s, it is the most common configuration of optical interferometer and remains a standard tool in physics laboratories.

Because the interference pattern is exquisitely sensitive to differences in the optical path lengths of the two arms — a change of a fraction of a wavelength shifts the fringes measurably — the instrument can measure distances, refractive indices, and spectral line structure with extraordinary precision.

Configuration

A Michelson interferometer consists of a light source (historically a sodium lamp or other spectral source, today usually a laser), a partially silvered beam splitter inclined at 45 degrees, two plane mirrors at the ends of perpendicular arms, and a viewing screen or detector. Half of the incident light is transmitted to one mirror and half reflected to the other. After reflection the two beams return to the beam splitter, where portions of each are combined and directed to the observer.

If the two optical paths differ, the recombined beams produce a pattern of alternating bright and dark fringes. Moving one mirror by half a wavelength changes the round-trip path by one full wavelength and shifts the pattern by exactly one fringe, which is the basis of the instrument's use as a length standard: counting fringes while a mirror moves a known distance calibrates length in terms of wavelength.

History

Michelson developed the interferometer while working in Berlin in 1881 and refined it at the Case School of Applied Science in Cleveland. Its most famous early application was the Michelson–Morley experiment of 1887, performed with Edward Morley, which searched for the motion of the Earth through the hypothetical luminiferous aether. The null result — no fringe shift as the apparatus rotated — became one of the key pieces of evidence against the aether theory and helped pave the way for Einstein's special theory of relativity.

Michelson also used the instrument to measure the standard metre in terms of the wavelength of the cadmium red line, and, with a large stellar version, to measure the angular diameters of stars such as Betelgeuse. He received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1907, in part for his optical precision instruments.

Applications

  • Metrology: interferometric length measurement and calibration of gauge blocks.
  • Fourier-transform spectroscopy: a scanning Michelson interferometer (with one mirror moving steadily) records an interferogram whose Fourier transform yields the spectrum of the source; FTIR spectrometers are built on this principle.
  • Optical coherence tomography: low-coherence versions image subsurface structure in tissue and materials.
  • Gravitational-wave astronomy: the LIGO and Virgo detectors are giant Michelson interferometers with kilometre-scale arms (augmented by Fabry–Pérot cavities) that measure the tiny strain of passing gravitational waves.
  • Astronomy: Michelson stellar interferometers measure star diameters and binary separations.
