Hydrogen sulfide is a chemical compound with the formula H2S. It is a colourless chalcogen-hydride gas, denser than air, and notorious for its foul odour of rotten eggs even at very low concentrations. It is poisonous, corrosive, and flammable, and is biologically significant both as a toxin and, at low levels, as a gasotransmitter signalling molecule produced in the body.

Occurrence and production

Hydrogen sulfide occurs naturally in volcanic gases, hot springs, crude oil, and natural gas, and is generated by the bacterial breakdown of organic matter in the absence of oxygen — as in swamps and sewers. Industrially, the largest source is the hydrodesulfurisation of petroleum and the processing of "sour" natural gas; the recovered sulfur is obtained by the Claus process, in which part of the H2S is burned to sulfur dioxide and reacted with the remainder to give elemental sulfur.

Toxicity

Hydrogen sulfide is a broad-spectrum poison that inhibits cytochrome c oxidase, interfering with cellular respiration in a manner similar to hydrogen cyanide. Its danger is compounded by olfactory fatigue: at concentrations around 100 ppm the sense of smell is deadened, removing the warning provided by its odour. Exposure to a few hundred parts per million can cause rapid unconsciousness and death; consequently, sewers, manure pits, and oil and gas facilities enforce strict monitoring and safety procedures.

Biological role and other uses

Endogenous H2S, produced by enzymes such as cystathionine γ-lyase, functions alongside nitric oxide and carbon monoxide as a gasotransmitter involved in vasodilation and neuromodulation. In chemistry it is used in qualitative inorganic analysis, in the preparation of metal sulfides, and in the Girdler sulfide process for producing heavy water.
