Datec. 1566/1568 – 1648
LocationLow Countries; European waters; the Americas and Asia

The Eighty Years' War (Dutch: Tachtigjarige Oorlog), also known as the Dutch Revolt, was the revolt of the Seventeen Provinces of the Habsburg Netherlands against the Spanish monarchy of Philip II and his successors. The war, conventionally dated from 1568 (or the troubles of 1566) to 1648, ended with the Peace of Münster, by which Spain recognised the independence of the Dutch Republic — the seven northern provinces that had declared their independence in the Act of Abjuration of 1581.

Origins

Resentment grew under Spanish rule over heavy taxation, centralisation, the Inquisition and the persecution of Protestantism. The iconoclastic fury of 1566 prompted Philip II to send the Duke of Alba with an army; Alba's Council of Troubles and taxes such as the "Tenth Penny" radicalised the provinces. The Sea Beggars' capture of Brielle in 1572 reignited open revolt under William of Orange, "the Silent".

Course of the war

Spanish forces under Alba, Don John of Austria and Alexander Farnese reconquered the southern provinces, while the north held out. The Union of Utrecht (1579) united the northern provinces; in 1581 they abjured Philip's sovereignty. England's Elizabeth I sent troops under the Earl of Leicester, and Spain's attempt to invade England with the Armada of 1588 failed. Under Maurice of Nassau and Johan van Oldenbarnevelt the Republic professionalised its army and expanded, while the Dutch East India Company attacked Spain's overseas empire. A Twelve Years' Truce (1609–1621) amounted to de facto recognition of independence before hostilities resumed as part of the wider Thirty Years' War.

Outcome

The Peace of Münster, signed on 30 January 1648 as part of the Peace of Westphalia, formally ended the war. The Dutch Republic emerged as a leading commercial and naval power, while the southern Netherlands (roughly modern Belgium) remained Spanish — a division that shaped the modern map of the Low Countries.
