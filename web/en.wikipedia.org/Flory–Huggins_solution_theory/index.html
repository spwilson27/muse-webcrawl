Flory–Huggins solution theory

Flory–Huggins solution theory is a lattice model of the thermodynamics of polymer solutions developed by Paul Flory and Maurice Huggins in the early 1940s. It adapts the regular solution model to long-chain molecules by accounting for the great difference in molecular size between polymer and solvent, and introduces the Flory–Huggins interaction parameter, χ, which measures the energetic cost of mixing.

The lattice model

The model imagines the solution as a lattice in which each site is occupied either by a solvent molecule or by one segment of a polymer chain. A polymer of degree of polymerization N occupies N connected sites. Counting the ways of arranging the chains yields the combinatorial entropy of mixing, which is much smaller per unit volume than for small-molecule mixtures — a key insight explaining why polymers are often poorly soluble.

Free energy of mixing

The theory gives the free energy of mixing per lattice site as ΔG/(kT) = φ₁ ln φ₁ + (φ₂/N) ln φ₂ + χ φ₁ φ₂, where φ₁ and φ₂ are the volume fractions of solvent and polymer. The parameter χ combines entropic and enthalpic contributions and depends on temperature, typically decreasing as temperature rises; when χ exceeds a critical value (about 1/2 for very long chains), the solution phase-separates into polymer-rich and polymer-poor phases.

Applications and limits

Flory–Huggins theory underlies the analysis of polymer solubility, swelling of networks, osmotic pressure, and phase diagrams of blends and block copolymers. Its mean-field assumptions neglect correlations and specific interactions such as hydrogen bonding, so quantitative refinements (χ depending on concentration and molecular weight) are common in practice.
