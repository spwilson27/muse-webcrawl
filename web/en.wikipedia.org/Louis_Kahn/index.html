Louis Kahn

Louis Isadore Kahn (born Itze-Leib Schmuilowsky; 20 February 1901 – 17 March 1974) was an Estonian-born American architect based in Philadelphia. One of the most influential architects of the twentieth century, he is known for monumental, heavy buildings in brick and concrete whose designs draw on ancient ruins and treat light as a central material.

Life and career

Kahn's family emigrated from Estonia to Philadelphia in 1906. He studied at the University of Pennsylvania under Paul Philippe Cret, graduating in 1924. His career developed slowly: early decades were spent on housing and planning work, and he did not receive his first major commission, the Yale University Art Gallery (1951–1953), until he was fifty. His design for the Richards Medical Research Laboratories (1957–1965) at the University of Pennsylvania, with its distinction between "served" and "servant" spaces, brought him international recognition.

Major works

The Salk Institute (1959–1965) in La Jolla, California, pairs laboratory wings around a travertine plaza bisected by a narrow channel of water, often considered one of the great architectural spaces of the century. The Kimbell Art Museum (1966–1972) in Fort Worth, Texas, uses cycloid vaults with slits of natural light. His largest project, the National Assembly Building (Jatiya Sangsad Bhaban) in Dhaka, Bangladesh, begun in 1962 and completed after his death in 1982, rises from an artificial lake as a citadel of geometric forms in concrete and marble.

Legacy

Kahn died of a heart attack in a men's room at New York's Pennsylvania Station in 1974, heavily in debt despite his fame; identification was delayed because he had crossed out the address on his passport. His son Nathaniel Kahn's documentary My Architect (2003) explored his life and work. Kahn's aphorisms — "Architecture is the thoughtful making of spaces" and his personified dialogue between brick and architect — remain widely quoted in architectural education.
