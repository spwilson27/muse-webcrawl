Sanctification

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Sanctification literally means "to set apart for special use or purpose", that is, to make holy. In Christian theology, sanctification is the process by which a believer is made holy, growing in righteousness and becoming more conformed to the image of Christ. The concept is closely related to the doctrines of justification and salvation, and different Christian traditions understand the relationship between these doctrines in different ways.

Terminology

The English word sanctification derives from the Latin sanctificare, "to make holy", itself from sanctus ("holy") and facere ("to make"). It translates the Greek hagiasmos (ἁγιασμός), used in the New Testament for the process of being made holy. Related terms include consecration, holiness, and in some traditions deification or theosis.

Views by tradition

In Roman Catholic theology, sanctification is generally understood as an ongoing process of interior renewal, in which the justified person grows in grace and charity through cooperation with God's grace, the sacraments, and good works. The Eastern Orthodox Church teaches theosis, the process by which a person becomes partaker of the divine nature through the uncreated energies of God. In most Protestant traditions descending from the Reformation, sanctification is distinguished from justification: justification is a once-for-all declaration of righteousness through faith, while sanctification is the subsequent lifelong process of growth in holiness worked by the Holy Spirit. The Wesleyan tradition additionally teaches the possibility of "entire sanctification" or Christian perfection, a second work of grace in which the believer is cleansed from original sin and filled with perfect love.

Judaism and other religions

In Judaism, sanctification (kiddush) refers to the blessing and setting apart of times, objects, and persons for sacred purposes, such as the sanctification of the Sabbath and festivals. The broader concept of holiness (kedushah) is central to Jewish religious life. Similar concepts of ritual consecration appear in many other religions, including Hinduism and Buddhism, where objects, places, and persons may be set apart for sacred use.
