Total quality management

TQM emphasises continuous improvement and customer satisfaction across the whole organisation.

Total quality management (TQM) is a management approach to long-term success through customer satisfaction. In a TQM effort, all members of an organisation participate in improving processes, products, services and the culture in which they work. TQM uses strategy, data, and effective communications to integrate the quality discipline into the culture and activities of the organisation.

Principles

Commonly cited primary elements of TQM include: a customer-focused organisation; total employee involvement; a process-centred approach; an integrated system; a strategic and systematic approach; continual improvement; fact-based decision making; and communications. Many of these concepts are present in modern quality management systems, the successor to TQM.

History

TQM grew out of the statistical quality control work of Walter Shewhart and was developed by consultants such as W. Edwards Deming, Joseph Juran and Armand Feigenbaum, who coined the phrase "total quality control." Deming's ideas were adopted enthusiastically in post-war Japan, where quality circles and kaizen flourished, before returning to North America and Europe in the 1980s as Western manufacturers sought to match Japanese quality. The Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Award (1987) and the ISO 9000 standards institutionalised many TQM practices; later approaches such as Six Sigma and Lean built upon them.

Criticism

Critics have argued that TQM programmes sometimes became bureaucratic fads, emphasising slogans and documentation over results, and that evidence for their effect on financial performance is mixed. Nevertheless, its core ideas—process thinking, measurement, and involving frontline workers in improvement—remain standard management practice.
