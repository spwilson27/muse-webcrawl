Strategic management

[Image placeholder: strategic management process diagram]

Strategic management is the formulation, implementation, and evaluation of major goals and initiatives taken by an organization's managers on behalf of its stakeholders, based on consideration of resources and an assessment of the internal and external environments in which the organization operates. It provides overall direction to the enterprise and involves specifying objectives, developing policies and plans to achieve them, and allocating resources to implement those plans.

Overview

The discipline emerged from business policy courses in American business schools in the 1950s and 1960s and developed through the work of scholars such as Alfred Chandler, Igor Ansoff, Kenneth Andrews, and Michael Porter. Strategic management is commonly divided into three phases: strategy formulation (analysis and choice), strategy implementation, and strategy evaluation and control.

Analytical frameworks

  • SWOT analysis evaluates internal strengths and weaknesses against external opportunities and threats.
  • Porter's five forces analyzes industry structure: rivalry among competitors, threat of new entrants, threat of substitutes, and the bargaining power of buyers and suppliers.
  • Generic strategies: Porter proposed cost leadership, differentiation, and focus as basic routes to competitive advantage.
  • Resource-based view holds that sustained advantage arises from valuable, rare, inimitable, and organized internal resources and capabilities.
  • Balanced scorecard translates strategy into financial, customer, internal-process, and learning objectives and measures.
  • PESTEL analysis scans political, economic, social, technological, environmental, and legal macro-environmental factors.

Schools of thought

Henry Mintzberg and colleagues distinguished prescriptive schools—design, planning, and positioning—that treat strategy as a deliberate process, from descriptive schools emphasizing strategy as emergent, learned, or shaped by power, culture, and environment. The tension between deliberate planning and emergent strategy remains a central debate, particularly in rapidly changing industries where critics argue formal planning can produce rigidity.

Levels of strategy

Corporate strategy concerns the overall scope of a diversified firm—what businesses to be in—while business strategy concerns how to compete within a particular market, and functional strategies concern marketing, operations, finance, and other departments. Related topics include mergers and acquisitions, strategic alliances, international strategy, and corporate governance.
