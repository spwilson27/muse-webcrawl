Water conflict

Water conflict is a term describing a conflict between countries, states, or groups over the right to access water resources. The United Nations recognises that water disputes result from opposing interests of water users, public or private, and that such disputes occur “when the demands of water users exceed the available supply, or when water is contaminated or access is restricted”. Conflicts range from legal and political disputes to violence and armed struggle.

Causes

Water conflicts arise from scarcity — driven by population growth, irrigation demand, drought and climate change — and from competing claims over shared rivers, lakes and aquifers. Water can be a trigger of conflict, a weapon (for example, flooding or cutting supplies in war), or a casualty of conflict, as when infrastructure is bombed or contaminated. The Pacific Institute maintains a chronology of water-related conflicts dating back some 5,000 years, beginning with disputes between the Mesopotamian city-states of Lagash and Umma.

Major disputes

The Nile basin has been a persistent source of tension: colonial-era treaties allocated most of the river’s flow to Egypt and Sudan, arrangements contested by upstream states, and Ethiopia’s Grand Ethiopian Renaissance Dam has produced a long-running diplomatic standoff with Egypt. The 1960 Indus Waters Treaty between India and Pakistan is often cited as a model of cooperation that has survived several wars, though it has come under strain. Other prominent disputes involve the Tigris–Euphrates basin (Turkey, Syria, Iraq), the Jordan River (Israel, Jordan, Syria, Lebanon and the Palestinian territories), Central Asia’s Amu Darya and Syr Darya, and the Mekong, where Chinese and Laotian dams affect downstream countries.

Cooperation and law

Despite “water wars” rhetoric, research by scholars such as Aaron Wolf shows that shared water has historically produced far more treaties than wars; the last unambiguous war fought solely over water is generally dated to ancient Mesopotamia. The 1997 UN Watercourses Convention provides a framework of equitable and reasonable use. Climate change is expected to intensify competition and make basin agreements more important and more difficult.
