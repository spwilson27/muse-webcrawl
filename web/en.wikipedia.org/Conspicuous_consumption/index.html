Conspicuous consumption

Luxury goods are classic instruments of conspicuous consumption.

Conspicuous consumption is the spending of money on and the acquiring of luxury goods and services to publicly display economic power — either the buyer's income or the buyer's accumulated wealth. The term was introduced by the economist and sociologist Thorstein Veblen in his book The Theory of the Leisure Class (1899), where he described it as a characteristic of the newly wealthy class emerging from the Second Industrial Revolution.

Theory

Veblen argued that consumption becomes a means of reputability: to gain and hold the esteem of others, wealth must be put in evidence, through lavish expenditure on superfluities, conspicuous leisure, and conspicuous waste. Sociologically, such display functions as a status signal; economically, goods purchased mainly for display are sometimes called Veblen goods, whose demand rises with price.

Modern usage

The concept has been applied to luxury branding, celebrity culture and social media display. Counter-movements include "inconspicuous consumption" — spending on education, health and childcare by affluent groups — described by Elizabeth Currid-Halkett, and minimalism, which rejects ostentation.
