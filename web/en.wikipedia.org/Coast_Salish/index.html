Coast Salish

The Coast Salish are a group of Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest Coast who share a family of related languages and many cultural traits. Their traditional territories center on the Salish Sea—the Strait of Georgia, Puget Sound, and adjacent waters—in what is now southwestern British Columbia and northwestern Washington State.

Peoples and languages

Coast Salish peoples include the Squamish, Musqueam, Tsleil-Waututh, Sto:lo, Cowichan, Snuneymuxw, Swinomish, Nisqually, Puyallup, and many others. Their languages belong to the Central (Coast) branch of the Salishan language family and include Halkomelem, Squamish, Lushootseed, and Straits Salish. Although related, the languages are distinct, and many are now endangered and the subject of revitalization efforts.

Culture

Coast Salish society was organized around extended-family households living in large cedar longhouses in winter villages. Salmon fishing was the economic and spiritual foundation of life, celebrated in first salmon ceremonies; other resources included shellfish, game, camas, and berries. Wealth and status were displayed and redistributed at potlatches. Coast Salish art—carving, weaving of wool blankets (historically including woolly dog hair), and basketry—has seen a major revival since the mid-twentieth century.

History

European contact in the late eighteenth century brought trade and devastating epidemics. Treaties in Washington Territory in the 1850s and reserve policies in British Columbia confined peoples to small land bases, and assimilation policies, including residential and boarding schools, suppressed languages and ceremonies. In recent decades Coast Salish nations have asserted treaty and Aboriginal rights, co-managed fisheries, and led language and cultural renewal.
