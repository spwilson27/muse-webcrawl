Grapefruit

Scientific name: Citrus × paradisi

Family: Rutaceae

Origin: Barbados, 18th century (hybrid of pomelo and sweet orange)

The grapefruit (Citrus × paradisi) is a subtropical citrus tree known for its relatively large, sour to semi-sweet, somewhat bitter fruit. It is an 18th-century hybrid that originated in Barbados as an accidental cross between the sweet orange (C. sinensis) and the pomelo (C. maxima), both of which had been introduced to the island from Asia.

Description

The grapefruit tree is evergreen, usually growing to about 5–6 metres (16–20 ft) tall, though it can reach 13–15 m (43–49 ft). The leaves are glossy dark green and the white flowers have four or five petals. The fruit is yellow-orange skinned and largely oblate, ranging in diameter from 10 to 15 cm (3.9 to 5.9 in). The flesh is segmented and acidic, varying in colour depending on the cultivar — pink, red and white varieties are common. The flavour is acidic, sour to sweet-tart, with a distinctive bitterness contributed by compounds such as naringin.

History

The hybrid was first documented in 1750 by the Reverend Griffith Hughes, who described specimens in Barbados in his Natural History of Barbados. It was once called the "forbidden fruit". The name "grapefruit" is commonly said to refer to the way the fruits sometimes grow in clusters resembling grapes. Grapefruit was brought to Florida in the early 19th century, and commercial cultivation expanded in Florida and Texas. The ruby red grapefruit arose as a bud mutation discovered in Texas in 1929 and patented in 1930, becoming one of the most popular varieties.

Production and uses

Grapefruit is eaten fresh — commonly halved and eaten with a spoon — and is also juiced or used in salads, desserts and marmalades. China and the United States are among the largest producers, with Florida, Texas and California the principal US growing states. The fruit is a good source of vitamin C and dietary fibre and contains modest amounts of vitamin A, potassium and lycopene (in pink and red varieties).

Drug interactions

Grapefruit and grapefruit juice are known to interact with numerous prescription medications. Furanocoumarins in the fruit inhibit the intestinal enzyme CYP3A4 and the transporter P-glycoprotein, which can increase the blood levels — and therefore the effects and side effects — of drugs such as some statins, calcium channel blockers, immunosuppressants and sedatives. Patients are commonly advised to check whether their medication is affected.
