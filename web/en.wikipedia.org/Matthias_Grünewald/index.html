Matthias Grünewald (c. 1470–1475 – 31 August 1528) was a German Renaissance painter of religious works who ignored Renaissance classicism to continue the style of late medieval Central European art into the 16th century. His first name is also given as Mathis and his surname as Gothart or Neithart; his full name is often given as Mathis Gothart Nithart. Only ten paintings — several consisting of multiple panels — and thirty-five drawings survive, all religious. He was not well known until the early 20th century, when he came to be regarded as one of the greatest German artists, comparable to Albrecht Dürer.

His masterpiece, the Isenheim Altarpiece, painted for the hospital chapel of the Monastery of St. Anthony in Isenheim, is famous for its harrowing depiction of the Crucifixion, in which the diseased body of Christ was intended to console hospital patients suffering from ergotism and other skin diseases.

Life

The details of Grünewald's life are unusually obscure for an artist of his importance. He was probably born in Würzburg around 1470–1475. From about 1500 he seems to have lived in Seligenstadt and Frankfurt, and in 1510 he entered the service of Uriel von Gemmingen, Archbishop of Mainz, working on the cathedral there. He was also court painter to Albrecht of Brandenburg, Archbishop of Mainz, for whom he painted several works, including the Stuppach Madonna. Grünewald was a Lutheran sympathiser and was obliged to leave Mainz after the German Peasants' War of 1525. He moved to Frankfurt and then to Halle, where he worked as a hydraulic engineer before his death in 1528.

Work

Grünewald's surviving works include the Lindenhardt Altarpiece (c. 1503), the Heller Altarpiece (copies survive), the altarpiece for Isenheim Monastery (1512–1516, now in the Unterlinden Museum, Colmar), the Meeting of St. Erasmus and St. Maurice (c. 1520–1524, Alte Pinakothek, Munich), and the Tauberbischofsheim altarpiece (1523–1525). His intensely expressive colour and distorted forms made him a major influence on German Expressionist artists in the early 20th century, and Paul Hindemith based his opera and symphony Mathis der Maler ("Mathis the Painter", 1934–1938) on Grünewald's life.
