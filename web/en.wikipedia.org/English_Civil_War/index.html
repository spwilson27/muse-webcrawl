The English Civil War (1642–1651) was a series of civil wars and political machinations between Royalists and Parliamentarians in the Kingdom of England over, principally, the manner of England's governance and issues of religious freedom. The wars — part of the wider Wars of the Three Kingdoms — pitted the supporters of Charles I against the supporters of the Long Parliament, and ended in Parliament's victory, the trial and execution of the king, and the establishment of the Commonwealth of England under Oliver Cromwell.

Causes

Conflict grew from Charles I's belief in the divine right of kings, his eleven years of Personal Rule without Parliament (1629–1640), unpopular taxes such as ship money, and Archbishop Laud's religious reforms, which alienated Puritans and the Scots. The Bishops' Wars with Scotland forced Charles to recall Parliament in 1640; the Long Parliament's demands and the Irish Rebellion of 1641 deepened the crisis until Charles raised his standard at Nottingham in August 1642.

Course of the wars

The First Civil War (1642–1646) saw indecisive battles at Edgehill and Marston Moor before Parliament's remodelled New Model Army, under Thomas Fairfax and Oliver Cromwell, destroyed the king's main army at Naseby (1645) and Langport. Charles surrendered to the Scots in 1646, but his secret intrigues provoked a Second Civil War in 1648, crushed at Preston. Pride's Purge removed moderate MPs, and the remaining Rump Parliament tried Charles for treason; he was executed on 30 January 1649. Cromwell then subdued Ireland (1649–1653) and defeated Charles II's Scottish-backed invasion at Dunbar (1650) and Worcester (1651), ending the wars.

Consequences

England became a republic — the Commonwealth — and from 1653 Cromwell ruled as Lord Protector. The war cost a higher proportion of the population than any other conflict in British history (an estimated 4% in England, and far more in Ireland). The monarchy was restored in 1660, but the precedent that a king could be tried and executed by his subjects, and that Parliament could not be dispensed with, shaped British constitutional development permanently.
