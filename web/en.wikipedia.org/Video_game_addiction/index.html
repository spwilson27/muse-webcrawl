Video game addiction

Video game addiction is the compulsive or uncontrolled use of video games in a way that causes problems in other areas of a person's life. It is a form of behavioural addiction characterized by excessive or compulsive use of video games that interferes with a person's everyday life. Video game addiction can present itself as compulsive gaming, social isolation, mood swings, diminished imagination, and hyper-focus on in-game achievements to the exclusion of other life events.

Classification

In 2018, the World Health Organization (WHO) included gaming disorder in the 11th revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11), defining it as a pattern of gaming behaviour characterized by impaired control over gaming, increasing priority given to gaming over other activities, and continuation or escalation of gaming despite negative consequences. The American Psychiatric Association's DSM-5, published in 2013, does not classify it as a formal disorder but lists Internet gaming disorder in Section III as a condition warranting more clinical research.

Symptoms and diagnosis

Proposed symptoms include preoccupation with gaming, withdrawal symptoms such as sadness or anxiety when unable to play, tolerance (needing to spend increasing amounts of time gaming), unsuccessful attempts to control participation, loss of interest in previous hobbies, continued excessive use despite psychosocial problems, deception of family members or therapists about time spent, use of games to escape a negative mood, and jeopardizing significant relationships, jobs, or educational opportunities.

History

Concerns about compulsive video game playing date back to the early 1980s with arcade games such as Space Invaders. The rise of massively multiplayer online role-playing games (MMORPGs) in the late 1990s and 2000s, social network games, and mobile games with microtransactions broadened the debate. Several governments, including those of China and South Korea, have enacted measures such as play-time limits for minors.

Treatment

Treatments studied include cognitive behavioural therapy, motivational interviewing, and self-help and peer-support groups modelled on twelve-step programs. Some dedicated treatment centres operate in countries such as South Korea, China, and the Netherlands.

Controversy

The classification remains debated: some researchers argue the evidence base is weak, definitions vary, and moral panic may inflate prevalence estimates, while others argue recognition is essential for research funding and treatment access.
