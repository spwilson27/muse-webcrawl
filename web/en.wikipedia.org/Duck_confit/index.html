Duck confit

CourseMain course
Place of originFrance (Gascony)
Main ingredientsDuck legs, duck fat, salt
Similar dishesConfit de canard, cassoulet (as a component)

Duck confit (French: confit de canard) is a classic dish of French cuisine, particularly associated with the region of Gascony in south-western France. It is made by curing duck legs in salt and then slowly poaching them in their own rendered fat — the technique known as confit.

Preparation

The traditional method begins by rubbing duck legs (thighs with drumsticks attached, cuisses) with coarse salt, often with garlic, thyme, bay and black pepper, and leaving them to cure for 12 to 36 hours. The salt is then rinsed off and the legs are submerged in melted duck fat and cooked very gently — typically at around 90 °C (195 °F) — for two to three hours, until the meat is extremely tender. Before serving, the legs are usually crisped in a hot oven or frying pan so that the skin becomes golden and crackling while the flesh remains succulent.

Preservation

Confit originated as a preservation method. Duck legs poached in fat and stored completely covered by that fat in earthenware pots or jars could be kept for months in a cool cellar, because the fat seals the meat away from air and inhibits spoilage. The technique predates refrigeration and was applied to goose and pork as well as duck. Canned duck confit is now produced commercially, especially in the Dordogne and the Landes.

Serving

Duck confit is often served with potatoes cooked in duck fat (pommes sarladaises), frequently with garlic and parsley, along with a sharply dressed green salad to cut the richness. It is also a traditional component of cassoulet, the slow-cooked bean stew of Castelnaudary, Toulouse and Carcassonne, and may be shredded into rillettes or used in salads and lentil dishes.
