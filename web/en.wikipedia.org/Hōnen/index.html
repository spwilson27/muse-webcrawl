Hōnen (法然, 1133–1212) was a Japanese Buddhist monk and the founder of Jōdo-shū, the first independent school of Japanese Pure Land Buddhism. He taught that in the present degenerate age (mappō), birth in Amida Buddha's Pure Land — and thus liberation — could be attained solely by reciting the nembutsu, "Namu Amida Butsu," relying entirely on Amida's Original Vow.

Life

Born in Mimasaka Province, Hōnen was ordained as a Tendai monk on Mount Hiei after his father's murder. Dissatisfied with conventional practice, he studied the Pure Land writings of the Chinese master Shandao and in 1175 experienced a conversion to the exclusive nembutsu. He settled in Kyoto, where his preaching attracted followers from all classes, including courtiers, warriors, and outcasts.

Teaching and persecution

In his main work, the Senchakushū (1198), Hōnen argued that Amida had selected the nembutsu as the practice suited to the age of mappō, and that other practices should be set aside. The established schools on Mount Hiei petitioned against him; in 1207 his movement was suppressed, several disciples were executed, and Hōnen was exiled to Shikoku, though he was pardoned and returned to Kyoto in 1211.

Legacy

Hōnen's disciples, including Shinran, founder of Jōdo Shinshū, spread Pure Land Buddhism throughout Japan. His emphasis on a simple practice open to everyone, regardless of learning or moral status, transformed Japanese Buddhism.
