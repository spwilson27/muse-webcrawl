The Yeniseian languages (also spelled Yeniseic or Jeniseian) are a family of languages spoken in central Siberia along the Yenisei River and its tributaries. The family once comprised at least six languages — Ket, Yugh, Kott, Assan, Arin, and Pumpokol — but today only Ket survives as a living language, spoken by a few hundred members of the Ket people in the Krasnoyarsk Krai of Russia.

Classification

Yeniseian is generally divided into a northern branch (Ket and the closely related, now extinct Yugh) and a southern branch (Kott, Assan, Arin, and Pumpokol), the southern languages having died out by the nineteenth century. For most of the twentieth century Yeniseian was treated as a language isolate family with no demonstrated relatives. In 2008, linguist Edward Vajda presented evidence for a genealogical link between Yeniseian and the Na-Dené languages of North America, proposing the Dené–Yeniseian family — the first widely discussed Old World–New World language connection supported by systematic morphological comparison. The hypothesis has gained cautious acceptance among many specialists, though it remains debated.

Characteristics

Ket is notable among Siberian languages for its complex verb morphology, in which the verb template can incorporate subject and object markers, tense, mood, and even incorporated nouns and adverbs. Yeniseian languages also possess a system of lexical tone or pitch accent, unusual for the region. Noun classification by animacy and gender-like categories plays a role in agreement.

History

Proto-Yeniseian is thought to have been spoken around two thousand years ago, possibly in the region between the Altai Mountains and Lake Baikal. Yeniseian-speaking groups once ranged over a much larger territory, as far south as the northern fringes of Mongolia, before being displaced or assimilated by Turkic, Samoyedic, and later Russian expansion. Place names of Yeniseian origin — especially river names ending in elements such as -ses or -tet — survive across a broad swath of Siberia, attesting to this wider former distribution.
