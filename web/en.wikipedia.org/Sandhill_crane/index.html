The sandhill crane (Antigone canadensis) is a species of large crane of North America and extreme northeastern Siberia. The common name of this bird refers to habitat like that at the Platte River, on the edge of Nebraska's Sandhills on the American Great Plains. Sandhill cranes are mainly herbivorous, but they will feed on various types of food depending on availability. They often dig into soil for seeds and tubers and occasionally eat small animals.

Description

Adults are grey overall, with a red forehead, white cheeks, and a long, dark, pointed bill; during the breeding season their plumage becomes ochre-coloured, presumably from deliberate application of iron-rich mud. They stand about 100–120 cm tall with a wingspan of roughly 1.9–2.1 m, and weigh 3–6.5 kg. Their loud, trumpeting calls, produced in an elongated trachea, can be heard over long distances, and migrating flocks are a celebrated sight on the Central Flyway, notably along the Platte River in Nebraska.

Distribution and subspecies

Six subspecies are generally recognised, divided into the migratory "lesser" and "greater" sandhill cranes of the north and the non-migratory Florida, Mississippi, and Cuban populations. The Mississippi and Cuban subspecies are endangered, while the species as a whole is abundant and classified as of least concern. Cranes are among the oldest living bird lineages, with fossil relatives dating back millions of years.

Behaviour

Sandhill cranes mate for life and perform elaborate courtship "dances" involving bowing, leaping, and wing-flapping. They nest in wetlands, laying usually two eggs, and the cinnamon-coloured chicks (called colts) can leave the nest within a day of hatching.
