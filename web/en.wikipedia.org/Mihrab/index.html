Mihrab

A mihrab (Arabic: محراب, miḥrāb) is a niche in the wall of a mosque that indicates the qibla, the direction of the Kaaba in Mecca towards which Muslims should face when praying. The imam leads prayers standing in front of or within the mihrab, which is usually placed midway along the qibla wall and is often richly decorated with tilework, carved stucco or marble.

[Image: The ornate tiled mihrab of a historic mosque]

History

The earliest mosques oriented prayer by means of a simple mark or a flat slab; the concave niche mihrab first appeared under the Umayyads, being introduced into the Prophet's Mosque in Medina during the rebuilding under al-Walid I (707–709 CE) and famously into the Great Mosque of Córdoba. Over time the mihrab became the decorative focus of the mosque interior, with masterpieces such as the carved mihrab of the Madrasa Imami in Isfahan and the luster-tile mihrabs of Kashan.

Design

Mihrabs are typically semicircular or polygonal apses, sometimes framed by inscriptions of Qur'anic verses — frequently the Light Verse (24:35), which compares God to a lamp in a niche. The niche also has an acoustic function, helping to project the imam's voice to the congregation.
