Henry Dreyfuss (March 2, 1904 – October 5, 1972) was an American industrial designer and one of the pioneers of the profession in the United States. He is celebrated for designs that combined clean styling with rigorous attention to the human body, most famously the Bell Telephone Model 302 (1937) and Model 500 (1950), long the standard American telephones.

Career

Apprenticed as a theatrical set designer, Dreyfuss opened his own office in 1929. His breakthrough came with commissions for Bell Telephone Laboratories, Honeywell (the round T87 thermostat, 1953), Polaroid (the SX-70 camera, with Edwin Land), and the New York Central Railroad, whose Mercury locomotive he streamlined. His firm, Henry Dreyfuss Associates, became one of the most influential design consultancies of the twentieth century.

Human factors

Dreyfuss founded his practice on anthropometrics — the measurement of the human body. His studio developed the reference figures "Joe" and "Josephine," standardised male and female body measurements used in design, and his book Designing for People (1955) and the Humanscale charts remain landmarks of ergonomics. His motto held that when a product creates friction with its user, the designer has failed.
