Gender pay gap

The gender pay gap is the average difference between the remuneration for men and women who are working. Women are generally found to be paid less than men. There are two distinct numbers: the unadjusted (or raw) pay gap, which compares median earnings of all men and women, and the adjusted pay gap, which takes into account differences in hours worked, occupations chosen, education and job experience.

Extent

Across the OECD the unadjusted gap in median full-time earnings is about 11–12 per cent, ranging from under 5 per cent in countries such as Belgium and Norway to over 30 per cent in Korea. In the United States, women's median weekly earnings are roughly 82–84 per cent of men's; in the European Union the average gap is around 12–13 per cent. The gap has narrowed substantially since the 1960s–70s but convergence has slowed.

Causes

Research attributes the gap to several overlapping factors:

  • Occupational segregation — women are concentrated in lower-paid sectors such as care, education and retail, and under-represented in high-paid STEM and finance roles.
  • The motherhood penalty — earnings drop sharply for women after the birth of a first child, while men's are largely unaffected ("child penalties" explain a growing share of the remaining gap).
  • Working time — women are more likely to work part-time; very long hours in some professions command disproportionate pay premiums.
  • Negotiation and promotion — differences in bargaining and progression to senior roles.
  • Discrimination — the adjusted residual, often estimated at a few per cent, may reflect direct pay discrimination, though its size is debated.

Policy responses

Measures include equal-pay legislation (such as the US Equal Pay Act of 1963 and EU directives), mandatory pay-gap reporting for large employers (introduced in the UK in 2017), pay transparency laws, parental-leave schemes designed to be shared between parents, and subsidised childcare.
