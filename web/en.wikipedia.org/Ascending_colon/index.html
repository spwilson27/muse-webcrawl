Ascending colon

PrecursorMidgut

The ascending colon is the first main part of the large intestine, passing upward from the caecum on the right side of the abdomen. It continues the path of digested material after the terminal ileum empties into the caecum through the ileocaecal valve.

Structure

The ascending colon is about 15–20 cm long and runs upward from the caecum in the right iliac fossa to the underside of the liver, where it bends sharply leftward at the right colic (hepatic) flexure to become the transverse colon. Unlike much of the small intestine it is retroperitoneal, fixed against the posterior abdominal wall, and lacks a mesentery in most people. Its blood supply comes from the ileocolic and right colic branches of the superior mesenteric artery.

Function

As chyme passes through the ascending colon, water and electrolytes — particularly sodium and potassium — are absorbed, and the remaining material is gradually formed into faeces. Bacteria in the colon also ferment some undigested carbohydrates.

Clinical significance

The ascending colon may be affected by colorectal cancer, inflammatory bowel disease such as Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, and by caecal volvulus. Because tumours here can grow large before causing obstruction, cancers of the right colon often present with iron-deficiency anaemia rather than bowel symptoms.
