Discount rate

The term discount rate has several related meanings in economics and finance. In discounted cash flow analysis it is the interest rate used to convert future cash flows into their present value, reflecting the time value of money and risk. In central banking it is the rate at which a central bank lends to commercial banks, such as the rate on loans from the Federal Reserve's discount window.

Use in valuation

Present value is computed as PV = FV / (1 + r)t, where r is the discount rate and t the number of periods. Choosing the rate — often the weighted average cost of capital (WACC) for a firm, or a risk-adjusted required return — strongly affects valuations: a higher discount rate lowers the present value of distant cash flows. In social cost–benefit analysis, governments choose a social discount rate for projects such as climate mitigation, a choice with major ethical and economic consequences.

Central bank discount rate

Commercial banks may borrow reserves directly from the central bank at the discount rate, typically set above the policy target rate to discourage routine use and to serve as a backstop in liquidity crises. Changes in the rate are one signal of monetary policy stance, although modern frameworks rely mainly on open market operations and interest on reserves.
