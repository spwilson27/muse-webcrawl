Battle of Horseshoe Bend

The Battle of Horseshoe Bend (also known as the Battle of Tohopeka) was fought on 27 March 1814 at a bend of the Tallapoosa River in present-day Alabama. United States forces and Indian allies under Major General Andrew Jackson defeated the Red Stick Creeks, ending the Creek War. The battle was the decisive engagement of the war and opened vast tracts of Creek land to American settlement.

Background

The Creek War began as a civil war within the Creek Nation, with the traditionalist Red Sticks resisting American expansion and cultural assimilation. After the Red Stick attack on Fort Mims in August 1813, American forces under Jackson campaigned into Creek territory. By March 1814 some 1,000 Red Stick warriors, led by Menawa, had fortified a position at Tohopeka, a bend in the Tallapoosa River, behind a stout log breastwork.

The battle

Jackson arrived with about 2,000 infantry, 700 cavalry and friendly Creek and Cherokee allies. His artillery bombarded the breastwork ineffectively for hours, but Cherokee warriors under Junaluska swam the river, seized the Red Sticks' canoes, and attacked the village from the rear. Jackson then ordered a frontal bayonet assault. Sam Houston was among the first over the breastwork. The fighting was savage and the Red Sticks refused quarter; of the roughly 1,000 defenders, some 800 were killed, compared with fewer than 50 American dead. Menawa, though wounded seven times, escaped.

Aftermath

The victory destroyed Red Stick resistance. In August 1814 the Creeks signed the Treaty of Fort Jackson, ceding 23 million acres—more than half the Creek ancestral territory, including land belonging to Jackson's own Creek allies—to the United States. The triumph propelled Jackson's rise to national fame and, eventually, the presidency.
