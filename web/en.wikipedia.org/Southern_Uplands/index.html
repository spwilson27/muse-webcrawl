Southern Uplands

The Moffat Hills, part of the Southern Uplands

The Southern Uplands are the southernmost and least populous of mainland Scotland's three major geographic areas (the others being the Central Lowlands and the Highlands). They lie south of the Southern Uplands Fault line that extends from Ballantrae on the Firth of Clyde to Dunbar in East Lothian, and form a range of rolling hills roughly 220 km long running from west to east.

Geography

The Southern Uplands consist largely of Silurian sedimentary rocks folded during the Caledonian orogeny. The highest point is the Merrick (843 m) in the Galloway Hills. Other significant summits include Broad Law, White Coomb, Hart Fell and the Cheviot, which straddles the border with England. The region is sparsely populated, with sheep farming and forestry the dominant land uses, and contains the towns of Dumfries, Galashiels, Hawick, Peebles and Moffat among its larger settlements.

Geology

The Southern Uplands terrane is an accretionary prism formed as the Iapetus Ocean closed during the Silurian period. Thick sequences of greywacke and mudstone were scraped off the descending ocean floor and stacked against the Laurentian margin. The Southern Uplands Fault marks the boundary with the Midland Valley to the north.

The Southern Upland Way

The Southern Upland Way is a 344 km (214 mile) coast-to-coast long-distance footpath running from Portpatrick in the west to Cockburnspath in the east, the first officially designated coast-to-coast route in the United Kingdom.
