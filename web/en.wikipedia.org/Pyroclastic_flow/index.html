Pyroclastic flow

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A pyroclastic flow (also known as a pyroclastic density current or a pyroclastic cloud) is a fast-moving current of hot gas and volcanic matter (collectively known as tephra) that flows along the ground away from a volcano at average speeds of 100 km/h (30 m/s) but is capable of reaching speeds of up to 700 km/h (190 m/s). The gases and tephra can reach temperatures of about 1,000 °C (1,800 °F).

Pyroclastic flows are the most deadly of all volcanic hazards, destroying everything in their path by incineration, impact and asphyxiation. They occur most commonly from the collapse of an eruption column or of a lava dome.

Mechanism

A pyroclastic flow consists of a dense basal avalanche of hot blocks and ash overlain by a turbulent cloud of gas and fine ash (the phoenix cloud or co-ignimbrite cloud). The flow is fluidised by escaping gas, allowing it to travel great distances and surmount obstacles. Deposits left by pyroclastic flows, when welded by their own heat, form ignimbrite.

Notable events

The eruption of Mount Pelée in 1902 sent a pyroclastic flow (a nuée ardente, "glowing cloud") into the city of Saint-Pierre, Martinique, killing some 29,000 people in minutes. Pyroclastic flows from Mount St. Helens (1980), Mount Unzen (1991) — which killed volcanologists Katia and Maurice Krafft and Harry Glicken — and the Soufrière Hills volcano (1997), which destroyed Plymouth, Montserrat, are among the best documented. The Roman cities of Pompeii and Herculaneum were devastated by pyroclastic surges and flows from Vesuvius in AD 79.
