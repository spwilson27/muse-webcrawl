Christ myth theory

The Christ myth theory, also known as the Jesus myth theory, Jesus mythicism, or the Jesus ahistoricity theory, is the view that the story of Jesus is a work of mythology with no historical person at its origin, or, in weaker forms, that the figure of Jesus as presented in the Gospels is almost entirely legendary and that little can be known of any historical individual behind it.

Proponents of the Christ myth theory argue the Gospel narratives are mythological constructions

History of the theory

Questions about the historicity of Jesus arose during the Enlightenment, with writers such as Constantin-François Volney and Charles-François Dupuis suggesting that Jesus was a purely mythical figure. In the 19th century Bruno Bauer argued that the Gospel of Mark was a literary invention. In the 20th century the thesis was advanced by writers such as Arthur Drews (The Christ Myth, 1909) and later popularized by authors including Earl Doherty, who proposed that the earliest Christians believed in a purely celestial Jesus, and by Robert M. Price.

Main arguments

Proponents point to the lack of contemporary non-Christian references to Jesus, the lateness and theological character of the Gospel narratives, parallels between the Jesus story and earlier pagan dying-and-rising god myths, and the Pauline epistles' relative silence about the biographical details of Jesus's life. Critics respond that these parallels are overstated, that the epistles do contain biographical references, and that near-contemporary sources such as Josephus and Tacitus attest to Jesus and his execution.

Scholarly reception

The theory is a fringe position in academic biblical studies: the overwhelming scholarly consensus holds that Jesus of Nazareth existed as a historical person who was baptized by John the Baptist and crucified under Pontius Pilate. Critics of the theory, including some non-Christian scholars such as Bart Ehrman (whose Did Jesus Exist? addresses it directly), describe mythicism as unsupported by mainstream historical method, while its supporters argue that the consensus rests on confessional traditions of scholarship.
