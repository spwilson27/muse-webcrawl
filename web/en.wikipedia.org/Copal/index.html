Copal

Copal is a type of tree resin, particularly the aromatic resins harvested from trees of the genus Bursera in Mexico and Central America and from other tropical species in Africa and Asia. The word derives from the Nahuatl copalli, meaning incense. Copal occupies an intermediate stage between fresh, soft resin and fully fossilised amber; younger, softer grades are sometimes distinguished from harder, semi-fossil deposits such as Zanzibar and Congo copal.

Uses

In Mesoamerica, copal was burned as incense in Aztec and Maya ritual from pre-Hispanic times, and it remains central to ceremonies including Day of the Dead observances and the rituals of traditional healers. White copal smoke was offered to deities and used for purification and divination.

Commercially, hard copals were prized in the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries as the principal ingredient of high-quality varnishes and lacquers, valued for their hardness and gloss before synthetic resins displaced them. Copal is still used in incense, traditional medicine, and some artists' mediums.

Sources

Important commercial sources have included kauri copal from the kauri pine of New Zealand, Manila copal from Agathis species in Southeast Asia, and East African copals dug from the soil where resin-bearing trees once grew. Because copal softens at lower temperatures than amber and dissolves in alcohol, simple tests distinguish the two materials in the gem and fossil trade, where copal containing insect inclusions is sometimes sold as young amber.
