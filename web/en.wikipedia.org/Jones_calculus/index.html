Jones calculus

Jones calculus describes fully polarised light.

In optics, polarised light can be described using the Jones calculus, a mathematical formalism invented by American physicist Robert Clark Jones in a series of papers between 1941 and 1956. The polarisation state of light is represented by a two-component complex vector, the Jones vector, and the effect of each optical element — a polariser, waveplate, rotator and so on — is represented by a 2×2 complex matrix, the Jones matrix. The state of light emerging from a train of elements is found by multiplying the matrices in sequence.

Jones vector

The Jones vector lists the complex amplitudes of the electric field's x and y components, encoding both the relative amplitude and phase that determine whether light is linearly, circularly or elliptically polarised. Normalised examples include (1, 0) for horizontally polarised light and (1, −i)/√2 for right-circularly polarised light.

Scope and limitations

Jones calculus handles completely polarised, coherent light exactly and is simple to combine with calculations of interference. It cannot describe unpolarised or partially polarised light, nor depolarisation in optical elements; for those cases the more general Stokes parameters and Mueller calculus, which use four real quantities and 4×4 matrices, are used instead.
