Neopythagoreanism was a revival of the doctrines of Pythagoras and the early Pythagoreans that flourished in the Greco-Roman world from the 1st century BC into the 3rd century AD. Blending Pythagorean number symbolism and ascetic practice with Platonic metaphysics and elements of Stoicism, it formed an important bridge between Hellenistic philosophy and Neoplatonism.

History

After centuries in which Pythagorean ideas survived mainly within the Platonic Academy and in popular lore, a distinct revival began in the 1st century BC, associated with figures such as the Roman scholar Nigidius Figulus and the Alexandrian Eudorus. Many works composed in this period were pseudepigraphically attributed to Pythagoras himself or to early Pythagoreans such as Archytas and Timaeus of Locri, and were written in a deliberate pseudo-Doric dialect to lend them antiquity.

In the 1st and 2nd centuries AD the movement produced the wandering sage Apollonius of Tyana — whose biography by Philostratus portrays him as a Pythagorean ascetic and miracle-worker — the arithmetician Nicomachus of Gerasa, whose Introduction to Arithmetic became a standard textbook for a millennium, and Moderatus of Gades. Numenius of Apamea (2nd century) combined Pythagorean and Platonic doctrines of a supreme God and a demiurge, and was read with interest by the Neoplatonists and by early Christians.

Doctrines

Neopythagoreans typically taught a dualism of principles — the Monad (unity, goodness, God) and the indefinite Dyad (plurality, matter, evil) — from which numbers, and through them the cosmos, derive. They emphasized the symbolic and theological meaning of the first ten numbers (the tetraktys), the immortality and transmigration of the soul, kinship of all living beings, and an ascetic way of life including dietary restrictions, purity rules, and contemplation as the path to assimilation to God. Number theory, harmonics, and astronomy were studied as sacred sciences disclosing the structure of reality.

Legacy

Neopythagoreanism merged into and helped shape Middle Platonism and the Neoplatonism of Plotinus, who was said to have considered writing against Numenius as a plagiarist of his own ideas — or rather, whose teacher Ammonius harmonized the same sources. Its number symbolism influenced late antique and medieval thought through Boethius's transmission of Nicomachus, and its ascetic, theurgic ethos fed into late pagan religious philosophy.
