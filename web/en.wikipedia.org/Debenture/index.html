Debenture

A debenture is a medium- to long-term debt instrument used by companies and governments to borrow money, usually at a fixed rate of interest. The precise legal meaning of the term differs between jurisdictions: in American usage, a debenture is an unsecured bond backed only by the general creditworthiness of the issuer, whereas in British usage a debenture is typically secured against the assets of the company.

Characteristics

Debentures pay interest (the coupon) at regular intervals and repay the principal at a stated maturity date. Holders are creditors of the company, not owners: they have no voting rights, but in a liquidation they rank ahead of shareholders. Debentures may be convertible into shares of the issuing company (convertible debentures), redeemable before maturity at the issuer's option, or issued in registered or bearer form. Because debenture holders rely on the issuer's promise to pay, interest rates reflect the issuer's credit rating.

History and use

The word derives from the Latin debentur, "they are owed", reflecting the traditional opening words of the instrument, Debentur mihi — "there are owed to me". Debentures were widely used in the nineteenth century to finance railways and canals, and famous British examples include the debenture-style season-ticket schemes used to finance football stadium construction. Today the term overlaps heavily with "bond", and its use depends on local legal tradition.
