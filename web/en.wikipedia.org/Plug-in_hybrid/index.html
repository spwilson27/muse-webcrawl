Plug-in hybrid

A plug-in hybrid charging its battery from the grid

A plug-in hybrid electric vehicle (PHEV) is a hybrid electric vehicle equipped with a rechargeable battery pack that can be charged from an external source of electricity, such as a wall socket or charging station, in addition to being charged internally by its on-board internal combustion engine-driven generator. Plug-in hybrids share the characteristics of both conventional hybrid vehicles and battery electric vehicles: they have an electric motor and a gasoline (or diesel) engine, but a much larger battery than a conventional hybrid, enabling extended all-electric driving.

History

The concept is old — the 1900 Lohner–Porsche Mixte was an early series hybrid — but the modern PHEV era began with conversions of the Toyota Prius in the mid-2000s and the 2008 launch of the Chinese BYD F3DM, the first mass-produced plug-in hybrid. General Motors' Chevrolet Volt (2010), a range-extended plug-in, and the plug-in version of the Prius (2012) popularised the format in North America, Europe and Japan. The Mitsubishi Outlander P-HEV became one of the world's best-selling plug-in hybrids.

Operation

Most PHEVs start in charge-depleting mode, driving mainly or entirely on electricity until the battery reaches a minimum state of charge, then switch to charge-sustaining hybrid operation. Typical all-electric ranges grew from roughly 15–20 km in early models to 50–80 km in models of the 2020s. Because daily driving distances are often shorter than the electric range, owners who charge regularly can do most driving electrically while retaining an engine for long trips, eliminating range anxiety.

Benefits and criticism

Plug-in hybrids can substantially reduce fuel consumption and tailpipe carbon dioxide emissions compared with conventional cars, and they provide a bridge technology where charging infrastructure is sparse. However, studies by European research groups and the ICCT in the early 2020s found that real-world fuel consumption of PHEVs — especially company cars rarely plugged in — was several times higher than official test figures, leading regulators in the European Union to revise the utility factors used in emissions calculations. Their environmental benefit therefore depends heavily on how often they are charged and driven electrically.

Market

China became the largest market for plug-in hybrids, where they count as "new energy vehicles" and models with extended-range configurations became popular in the 2020s. Global sales of plug-in hybrids reached well over a million units per year, with manufacturers including BYD, Toyota, BMW, Volvo, Hyundai and Jeep offering PHEV variants across their line-ups.
