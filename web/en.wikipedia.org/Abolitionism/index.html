Abolitionism

Abolitionism is the movement to end slavery and the slave trade. Emerging in the late eighteenth century in Britain, France and North America, it grew from religious and Enlightenment critiques of slavery into mass political campaigns that, over roughly a century, achieved the legal abolition of slavery throughout the Atlantic world and beyond.

Origins

Early opposition to slavery came from Quakers and other religious groups; in 1688 Quakers in Germantown, Pennsylvania, issued the first organised protest against slavery in the English colonies. Enlightenment thinkers attacked slavery as contrary to natural rights, and enslaved people themselves resisted continuously — most dramatically in the Haitian Revolution (1791–1804), which destroyed slavery in French Saint-Domingue and founded Haiti. In Britain, the 1772 Somerset case held that slavery was unsupported by English common law. Activists such as Thomas Clarkson and William Wilberforce, and Olaudah Equiano, a formerly enslaved author, built a popular movement; Josiah Wedgwood's medallion "Am I Not a Man and a Brother?" became its emblem.

Abolition of the trade and of slavery

Britain and the United States banned the transatlantic slave trade in 1807–1808, and the British Slavery Abolition Act of 1833 ended slavery across most of the British Empire. France abolished colonial slavery in 1848. In the United States, abolitionists such as William Lloyd Garrison, Frederick Douglass and Harriet Tubman demanded immediate emancipation; slavery was destroyed during the Civil War by the Emancipation Proclamation (1863) and the Thirteenth Amendment (1865). Brazil, in 1888, was the last Western nation to abolish slavery, and legal abolition continued elsewhere into the twentieth century; the 1926 Slavery Convention and the Universal Declaration of Human Rights condemned slavery universally, though forms of forced labour persist and are targeted by modern anti-slavery movements.
