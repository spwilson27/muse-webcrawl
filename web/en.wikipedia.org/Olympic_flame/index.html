Olympic flame

The Olympic flame is a symbol of the Olympic Games. It is lit at Olympia, Greece, from the rays of the sun, carried by relay to the host city, and used to ignite the cauldron in the Olympic Stadium at the opening ceremony, where it burns until the closing ceremony. The flame commemorates the theft of fire from Zeus by Prometheus in Greek myth and recalls the sacred fires kept burning at ancient Olympia during the original Games.

History

A flame first burned at the modern Games at Amsterdam 1928, in a tower at the Olympic Stadium. The torch relay was introduced for Berlin 1936, organised by Carl Diem: runners carried the flame from Olympia to Berlin, a ceremony since elaborated for every Games. The Winter Games adopted the flame in 1952 (with the fire first lit in Morgedal, Norway) and have used the Olympia lighting since 1964.

Lighting ceremony and relay

At Olympia, an actress playing the high priestess of Hera uses a parabolic mirror to focus sunlight and kindle the flame, then passes it to the first torchbearer. Relays have travelled by unusual means — by Concorde, underwater in the Great Barrier Reef, aboard the International Space Station, and to the summit of Mount Everest in 2008. Security lamps carrying the "mother flame" travel with the relay so an extinguished torch can be relit from the original fire.

The cauldron

The final torchbearer is usually a sporting hero of the host nation; memorable lightings include the archer Antonio Rebollo firing a flaming arrow into the cauldron at Barcelona 1992 and Muhammad Ali at Atlanta 1996. Cauldron designs have ranged from simple bowls to elaborate moving sculptures, such as the 204-petal cauldron of London 2012.
