Western esotericism

Western esotericism is a scholarly term for a wide range of related ideas and movements in Western history generally characterised by the pursuit of hidden or "inner" knowledge. It encompasses Hermeticism, Renaissance magic, alchemy, astrology, Christian Kabbalah, Rosicrucianism, Freemasonry, Theosophy and various currents of the modern occult revival.

History

Its roots lie in late antiquity, in Gnosticism, Neoplatonism and the Hermetic writings attributed to Hermes Trismegistus. During the Renaissance, the translation of the Corpus Hermeticum by Marsilio Ficino and the work of figures such as Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, Cornelius Agrippa, Paracelsus and John Dee fused these currents with natural philosophy and Christian theology. With the Scientific Revolution and the Enlightenment, such ideas were progressively marginalised as superstition — what historian Wouter Hanegraaff calls the "rejected knowledge" of the West — but they persisted in fraternal and occult orders, and flowered again in the nineteenth century with Spiritualism, the Theosophical Society (founded 1875) and the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn.

Study

The academic study of Western esotericism developed in the later twentieth century, notably through the work of Frances Yates, Antoine Faivre — who proposed a set of intrinsic characteristics including correspondences, living nature, imagination and transmutation — and Wouter Hanegraaff. It is now taught at universities including the University of Amsterdam and the Sorbonne.
