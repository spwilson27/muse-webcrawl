Allais paradox

The Allais paradox is a choice problem designed by Maurice Allais (1953) to show an inconsistency of actual observed choices with the predictions of expected utility theory. The paradox arises when comparing participants' choices in two different experiments, each of which consists of a choice between two gambles.

The problem

In the first choice, most people prefer a certain $1 million over a gamble offering an 89% chance of $1 million, a 10% chance of $5 million, and a 1% chance of nothing. In the second choice, most people prefer a gamble with a 10% chance of $5 million and a 90% chance of nothing over a gamble with an 11% chance of $1 million and an 89% chance of nothing.

These two popular choices are inconsistent with each other under expected utility theory: subtracting the common consequence from both gambles in each pair shows that the pairs are structurally identical, so a decision maker who obeys the independence axiom of expected utility theory must make corresponding choices in both cases. The typical pattern of responses violates that axiom, largely because of the certainty effect — people overweight outcomes that are considered certain relative to outcomes that are only probable.

Significance

The Allais paradox was one of the earliest and most influential demonstrations that real human decision making departs systematically from the axioms of rational choice theory. It helped motivate the development of non-expected-utility theories of choice under risk, including prospect theory developed by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, who also reported experimental replications of the effect.
