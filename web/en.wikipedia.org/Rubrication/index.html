Rubrication

Rubrication is the addition of text in red ink to a manuscript for emphasis. Practised in both ancient and medieval texts, rubrication is one of several ways used by scribes to emphasise text or indicate structure within a work. The term derives from the Latin rubrica, meaning red ochre or red chalk, and the practice gave its name to the rubric — a heading, rule or instruction in liturgical books, traditionally printed in red.

History

Rubrication was first used in Egyptian papyrus texts of the New Kingdom period, where important passages — such as the titles of chapters in the Book of the Dead — were written in red ink against the black of the main text. The practice passed into classical and then medieval scribal culture. In medieval manuscripts, rubrics were typically added by a specialist scribe called a rubricator, who worked through a finished book adding headings, chapter titles, paragraph marks (pilcrows), highlighted initials and strokes through capital letters in red. Space for these additions was left by the main copyist, sometimes with small guide letters written in the margin to show what the rubricator should write.

Function

Red text served a navigational and interpretive purpose. Medieval books often lacked title pages, tables of contents and running heads; rubrics marked the beginnings of chapters, identified the speakers in dialogues and dialogic works, flagged liturgical instructions in service books (distinguishing what was to be done from what was to be said), and highlighted authorities or quotations. In legal and scholastic manuscripts, rubricated headings summarised the following passage, functioning much like modern section headings.

Materials and technique

The red pigment most often used was vermilion (mercuric sulphide), or cheaper alternatives such as red lead (minium) and red ochre. The rubricator worked with a fine brush or quill, following the spaces and guide letters left by the scribe. Because rubrication was a separate production stage, some manuscripts survive unfinished, with blank spaces where rubrics were never added — a useful source of evidence for historians of the book about workshop practice.

Legacy

With the coming of printing, rubrication was initially imitated by hand in early printed books, and red printing was gradually introduced using a second pass through the press. The word survives in the modern senses of "rubric": the instructions printed at the head of an examination paper or form, and directions in liturgical books.
