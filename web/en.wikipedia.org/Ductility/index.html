Ductility

Ductility refers to the ability of a material to sustain significant plastic deformation before fracture. Ductile materials can be drawn into wires or hammered into thin sheets without breaking, a property related to malleability. In materials science and engineering, ductility is an important consideration in manufacturing, since it governs whether a material can be shaped by processes such as rolling, extrusion and drawing, and in structural design, because ductile materials absorb energy and deform visibly before failing, providing warning of overload.

Measurement

Ductility is commonly quantified from a tensile test as the percent elongation at fracture or the percent reduction of cross-sectional area at fracture. Materials with elongation above roughly 5% are often described as ductile, while those fracturing with little plastic deformation are described as brittle. Gold, silver, platinum, copper, aluminium and mild steel are highly ductile; cast iron and concrete are brittle.

Factors affecting ductility

Temperature strongly affects ductility: many metals undergo a ductile-to-brittle transition at low temperatures, historically significant in failures such as welded Liberty ships fracturing in cold water. Alloying, grain size, strain rate and radiation exposure also matter; neutron irradiation can embrittle reactor steels. In metals, ductility arises from the movement of dislocations in the crystal lattice, which is easier in face-centred cubic metals such as copper and aluminium than in hexagonal metals.

Geology

In Earth science, ductility describes the ability of rock to deform under stress without fracturing; deep in the crust and in the mantle, high temperatures make rocks behave ductilely, folding rather than faulting.
