Kos (Greek: Κως) is a Greek island in the southeastern Aegean Sea, part of the Dodecanese chain, lying a few kilometres off the coast of Turkey near Bodrum. The third-largest island of the Dodecanese by area and second by population after Rhodes, it is known for its long sandy beaches, fertile plain, and rich classical heritage — above all as the birthplace of Hippocrates, the "father of medicine."

History

Inhabited since the Bronze Age, Kos flourished in the Hellenistic period, when the Asklepieion — a sanctuary and healing centre dedicated to Asclepius — drew the sick from across the Greek world, and the island's medical school and library rivalled Alexandria's. The island passed through Roman, Byzantine, and Knights Hospitaller rule; the knights built the great Nerantzia Castle guarding the harbour of Kos Town. The Ottomans held Kos from 1523 until 1912, when Italy occupied the Dodecanese; after German and British interludes during the Second World War, the island was united with Greece in 1948. A destructive earthquake struck in 1933, and another in 2017.

Sights and economy

Kos Town combines Roman ruins — the agora, the Casa Romana, and a Roman odeon — with Ottoman mosques and the plane tree under which, legend says, Hippocrates taught his students. The Asklepieion, a few kilometres inland, remains the island's most famous archaeological site. Tourism is the economic mainstay, supported by beach resorts at Kardamena, Tigaki, and Mastichari, while the flat terrain and extensive cycle paths have made Kos one of Greece's most bicycle-friendly islands. Agriculture produces grapes, figs, almonds, olives, and tomatoes.
