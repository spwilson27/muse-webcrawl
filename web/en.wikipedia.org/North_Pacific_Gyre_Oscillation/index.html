North Pacific Gyre Oscillation

The North Pacific Gyre Oscillation (NPGO) is a climate pattern of the North Pacific Ocean identified in 2009 by Emanuele Di Lorenzo and colleagues. It is defined as the second dominant mode of sea surface height variability in the Northeast Pacific and reflects fluctuations in the strength of the North Pacific subtropical and subpolar gyres.

Characteristics

Whereas the Pacific Decadal Oscillation (PDO) is tied mainly to sea surface temperature patterns driven from the tropics and mid-latitude winds, the NPGO captures variability in ocean circulation and salinity, nutrients, and chlorophyll in the California Current and Alaskan Gyre systems. In its positive phase, the subtropical gyre strengthens, upwelling-favorable winds along the United States West Coast intensify, and waters there tend to be cooler, saltier, and more nutrient-rich.

Ecological significance

The NPGO has been linked to changes in plankton productivity, salmon survival, and the distribution of marine species along the Pacific coast of North America. It provides a better explanation than the PDO for observed trends in salinity and nutrients in the California Current since the late 1990s.

Climate connections

Research suggests the NPGO responds to wind forcing associated with the North Pacific Oscillation and to tropical Pacific variability, and that it may become an increasingly important mode as the climate warms.
