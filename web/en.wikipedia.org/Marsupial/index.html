Marsupial

InfraclassMarsupialia
SpeciesOver 330
RangeAustralasia and the Americas

Marsupials are a group of mammals comprising the infraclass Marsupialia. They are characterised by giving birth to relatively undeveloped young that typically continue to develop within a pouch (marsupium) on the mother's abdomen, attached to a teat.

Reproduction

After a very short gestation, the tiny, blind newborn crawls to the pouch and nurses for weeks or months. Female marsupials have two vaginas and two uteri; males often have a bifurcated penis. Marsupial reproduction contrasts with placental mammals, which nourish young internally for longer via a placenta (though marsupials also form a short-lived yolk-sac placenta).

Distribution and diversity

About 70% of the more than 330 living species occur in Australia and New Guinea — kangaroos, koalas, wombats, possums and the Tasmanian devil among them — and most of the rest, chiefly opossums, in the Americas. Marsupials evolved in the Americas and reached Australia via Antarctica before the continents separated.

Convergent evolution

Isolated in Australasia, marsupials evolved forms strikingly parallel to placental mammals elsewhere: marsupial moles, gliders, anteaters and the extinct thylacine, a wolf-like predator. Many species are threatened by habitat loss and introduced predators such as foxes and cats.
