The Anthesteria was one of the four Athenian festivals in honour of Dionysus. It was held annually for three days, from the eleventh to the thirteenth of the month of Anthesterion, around the time of the full moon, in late February or early March. The festival celebrated the maturing of the wine stored over the previous winter and the beginning of spring.

The three days

The first day, Pithoigia ("jar-opening"), saw the opening of the pithoi containing the new wine, with libations offered to Dionysus. The second day, Choës ("jugs"), was a day of drinking contests held in an eerie silence, each participant drinking from his own jug at his own table; it was also associated with the dead walking among the living, and with the ceremonial marriage of the basilinna, the wife of the archon basileus, to Dionysus. The third day, Chytroi ("pots"), was dedicated to the dead: pots of cooked grains and seeds were offered to Hermes Chthonius and the souls of the departed, who were then dismissed from the city.

Significance

The Anthesteria combined celebration of new wine with a strong chthonic, ancestral character, making it one of the most distinctive and haunting of Greek festivals. Small children were presented with miniature choes jugs, many of which survive from graves.
