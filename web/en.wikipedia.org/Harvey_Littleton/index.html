Harvey Littleton, founder of the studio glass movement.

Harvey Littleton (June 14, 1922 – May 13, 2013) was an American glass artist and educator, widely regarded as the founder of the studio glass movement, which made glassblowing possible in small individual artist studios rather than only in factories.

Early life and education

Littleton was born in Corning, New York, the son of Jesse Littleton, a physicist at Corning Glass Works who contributed to the development of Pyrex. After serving in the U.S. Army Signal Corps in the Second World War, he studied physics at the University of Michigan before switching to design and earning degrees in industrial design and ceramics, completing an MFA at the Cranbrook Academy of Art in 1954. He taught ceramics at the University of Wisconsin–Madison from 1951.

Studio glass

Convinced that glass could be worked in a studio like clay, Littleton organized two experimental workshops at the Toledo Museum of Art in 1962. With the help of glass researcher Dominick Labino, who developed a low-melting-point glass and a small furnace design, Littleton demonstrated that individual artists could melt and blow glass independently. That same year he established the first university glass program at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, training a generation of artists including Dale Chihuly and Marvin Lipofsky, who spread glass programs across the United States.

Later career

Littleton retired from teaching in 1976 and moved to Spruce Pine, North Carolina, where he continued making work — known for colorful looping forms and later for prints made from glass plates (vitreographs). His work is held by museums including the Metropolitan Museum of Art, the Smithsonian American Art Museum, and the Victoria and Albert Museum.
