Recycling is the process of converting waste materials into new materials and objects. This concept often includes the recovery of energy from waste materials. The recyclability of a material depends on its ability to reacquire the properties it had in its original state. It is an alternative to "conventional" waste disposal that can save material and help lower greenhouse gas emissions. It can also prevent the waste of potentially useful materials and reduce the consumption of fresh raw materials, reducing energy use, air pollution (from incineration) and water pollution (from landfilling).

Recycling is the third component of the waste hierarchy: "Reduce, Reuse, and Recycle". In many countries, households sort paper, glass, metal, and certain plastics for kerbside collection; material recovery facilities then sort, clean, and bale the streams for reprocessing.

Materials

Metals

Aluminium and steel are among the most efficiently recycled materials: recycling aluminium saves around 95% of the energy needed to produce it from bauxite ore, and steel is recycled more than any other material by weight.

Paper and glass

Paper fibers shorten with each cycle, so paper can typically be recycled five to seven times. Glass is indefinitely recyclable without loss of quality, though colour sorting matters for container glass.

Plastics

Plastics are identified by resin identification codes 1–7. PET (1) and HDPE (2) are widely recycled; many other plastics are downcycled, incinerated, or landfilled. Global plastic recycling rates remain low — roughly 9% of plastic waste has been estimated as recycled.

Electronics and other streams

E-waste contains valuable metals such as gold and copper alongside hazardous substances, and is increasingly covered by extended producer responsibility schemes. Composting and anaerobic digestion handle organic waste in a similar recovery role.

Economics and markets

Recycling economics depend on commodity prices, contamination rates, and end markets. China's 2018 "National Sword" policy, which banned imports of most plastic and mixed-paper waste, disrupted global recycling markets and pushed exporting countries to invest in domestic processing capacity.

Criticism and challenges

Critics note that collection and reprocessing consume energy and water, that much collected material has historically been exported rather than recycled domestically, and that recycling can serve as a justification for single-use consumption. Supporters respond that life-cycle analyses still favour recycling most common materials over virgin production and landfill.
