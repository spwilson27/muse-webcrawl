Prenuptial agreement

A prenuptial agreement, commonly called a prenup, is a written contract entered into by a couple before marriage, specifying how their assets, debts and other matters will be divided in the event of divorce or death. Prenups typically address property division, spousal support, and the treatment of businesses, inheritances and pre-marital assets; they generally cannot determine child custody or child support.

Enforceability

Requirements vary by jurisdiction. In the United States, many states have adopted versions of the Uniform Premarital Agreement Act (1983) or the Uniform Premarital and Marital Agreements Act (2012); courts commonly require voluntary execution, full financial disclosure, and independent legal advice, and may refuse enforcement if terms are unconscionable. In England and Wales, prenups are not automatically binding, but the Supreme Court's decision in Radmacher v Granatino (2010) gave them decisive weight where freely entered into with full appreciation of their implications.

History and attitudes

Antenuptial contracts have ancient precedents, including the Jewish ketubah and Islamic mahr arrangements. Their modern use increased with rising divorce rates, later marriages and greater personal wealth; critics argue they undermine commitment, while supporters see them as a practical way to reduce conflict and protect family businesses or children from earlier relationships.
