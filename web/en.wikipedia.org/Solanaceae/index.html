The Solanaceae, or the nightshades, are a family of flowering plants that ranges from annual herbs to trees, and includes a number of important agricultural crops as well as many toxic and medicinal species. The family belongs to the order Solanales, in the asterid group of dicotyledons. The Solanaceae consists of some 98 genera and some 2,700 species, with a great diversity of habitats, morphology and ecology.

Description

Members typically have alternate leaves and radially symmetric flowers with five fused petals and five stamens. The fruit is a berry or a capsule. Many species accumulate alkaloids, which make them valuable as medicines and poisons alike — atropine, nicotine, capsaicin, scopolamine and the tropane alkaloids among them.

Economic importance

The family's largest genus, Solanum, contains the potato, the tomato and the aubergine (eggplant). Other major crops include peppers (Capsicum), tobacco (Nicotiana tabacum), groundcherries and tomatillos (Physalis), and the ornamental petunia. Since many of these originated in the Americas, the family played a central role in the Columbian exchange of food plants between the Old and New Worlds.

Distribution

Solanaceae are cosmopolitan, but the greatest diversity occurs in Central and South America. Australia is another centre of diversity, particularly for Nicotiana and the "bush tomato" species of Solanum.
