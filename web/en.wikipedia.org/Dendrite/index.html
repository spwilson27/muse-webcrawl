Dendrite

Dendrites are the branched extensions of a neuron (nerve cell) that receive electrochemical signals from other neurons and convey them toward the cell body, or soma. Their tree-like structure — the name comes from the Greek dendron, tree — greatly increases the surface available for synaptic connections; a single neuron may receive input from thousands of others.

Structure and function

Dendrites taper as they branch away from the soma and are often studded with tiny protrusions called dendritic spines, the sites of most excitatory synapses. Unlike axons, dendrites generally lack myelin and transmit graded potentials rather than all-or-nothing action potentials, though many dendrites can generate local spikes that boost or filter incoming signals. The arrangement and shape of the dendritic tree ("dendritic arbour") is characteristic of each neuron type — from the vast fan of a cerebellar Purkinje cell to the compact arbor of a retinal cell — and determines what inputs a neuron samples.

Plasticity

Dendrites grow and remodel throughout life. Spines form, enlarge and disappear as synapses strengthen or weaken, a process central to learning and memory; abnormalities in dendritic structure are associated with developmental and psychiatric disorders.
