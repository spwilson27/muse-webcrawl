Dig for Victory was a British propaganda and food-production campaign of the Second World War, launched in October 1939 by the Ministry of Agriculture, which urged civilians to grow their own food in gardens and allotments in order to ease the pressure on shipping and imports caused by the German U-boat campaign. Its slogan, posters and leaflets became some of the most recognisable images of the British home front, and the phrase has since become a byword for self-reliant wartime gardening.

The campaign

Before the war, Britain imported roughly two-thirds of its food, making it highly vulnerable to blockade. The campaign distributed a stream of practical material — the monthly Dig for Victory leaflets, crop plans for a standard ten-rod allotment plot, and advice on composting, pest control and preserving. The BBC's radio gardener C. H. Middleton broadcast weekly guidance to a mass audience. Parks, railway embankments, sports grounds and even the moat of the Tower of London were dug up for vegetables; the lawns of Buckingham Palace and Windsor Castle were turned over to crops. Lord Woolton, the Minister of Food, lent his name to the meatless "Woolton pie" of root vegetables.

Results

The number of allotments in England and Wales rose from about 815,000 in 1939 to a peak of around 1.75 million in 1943, in addition to millions of private gardens. By 1945, home production supplied a significant share of fresh vegetables and was credited with saving shipping space for war materiel, improving the national diet's vitamin content and sustaining morale. Combined with rationing and the "Kitchen Front" recipes, the campaign contributed to the surprising wartime result that the population's average nutrition, for poorer families, improved despite the conflict.

Legacy

Dig for Victory has been repeatedly invoked by later grow-your-own movements, allotments campaigns and sustainability advocates, and its poster art is reproduced widely. The campaign was consciously echoed during the COVID-19 pandemic seed shortages and in periodic government-backed pushes for community gardening.
