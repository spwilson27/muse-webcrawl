Compatibilism

Compatibilism is the belief that free will and determinism are mutually compatible and that it is possible to believe in both without being logically inconsistent. Compatibilists believe that freedom can be present or absent in situations for reasons that have nothing to do with metaphysics; for instance, courts of law define freedom without reference to determinism.

Defining free will

Compatibilist strategies commonly redefine free will. Classical compatibilists such as Thomas Hobbes and David Hume understood freedom as the absence of external constraint: an agent acts freely when she could have done otherwise if she had chosen otherwise. Later accounts emphasize acting on one's own desires, identification with one's motives (Harry Frankfurt's hierarchy of first- and second-order desires), or reasons-responsiveness (John Martin Fischer). Frankfurt's famous counterexamples — cases in which an agent acts on her own yet could not have done otherwise — challenged the "principle of alternate possibilities" and shifted debate toward moral responsibility.

Criticism

Incompatibilists argue that determinism genuinely conflicts with free will: libertarians maintain that free will exists and determinism is false, while hard determinists maintain the opposite. Peter van Inwagen's "consequence argument" contends that if determinism is true, our acts are consequences of the laws of nature and events in the remote past, and thus not up to us. Compatibilists reply that this conflates determinism with compulsion or fatalism.

Semantics and stakes

Some philosophers, notably in the 20th-century analytic tradition, treat compatibilism as partly a thesis about the correct analysis of ordinary concepts of freedom and responsibility. Others, such as Derk Pereboom, defend "hard incompatibilism," arguing that neither determinism nor indeterminism secures the kind of responsibility that would justify retributive punishment — while still allowing a forward-looking practice of moral accountability similar to what compatibilists defend.
