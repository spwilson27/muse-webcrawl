Genetic drift

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Genetic drift, also known as random genetic drift, is the change in the frequency of an existing gene variant (allele) in a population due to random chance. Unlike natural selection, drift does not distinguish between alleles that are beneficial, neutral or harmful; it is a sampling effect, and it is strongest in small populations.

Mechanism

In each generation, the alleles passed to offspring are a sample of those in the parents. Random variation in survival and reproduction causes allele frequencies to wander. Over time, drift tends to eliminate alleles entirely: each allele eventually is either lost or reaches fixation (100% frequency), and overall genetic variation declines. In a population of effective size N, the expected time to fixation for a neutral allele is proportional to N generations.

Bottlenecks and founder effects

Two special cases produce especially strong drift. A population bottleneck occurs when a population crashes to a small size, so the survivors carry only a fraction of the original diversity — as happened to cheetahs, which show very low genetic variation. The founder effect occurs when a new population is established by a few individuals whose allele frequencies differ from the source population; it helps explain, for example, elevated rates of certain inherited diseases in historically isolated communities.

Drift versus selection

Sewall Wright emphasised drift as a major evolutionary force, and Motoo Kimura's neutral theory of molecular evolution (1968) argued that most evolutionary change at the molecular level is caused by drift of neutral mutations rather than selection. Both drift and selection operate simultaneously in real populations; selection dominates for alleles with strong fitness effects in large populations, while drift dominates for nearly neutral alleles in small ones.
