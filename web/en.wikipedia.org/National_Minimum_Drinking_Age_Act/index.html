National Minimum Drinking Age Act

National Minimum Drinking Age Act of 1984
EnactedJuly 17, 1984
Signed byPresident Ronald Reagan
Public law98-363
EffectWithholding of federal highway funds from states with purchase age under 21

The National Minimum Drinking Age Act of 1984 is a United States federal law that requires states to prohibit persons under 21 years of age from purchasing or publicly possessing alcoholic beverages as a condition of receiving federal highway aid.

Background

After the Twenty-sixth Amendment lowered the voting age to 18 in 1971, many states lowered their drinking ages, setting different ages for beer, wine and spirits. Campaigns by groups such as Mothers Against Drunk Driving (MADD) pressed for uniformity, citing alcohol-related traffic deaths among young drivers and "blood borders" where teenagers drove to states with lower ages.

Provisions and legal challenges

Rather than directly mandating a national drinking age — which the Twenty-first Amendment arguably reserves to the states — Congress used its spending power, withholding a percentage of federal highway funds from states that did not set 21. In South Dakota v. Dole (1987), the Supreme Court upheld the law as a valid exercise of the spending power. By 1988 every state had complied.

Effects and debate

Studies generally credit the higher drinking age with reductions in alcohol-related crashes among young people, though debate continues over binge drinking and proposals to lower the age, including the Amethyst Initiative launched by college presidents in 2008.
