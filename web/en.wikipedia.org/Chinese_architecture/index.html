Chinese architecture

Chinese architecture is the embodiment of an architectural style that developed over millennia in China and has influenced architecture throughout East Asia. Since its emergence during the early ancient era, structural principles of Chinese architecture have remained largely unchanged; major changes came only in decorative details. Since the Tang dynasty, Chinese architecture has had a major influence on the architectural styles of Japan (and later the country's distinct Zen style), Korea, Mongolia, and Vietnam, with lesser influences on Southeast and South Asian countries including Malaysia, Singapore, Indonesia, Sri Lanka, Thailand, Laos, Cambodia and the Philippines.

Chinese architecture
Key features:Timber frame, dougong brackets, courtyard layouts, axial symmetry, feng shui
Famous examples:Forbidden City, Temple of Heaven, Great Wall

Characteristics

Traditional Chinese buildings emphasise horizontal lines, bilateral symmetry along an axis, and enclosure within walls and courtyards, as in the siheyuan courtyard house. Timber-frame construction with interlocking dougong bracket sets carries wide, overhanging tiled roofs whose upturned eaves are a hallmark of the style. Colours and numbers carry symbolic meaning under the principles of feng shui and the cosmology of the five elements.

Building types

The tradition embraces palaces (the Forbidden City in Beijing), temples and pagodas, altars (the Temple of Heaven), imperial tombs, gardens (Suzhou's classical gardens), fortifications (the Great Wall and city walls), bridges, and vernacular dwellings from cave houses (yaodong) to Fujian tulou. The Song dynasty manual Yingzao Fashi (1103) codified state building standards.

Modern era

The 20th and 21st centuries brought radical change: colonial treaty-port buildings, Soviet-influenced monuments, and after the 1980s a construction boom making Chinese cities centres of experimental contemporary architecture, while preservation of historic fabric remains contested.
