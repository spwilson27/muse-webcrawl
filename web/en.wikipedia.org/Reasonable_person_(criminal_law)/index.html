Reasonable person (criminal law)

The reasonable person standard asks how a person of ordinary prudence would have acted in the defendant's situation.

In criminal law, the reasonable person is a hypothetical individual whose conduct supplies an objective standard against which a defendant's behaviour is measured. The test is used principally in assessing negligence-based offences and in certain defences, most prominently provocation and self-defence. Unlike the defendant's own beliefs, the reasonable person's reactions are those the law regards as ordinary and acceptable.

Applications

Criminal negligence. For offences such as negligent homicide, the prosecution must typically show that the defendant departed grossly from the standard of care a reasonable person would have exercised. The standard is objective in that the defendant's own carelessness about the risk is not the measure; it is enough that a reasonable person would have foreseen it.

Provocation. In jurisdictions retaining the partial defence of provocation, the defendant's loss of self-control must be such as might have affected a reasonable person of the same age and sex placed in the same circumstances. English law, in cases such as R v Camplin [1978], allowed certain characteristics of the defendant to be attributed to the reasonable person in judging the gravity of the provocation, but not characteristics affecting the ordinary power of self-control.

Self-defence. Many jurisdictions require that the defendant's belief in the need for defensive force be not only honestly held but reasonable — that is, one a reasonable person in the same situation might have held.

Objective and subjective elements

The criminal law's reasonable person is generally less individualised than the figure in civil negligence. Courts have been reluctant to excuse defendants whose personal shortcomings — short temper, low intelligence, or inexperience — made them fall below the standard, on the ground that the criminal law sets minimum standards of conduct for all. Age is the principal characteristic routinely attributed, notably for child defendants.

Criticism

Commentators have questioned whose norms the reasonable person reflects, arguing that the standard can embed majority assumptions about gender, culture and behaviour. Feminist legal scholars have criticised its application in provocation and self-defence cases involving abused partners, prompting statutory reforms in several jurisdictions that replaced provocation with new partial defences, such as loss of control under the Coroners and Justice Act 2009 in England and Wales.
