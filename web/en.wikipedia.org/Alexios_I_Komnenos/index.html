Alexios I Komnenos (c. 1048/1056 – 1118) was Byzantine emperor from 1081 to 1118. Coming to power amid catastrophe — the loss of most of Anatolia to the Seljuk Turks after Manzikert (1071), Norman invasion in the Balkans, and a bankrupt, debased currency — he stabilised and partly restored the empire, founding the Komnenian dynasty that ruled for a century. His reign also set the stage for the Crusades.

Accession and the Norman war

A brilliant young general of the military aristocracy, Alexios seized Constantinople in a coup in April 1081 with his brother Isaac and the support of the Doukas family, into which he had married. Almost at once he faced Robert Guiscard's invasion: the Normans took Dyrrhachium after defeating Alexios at the Battle of Dyrrhachium (October 1081). Alexios survived through diplomacy (subsidising Henry IV of Germany to attack the Normans' papal allies in Italy), Venetian naval help — purchased with sweeping trade privileges — and the Normans' eventual withdrawal after Guiscard's death in 1085.

Recovery

Alexios went on to crush the Pechenegs at Levounion (1091), reform the coinage (introducing the gold hyperpyron in 1092), rebuild the army around a system of aristocratic household troops and foreign mercenaries, and govern through a family-based regime headed by his formidable mother Anna Dalassene. His diplomacy used gifts, titles and marriages to manage enemies he could not defeat.

The First Crusade

In 1095 Alexios appealed to Pope Urban II at the Council of Piacenza for Western mercenaries against the Turks. What arrived instead, from 1096, was the mass armed pilgrimage of the First Crusade. Alexios extracted oaths from its leaders to restore former Byzantine lands, supplied and ferried the crusaders, and recovered Nicaea, western Anatolia and northern Syria's coast in their wake — but relations soured over Antioch, retained by Bohemond, and Byzantine–crusader mistrust lasted generations. His daughter Anna Komnene's Alexiad is the great narrative source for his reign.

Death and legacy

Alexios died in 1118 after a long illness, succeeded smoothly by his son John II — itself a mark of the stability he had restored. Historians see him as one of Byzantium's ablest emperors: a survivor who rebuilt the state's finances, army and prestige from near-collapse.
