Josephus on Jesus

The writings of Josephus contain the earliest non-Christian references to Jesus.

The extant manuscripts of the works of the first-century Romano-Jewish historian Flavius Josephus include two passages about Jesus of Nazareth, both in the Antiquities of the Jews (written c. AD 93–94). They are among the earliest non-Christian attestations of Jesus and have been intensely studied and debated.

The Testimonium Flavianum

The longer passage, the Testimonium Flavianum (Antiquities 18.63–64), describes Jesus as a wise man and wonder-worker, condemned to the cross by Pontius Pilate, whose followers — the Christians — persisted after his reported resurrection. Because several phrases, such as "He was the Christ", seem unlikely from a non-Christian Jew, most scholars hold that the passage is authentically Josephan at its core but was partially interpolated by later Christian copyists; a neutral reconstruction is widely attempted, and a 10th-century Arabic version recorded by Agapius supports a more restrained original wording.

The James passage

The second passage (Antiquities 20.200) recounts the execution in AD 62 of "the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, whose name was James". Its authenticity is more widely accepted, providing an independent reference to Jesus, to James the Just, and to the early Jerusalem church.

Scholarly significance

Together the passages are key evidence in historical-Jesus research for the existence of Jesus, his execution under Pilate and the continuation of his movement, though the degree of interpolation in the Testimonium remains contested between a minority who reject it entirely and those who accept a partially authentic core.
