German nuclear program during World War II

The German nuclear program during World War II, known informally as the Uranverein ("Uranium Club"), was Nazi Germany's effort to develop nuclear technology after the discovery of nuclear fission in Berlin in December 1938. Despite the involvement of leading physicists such as Werner Heisenberg, the programme never came close to producing a nuclear weapon and achieved only small experimental reactor piles.

Organisation and research

Research began in 1939 under both civilian and Army Ordnance auspices; scientists including Heisenberg, Otto Hahn, Walther Bothe, Kurt Diebner and Carl Friedrich von Weizsäcker worked on reactor theory, isotope separation and plutonium. The Germans pursued heavy water as a moderator, sourcing it from the Norsk Hydro plant at Vemork in occupied Norway — a supply disrupted by famous Allied commando raids and the sinking of the ferry carrying the heavy water in 1944.

Why it fell behind

Historians cite several factors: a 1942 decision that a bomb could not be finished in time to affect the war, which kept the programme at research scale; fragmentation between competing groups; limited industrial resources; errors in calculating critical mass and in measuring graphite's neutron absorption, which ruled out the graphite moderator used successfully by the Americans; and the loss of many scientists driven out by Nazi racial policies. The largest German experimental pile, the B-VIII reactor assembled in a cellar at Haigerloch in early 1945, remained subcritical.

Farm Hall

After the war, ten German nuclear scientists were interned at Farm Hall in England under Operation Epsilon, and their secretly recorded conversations — released decades later — have fuelled lasting debate about how hard the German scientists had really tried to build a bomb for Hitler.
