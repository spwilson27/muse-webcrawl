Filipino cuisine

Filipino cuisine (Filipino: lutuing Pilipino / pagkaing Pilipino) is composed of the cuisines of more than a hundred distinct ethnolinguistic groups found throughout the Philippine archipelago. Shaped by indigenous Austronesian traditions and centuries of trade and colonisation, it draws influences from Chinese, Malay, Spanish, Mexican, and American cooking.

Characteristics

Meals are centred on rice, eaten at breakfast, lunch, and dinner, and frequently at merienda (snacks). Characteristic flavour combinations balance sour, salty, and sweet: vinegar-based adobo, tamarind-soured sinigang soup, and kare-kare (oxtail in peanut sauce) served with fermented shrimp paste (bagoong). Soy sauce, fish sauce (patis), coconut milk, calamansi, and garlic are staple seasonings.

Notable dishes

Adobo — meat braised in vinegar, soy sauce, garlic, and peppercorns — is often called the unofficial national dish. Others include lechon (whole roast pig), sisig (seasoned chopped pork served sizzling), lumpia (spring rolls of Chinese origin), pancit noodles, longganisa sausages, and the shaved-ice dessert halo-halo. Regional cuisines differ markedly: Ilocano cooking to the north is bitter and vegetable-heavy, Bicolano food uses chillies and coconut milk, and Kapampangan cuisine is famed for elaborate festive dishes.

History

Pre-colonial Filipinos traded with China and Southeast Asia; Spanish rule (1565–1898) brought tomatoes, chillies via the Manila galleon trade with Mexico, and Catholic fiesta culture; American and Japanese occupations added further layers. Contemporary Filipino cuisine has gained international attention through restaurants in the Filipino diaspora.
