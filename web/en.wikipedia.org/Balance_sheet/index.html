Balance sheet

A balance sheet summarises assets, liabilities and equity at a point in time. [Image placeholder]

In financial accounting, a balance sheet (also known as a statement of financial position) is a summary of the financial balances of an individual, organisation or business entity at a specific point in time. It lists the entity's assets, liabilities and ownership equity, and is built on the fundamental accounting equation:

Assets = Liabilities + Equity

The two sides must always "balance", hence the name. Along with the income statement and the cash flow statement, the balance sheet is one of the core financial statements used to evaluate a business's financial health.

Components

Assets

Assets are resources controlled by the entity. They are usually listed in order of liquidity and divided into current assets (cash, accounts receivable, inventory — expected to be converted to cash within a year) and non-current assets (property, plant and equipment, long-term investments, intangible assets such as goodwill and patents).

Liabilities

Liabilities are obligations owed to outsiders, divided into current liabilities (accounts payable, short-term debt, wages payable) and non-current liabilities (long-term loans, bonds payable, pension obligations).

Equity

Equity is the residual interest of the owners after liabilities are deducted from assets. For a corporation it includes share capital and retained earnings; for a sole proprietorship it is the owner's capital account.

Formats and standards

Balance sheets are presented either in a horizontal "account form" (assets on the left, liabilities and equity on the right) or in a vertical "report form". Public companies prepare them under frameworks such as International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS), under which the document is titled a "statement of financial position", or US Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP).

Uses and limitations

Analysts use the balance sheet to assess liquidity (e.g. the current ratio), leverage (debt-to-equity), and solvency. Its main limitation is that many assets are recorded at historical cost rather than current market value, and some valuable resources — such as internally generated brand value — may not appear at all.
