Caliphate

A caliphate (Arabic: خِلَافَة, khilāfa, "succession") is an institution or public office under the leadership of an Islamic steward with the title of caliph (Arabic: خَلِيفَة, khalīfa, "successor, replacement, deputy"), a person considered a political and religious successor to the Islamic prophet Muhammad and a leader of the entire Muslim world.

The Rashidun Caliphate (632–661)

The first caliphate was established after Muhammad's death in 632, when Abu Bakr was chosen as his successor. The four "Rightly Guided" caliphs — Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, and Ali — oversaw the rapid expansion of Muslim rule across Arabia, the Levant, Egypt, Iraq, and Persia. Disputes over the succession, culminating in the First Fitna (civil war) and the schism between Sunni and Shia Islam, followed Ali's assassination.

The Umayyad and Abbasid caliphates

The Umayyad Caliphate (661–750), ruled from Damascus, extended Muslim dominion from Iberia to Central Asia and made the caliphate hereditary. The Abbasid Caliphate (750–1258), ruled from Baghdad (and later, in exile, from Cairo), presided over the Islamic Golden Age of science, philosophy, and trade, though real power often fragmented among regional dynasties. Baghdad's fall to the Mongols in 1258 destroyed the caliphate in the east; a line of Abbasid caliphs continued under Mamluk protection in Cairo until 1517.

Later caliphates

Rival caliphates included the Fatimid Caliphate (909–1171) in North Africa and Egypt and the Umayyad Caliphate of Córdoba (929–1031) in al-Andalus. After conquering Egypt, the Ottoman sultans assumed the caliphal title, which they and their successors held until the Turkish Grand National Assembly abolished the Ottoman Caliphate in 1924. Calls for its restoration have recurred since; the militant group Islamic State proclaimed a "caliphate" in 2014, a claim rejected across the Muslim world.
