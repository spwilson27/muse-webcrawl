The pipa (Chinese: 琵琶; pinyin: pípá) is a traditional Chinese musical instrument belonging to the plucked category of string instruments. Sometimes called the "Chinese lute", the instrument has a pear-shaped wooden body with a varying number of frets ranging from 12 to 31. Another Chinese four-string plucked lute is the liuqin, which looks like a smaller version of the pipa. The pear-shaped instrument may have existed in China as early as the Han dynasty, and although historically the term pipa was once used to refer to a variety of plucked chordophones, its usage since the Song dynasty refers exclusively to the pear-shaped instrument.

The pipa is one of the most popular Chinese instruments and has been played for almost two thousand years in China. Several related instruments are derived from the pipa, including the Japanese biwa and Korean bipa in East Asia, and the Vietnamese đàn tỳ bà.

History

There is some uncertainty and disagreement about the origins of the pipa. Instruments with a pear-shaped body and short neck, played held in the lap, reached China from Central Asia along the Silk Road during the Han and Tang dynasties. By the Tang dynasty the pipa had become a favoured instrument at court and among literati, depicted frequently in murals such as those at the Mogao Caves in Dunhuang, where celestial musicians play the instrument while flying. Over the following centuries the frets multiplied and the playing technique shifted from the use of a large plectrum to the player's fingernails, making the modern pipa a highly expressive solo instrument capable of great virtuosity.

Construction and technique

The body of the pipa is hollowed from a single block of hardwood, often rosewood or sandalwood, with a thin soundboard of paulownia or similar light wood. Four strings, traditionally of silk and now usually of steel or nylon-wound steel, run over a series of raised frets made of bamboo, wood or ivory. The instrument is played with the five fingers of the right hand, often fitted with artificial nails, using a wide vocabulary of techniques: rapid tremolo (lun), string bending, harmonics, and percussive effects that can evoke battle scenes in famous pieces such as "Ambush from Ten Sides" (Shimian Maifu).

Repertoire

The traditional solo repertoire divides into wen (civil, lyrical) and wu (martial) pieces. Celebrated compositions include "Ambush from Ten Sides", "The Moon Rises High" (Yue'er Gao), "White Snow in Spring" (Yangchun Baixue), and "Flowing Water". In the twentieth century the pipa entered the Chinese orchestra and contemporary composition; modern virtuosi such as Liu Dehai and Wu Man have carried the instrument into concert halls worldwide and into collaborations across genres.
