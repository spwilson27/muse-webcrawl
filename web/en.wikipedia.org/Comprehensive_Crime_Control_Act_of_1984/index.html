Comprehensive Crime Control Act of 1984

Enacted by98th United States Congress
Signed12 October 1984 (President Ronald Reagan)
Public law98-473

The Comprehensive Crime Control Act of 1984 was the first comprehensive revision of the United States federal criminal code since the early 1900s. Enacted as part of the Continuing Appropriations Act for fiscal year 1985 and signed by President Ronald Reagan on 12 October 1984, it substantially reshaped federal sentencing, bail, forfeiture, and insanity defence law.

Major provisions

The Act's Sentencing Reform Act abolished federal parole, created the United States Sentencing Commission, and directed it to develop the Federal Sentencing Guidelines to reduce disparities in sentencing; it also made sentences determinate and provided for appellate review of sentences. The Bail Reform Act of 1984 permitted federal courts to detain defendants before trial on grounds of dangerousness. Other chapters broadened criminal and civil asset forfeiture, tightened the federal insanity defence following the acquittal of John Hinckley Jr., increased penalties for drug offences and for crimes on federal land, and created new offences relating to credit card fraud and computer crime.

Legacy

The Act is regarded as a landmark of the era's "tough on crime" politics. The Guidelines system it created operated as mandatory until the Supreme Court's decision in United States v. Booker (2005) rendered them advisory. Its forfeiture provisions have remained controversial, and its sentencing framework continues to structure federal criminal practice.
