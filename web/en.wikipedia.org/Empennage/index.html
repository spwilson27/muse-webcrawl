Empennage

The empennage (pronounced "emp-in-AHZH"), also known as the tail or tail assembly, is the structure at the rear of an aircraft that provides stability and control in flight. It typically consists of a horizontal stabiliser with elevators, which control pitch, and a vertical stabiliser (fin) with a rudder, which controls yaw. The word derives from the French empenner, "to feather an arrow", reflecting the empennage's role in keeping the aircraft pointed into the airflow like the fletching of an arrow.

Conventional empennage of a passenger aircraft

Function

The tail surfaces generate aerodynamic forces at a long moment arm behind the centre of gravity, giving the aircraft longitudinal and directional stability. The horizontal stabiliser balances pitching moments from the wing, while the fin keeps the nose aligned with the flight path. Movable elevators and rudders allow the pilot to control pitch and yaw, and trim tabs or adjustable stabilisers relieve control forces.

Configurations

The most common arrangement is the conventional tail, with a low-mounted horizontal stabiliser and a single fin. Alternatives include the T-tail, with the horizontal stabiliser atop the fin (used on many business jets and military transports); the cruciform tail; the V-tail, which combines rudder and elevator functions in two surfaces (as on the Beechcraft Bonanza); twin tails with two fins; and tailless designs, which dispense with the empennage entirely and rely on wing design and flight controls for stability.

Design considerations

Tail size is governed by the "tail volume coefficient", which relates tail area and moment arm to wing size. Designers must ensure adequate control authority at low speeds and in engine-out conditions while minimising drag and weight. In T-tails, the horizontal stabiliser can be blanketed by the wing wake at high angles of attack, producing the dangerous "deep stall" phenomenon.
