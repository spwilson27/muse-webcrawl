Turbine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A turbine (from the Greek τύρβη, tyrbē, or Latin turbo, meaning vortex) is a rotary mechanical device that extracts energy from a fluid flow and converts it into useful work. The work produced by a turbine can be used for generating electrical power when combined with a generator.

Principles

A turbine consists of a rotating element (rotor) fitted with blades, vanes or buckets, around which stationary guide vanes direct the working fluid. In an impulse turbine the fluid's pressure changes only in the stationary nozzles, while in a reaction turbine pressure drops across both stator and rotor; most modern machines combine both principles.

Types

  • Steam turbines — drive most of the world's thermal and nuclear electricity generation; developed from the work of Charles Parsons (1884) and Gustaf de Laval
  • Gas turbines — the core of jet engines and many power stations
  • Water turbines — Pelton, Francis and Kaplan designs for hydroelectric power
  • Wind turbines — convert wind energy directly to electricity

Uses

Beyond electricity generation, turbines power ships (via steam or gas), aircraft (turbojets and turbofans), and drive pumps, compressors and turbochargers. Steam and gas turbines achieve some of the highest efficiencies of any heat engines, especially in combined-cycle plants.
