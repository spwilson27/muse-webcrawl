The Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) is an intergovernmental organization of oil-exporting developing nations that coordinates and unifies the petroleum policies of its member countries. Founded in Baghdad in 1960 by Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and Venezuela, OPEC has had its headquarters in Vienna, Austria, since 1965.

As of the mid-2020s, OPEC's members are Algeria, Congo, Equatorial Guinea, Gabon, Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Libya, Nigeria, Saudi Arabia, the United Arab Emirates, and Venezuela. Angola left the organization in 2024, following earlier departures by Qatar (2019) and Indonesia. OPEC member countries hold the majority of the world's proven crude oil reserves and account for roughly a third to two-fifths of global production.

History

OPEC was formed in response to unilateral price cuts by the major international oil companies. Through the 1960s it pressed for higher revenues and greater sovereignty over national resources; during the 1970s members nationalized their industries and OPEC prices rose sharply, especially during the 1973 oil embargo by Arab members (OAPEC) during the Yom Kippur War and the 1979 Iranian Revolution, producing the two great "oil shocks."

OPEC+

Since 2016 OPEC has cooperated with a group of non-OPEC producers, led by Russia, in the so-called OPEC+ alliance, agreeing joint production quotas to manage global supply and prices. The group periodically adjusts output targets in response to market conditions, as during the COVID-19 demand collapse of 2020.

Influence and criticism

OPEC's influence over prices has fluctuated with the rise of non-OPEC supply, particularly U.S. shale oil. The organization is frequently described as a cartel, and legislation such as the proposed "NOPEC" bills in the United States has sought to expose it to antitrust suits, though none has been enacted.
