Aedes aegypti, the yellow fever mosquito, is a mosquito that can spread dengue fever, chikungunya, Zika fever, Mayaro and yellow fever viruses, and other disease agents. It is recognised by black-and-white markings on its legs and a lyre-shaped marking of silvery scales on the thorax.

Biology and distribution

Native to Africa, the species spread worldwide through shipping and trade and is now found throughout the tropics and subtropics, closely associated with human habitation. Females lay eggs just above the waterline in artificial containers — tyres, buckets, water-storage vessels — and the eggs can survive dry for months. Adults feed preferentially on humans and bite mainly at dawn and dusk, often resting indoors.

Disease transmission and control

Only females bite, taking blood meals needed for egg production; viruses acquired from an infected person can be passed to subsequent victims. Control relies on eliminating standing water, larvicides, insecticide spraying and, increasingly, biological methods such as releasing Wolbachia-infected or genetically modified males to suppress populations.
