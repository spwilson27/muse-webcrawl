A Spartiate (Greek: Σπαρτιάτης, plural Σπαρτιάται) was a full citizen of the ancient Greek city-state of Sparta. Spartiates called themselves the homoioi, the "equals" — a small warrior elite who monopolised political power, devoted their lives to military training, and lived off estates worked by the enslaved helots.

Status and qualifications

Citizenship belonged by birth to legitimate children of Spartiate parents, but it had to be earned and maintained. Boys entered the agoge, the state's rigorous communal upbringing, at age seven. As adults, a Spartiate had to belong to a syssition (common mess) and contribute a fixed share of produce from his allotted estate (kleros); failure to contribute meant demotion to the ranks of the "inferiors" (hypomeiones). Spartiates could not engage in trade or manual work, and were forbidden to accumulate wealth openly — at least in the classical ideal.

Role in society

Spartiates voted in the assembly (apella), served as the heavy infantry core of Sparta's army, and alone supplied the state's officers and the members of the gerousia (council of elders). Around them stood the perioikoi — free but politically excluded inhabitants of surrounding towns — and beneath all, the helots, whose labour freed the Spartiates for war and whose potential revolt haunted Spartan policy.

Decline

The citizen body shrank catastrophically over time — a process Aristotle already criticised: land concentrated in few hands (including those of wealthy women), mess contributions became unaffordable, casualties mounted, and citizenship numbers fell from some 8,000 in the early fifth century BC to around 1,000 by the mid-fourth century, and only a few hundred by the third. This oliganthropia ("shortage of men") is widely seen as a root cause of Sparta's decline after its defeat by Thebes at Leuctra (371 BC). Reforming kings Agis IV and Cleomenes III tried, and failed, to restore the citizen body by redistributing land and enfranchising others.
