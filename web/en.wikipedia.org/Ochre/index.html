Ochre

Ochre (from Ancient Greek: ὤχρα, ṓkhra, "pale yellow"), or ocher in American English, is a natural clay earth pigment, a mixture of ferric oxide and varying amounts of clay and sand. It ranges in colour from yellow to deep orange or brown. Wider use of the term includes red ochre, which takes its colour from hematite, and purple and sienna variants.

History

Ochre is among the oldest pigments used by humans. Red ochre pieces engraved with abstract designs were found at Blombos Cave in South Africa, dated to roughly 75,000 years ago. Ochre was used extensively in prehistoric cave paintings such as those at Lascaux and Altamira, in burials, and for body decoration. Aboriginal Australians have used ochre for tens of thousands of years in ceremony and art, and it remains culturally significant today.

Types

  • Yellow ochre — hydrated iron oxide (limonite), goethite-bearing.
  • Red ochre — anhydrous iron oxide (hematite); yellow ochre converts to red when heated.
  • Umber — ochre containing manganese oxide, giving a darker brown.
  • Sienna — a form of limonite-rich earth from Italy; burnt sienna is its heated form.

Production and use

Ochre is obtained by mining iron-rich earths, then washing, drying, and grinding them. Historically important sources included Roussillon in France. As a pigment (Colour Index PY43, PR102), ochre is lightfast, stable, and non-toxic, and it remains in use in artists' paints, building colorants, and cosmetics, although synthetic iron oxides have largely replaced natural ochre industrially.
