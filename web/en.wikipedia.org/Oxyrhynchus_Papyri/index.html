Oxyrhynchus Papyri

The Oxyrhynchus Papyri are a huge group of manuscripts discovered since the late 19th century at Oxyrhynchus (modern el-Bahnasa) in central Egypt, an archaeological site that has yielded by far the largest cache of ancient papyrus texts known. The texts date mainly from the Ptolemaic and Roman periods (roughly the 3rd century BC to the 7th century AD) and were preserved by Egypt's dry climate in the town's ancient rubbish heaps.

Excavation and publication

Excavations were begun for the Egypt Exploration Fund in 1896–97 by the young Oxford papyrologists Bernard Grenfell and Arthur Hunt, who shipped hundreds of boxes of fragments to Oxford. Publication in the series The Oxyrhynchus Papyri (from 1898 onward, eventually more than 80 volumes) has continued for well over a century; only a fraction — on the order of a few thousand of an estimated half-million fragments — has been published. The collection is held principally by the Sackler Library and Ashmolean Museum, Oxford, and administered by the Egypt Exploration Society.

Contents and significance

Most papyri are documentary — census returns, tax receipts, leases, private letters, petitions and court records — giving unparalleled evidence for daily life in Greco-Roman Egypt. The literary minority is extraordinary: lost plays of Menander and Euripides, poems of Sappho, Pindar and Callimachus, fragments of lost historians and of the Hellenica Oxyrhynchia, early Christian texts including portions of the Gospels and the Gospel of Thomas, and the earliest surviving diagram from Euclid's Elements. Famous individual items include P.Oxy. 1 (sayings of Jesus, later identified with the Gospel of Thomas) and P.Oxy. 52, a medical treatise.

Modern techniques

Since the 2000s, multispectral imaging, the volunteer transcription project Ancient Lives (Zooniverse), and machine-learning approaches have accelerated decipherment of the still-unpublished fragments.
