Mughal architecture

Mughal architecture is the type of Indo-Islamic architecture developed by the Mughals in the 16th, 17th and 18th centuries throughout the ever-changing extent of their empire in the Indian subcontinent. It developed from the architectural styles of earlier Muslim dynasties in India and from Iranian and Central Asian architectural traditions, particularly Timurid architecture. It also further incorporated and syncretized influences from wider Indian architecture, especially during the reign of Akbar (r. 1556–1605). Mughal buildings have a uniform pattern of structure and character, including large bulbous domes, slender minarets at the corners, massive halls, large vaulted gateways, and delicate ornamentation; examples of the style can be found in modern-day India, Afghanistan, Bangladesh, and Pakistan.

Development

The dynasty's founder Babur commissioned gardens and mosques, but the style took monumental form under his grandson Akbar, who built in red sandstone at Agra Fort, Fatehpur Sikri, and Humayun's Tomb in Delhi (the first garden-tomb in India, completed around 1570). Under Jahangir and especially Shah Jahan (r. 1628–1658), the style reached its classical peak: red sandstone gave way to white marble inlaid with semi-precious stones (pietra dura), perfect bilateral symmetry, and refined forms. Shah Jahan's works include the Taj Mahal at Agra (1632–1653), the Red Fort and Jama Masjid in Delhi. Aurangzeb's reign saw a retrenchment, though major works such as the Badshahi Mosque in Lahore (1673) were still produced.

Features

Characteristic elements include the charbagh four-part paradise garden, the four-centred Persian ("Tudor") arch, the onion dome on a high drum with an inverted lotus finial, chhatri pavilions, carved jali screens, calligraphy and floral inlay, and extensive use of water channels and reflecting pools. Fort-palaces combined audience halls (Diwan-i-Am and Diwan-i-Khas), mosques, baths, and gardens within fortified walls.

Legacy

Mughal architecture influenced later Indian styles, including Sikh and Rajput palaces and, in the 19th and 20th centuries, the Indo-Saracenic style used by the British Raj — visible in Edwin Lutyens's New Delhi. Several monuments, including the Taj Mahal, Humayun's Tomb, and the Red Fort, are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.
