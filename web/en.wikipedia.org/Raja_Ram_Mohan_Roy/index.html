Raja Ram Mohan Roy (22 May 1772 – 27 September 1833) was an Indian reformer and writer who was one of the founders of the Brahmo Sabha in 1828, the precursor of the Brahmo Samaj, a social-religious reform movement in the Indian subcontinent. He was given the title of Raja by Akbar II, the Mughal emperor, and is often called the "Father of Indian Renaissance".

Early life and thought

Roy was born into a Brahmin family in Radhanagar, Bengal, and educated in Patna and Varanasi, learning Persian, Arabic, Sanskrit, and later English, Latin, Greek, and Hebrew. His study of Islam, Christianity, and the Upanishads led him to a monotheistic, rationalist faith. In works such as Tuhfat-ul-Muwahhidin (A Gift to Deists) and his translations of the Upanishads, he argued against idolatry and priestly mediation and for the worship of one formless God.

Social reform

Roy campaigned most famously against sati, the practice of widows immolating themselves on their husbands' funeral pyres. Through petitions, pamphlets, and public debate, he influenced Governor-General Lord William Bentinck to abolish the practice by the Bengal Sati Regulation of 1829. He also advocated women's education and property rights, freedom of the press—protesting the Press Ordinance of 1823—and English-language scientific education, supporting the founding of Hindu College in Calcutta in 1817.

Later life and legacy

In 1830 Roy traveled to England as an envoy of the Mughal emperor to lobby against the East India Company's appeal to restore sati and to present the emperor's grievances. He died of meningitis in Bristol in 1833 and was buried at Arnos Vale Cemetery. His synthesis of rationalism and Upanishadic monotheism helped launch the Bengal Renaissance and made him a foundational figure of modern India.
