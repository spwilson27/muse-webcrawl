Stranger in a Strange Land

Stranger in a Strange Land is a 1961 science fiction novel by American author Robert A. Heinlein. It tells the story of Valentine Michael Smith, a human born and raised on Mars by Martians, who returns to Earth as a young man and, viewing human culture as an outsider, challenges its institutions — government, organized religion, money, and sexual mores — while founding a religion of his own. The novel won the Hugo Award for Best Novel in 1962 and became a defining countercultural text of the 1960s.

Plot summary

Smith is the sole survivor's child of the first, lost expedition to Mars, raised entirely by the planet's native inhabitants. Brought to Earth by a second expedition, he is held by the world government because, under an old legal precedent, he may personally own Mars. Wealthy author and gadfly Jubal Harshaw helps free him. Smith learns human ways while teaching Martian concepts such as "grokking" — understanding something so fully that it becomes part of oneself — and the Martian language's worldview. He eventually founds a church whose members learn Martian, share water as a ritual of kinship, and practise communal living and free love. Smith is ultimately killed by a mob, becoming a martyr whose movement continues after him.

Publication history

Heinlein completed a long manuscript in 1960; the publisher Putnam required cuts, and the 1961 edition appeared at about 160,000 words. The fuller text, restored from Heinlein's original manuscript by his widow Virginia, was published in 1991 after his death. The title is taken from the Book of Exodus: "I have been a stranger in a strange land."

Influence

The novel introduced the word "grok" into the English language and popular usage, influenced the 1960s counterculture, and helped bring science fiction to a mainstream literary audience. Its Church of All Worlds inspired a real religious movement of the same name. The book remains one of Heinlein's best-known and most debated works.
