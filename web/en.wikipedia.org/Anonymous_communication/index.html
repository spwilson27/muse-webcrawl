Anonymous communication refers to methods of communicating in which the identity of the sender, the recipient, or both is concealed from observers and from the other party. In computer security and privacy research, anonymity is usually defined as the state of being not identifiable within a set of subjects, the "anonymity set".

Techniques

David Chaum's mix network (1981) routes messages through a chain of proxies that shuffle and re-encrypt them, breaking the link between sender and recipient. Onion routing, developed in the mid-1990s and popularised by Tor, wraps messages in layers of encryption peeled off at successive relays so that no single relay knows both source and destination. Other approaches include anonymous remailers (such as Cypherpunk and Mixmaster remailers), broadcast and multicast channels, dining cryptographers networks (DC-nets), and anonymous peer-to-peer systems such as I2P and Freenet.

Applications and trade-offs

Anonymous communication supports whistleblowing, political dissent, censorship circumvention, and private browsing, but can also facilitate illicit activity. Strong anonymity generally carries costs in latency, bandwidth, and usability, and real-world systems remain vulnerable to traffic analysis and global passive adversaries.
