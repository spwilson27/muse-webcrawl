The Polabian language is an extinct West Slavic language that was spoken by the Polabian Slavs (Wenden) in present-day northeastern Germany, around the town of Lüchow (Wendland) in the district of Lüchow-Dannenberg in Lower Saxony. It was the westernmost surviving Slavic language and the last of the languages of the Obotrite tribal confederation.

History

Polabian developed from the dialects of Slavic settlers who moved into the region east of the lower Elbe beginning in the 7th century. Over the following centuries it was steadily displaced by Low German as the area was incorporated into German-speaking states. By the early 18th century the language survived only in a few villages of the Wendland. Its last fluent native speaker is traditionally said to have died in 1756; the language lingered in fragments and remembered phrases into the later 18th century.

Documentation

Although never a literary language, Polabian is comparatively well documented for an extinct vernacular. Key sources include the vocabulary compiled by Christian Hennig von Jessen (the Vocabularium Venedicum, c. 1705), the chronicle notes of Johann Parum Schultze, and texts such as the Polabian version of the Lord's Prayer. These records preserve several thousand words and reveal features such as the retention of nasal vowels lost in most other Lechitic languages.

Linguistic features

  • Retention of the nasal vowels *ę and *ǫ, unlike Polish where they merged.
  • Strong influence of Low German vocabulary in later stages.
  • Preservation of archaic Slavic accentual patterns studied by modern Slavists.

Legacy

Traces of Polabian survive in local place names throughout the Wendland (many ending in -ow, -in or -itz) and in regional German dialect words. The language is an important source for reconstructing the history of the West Slavic languages.
