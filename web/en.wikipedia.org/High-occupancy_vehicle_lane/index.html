High-occupancy vehicle lane

A high-occupancy vehicle lane (also known as an HOV lane, carpool lane, diamond lane, 2+ lane, and transit lane) is a restricted traffic lane reserved for the exclusive use of vehicles with a driver and at least one passenger, including carpools, vanpools, and transit buses. The normal limit is "2+" — at least two persons per vehicle — although some congested urban areas use "3+" thresholds. The purpose of HOV lanes is to move more people in fewer vehicles, reducing congestion and air pollution and encouraging ridesharing.

History

The first HOV facilities in the United States were bus-only lanes, such as the 1969 Shirley Highway (I-395) busway in Virginia, opened to carpools in 1973. The 1973 oil crisis and the Clean Air Act accelerated adoption, and HOV lanes spread across North America, particularly in California, Texas, and the Seattle and Phoenix areas. HOV lanes also exist in Canada, parts of Europe, Australia, China, and elsewhere; in Indonesia, the "three-in-one" system in Jakarta famously gave rise to an informal occupation of paid passengers ("car jockeys") until it was abolished in 2016.

Operation

HOV lanes may operate full-time or only during peak hours, may be separated by barriers (with reversible flow) or merely by paint and signage, and are enforced by police and increasingly by automated occupancy-detection cameras. Violations are common and enforcement can be difficult. Some HOV lanes have been converted into high-occupancy toll (HOT) or express toll lanes, and many jurisdictions allow certain single-occupant vehicles — motorcycles, low-emission vehicles, emergency vehicles — to use them.
