Chorale

A chorale is a melody to which a hymn is sung by a congregation in a German Protestant church service. The term is most often applied to the metrical hymn tunes of the Lutheran tradition and, by extension, to four-part harmonisations of those tunes and to choral works built on them.

History

Martin Luther and his collaborators, including Johann Walter, created the first chorales in the 1520s, adapting Gregorian chant, Latin hymns, and popular songs — and composing new melodies such as "Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott" — so that congregations could sing in German. The tunes were collected in hymnals and became the backbone of Lutheran church music. Composers from Michael Praetorius and Heinrich Schütz to J. S. Bach harmonised them in four voices; Bach's 389 chorale harmonisations remain models of voice leading and staples of music theory teaching. Chorales also anchor large works: Bach's chorale cantatas and organ chorale preludes, and Mendelssohn's and others' oratorios.

Characteristics

Chorale melodies are simple, syllabic, and strophic, usually in bar form (AAB). In four-part settings the melody sits in the soprano while alto, tenor, and bass provide largely homophonic harmony punctuated by cadential fermatas at phrase ends.
