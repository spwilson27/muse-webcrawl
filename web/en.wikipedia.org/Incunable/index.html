An incunable (also incunabulum, plural incunabula) is a book, pamphlet, or broadside printed in Europe before 1501, in the earliest stages of printing with movable type. The word derives from the Latin incunabula, "swaddling clothes" or "cradle", referring to the infancy of the printed book. Incunabula are distinguished from manuscripts and from later printed books by their closeness to manuscript practice in layout and production.

Characteristics

Early printed books imitated manuscripts: they often lacked title pages, used gothic typefaces, left spaces for hand-painted initials and rubrication, and were sold unbound. Printers such as Johannes Gutenberg (whose Bible of c. 1455 is the most famous incunable), Erhard Ratdolt, Anton Koberger — publisher of the richly illustrated Nuremberg Chronicle (1493) — and Aldus Manutius in Venice gradually developed recognisably modern book features. By 1500 presses operated in some 270 European towns and had produced an estimated 27,000–30,000 editions, of which millions of copies once existed.

Types and distribution

The largest categories were religious works (Bibles, breviaries, books of hours), classical and humanist texts, and legal and scholastic works. Italy and Germany account for the majority of surviving incunabula; Latin dominates, though vernacular printing grew steadily through the period.

Study and cataloguing

Incunabula are inventoried in the Gesamtkatalog der Wiegendrucke (begun 1925) and the British Library's Incunabula Short Title Catalogue (ISTC), which records editions and their locations in libraries worldwide. Major collections are held by the British Library, the Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, the Bibliothèque nationale de France and the Library of Congress.
