Alluvium

Alluvial sediment exposed in a river bank.

Alluvium (from Latin alluvius, "to wash against") is loose clay, silt, sand or gravel that has been deposited by running water in a stream bed, on a floodplain, in an alluvial fan or in other fluvial environments. It is typically made up of a variety of materials, including fine particles of silt and clay and larger particles of sand and gravel.

Formation

When a stream's velocity decreases — because the gradient lessens, the channel widens, or discharge falls — it can no longer carry its sediment load, and material settles out. The heaviest particles are deposited first, so alluvium is often sorted, with gravels near the channel and fine silts spread across the floodplain during overbank floods.

Landforms

Accumulations of alluvium build characteristic landforms: floodplains, natural levees, alluvial fans at mountain fronts, deltas where rivers enter standing water, and point bars on the inside of meanders. The fertile soils developed on alluvium supported early agriculture in valleys such as the Nile, Tigris–Euphrates, Indus and Yellow River.

Economic significance

Alluvial deposits are important aquifers, sources of sand and gravel for construction, and hosts of placer deposits of gold, tin and diamonds concentrated by flowing water. Much of the world's population lives on alluvial plains, which are agriculturally productive but exposed to flooding.
