Lent

Lent (Latin: Quadragesima, "Fortieth") is a solemn religious observance in the Christian liturgical calendar, a season of prayer, fasting and almsgiving in preparation for Easter. In Western Christianity it begins on Ash Wednesday and ends on Holy Saturday, spanning 40 days of fasting (excluding Sundays) in imitation of the forty days Jesus spent fasting in the desert while enduring the temptation of Satan. Eastern Christians observe the corresponding season, Great Lent, beginning on Clean Monday.

Ash Wednesday marks the beginning of Lent in the West.

Observance

Traditionally, Lent required fasting to one meal a day and abstinence from meat. In the modern Roman Catholic discipline, fasting (for those aged 18–59) is obligatory on Ash Wednesday and Good Friday, and abstinence from meat on Fridays in Lent; many faithful also voluntarily "give up" a luxury for the season. Orthodox practice is stricter, abstaining from meat, dairy and eggs throughout Great Lent. Almsgiving and works of mercy are emphasised alongside abstinence, and the season culminates in Holy Week — Palm Sunday, Maundy Thursday, Good Friday — and Easter.

Traditions

The day before Lent, Shrove Tuesday (Mardi Gras), is marked by confession and, in many countries, by feasting on rich foods — pancakes in Britain, carnival celebrations elsewhere. Palms from the previous Palm Sunday are burned to provide the ashes for Ash Wednesday, imposed with the words "Remember you are dust, and to dust you shall return." During Lent, the Gloria and Alleluia are omitted from Mass, statues may be veiled, and violet vestments are worn.

History

Fasting before Easter is attested by the 2nd century and was standardised as a forty-day discipline by the 4th century, referencing Jesus's forty days in the wilderness as well as the fasts of Moses and Elijah. Practices have varied considerably across churches and centuries.
