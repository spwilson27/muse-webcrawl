Meritocracy

Meritocracy is a political and social system in which economic goods, authority and social status are assigned to individuals on the basis of merit — demonstrated talent, effort and achievement — rather than wealth, birth, class or other inherited advantages. In a meritocratic system, advancement depends on performance measured by examinations, qualifications or competition.

Origins of the term

The word was coined by the British sociologist Michael Young in his 1958 satirical dystopia The Rise of the Meritocracy, where it was deliberately pejorative: Young imagined a society stratified by intelligence and effort that had become smug, rigid and oppressive. Despite Young's intent, the term was widely adopted as a positive ideal. Earlier antecedents include the Chinese imperial examination system — selection of officials by competitive examination for over a millennium — Confucian ideals of rule by the worthy, and Napoleonic and civil-service reforms that replaced patronage with open competition.

Meritocracy as ideal

Supporters argue meritocracy promotes fairness, efficiency and social mobility: positions go to those best able to fill them, and anyone can rise through education and work. It underpins modern ideas of equal opportunity, standardised testing, open hiring and promotion on performance.

Criticisms

Critics contend that real societies never start from a level field: family wealth, schooling, social capital and inherited advantage shape measured "merit," so meritocracy can legitimate inequality and breed hubris among winners and humiliation among losers — arguments developed by Michael Sandel in The Tyranny of Merit (2020) and by Young himself, who protested the celebratory use of his term. Others note that merit is hard to define and measure, and that hereditary privilege persists within formally meritocratic systems.
