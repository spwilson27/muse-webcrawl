Women and children first

Women and children first, known in a popular variant as the Birkenhead drill, is a code of conduct whereby the lives of women and children are to be saved first in a life-threatening situation, typically during the evacuation of a ship or aircraft in an emergency. The phrase encapsulates a norm of chivalry associated particularly with nineteenth- and early twentieth-century maritime disasters.

History

The practice gained its name from the wreck of the British troopship HMS Birkenhead in 1852 off the coast of South Africa. The soldiers aboard stood in ranks on deck while the women and children were loaded into the few available boats; most of the men, including the commanding officer, perished. The episode became famous through Rudyard Kipling's poem and the phrase "the Birkenhead drill" entered the language.

Earlier examples include the wreck of the French frigate Méduse (1816), though there the abandonment of passengers was notorious rather than chivalrous. The norm was famously applied during the sinking of RMS Titanic in 1912, where officers — most strictly on the port side under First Officer William Murdoch's more permissive counterpart practices aside — loaded lifeboats with women and children first, resulting in much higher survival rates for women (about 74%) than for men (about 20%).

Modern practice

Modern maritime safety does not formally prioritize by sex: evacuation procedures aim to save all aboard, and International Maritime Organization guidance stresses orderly mustering rather than demographic ordering. Studies of historical shipwrecks, notably by Mikael Elinder and Oscar Erixson (2012), found that women generally survived at lower rates than men across most disasters, with Titanic a notable exception; the norm was most effective where captains explicitly enforced it.

In popular culture

The phrase has entered general usage as a byword for gallantry and self-sacrifice, and is frequently referenced, parodied or questioned in discussions of gender roles and emergency ethics.
