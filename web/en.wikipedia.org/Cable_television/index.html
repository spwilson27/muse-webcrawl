Cable television

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cable television is a system of delivering television programming to consumers via radio frequency signals transmitted through coaxial cables, or, in newer systems, light pulses through fibre-optic cables. It contrasts with broadcast television, received over the air by antenna, and with satellite television. Cable also commonly carries FM radio, high-speed internet and telephone services.

History

Cable began in the United States in the late 1940s as "community antenna television" (CATV), relaying distant broadcast signals to communities in mountainous or remote areas with poor reception — early systems are credited to operators in Pennsylvania, Oregon and Arkansas in 1948–49. From the 1970s, satellite distribution allowed national cable networks such as HBO (1972), ESPN (1979) and CNN (1980) to flourish, transforming television with dozens, then hundreds, of specialised channels.

Technology

Signals are gathered at a headend, combined and sent through trunk and feeder cables with amplifiers to subscriber homes, where a set-top box or cable card decodes them. Modern hybrid fibre-coaxial (HFC) networks carry digital television, video on demand and DOCSIS broadband internet on the same infrastructure.

Market changes

Since the 2010s, traditional cable subscriptions in many countries have declined — "cord-cutting" — as viewers shift to streaming services delivered over the internet, though cable companies remain major broadband providers.
