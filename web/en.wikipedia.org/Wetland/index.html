Wetland

A wetland is a distinct ecosystem that is flooded or saturated by water, either permanently (for years or decades) or seasonally (for weeks or months). Flooding results in oxygen-free (anoxic) processes prevailing, especially in the soils. The primary factor that distinguishes wetlands from terrestrial land forms or water bodies is the characteristic vegetation of aquatic plants, adapted to the unique anoxic hydric soils.

Types

Wetlands are commonly classified into marine (coastal), estuarine, riverine, lacustrine (lake-associated) and palustrine (marshy) systems. The best-known categories include marshes, dominated by herbaceous plants; swamps, dominated by woody plants; bogs, acidic peatlands fed mainly by precipitation; and fens, peatlands fed by groundwater. Mangrove forests and tidal mudflats are important coastal wetlands.

Ecology and importance

Wetlands are among the most productive ecosystems in the world, comparable to rainforests and coral reefs. They provide habitat for a disproportionate share of species: around 40% of the world's plant and animal species live or breed in wetlands. Wetlands purify water by trapping sediments and nutrients, store carbon in peat and soils, buffer floods, and protect coastlines from storm surges. They are sometimes described as "the kidneys of the landscape" and as biological "supermarkets" because of the food webs they support.

Threats and conservation

Despite their value, wetlands are disappearing three times faster than forests, largely through drainage for agriculture, urban development, and pollution. Since 1700, an estimated large fraction of the world's wetlands has been lost. The Ramsar Convention on Wetlands, signed in 1971 in Ramsar, Iran, provides the framework for international cooperation, designating Wetlands of International Importance ("Ramsar sites"). Constructed wetlands are increasingly built to treat wastewater and restore ecological function.
