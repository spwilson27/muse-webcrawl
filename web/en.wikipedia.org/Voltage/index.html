Voltage

Voltage, also known as electric potential difference, electric pressure, or electric tension, is the difference in electric potential between two points. In a static electric field, it corresponds to the work needed per unit of charge to move a positive test charge from the first point to the second point. Voltage is measured in volts (V), the SI derived unit named after Alessandro Volta, and can be measured with a voltmeter.

Formally, the voltage between points A and B is the line integral of the electric field along a path from A to B. In circuits, Kirchhoff's voltage law states that the sum of voltages around any closed loop is zero. Voltage may be produced by electrochemical cells (batteries), electromagnetic induction in generators, photovoltaic cells, and thermoelectric effects.

Analogy and practical significance

A common hydraulic analogy compares voltage to water pressure: just as pressure difference drives water through a pipe, voltage drives electric current through a conductor, with current analogous to flow rate and resistance analogous to pipe constriction. Voltages in everyday life range from about 1.5 V for a small battery and 120 or 230 V for household mains to hundreds of kilovolts on long-distance transmission lines.

High and low voltage

Electrical safety standards distinguish low voltage (generally up to 1,000 V AC) from high voltage; contact with sufficiently high voltages can drive dangerous currents through the body, while high voltages can also cause arcing through air.
