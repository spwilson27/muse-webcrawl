Yukio Mishima (14 January 1925 – 25 November 1970), born Kimitake Hiraoka, was a Japanese author, playwright, actor, and film director, regarded as one of the most important Japanese writers of the 20th century. His prolific output — some 34 novels, about 50 plays, and numerous short stories and essays — fused modern and traditional aesthetics and explored themes of beauty, death, eroticism, and Japan's cultural transformation after the Second World War. He was considered for the Nobel Prize in Literature several times.

Life and works

Raised largely by his grandmother in Tokyo, Mishima published his first major work while still a schoolboy. After graduating from the University of Tokyo he worked briefly in the Ministry of Finance before devoting himself to writing. His breakthrough came with the semi-autobiographical novel Confessions of a Mask (1949). Major works include The Temple of the Golden Pavilion (1956), inspired by the burning of Kinkaku-ji by a novice monk; The Sailor Who Fell from Grace with the Sea (1963); the patriotic story "Patriotism" (1961), which he also filmed; and his masterpiece, the tetralogy The Sea of Fertility (1965–1971), completed on the day of his death. He also revived classical Japanese theatre forms and wrote modern Noh plays, while his disciplined bodybuilding and martial-arts training reflected his ideal of uniting art and action.

Political activism and death

Increasingly alarmed by what he saw as Japan's postwar materialism and the erosion of the emperor-centred tradition, Mishima founded in 1968 a small private militia, the Tatenokai (Shield Society). On 25 November 1970, Mishima and four followers seized the headquarters of the Japan Self-Defense Forces at Ichigaya in Tokyo; from a balcony he delivered a speech urging the assembled soldiers to rise up and restore imperial rule. Jeered by the crowd, he returned inside and committed seppuku (ritual suicide). The act shocked Japan and the world and remains one of the most debated episodes in modern Japanese cultural history.

Legacy

Mishima's works are widely translated, and a major literary prize, the Mishima Yukio Prize, bears his name. His life and death have been treated in numerous biographies, and Paul Schrader's film Mishima: A Life in Four Chapters (1985) dramatised his career. Critics continue to debate the relationship between his aesthetic achievement and his ultranationalist politics.
