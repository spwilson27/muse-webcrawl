Azeotrope

An azeotrope or a constant heating point mixture is a mixture of two or more components in fluidic states whose proportions cannot be altered or changed by simple distillation. This happens because when an azeotrope is boiled, the vapour has the same proportions of constituents as the liquid mixture. Because their composition is unchanged by distillation, azeotropes are also called constant boiling point mixtures.

Types

Azeotropes can be classified as positive or negative depending on how their boiling point compares to that of their components. A positive azeotrope boils at a lower temperature than any of its constituents; the classic example is the ethanol–water mixture, which at 95.63% ethanol by mass boils at 78.2 °C, below the boiling point of pure ethanol. A negative azeotrope boils at a higher temperature than any of its constituents, an example being hydrochloric acid (20.2% HCl in water). Azeotropes may also be homogeneous, with components fully miscible, or heterogeneous, where they are not.

Separation

Because simple fractional distillation cannot separate an azeotrope beyond its azeotropic composition, industrial practice uses techniques such as azeotropic distillation (adding an entrainer), extractive distillation, pressure-swing distillation, or membrane separation. Absolute ethanol, for example, is commonly produced from the ethanol–water azeotrope using molecular sieves or entrainers.
