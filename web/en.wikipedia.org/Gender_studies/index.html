Gender studies

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gender studies is an interdisciplinary academic field devoted to analysing gender identity and gendered representation. The field includes women's studies (concerning women, feminism, gender, and politics), men's studies and queer studies. Its rise to prominence, especially in Western universities, came after 1990 as a unification of several stand-alone disciplines: initially the study of categories of sexuality, then the study of categories of sexual differences, and finally the study of gender itself as distinct from biological sex.

History

The field grew out of women's studies programmes founded from around 1970, themselves rooted in the women's liberation movement and in scholarship recovering women's contributions to history, literature and science. Scholars such as Simone de Beauvoir (The Second Sex, 1949) were foundational. From the 1990s, gender studies broadened to study masculinity, sexuality, and the social construction of gender categories, drawing on poststructuralism, psychoanalysis, sociology and postcolonial studies. Judith Butler's Gender Trouble (1990) argued that gender is performative — constituted by repeated acts and norms rather than expressing an inner essence.

Scope and methods

Gender studies uses the methods of literary analysis, history, anthropology, sociology, political science, media studies and law. Central concepts include the sex/gender distinction, patriarchy, intersectionality (the interaction of gender with race, class and other axes of inequality), representation, and the social construction of categories. The field informs policy debates over equal pay, reproductive rights, violence, education and media representation.

Criticism and debate

Gender studies has been criticised from several directions: by some scientists for downplaying biology; by political movements that view it as ideological; and internally by scholars who debate its theoretical foundations. In some countries governments have restricted or abolished gender studies programmes, while in others it is an established part of the humanities and social sciences curriculum.
