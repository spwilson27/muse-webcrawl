Charles Scott Sherrington

Sir Charles Scott Sherrington
Born: 27 November 1857, London
Died: 4 March 1952, Eastbourne
Known for: The synapse; integrative action of the nervous system
Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine, 1932

Sir Charles Scott Sherrington (1857–1952) was an English neurophysiologist whose work laid the foundations of modern neuroscience. He coined the term "synapse" for the junction between neurons and described how the nervous system integrates reflexes into coordinated behaviour.

Work

Educated at Cambridge and St Thomas' Hospital, Sherrington held chairs at Liverpool and Oxford. Studying spinal reflexes in animals, he mapped the motor areas of the cortex, described reciprocal innervation (when one muscle contracts, its antagonist relaxes), and developed the concept of the "final common path" of motor neurons. His 1906 book The Integrative Action of the Nervous System is a classic of physiology. He also introduced terms such as proprioception and the nociceptor.

Recognition

Sherrington shared the 1932 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Edgar Adrian "for their discoveries regarding the functions of neurons". He was president of the Royal Society (1920–1925) and received the Order of Merit. His later book Man on His Nature (1940) reflected on mind and brain. Among his students were John Eccles and Wilder Penfield.
