Monsieur de Sainte-Colombe (c. 1640 – c. 1700) was a French composer and violist, the outstanding master of the seven-string bass viol in the France of Louis XIV and the teacher of Marin Marais. Remarkably little is known of his life — even his first name was long uncertain (modern research has proposed "Jean", possibly Jean de Sainte-Colombe) — and he is remembered largely through his music and through the accounts of his pupils.

Life and legend

According to the violist Jean Rousseau and the writer Titon du Tillet, Sainte-Colombe lived reclusively in Paris, practising in a small wooden cabin built in a mulberry tree so as not to disturb the household, and giving intimate concerts of his works for two viols with his daughters. He is credited with adding a seventh (low A) string to the bass viol and with introducing silver-wound bass strings to France. Marais, his most famous pupil, commemorated him in the Tombeau pour Monsieur de Sainte-Colombe (1701). The composer's life inspired Pascal Quignard's 1991 novel Tous les matins du monde and its film adaptation, in which the music of Sainte-Colombe and Marais reached a wide modern audience.

Works

Surviving works include 67 Concerts à deux violes esgales — duets for two equal bass viols, preserved in a manuscript at the Bibliothèque nationale — and some 170 pieces for solo viol preserved in manuscript at Edinburgh, many bearing evocative titles. His music is notable for its intimate, exploratory harmony and the virtuoso demands of its chords and ornaments.
