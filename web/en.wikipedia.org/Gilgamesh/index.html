Gilgamesh

Gilgamesh (Akkadian: Gilgameš; originally Sumerian: Bilgames) was a hero of ancient Mesopotamian mythology and the protagonist of the Epic of Gilgamesh, an epic poem written in Akkadian during the late second millennium BCE. He is traditionally held to have been a historical king of the Sumerian city-state of Uruk in the early third millennium BCE, later deified and celebrated in legend as two-thirds divine and one-third human.

The Epic

In the epic, Gilgamesh, the mighty but arrogant king of Uruk, befriends the wild man Enkidu, created by the gods to temper him. Together they defeat the monster Humbaba in the Cedar Forest and slay the Bull of Heaven sent by the goddess Ishtar, whom Gilgamesh had spurned. When Enkidu dies, the grief-stricken Gilgamesh undertakes a perilous quest for immortality, seeking Utnapishtim, survivor of the Great Flood. He ultimately fails, returning to Uruk reconciled to mortality and to the enduring fame of his works — above all the city's walls.

Historical background

The Sumerian King List places Gilgamesh as the fifth king of the First Dynasty of Uruk, and inscriptions and later tradition credit him with rebuilding the city's temples and walls. Individual Sumerian poems about Bilgames circulated from around 2100 BCE; these were woven into the Akkadian epic, whose most complete version survives on twelve clay tablets from the library of Ashurbanipal at Nineveh, rediscovered in the 19th century.

Legacy

The epic's flood narrative closely parallels the story of Noah, and Gilgamesh remains a touchstone of world literature — often described as the earliest surviving great work of literature. His figure has inspired novels, operas, poems and popular culture into the present day.
