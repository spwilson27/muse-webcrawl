A mitochondrion (plural mitochondria) is a membrane-bound organelle found in the cytoplasm of almost all eukaryotic cells. Often described as the "powerhouse of the cell," mitochondria generate most of the cell's supply of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), the molecule that carries chemical energy within cells. They are unusual among organelles in having their own small genome and a double membrane, features explained by their origin as free-living bacteria engulfed by an ancestral eukaryotic cell.

Structure

A mitochondrion is bounded by two membranes. The smooth outer membrane encloses the organelle; the inner membrane is extensively folded into shelf-like or tubular invaginations called cristae, greatly increasing its surface area. The space enclosed by the inner membrane is the matrix, which contains enzymes of the citric acid cycle, mitochondrial ribosomes, and the mitochondrial DNA — in humans a circular molecule of 16,569 base pairs encoding 37 genes. The compartment between the two membranes is the intermembrane space.

Energy conversion

Mitochondria carry out the later stages of cellular respiration. Pyruvate from glycolysis is imported and oxidised; the citric acid cycle in the matrix produces the electron carriers NADH and FADH2. The electron transport chain, embedded in the inner membrane, uses these carriers to pump protons into the intermembrane space, creating an electrochemical gradient (chemiosmosis). Protons flowing back through ATP synthase drive the production of ATP from ADP and inorganic phosphate — the process of oxidative phosphorylation, which yields far more ATP per glucose molecule than glycolysis alone.

Origin

The endosymbiotic theory, championed by Lynn Margulis, proposes that mitochondria descend from an alphaproteobacterium taken up by an early eukaryotic cell roughly 1.5–2 billion years ago. Supporting evidence includes their double membrane, bacterial-style circular genome, bacterial-type ribosomes, and reproduction by binary fission. Over evolutionary time most of the endosymbiont's genes were transferred to the host nucleus, so most mitochondrial proteins are now encoded in nuclear DNA and imported.

Other functions and disease

Beyond ATP, mitochondria participate in calcium storage, haem and iron–sulfur cluster synthesis, steroid production, and programmed cell death (apoptosis) via release of cytochrome c. Because mtDNA mutates faster than nuclear DNA and is usually inherited only from the mother, it is widely used in genealogy and evolutionary studies. Mutations in mitochondrial genes cause a class of maternally inherited diseases, including MELAS and Leber's hereditary optic neuropathy, typically affecting energy-hungry tissues such as muscle, brain, and retina.
