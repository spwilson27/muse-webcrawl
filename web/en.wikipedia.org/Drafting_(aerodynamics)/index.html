Drafting (aerodynamics)

Cyclists drafting in a paceline to reduce wind resistance

Drafting or slipstreaming is an aerodynamics technique where two moving objects align in a close group reducing the overall effect of drag due to exploiting the lead object's slipstream. It is used by drivers and cyclists to reduce the effort needed to maintain speed, and is observed in nature in formations of migrating birds and fish.

Physics

A moving body leaves behind a region of lower-pressure, turbulent air — the wake or slipstream. A following object entering this region experiences reduced aerodynamic drag: the front of the follower faces lower dynamic pressure, and in close vehicle pairs the lead vehicle also benefits because the follower fills its low-pressure trailing zone, reducing its wake drag too. In cycling, riding in a peloton can reduce a rider's drag by 30–40% or more in the middle of a large group.

Applications

Cycling and running

Cyclists ride in pacelines, rotating the lead position, and teams use drafting strategically in road racing. Distance runners also draft behind pacemakers; Eliud Kipchoge's sub-two-hour marathon in 2019 used rotating pacemakers partly as a wind shield. Drafting is restricted in non-draft triathlons, where a minimum following distance is enforced.

Motorsport

In NASCAR and IndyCar racing, cars draft nose-to-tail, and a following car can use the slipstream to build momentum for a "slingshot" pass. Two cars drafting together can run faster than either alone. In Formula One, the "tow" down straights is an important qualifying and overtaking consideration, though turbulent air harms the following car's downforce in corners.

Other uses

Truck platooning trials exploit drafting to save fuel on highways. Migrating geese fly in a V formation, each bird gaining lift from the wingtip upwash of the bird ahead, and drafting also occurs in speed skating, cross-country skiing, swimming and speedcubing of pelotons of animals such as ducklings following their mother.
