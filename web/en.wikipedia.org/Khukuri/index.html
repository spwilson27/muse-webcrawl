Khukuri

The khukuri (Nepali: खुकुरी), also spelled kukri, is a knife with a pronounced forward-curving blade originating from the Indian subcontinent and strongly associated with Nepal and the Gurkha regiments of the British, Indian and Nepalese armies. It serves both as a fighting knife and as an everyday utility tool for chopping wood, clearing brush and preparing food.

Design

The blade, typically 40–46 cm in overall length for military patterns, curves inward with the cutting edge on the concave side, concentrating force at the point of impact. A distinctive notch, the kaudi or cho, sits near the handle; interpretations of its purpose range from the practical (stopping blood or sap running onto the grip) to the symbolic. The sheath traditionally carries two small tools: the karda, a small utility knife, and the chakmak, an unsharpened blade for honing the edge and striking sparks.

History and use

Curved knives of similar form date back centuries in the hills of Nepal. The khukuri gained international fame through the Gurkhas, whose regiments were raised by the British East India Company after the Anglo-Nepalese War of 1814–16. It remains the Gurkha regimental sidearm and an emblem of Nepal, appearing on the country's former coat of arms. Decorative and ceremonial versions are given as gifts and awards.
