Wu experiment

Schematic of the apparatus used in the Wu experiment

The Wu experiment was a nuclear physics experiment conducted in 1956 by the Chinese-American physicist Chien-Shiung Wu in collaboration with the Low Temperature Group of the US National Bureau of Standards (NBS). The experiment's purpose was to establish whether or not conservation of parity (P-conservation), which was previously established in the electromagnetic and strong interactions, also applied to weak interactions. If P-conservation were true, a mirrored version of the world (where left is right and right is left) would behave as the mirror image of the current world. If P-conservation were violated, however, it would be possible to distinguish between a mirrored version of the world and the mirror image of the current world.

The experiment established that conservation of parity was violated (P-violation) by the weak interaction, providing a way to operationally define left and right without reference to the human body. This result was not expected by the physics community, which had previously regarded parity as a symmetry applying to all forces of nature. Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen-Ning Yang, the theoretical physicists who originated the idea of parity nonconservation and proposed the experiment, received the 1957 Nobel Prize in Physics for this result. While the Nobel Prize was not awarded to Chien-Shiung Wu, her role was mentioned in the Nobel Prize acceptance speech of Yang and Lee.

History

In 1927, Eugene Wigner formalized the principle of the conservation of parity by stating that the physical world should not depend on its mirror image. If a law of physics is violated under a parody transformation, this principle would be false. Weak interaction theorists were unaware for several decades that parity violation was possible in weak interactions, although electromagnetic and strong interactions appeared to respect the law. In 1956, Tsung-Dao Lee and Chen-Ning Yang reviewed existing experimental evidence and found that while parity conservation had been verified in decay reactions involving the strong or electromagnetic interactions, it had not been tested in the weak interaction. They proposed several possible direct experimental tests. The first was based on beta decay of polarized cobalt-60 nuclei, also suggested by Wu.

Experiment

The experiment monitored the decay of cobalt-60 (60Co) atoms aligned by a uniform magnetic field (the polarizing field) and cooled to near absolute zero so that thermal motions did not destroy the alignment. Cobalt-60 is an unstable isotope of cobalt that decays by beta decay to the stable isotope nickel-60 (60Ni). During this decay, one of the neutrons in the cobalt-60 nucleus changes to a proton, emitting an electron and an electron antineutrino. The resulting nickel nucleus, however, is in an excited state and promptly decays to its ground state by emitting two gamma rays.

The gamma rays were observed in the equatorial and polar direction and compared, in order to measure the polarization of the cobalt nuclei. The electrons, on the other hand, were observed in the polar directions only, comparing the emission parallel and antiparallel to the polarizing field. If the electrons were emitted in a preferred direction, this would suggest that parity was violated in beta decay. Wu found that the electrons were emitted preferentially in the direction opposite to that of the nuclear spin and the applied magnetic field, demonstrating that parity is violated in the weak interaction.

Impact and influence

The results surprised the physics community. Some researchers then set out to reproduce Wu's results, while others reacted with disbelief. Wolfgang Pauli, upon hearing the results of the Wu experiment from his assistant, wrote a letter to Victor Weisskopf stating that it was not the first shock after his discovery of the nonconservation of parity. Subsequent experiments confirmed Wu's result, and parity violation became a cornerstone of the Standard Model of particle physics.
