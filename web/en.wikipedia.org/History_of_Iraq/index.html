History of Iraq

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The history of Iraq encompasses the history of Mesopotamia—"the land between the rivers" Tigris and Euphrates—one of the cradles of civilisation, and of the modern Republic of Iraq, created from former Ottoman provinces after the First World War. Modern Iraq's history includes the British Mandate, the Hashemite monarchy, the republican era after 1958, the Ba'athist regime of Saddam Hussein, the Iran–Iraq War, the Gulf War and sanctions, the 2003 US-led invasion and its aftermath, and the war against the Islamic State.

Ancient and medieval periods

Mesopotamia saw the rise of Sumer, Akkad, Babylon, and Assyria, the invention of writing (cuneiform), the Code of Hammurabi, and successive empires culminating in the Neo-Babylonian Empire before Persian, Greek, Parthian, and Sasanian rule. The Arab Muslim conquest of the 630s–640s incorporated the region into the caliphate; Baghdad, founded in 762 by the Abbasid caliph al-Mansur, became one of the world's largest and most learned cities during the Islamic Golden Age, until its destruction by the Mongols under Hulagu in 1258. Iraq subsequently passed between Persian and Ottoman hands, becoming a frontier province of the Ottoman Empire from the 16th century.

The Mandate and the monarchy

British forces occupied Baghdad in 1917 during the First World War. In 1920 Britain received a League of Nations Mandate for Mesopotamia, combining the Ottoman vilayets of Basra, Baghdad, and (later) Mosul; a major revolt that year was suppressed with difficulty. Faisal ibn Husayn was installed as King Faisal I in 1921, and Iraq formally became independent in 1932 while remaining closely tied to Britain by treaty. The discovery of oil near Kirkuk in 1927 made Iraq a significant producer. The monarchy of Faisal II and the regent Abd al-Ilah, dominated politically by Nuri al-Said, pursued pro-Western policies (including the 1955 Baghdad Pact) while facing rising Arab nationalist and communist opposition.

The republic and the Ba'ath

On 14 July 1958 the Hashemite monarchy was overthrown in a coup by officers under Abd al-Karim Qasim; the king and royal family were killed. Qasim's republic withdrew from the Baghdad Pact and enacted land reform but was overthrown in February 1963 by a Ba'ath Party–military coup. After a brief first Ba'ath government and further coups, the Ba'ath seized power definitively on 17 July 1968 under Ahmed Hassan al-Bakr, with Saddam Hussein rising as the regime's strongman. Oil nationalisation (1972) and high oil prices funded rapid development, while the regime violently repressed communists, Kurds, and Shia movements. Saddam formally assumed the presidency in 1979 and immediately purged the party leadership.

Wars under Saddam Hussein

In September 1980 Iraq invaded revolutionary Iran, beginning the Iran–Iraq War, an eight-year conflict of trench warfare, missile attacks on cities, and chemical weapons use that killed hundreds of thousands and ended in stalemate in 1988. That year the regime's Anfal campaign and the chemical attack on Halabja killed thousands of Kurds. In August 1990 Iraq invaded and annexed Kuwait, provoking the Gulf War: a US-led coalition expelled Iraqi forces in early 1991, and uprisings by Shia in the south and Kurds in the north were brutally suppressed under the eyes of coalition forces, though a Kurdish autonomous zone emerged in the north under a no-fly zone. Punishing UN sanctions through the 1990s devastated the civilian economy while Saddam retained power.

2003 invasion and aftermath

On 20 March 2003 a US-led coalition invaded Iraq, justifying the war principally by claims—later shown to be unfounded—that Iraq possessed weapons of mass destruction and had links to al-Qaeda. Baghdad fell within weeks; Saddam was captured in December 2003, tried by an Iraqi tribunal, and executed in 2006. The occupation authority's decisions to disband the army and purge Ba'athists fed a growing insurgency, and by 2006–07 Iraq descended into sectarian civil war between Sunni and Shia militias. The US "surge" of 2007, together with the Sunni Awakening, reduced violence, and US combat forces withdrew by the end of 2011 under a status-of-forces agreement.

The collapse of Iraqi forces before the Islamic State (ISIS) offensive of 2014, which seized Mosul and much of the north and west and perpetrated genocide against the Yazidis, drew a new international intervention; Iraqi forces, Kurdish Peshmerga, and Shia Popular Mobilisation Forces, backed by coalition airpower, retook all ISIS territory by 2017. Since then Iraq has struggled with corruption, militia power, Iranian and American rivalry—including the US killing of Iranian general Qasem Soleimani in Baghdad in 2020—and mass protest movements, notably the 2019 Tishreen protests demanding systemic reform.
