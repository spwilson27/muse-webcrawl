Engel curve

In microeconomics, an Engel curve describes how a household's expenditure on a particular good or service varies with the household's income, holding prices and preferences constant. The curve is named after the German statistician Ernst Engel (1821–1896), whose pioneering work on budget studies led to Engel's law: the observation that as income rises, the proportion of income spent on food falls, even though absolute food expenditure may increase.

Shapes of Engel curves

For a normal good, the Engel curve slopes upward — demand rises with income. For a necessity, demand rises less than proportionally (income elasticity below one), so the curve flattens; for a luxury good, demand rises more than proportionally (elasticity above one) and the curve steepens. For an inferior good the curve bends backward: beyond some income level, consumers substitute away from it and demand falls.

Engel's law and its applications

Engel's law, derived from Engel's 1857 study of Belgian working-class budgets, is among the earliest and most robust empirical regularities in economics. It underlies the use of food budget shares as a welfare indicator, informs demand projections as economies grow (with implications for agricultural policy), and appears in the aggregation conditions of demand theory, which restrict how Engel curves of individual goods can behave jointly (the Engel aggregation condition).
