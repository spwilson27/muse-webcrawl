Sati (practice)

Sati (also spelled suttee) was a historical practice in parts of India in which a widow sacrificed herself by sitting atop her deceased husband's funeral pyre. The term derives from the Sanskrit word satī, meaning a good and faithful wife, and is connected with the goddess Sati in Hindu mythology.

An 18th-century depiction of a widow's self-immolation

History

References to widow-burning appear in ancient texts and inscriptions, including memorial stones known as sati stones. The practice was most prevalent among some Rajput and other upper-caste communities, and was never universal in India. Greek accounts of Alexander the Great's campaigns mention a form of the rite, and later European travellers described it during the Mughal period, when emperors such as Akbar attempted to regulate or discourage it.

Abolition

Social reformers including Raja Ram Mohan Roy campaigned against sati in the early 19th century. The Bengal Sati Regulation of 1829, enacted under Governor-General Lord William Bentinck, banned the practice in British India, and similar bans followed in princely states. After independence, the Commission of Sati (Prevention) Act of 1987 criminalised sati and its glorification, following the widely publicised death of Roop Kanwar in Rajasthan that year.

Debate

Scholars continue to debate the origins of sati, the extent of coercion involved, and the role of colonial-era accounts in shaping modern understandings of the practice.
