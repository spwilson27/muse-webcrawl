Wood-burning stove

A wood-burning stove (or wood burner) is a heating appliance capable of burning wood fuel and wood-derived biomass fuel, such as sawdust bricks. Generally the appliance consists of a solid metal closed firebox, often lined with fire brick, and one or more air controls. The first wood stoves were made in the 16th century, and Benjamin Franklin's "Franklin stove" of 1742 popularised an improved design in North America.

Design and operation

Unlike an open fireplace, which loses much of its heat up the chimney, a stove radiates heat from its metal body and can achieve efficiencies of 60–80%. Modern stoves use either catalytic combustors or secondary air tubes that reburn smoke particles and gases, greatly reducing emissions. A flue carries exhaust gases away, and correct sizing of stove and chimney is essential for safe operation and to limit creosote build-up, which can cause chimney fires.

Environmental and health issues

Wood smoke contains fine particulate matter (PM2.5), carbon monoxide and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons. Old stoves and open burning are significant sources of winter air pollution in many towns, prompting emission standards such as the United States EPA limits and the European Ecodesign rules, as well as local smoke-control areas. Properly seasoned wood with low moisture content burns hotter and cleaner.
