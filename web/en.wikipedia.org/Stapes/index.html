The stapes (Latin for "stirrup") is a bone in the middle ear of humans and other animals that is involved in the conduction of sound vibrations to the inner ear. It is the smallest and lightest bone in the human body, measuring roughly 3 mm × 2.5 mm, and is one of the three auditory ossicles, along with the malleus (hammer) and the incus (anvil).

Structure

The stapes is stirrup-shaped, with a head that articulates with the incus, two crura (anterior and posterior), and a flat base (footplate) that sits in the oval window of the cochlea. The stapedius muscle, the smallest skeletal muscle in the human body, attaches to the neck of the stapes and dampens its movement in response to loud sounds, forming part of the acoustic reflex.

Function

The ossicles amplify and transmit vibrations from the tympanic membrane (eardrum) to the fluid of the inner ear. The footplate of the stapes pushes in and out of the oval window, setting up pressure waves in the perilymph of the cochlea. The ratio of the eardrum area to the footplate area, together with the lever action of the ossicles, provides an impedance-matching gain of about 22 times, overcoming the loss that would occur if airborne sound struck the cochlear fluid directly.

Clinical significance

In otosclerosis, abnormal bone growth fixes the stapes footplate to the oval window, causing conductive hearing loss; it may be treated surgically by stapedectomy or stapedotomy, replacing the stapes with a prosthesis. The stapes develops from the second pharyngeal arch and was first described by Giovanni Filippo Ingrassia in 1546.
