Enumerated powers

The enumerated powers (also called delegated powers) of the United States Congress are the powers granted to the federal government that are specifically listed in the Constitution. They are set out principally in Article I, Section 8, which authorizes Congress among other things to levy taxes, borrow money, regulate commerce with foreign nations and among the several states, coin money, establish post offices, declare war, and raise and support armies and a navy.

Implied powers

The final clause of Article I, Section 8 — the Necessary and Proper Clause — empowers Congress "to make all Laws which shall be necessary and proper for carrying into Execution the foregoing Powers". In McCulloch v. Maryland (1819), Chief Justice John Marshall read this clause broadly, holding that Congress may choose any means not prohibited by the Constitution to execute its enumerated ends, giving rise to the doctrine of implied powers.

Reserved powers

The Tenth Amendment provides that powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the states, are reserved to the states respectively, or to the people. The balance between enumerated federal power and reserved state power has been a central question of American constitutional law, fought over in debates about the Commerce Clause from the New Deal era to the present day.
