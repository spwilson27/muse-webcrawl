Cofactor (biochemistry)

A cofactor is a non-protein chemical compound or metallic ion that is required for an enzyme's activity as a catalyst. Cofactors can be considered "helper molecules" that assist in biochemical transformations. An enzyme without its cofactor is called an apoenzyme; the complete, active complex is a holoenzyme.

Classification

Cofactors divide into inorganic ions — such as Mg2+, Zn2+, Fe2+/3+, and Mn2+ — and organic compounds called coenzymes, many of which derive from vitamins (for example NAD+ from niacin, FAD from riboflavin, and coenzyme A from pantothenate). A cofactor that binds tightly or covalently to its enzyme is termed a prosthetic group, as with the heme group of cytochromes.

Function

Cofactors participate directly in catalysis: metal ions can stabilize charges, accept or donate electrons, or polarize substrate bonds, while coenzymes shuttle chemical groups — hydride ions, acyl groups, methyl groups, electrons — between enzymes in metabolic pathways. Because coenzymes are chemically altered during reactions, they must be regenerated by other enzymes, often using ATP.

Examples

Well-known cofactors include ATP, NADH, FAD, coenzyme A, biotin, thiamine pyrophosphate, pyridoxal phosphate, and metal clusters such as iron–sulfur clusters in respiratory chain complexes. Many drugs and toxins act by interfering with cofactor binding or synthesis.
