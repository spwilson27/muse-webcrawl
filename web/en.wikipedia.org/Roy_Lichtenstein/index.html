Roy Lichtenstein

Roy Fox Lichtenstein (October 27, 1923 – September 29, 1997) was an American pop artist. During the 1960s, along with Andy Warhol, Jasper Johns, and James Rosenquist, he became a leading figure in the new art movement. His work defined the premise of pop art through parody: inspired by comic strips and advertising, he produced large-scale paintings using Ben Day dots, bold black outlines, and primary colours, including his most famous works Whaam! (1963) and Drowning Girl (1963).

Early life and education

Lichtenstein was born in New York City and studied art at Ohio State University, interrupted by service in the US Army in Europe during the Second World War (1943–1946). He completed his master's degree in 1949 and taught at Ohio State and later at Rutgers University, where he met Allan Kaprow and came into contact with happenings and Fluxus circles. His early work moved between Cubism, Expressionism, and Abstract Expressionism, often with humorous takes on American history and the Old West.

Pop art breakthrough

In 1961, prompted in part by his children's challenge that he could not paint as well as the pictures in their comic books, Lichtenstein began making paintings based on comic strips and product packaging, beginning with Look Mickey. His first solo exhibition at the Leo Castelli Gallery in 1962 sold out. Works such as Whaam!, based on a panel from All-American Men of War, and Drowning Girl, adapted from a romance comic, established his signature style: mechanically reproduced imagery rendered by hand at monumental scale. His use of appropriated imagery provoked debates about originality and criticism from the original comic artists, whose work he adapted without credit.

Later work

From the mid-1960s Lichtenstein extended his method to art history, producing comic-style versions of works by Picasso, Matisse, Mondrian, and Monet, as well as landscapes, seascapes, mirrors, brushstroke paintings, and large-scale sculpture, including public works in cities worldwide. His Brushstrokes series parodied the gestural marks of Abstract Expressionism. In the 1990s he painted a series of nudes and Chinese-style landscapes. He died of pneumonia in New York in 1997.

Legacy

Lichtenstein's works are among the most valuable of the pop era; Masterpiece (1962) sold for $165 million in 2017. A major retrospective was organised by the Art Institute of Chicago and Tate Modern in 2012–2013.
