Hannah Arendt

Hannah Arendt (1906–1975) was a German-American historian and philosopher, one of the most influential political theorists of the 20th century.

Hannah Arendt (born Johanna Arendt, 14 October 1906 – 4 December 1975) was a German-American historian and philosopher. She was one of the most influential political theorists of the twentieth century. Her works cover a broad range of topics, but she is best known for those dealing with the nature of power, authority, totalitarianism, and evil.

Born in Linden, Hanover, to a secular Jewish family, Arendt studied philosophy at the University of Marburg under Martin Heidegger, with whom she had a brief affair, and completed her doctorate at Heidelberg under Karl Jaspers in 1929, on the concept of love in Saint Augustine. After the Nazi seizure of power in 1933 she was briefly arrested by the Gestapo, fled to Paris, and worked for Jewish refugee organisations. Interned at Gurs in 1940, she escaped and emigrated to the United States in 1941, becoming a US citizen in 1950. She taught at institutions including the University of Chicago and the New School for Social Research.

Major works

The Origins of Totalitarianism (1951) analysed antisemitism, imperialism, and the rise of Nazi and Stalinist totalitarianism as novel forms of government based on terror and ideology rather than mere tyranny. In The Human Condition (1958) she distinguished the activities of labor, work, and action, arguing that the modern age had elevated labor at the expense of political action — the disclosure of the self in speech and deed before others in a public realm. On Revolution (1963) contrasted the American and French revolutions, and her essays collected in Between Past and Future explored authority, freedom, and education.

Eichmann in Jerusalem and the "banality of evil"

Arendt reported on the 1961 trial of Adolf Eichmann in Jerusalem for The New Yorker; the book Eichmann in Jerusalem: A Report on the Banality of Evil (1963) portrayed Eichmann not as a fanatical monster but as a thoughtless bureaucrat who participated in genocide through obedience, careerism, and an inability to think from the standpoint of others. The phrase "the banality of evil" became famous, while her criticisms of the trial's conduct, of the role of Jewish councils (Judenräte), and of Israeli leadership provoked intense controversy that lasted for decades. Her final work, The Life of the Mind, on thinking, willing, and judging, was left unfinished at her death in New York in 1975.

Legacy

Arendt resisted the label "philosopher", preferring "political theorist". Her concepts — totalitarianism, the banality of evil, natality, the public realm, and the "right to have rights" — remain central to political science, philosophy, and debates about refugees, statelessness, and democratic participation.
