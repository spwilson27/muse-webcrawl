Reuptake

Reuptake is the process by which a neurotransmitter released into a synaptic cleft is transported back into the presynaptic neuron or into a neighbouring glial cell. It terminates or moderates synaptic signalling, recycles transmitter molecules, and regulates the concentration and duration of neurotransmission.

Mechanism

Plasma-membrane transporters use ion gradients, especially the sodium gradient, to move neurotransmitters against their concentration gradients. Examples include the serotonin transporter (SERT), dopamine transporter (DAT), norepinephrine transporter (NET), and glutamate transporters on neurons and astrocytes. After uptake, a transmitter may be repackaged into synaptic vesicles by a vesicular transporter or broken down by enzymes such as monoamine oxidase.

Pharmacology

Reuptake inhibitors prolong neurotransmitter action. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors are widely used antidepressants and anxiolytics; serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors also affect noradrenergic signalling. Cocaine blocks dopamine, norepinephrine, and serotonin transporters, especially DAT. Other medicines target reuptake for attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, pain, epilepsy, and anxiety.

Physiological importance

Rapid glutamate uptake prevents excessive excitation and excitotoxicity. Transporter reversal can occur when ionic gradients collapse during energy failure, potentially increasing extracellular transmitter. Genetic variation and disease can alter transporter expression, but psychiatric and neurological disorders cannot generally be reduced to a simple excess or deficiency of one neurotransmitter.
