A sovereign wealth fund (SWF), or sovereign investment fund, is a state-owned investment fund that invests in financial and real assets such as stocks, bonds, real estate, infrastructure and private equity. Sovereign wealth funds are funded from a country's surplus reserves — most commonly revenues from commodity exports such as oil and gas, or from foreign-exchange reserves accumulated through trade surpluses.

History

The term was coined in 2005, but the institutions are older: the Kuwait Investment Authority, founded in 1953 to invest surplus oil revenues, is usually regarded as the first. Singapore's Temasek (1974) and GIC (1981), Abu Dhabi's ADIA (1976) and Norway's Government Pension Fund Global (1990) followed. Assets ballooned in the 2000s as oil prices and Asian trade surpluses rose, and the funds became major buyers during the 2007–08 financial crisis, when they injected capital into Western banks.

Purposes

  • Stabilisation funds smooth government budgets against commodity price swings.
  • Savings (future generations) funds convert exhaustible resources into financial assets for posterity, as in Norway.
  • Development funds finance domestic infrastructure and industrial policy; Temasek holds stakes across Singapore's economy.
  • Reserve investment funds manage official reserves beyond central-bank liquidity needs.

Governance and debate

Because the funds are instruments of states, their growth prompted concerns about strategic investment, transparency and political influence. In 2008, funds and recipient countries agreed the Santiago Principles, a voluntary code of governance and disclosure administered by the International Forum of Sovereign Wealth Funds. Norway's fund is frequently cited as a transparency benchmark; it excludes companies on ethical grounds under guidelines set by its Council on Ethics. Critics continue to debate the funds' geopolitical role and the treatment of assets in sanctions regimes, such as the freezing of Russian sovereign assets after 2022.
