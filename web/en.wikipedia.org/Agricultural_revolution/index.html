Agricultural revolution

Agricultural revolution may refer to several distinct transformations in farming practice. Most commonly the term denotes the Neolithic Revolution, the transition of human societies from hunting and gathering to settled agriculture beginning around 10,000 BCE, and the British Agricultural Revolution of the 17th to 19th centuries, which dramatically raised crop yields in Britain. A third usage covers the Green Revolution of the mid-20th century, when high-yielding crop varieties, synthetic fertilizers, and pesticides spread through the developing world.

Neolithic Revolution

The Neolithic transition began independently in several regions, including the Fertile Crescent, China, Mesoamerica, and New Guinea. Domestication of wheat, barley, rice, maize, cattle, sheep, and pigs allowed permanent settlements, population growth, and eventually cities and states. The shift also brought new diseases, social hierarchy, and heavier labor.

British Agricultural Revolution

Between roughly 1650 and 1850, British output rose through the Norfolk four-course rotation (wheat, turnips, barley, clover), enclosure of common fields, selective livestock breeding, and new implements such as Jethro Tull's seed drill. Rising food production supported rapid population growth and supplied labor for the Industrial Revolution.

Green Revolution

From the 1940s to the 1970s, research led by Norman Borlaug produced dwarf wheat and rice varieties that, combined with irrigation and fertilizer, multiplied harvests in Mexico, India, and elsewhere, averting predicted famines — though critics note environmental costs and unequal benefits.
