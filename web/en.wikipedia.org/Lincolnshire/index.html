Lincolnshire is a ceremonial county in eastern England, on the North Sea coast. It is the second-largest of the English counties by area, and one of the most rural and agricultural. The county town is the city of Lincoln, an ancient cathedral and university city whose origins reach back to the Roman Lindum Colonia. Lincolnshire is bordered by Norfolk, Cambridgeshire, Rutland, Leicestershire, Nottinghamshire, South Yorkshire, the East Riding of Yorkshire, and Northamptonshire.

The county is divided for administrative purposes into the county of Lincolnshire (administered by Lincolnshire County Council), together with the unitary authorities of North Lincolnshire and North East Lincolnshire, the latter centred on Grimsby and Scunthorpe.

Geography

Lincolnshire is a county of contrasting landscapes. The east is dominated by the Lincolnshire Fens, a low-lying, extremely fertile marshland reclaimed from the sea over centuries by drainage works and sea defences; towns such as Boston and Spalding sit amid some of the richest arable land in Britain. Running north–south through the middle of the county are two chalk and limestone ridges: the Lincolnshire Wolds, an Area of Outstanding Natural Beauty of rolling hills and dry valleys in the north-east, and the Lincoln Edge (or Cliff), a limestone escarpment on which Lincoln itself stands. To the west, the county falls away to the Trent valley; the Isle of Axholme in the north-west was once a marshy island before its drainage by Cornelius Vermuyden in the 17th century.

The coast includes the working port of Grimsby, once the largest fishing port in the world, the resort of Skegness, and the great tidal estuary of the Wash, shared with Norfolk and noted for its wildlife.

History

Roman Lincoln (Lindum) was one of the four provincial capitals' colonies; the Romans built Ermine Street and the Fosse Way through the region. After the Roman withdrawal the area formed part of the Anglo-Saxon kingdom of Lindsey, later absorbed into Mercia and then the Danelaw, where Danish influence survives in many place-names ending in -by and -thorpe. The county was historically divided into three "parts" — Lindsey, Kesteven and Holland — each with its own quarter sessions.

Lincoln's medieval cathedral, begun in 1072 and rebuilt in Gothic style after 1185, was for a time the tallest building in the world. In the Second World War the flat county hosted numerous RAF airfields and became known as "Bomber County"; the Dambusters of 617 Squadron flew from RAF Scampton.

Economy

Agriculture dominates: Lincolnshire produces a large share of the United Kingdom's vegetables, potatoes, cereals and sugar beet, and its food-processing industry around Spalding, Boston and Grimsby is nationally significant. Tourism centres on the coast (Skegness, Cleethorpes, Mablethorpe) and the historic towns of Stamford and Louth. The county is also known for its sausages, stuffed chine, and the Lincolnshire Poacher cheese.

Places of interest

Lincoln Cathedral and Lincoln Castle (home to a copy of Magna Carta), Burghley House near Stamford, Belton House near Grantham, Tattershall Castle, and the Battle of Britain Memorial Flight at RAF Coningsby are among the county's landmarks.
