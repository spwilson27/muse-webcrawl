Diocletian (c. 242/245 – 311/312) was Roman emperor from 284 until his abdication in 305. He is famous for his wide-ranging reforms, which ended the Crisis of the Third Century and stabilised the Roman Empire, and for the Diocletianic Persecution, the empire's last and most severe persecution of Christians. A low-born soldier from Dalmatia, he rose through the cavalry to become commander of the emperor's bodyguard before being proclaimed emperor by the army after the death of Numerian.

The Tetrarchy

Concluding that one man could not rule the vast empire, Diocletian appointed Maximian as co-emperor (Augustus) in the West in 286, and in 293 each Augustus adopted a junior Caesar — Galerius under Diocletian, Constantius Chlorus under Maximian — creating the Tetrarchy, a college of four rulers. The empire was administered from frontier capitals including Nicomedia, Mediolanum, Augusta Treverorum and Sirmium; Rome ceased to be the seat of government.

Reforms

Diocletian doubled the number of provinces, separating civil from military authority in them, expanded the army and the bureaucracy, and reformed the coinage. His Edict on Maximum Prices (301) attempted, unsuccessfully, to curb inflation by decreeing empire-wide price ceilings. A new tax system based on land and head counts (capitatio-iugatio) put imperial finances on a regular footing. He styled himself dominus (lord) and adopted elaborate court ceremonial.

Persecution and abdication

From 303, edicts ordered the destruction of churches, the burning of scriptures, and sacrifices to the Roman gods; enforcement was harshest in the East under Galerius. In 305, in failing health, Diocletian abdicated at Nicomedia — the first Roman emperor to retire voluntarily — and compelled Maximian to do the same. He spent his retirement in his vast palace at Aspalathos (modern Split, Croatia), famously telling envoys who urged his return that he preferred growing his cabbages. The tetrarchic system collapsed into civil war after his retirement, from which Constantine emerged supreme.
