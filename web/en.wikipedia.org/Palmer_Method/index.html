The Palmer Method of penmanship instruction was developed and promoted by Austin Norman Palmer in the late 19th and early 20th centuries. It was intended to become a simplified, faster, more legible and more businesslike style of handwriting than the ornate Spencerian script then dominant, and it became the most widely taught form of penmanship in American schools during the first half of the twentieth century.

Technique

Palmer's system was built around what he called "muscular movement writing": instead of forming letters mainly with the fingers, students were taught to drive the pen with the muscles of the forearm, resting the arm on a desk and gliding it smoothly. The hand was positioned with the wrist slightly raised, and students practised rhythmic drills — ovals, push-pull strokes and repeated letterforms — often to a counted beat, until speed and uniformity became habitual. Palmer published the method in Palmer's Guide to Business Writing (1894) and later in the widely used The Palmer Method of Business Writing (1901).

History and influence

The A. N. Palmer Company trained teachers, ran correspondence courses and published copybooks used across the United States. Palmer Method instruction emphasised plainness and speed for clerks and business correspondents, deliberately stripping away the shaded flourishes of Spencerian penmanship. From the 1950s onward, competing systems such as the Zaner-Bloser method gradually displaced it, and later in the century the typewriter and then the computer reduced school penmanship instruction overall, though Palmer-derived cursive remains recognisable in generations of American handwriting.
