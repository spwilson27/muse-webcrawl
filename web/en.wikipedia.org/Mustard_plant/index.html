The mustard plant is any of several plant species in the genera Brassica, Rhaphanus and Sinapis in the family Brassicaceae. The principal culinary species are brown mustard (Brassica juncea), black mustard (Brassica nigra), and white/yellow mustard (Sinapis alba). Their small, round seeds are ground and mixed with water, vinegar or other liquids to make the condiment mustard, and the leaves of some species are eaten as mustard greens.

Cultivation and uses

Mustards are cool-season annuals grown worldwide. Canada and Nepal are among the largest producers of mustard seed. Seeds are pressed for mustard oil (widely used in South Asian cooking), and the plants serve as cover crops and green manure; some forms, such as giant red mustard, are grown as vegetables. The pungency of mustard comes from glucosinolates, which convert to sharp-tasting isothiocyanates when the seed is crushed and wetted.

Cultural significance

The tiny mustard seed is a familiar metaphor: in the New Testament, the Parable of the Mustard Seed compares the kingdom of heaven to a seed that grows into a great plant, and faith "as small as a mustard seed" is said to move mountains. Mustard has been cultivated since antiquity and was used medicinally by the Greeks and Romans.
