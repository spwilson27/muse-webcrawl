Asclepeion

An asclepeion (Ancient Greek: Ἀσκληπιεῖον; Latin: aesculapium) was a healing temple in ancient Greece and Rome, sacred to Asclepius, the god of medicine. More than 300 asclepeia are known; the most famous stood at Epidaurus, Kos, Athens and Pergamum.

Healing practices

Pilgrims seeking cures underwent purification, made offerings, and slept within the temple precinct in a ritual called incubation (enkoimesis), hoping the god would visit them in a dream and prescribe treatment or heal them directly. Recovered patients left votive offerings, sometimes models of the healed body part, and inscribed testimonials. Non-venomous snakes, sacred to Asclepius, roamed the dormitories, and sacred dogs were also kept.

Major sites

Epidaurus

The sanctuary at Epidaurus, the cult's most important centre, included a temple, the circular Thymele, a stadium and a great theatre famed for its acoustics. Cure inscriptions (iamata) were displayed there.

Kos and Pergamum

The Asclepeion of Kos was associated with Hippocrates and housed a medical school. At Pergamum, the physician Galen trained, and the sanctuary featured a theatre, library and treatment rooms, including tunnels used in incubation therapy.
