Rangefinder camera

TypeCamera with coupled rangefinder focusing
FocusingCoincidence (split-image) rangefinder, cam-coupled to lens

A rangefinder camera is a camera fitted with a rangefinder: a range-finding focusing mechanism that allows the photographer to measure the subject distance and take photographs that are in sharp focus. Most rangefinders show two images of the same subject that move together as a dial is turned; when the two images coincide, the distance can be read off, and in coupled designs the lens focus is set automatically by the same movement.

History

Rangefinders were first produced as external accessories in the 1910s–20s, then built into cameras such as the Leica II in 1932. Rangefinder designs dominated serious 35mm photography through the mid-20th century: the Leica M3 (1954) set the pattern with bright-line frame illumination and bayonet lens mount, and rangefinders were the tools of much classic photojournalism and street photography.

Characteristics

Because a rangefinder has no reflex mirror, its lenses can sit close to the film plane and the camera is compact and quiet, with a viewfinder that stays bright and never blacks out at the moment of exposure. Against this, the separate viewfinder suffers parallax error at close distances, framing with long telephoto lenses is imprecise, and previewing depth of field is impossible — disadvantages that led professionals to adopt the single-lens reflex for most work from the 1960s onward. The design survives in Leica's digital M series and in fixed-lens cameras such as Fujifilm's X100 series that evoke rangefinder styling.
