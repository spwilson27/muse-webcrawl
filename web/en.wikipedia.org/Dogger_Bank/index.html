Dogger Bank

LocationNorth Sea, off the east coast of England
Areaabout 17,600 km²
Depth15–36 m below sea level

The Dogger Bank is a large sandbank in a shallow area of the North Sea about 100 km off the east coast of England. It extends over roughly 17,600 square kilometres, with water depths of 15 to 36 metres — about 20 metres shallower than the surrounding seabed. The name comes from dogger, an old Dutch word for a fishing vessel.

Geology and prehistory

The bank is a moraine formed during the last ice age. In the Mesolithic period it was part of Doggerland, the dry land bridge connecting Britain to continental Europe, inhabited by hunter-gatherers until rising seas submerged it around 6500–6200 BC. Trawlers have recovered peat, mammoth and rhino bones, and Palaeolithic tools from the bank.

Fishing and incidents

The shallow, nutrient-rich waters are a productive fishing ground, long worked by British, Dutch, and Scandinavian fleets; the bank lends its name to the sea area "Dogger" in the BBC shipping forecast. In 1904 the Russian Baltic Fleet, en route to the Pacific during the Russo-Japanese War, mistakenly fired on British trawlers on the bank — the Dogger Bank incident — nearly causing war between Britain and Russia. A naval battle was also fought near the bank in 1915 between British and German battlecruisers.

Renewable energy

The bank's shallow water and strong winds have made it a major zone for offshore wind development, including the Dogger Bank Wind Farm, which on completion would be among the largest offshore wind farms in the world.
