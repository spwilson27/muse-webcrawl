Origami

Origami (Japanese: 折り紙, from oru, "to fold", and kami, "paper") is the art of paper folding, which is often associated with Japanese culture. In modern usage, the word "origami" is used as an inclusive term for all folding practices, regardless of their culture of origin. The goal is to transform a flat square sheet of paper into a finished sculpture through folding and sculpting techniques, without cuts, glue, or markings on the paper.

History

Paper folding developed after paper spread from China to Japan, with ceremonial folding (such as the noshi) documented during the Edo period. The first known Japanese book on recreational origami, Hiden Senbazuru Orikata (1797), taught the folding of connected cranes. In the 20th century, Akira Yoshizawa created thousands of models and devised the standard notation of dashed and solid lines for folding instructions, sparking a worldwide renaissance; Sam Randlett and others popularised diagramming in the West.

Techniques and mathematics

Basic folds (valley, mountain, squash, petal, reverse, sink) combine into bases such as the preliminary, waterbomb, bird, and frog bases. Wet-folding, pioneered by Yoshizawa, uses slightly dampened paper to hold curves. The mathematics of paper folding — including the Huzita–Hatori axioms and Kawasaki's theorem — has grown into a research field, and origami principles are applied to solar arrays, airbags, stents, and robotics. The paper crane (orizuru) is the most famous model, symbolising peace through the story of Sadako Sasaki.
