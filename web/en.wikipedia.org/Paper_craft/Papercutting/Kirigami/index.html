Kirigami

Kirigami (Japanese: 切り紙, from kiru, "to cut", and kami, "paper") is a variation of origami that includes cutting of the paper, rather than solely folding as in origami. Typically, kirigami starts with a folded base, which is then unfolded after cuts are made; the cuts are then opened and flattened to make the finished design. Simple kirigami are usually symmetrical, such as snowflakes, pentagrams, or orchid blossoms.

Terminology and origins

The term "kirigami" was coined by Florence Temko, who introduced the art to the United States with her 1962 book Kirigami, the Creative Art of Paper Cutting. Paper cutting itself has ancient roots in China (jianzhi, from the 6th century) and Japan (monkiri), and related traditions include Polish wycinanki, German Scherenschnitte, and Mexican papel picado.

Techniques and applications

Kirigami underlies pop-up cards and books and many decorative paper crafts. In recent decades, kirigami principles — the geometry of cuts that allow a flat sheet to transform into a three-dimensional shape — have been applied in engineering and materials science, in deployable structures, stretchable electronics, solar panel arrays, and metamaterials. Researchers also use "origami/kirigami engineering" for medical stents and nano-scale devices.
