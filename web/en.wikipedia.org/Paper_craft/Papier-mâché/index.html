Papier-mâché

Papier-mâché
MaterialsPaper strips or pulp, adhesive (flour paste, glue)
UsesMasks, piñatas, sculpture, carnival floats, furniture
Name originFrench for "chewed paper"

Papier-mâché is a composite material made from paper pieces or pulp bound with an adhesive such as glue, starch or flour paste. Despite its French name, it originated in China around the time paper itself was invented, where helmets and vessels were lacquered for strength. The technique spread through Persia and India to Europe, where by the 18th century it was used for trays, snuff boxes, dolls' heads and even furniture.

Methods

Two main methods are used. In the strip method, pieces of paper are soaked in paste and layered over a mould or form — a balloon for a piñata, for example — then left to dry into a rigid shell. In the pulp method, paper is soaked and mashed into a clay-like mass that can be modelled directly or pressed into moulds.

Uses

Papier-mâché is light, cheap and surprisingly durable when sealed and painted. It is used for Venetian carnival masks, Mexican alebrijes and piñatas, theatre props and stage scenery, giant parade puppets, and the great sculpted floats of Viareggio's carnival in Italy. In Kashmir and Iran it is finished with fine painted and lacquered decoration.
