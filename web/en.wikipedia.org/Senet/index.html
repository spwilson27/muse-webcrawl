Senet (Ancient Egyptian: znt, "passing") is a board game from ancient Egypt. It is among the oldest known board games: fragments of boards date to the Predynastic period around 3100 BC, and a painting in the tomb of Merknera (c. 3300–2700 BC) is among the earliest depictions of the game. Senet boards were found in the tomb of Tutankhamun, and a famous scene in the tomb of Queen Nefertari shows her playing the game.

The board

The game is played on a grid of three rows of ten squares — thirty squares in total. Pieces move along a boustrophedon (S-shaped) path. Some squares bear special markings: commonly identified special squares include a house of happiness, a house of water, and squares associated with the three judges and the gods Horus and Re-Atum in the final five squares of New Kingdom boards.

Gameplay

Two players each had a set of pieces (commonly five or seven, cones versus spools). Movement was determined by throwing sticks or knucklebones, which gave values according to the number of light or dark sides showing; a throw of one, four or five usually granted another turn. The goal was to bear all one's pieces off the board; landing on an opponent's piece could send it backwards, and certain squares were safe while others forced a piece back or off the board. The exact original rules have been lost and modern rules are reconstructions by scholars and enthusiasts.

Religious significance

By the New Kingdom, senet had acquired a religious dimension: the game was understood as an enactment of the soul's journey through the underworld (the Duat), as described in funerary texts such as the Book of the Dead, with the player's progress and hazards mirroring the perils faced by the deceased before reaching the afterlife. This is why boards were placed in tombs as grave goods.
