Modern display of the Confederate battle flag

The Army of Northern Virginia battle flag pattern is the design most commonly displayed today

The modern display of the Confederate battle flag concerns the contemporary use of flags of the Confederate States of America (1861–1865), principally the battle flag of the Army of Northern Virginia — a blue diagonal cross (saltire) bearing white stars on a red field. The flag's display in public spaces, on state grounds, and in popular culture has been a subject of sustained controversy in the United States, with supporters describing it as a symbol of Southern heritage and defenders' critics associating it with slavery, segregation, and white supremacy.

History

During the American Civil War the saltire design served as a battle flag for several Confederate armies. After the war it was largely confined to veterans' commemorations for decades. Its prominence grew during the mid-20th century: it was carried by the Dixiecrat segregationist campaign of 1948, incorporated into the Georgia state flag in 1956, and raised over the South Carolina State House in 1962, where such displays were widely understood as statements of resistance to the civil rights movement and school desegregation.

Controversy and removals

Debate intensified after the 2015 Charleston church shooting, in which the perpetrator had posed with the flag. South Carolina removed the flag from its State House grounds that year; major retailers including Walmart and Amazon stopped selling flag merchandise. Mississippi, whose state flag had incorporated the battle flag since 1894, adopted a new flag without it in 2020. Numerous monuments and flags were removed from public land during the protests of 2020, and the United States military moved to restrict display on installations.

Public opinion

Surveys consistently find a sharp racial and regional divide in perceptions: majorities of Black Americans regard the flag primarily as a symbol of racism, while white Southerners are more likely to describe it as a symbol of regional pride. Historians note that the Confederacy's own leaders identified the preservation of slavery as the cause of secession.
