The Great Famine (Irish: an Gorta Mór), also known in Irish as an Drochshaol ("the bad times"), was a period of mass starvation and disease in Ireland from 1845 to 1852, which constituted a historical social crisis with major effects on Irish society and history as a whole. With the most severely affected areas in the west and south of Ireland, where the Irish language was dominant, the period was contemporaneously known in Irish as an Drochshaol, loosely translated as "the hard times" or "the bad life". The worst year of the famine was 1847, known as "Black '47".

During the Great Hunger, roughly 1 million people died and over 1 million more fled the country, causing the country's population to fall by 20–25% between 1841 and 1871, with some sources estimating the population declined by as much as a third over a longer period. Between 1845 and 1855, at least 2.1 million people left Ireland, primarily on packet ships but also steamboats and barques, one of the greatest exoduses from a single island in history.

Causes

The proximate cause of the famine was the infection of potato crops by Phytophthora infestans, also known as potato blight. Though the potato crop had failed in many European countries during the 1840s (the period of the "Hungry Forties"), the impact in Ireland was disproportionate, as one-third of the population was dependent on the potato for a range of ethnic, religious, political, social and economic reasons, such as land acquisition and distribution, the crop system, and emigration. The famine and its effects permanently changed the island's demographic, political and cultural landscape, producing an estimated 2 million refugees and spurring a century-long population decline.

Land system and dependence on the potato

By the 1840s, the Irish population of about 8.2 million was heavily concentrated in small tenant holdings. The potato, particularly the high-yielding but disease-prone "Lumper" variety, could feed a family from a tiny plot, and subdivision of land drove ever-greater reliance on it. When the blight destroyed successive harvests from 1845 onwards, the poorest cottiers and labourers had no alternative food source.

Government response

The British government's response was shaped by laissez-faire economic doctrine and, later, by the policies of the Whig administration of Lord John Russell and his assistant secretary to the Treasury, Charles Trevelyan. Peel's Conservative government imported Indian corn (maize) in 1845–46 and organised public works, but relief was subsequently wound down. Soup kitchens briefly fed up to 3 million people daily in 1847, before being closed. The Poor Law Extension Act of 1847 shifted the cost of relief onto Irish landlords and ratepayers, encouraging mass evictions. Food continued to be exported from Ireland during the famine, a fact central to later nationalist critiques of British rule.

Emigration and the diaspora

Emigrants flooded to the United States, Canada, Britain and Australia. Many crossed the Atlantic on overcrowded "coffin ships" with high mortality from typhus and other diseases; quarantine stations such as Grosse Île in Canada recorded thousands of deaths in 1847. The famine greatly accelerated the growth of the Irish diaspora and fuelled political movements such as Fenianism and Irish republicanism in the United States.

Legacy

The famine remains a defining event in Irish history. The population of Ireland has never returned to its pre-famine level; it stood at roughly 5.1 million in the Republic and 1.9 million in Northern Ireland in the early 21st century. The event is remembered in memorials worldwide, and the debate over responsibility — with some describing British policy as amounting to genocide and most historians emphasising ideology, administrative failure and landlordism — continues. In 1997, British Prime Minister Tony Blair issued a statement acknowledging that "those who governed in London at the time failed their people."
