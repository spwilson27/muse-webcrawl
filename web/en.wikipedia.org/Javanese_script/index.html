Javanese script (natively known as Aksara Jawa, and also called Hanacaraka, Carakan, or Dentawyanjana) is one of Indonesia's traditional scripts developed on the island of Java. The script is an abugida: each letter represents a consonant with an inherent vowel /a/ or /ɔ/, modified by diacritics (sandhangan) to change the vowel or mark final consonants.

History

The script descends from the Kawi script, which itself derived from the Pallava and ultimately Brahmi scripts of India. Modern Javanese script took shape in the courts of the Mataram Sultanate from roughly the 16th–17th centuries and was used for literature, chronicles (babad), poetry (tembang), and official documents written on palm leaf (lontar) and later paper.

Structure

The basic letters (aksara nglegena) number twenty, traditionally ordered by the hanacaraka sequence, which itself forms a poem telling the legend of Aji Saka and his two servants. Consonant clusters are written with subordinate forms (pasangan) placed below or after the preceding letter. Special letters (aksara murda) serve as honorific capitals, and additional characters (aksara rekan) were adopted for foreign sounds from Arabic and Dutch.

Modern use

Since the 20th century the Latin alphabet has largely replaced the script in everyday writing, but Javanese script remains taught in schools in Central and East Java as part of local-content curricula, and appears on signage, in cultural publications, and in ceremonial contexts. It was added to the Unicode Standard in 2009 (version 5.2).
