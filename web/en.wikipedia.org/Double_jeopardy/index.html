Double jeopardy is a procedural legal defence in a criminal prosecution that prevents an accused person from being subsequently tried again on the same (or similar) charges after having been legally acquitted or convicted. As a fundamental principle of criminal justice, it protects individuals from the harassment, expense, and risk of error of repeated prosecutions by the state for the same offence, and preserves the finality of verdicts.

Origins

The principle descends from Roman law's maxim nemo bis vexari pro una et eadem causa — no one should be vexed twice for one and the same cause — and the canon-law rule that God himself does not punish twice for the same act. In English common law it appeared as the pleas of autrefois acquit and autrefois convict ("formerly acquitted/convicted"), and it was commented on by Blackstone as a universal maxim of justice.

United States

In the United States the Double Jeopardy Clause of the Fifth Amendment provides: "nor shall any person be subject for the same offence to be twice put in jeopardy of life or limb." Under the Blockburger test, two offences are the "same" unless each requires proof of a fact the other does not. Jeopardy generally attaches when the jury is sworn. Notable limits include the separate sovereigns doctrine, reaffirmed in Gamble v. United States (2019), under which federal and state governments — or two states — may each prosecute the same act, and the rule that a defendant may be retried after a mistrial or a successful appeal (other than an appeal resolved for insufficient evidence).

Other jurisdictions

Most legal systems recognise some form of the rule, expressed in the maxim ne bis in idem. England and Wales, which once treated it as absolute, created a statutory exception in the Criminal Justice Act 2003 permitting retrial for serious offences where new and compelling evidence emerges — the reform under which the killers of Stephen Lawrence were retried and convicted in 2012. Article 4 of Protocol No. 7 to the European Convention on Human Rights and Article 14(7) of the ICCPR protect the principle across much of the world, usually within a single state only.
