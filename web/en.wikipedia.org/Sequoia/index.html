Sequoia

Sequoia is a genus of redwood coniferous trees in the subfamily Sequoioideae of the family Cupressaceae. The only extant species of the genus is Sequoia sempervirens, the coast redwood, native to the coastal mountains of northern California and southwestern Oregon. The name is also used in common speech for the related giant sequoia (Sequoiadendron giganteum) of the Sierra Nevada. Fossil species of Sequoia were once widespread across the Northern Hemisphere.

Etymology

The name Sequoia was published by the Austrian botanist Stephan Endlicher in 1847. It is generally believed to honour Sequoyah, the Cherokee silversmith who created the Cherokee syllabary, although this derivation has been debated by historians.

Description

Coast redwoods are evergreen, long-lived trees reaching heights of up to about 115 metres (379 ft) — the tallest living trees on Earth — with trunks up to 7 metres in diameter at the base. Their reddish-brown bark is thick, fibrous, and fire-resistant, and their tiny cones release seeds best after heat opens them. The current tallest known specimen, named Hyperion, was measured at 115.9 metres.

Distribution and ecology

The natural range of Sequoia sempervirens is a narrow strip of the Pacific coast from central California to southern Oregon, where summer fog supplies crucial moisture. Much of the remaining old-growth forest is protected in Redwood National and State Parks and other reserves; the species is nevertheless listed as Endangered because of extensive historical logging.
