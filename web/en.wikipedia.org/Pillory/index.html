Pillory

The pillory was a device used in punishment by public humiliation and physical abuse, consisting of a wooden or metal framework erected on a post, with holes in hinged boards through which the offender's head and hands were locked. Set up in a market place or other public spot, the pillory exposed the victim for a set period—often an hour or more on successive days—to the jeers and missiles of the crowd.

Use

From the Middle Ages into the nineteenth century, the pillory was used across Europe and its colonies for offences such as fraud, perjury, seditious libel, cheating in trade and sexual offences. Unlike the stocks, which restrained the legs of a seated offender, the pillory held the victim standing with head and hands fast. The crowd's response was unpredictable: popular offenders might be garlanded and cheered—the writer Daniel Defoe was famously pelted with flowers in 1703—while hated ones could be seriously injured or killed by stones and refuse.

Abolition

Use of the pillory declined as reformers attacked public corporal punishment. France abolished it in the early nineteenth century; in England it was restricted in 1816 to cases of perjury and finally abolished in 1837, though the last uses had been earlier. In the United States it survived longest in Delaware, where it was not formally abolished until 1905. The word survives figuratively, "to be pilloried" meaning to be publicly ridiculed or denounced.
