National Emergencies Act

Enacted: 14 September 1976

Signed by: President Gerald Ford

Codified: 50 U.S.C. §§ 1601–1651

The National Emergencies Act (NEA) is a United States federal law, enacted in 1976, that formalises the emergency powers of the President of the United States. The Act authorises the president to declare a national emergency, which activates a wide range of standby statutory authorities, but it also imposes procedural requirements: the declaration must specify the powers being invoked, be transmitted to Congress and published in the Federal Register, and be renewed annually or it lapses. Congress may terminate a declaration by joint resolution.

Background

The Act arose from the work of a special Senate committee in the 1970s, which found that hundreds of emergency provisions had accumulated in federal law and that the United States had technically remained in states of emergency declared in 1933, 1950, 1970 and 1971. The NEA terminated those existing emergencies, imposed reporting and oversight requirements, and was passed alongside reforms such as the International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) of 1977.

Use

Dozens of national emergencies have been declared under the Act, many of them annually renewed sanctions regimes directed at foreign governments and individuals under IEEPA. Notable declarations include the 1979 Iran hostage crisis emergency (still in effect), emergencies after the September 11 attacks, and the 2019 declaration concerning the southern border of the United States, which prompted legal and political controversy over the use of emergency powers to fund border wall construction. As of the mid-2020s, more than 40 declared emergencies remained simultaneously in effect.

Reform efforts

Critics argue that the Act gives presidents excessive latitude, since emergencies can persist for decades with only perfunctory renewal. Bipartisan reform proposals have sought to require affirmative congressional approval for emergencies to continue beyond a short initial period, but none had been enacted as of the mid-2020s.
