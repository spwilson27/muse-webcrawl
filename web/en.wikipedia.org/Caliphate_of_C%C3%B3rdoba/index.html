The Caliphate of Córdoba (Arabic: خلافة قرطبة) was an Islamic state in the Iberian Peninsula ruled by the Umayyad dynasty from 929 to 1031. It succeeded the Emirate of Córdoba founded in 756 by Abd al-Rahman I, a survivor of the fallen Umayyad Caliphate of Damascus. Under the caliphate, al-Andalus reached its greatest territorial extent and cultural brilliance, and Córdoba became one of the largest and most sophisticated cities in the world.

History

In 929 the emir Abd al-Rahman III proclaimed himself caliph, asserting religious as well as political authority in response to the rival Fatimid Caliphate in North Africa and the Abbasid Caliphate in Baghdad. His long reign (912–961) unified Muslim Iberia, subdued Christian kingdoms in the north, and projected power across the western Mediterranean. His son al-Hakam II (961–976) was a great patron of learning whose library reportedly held hundreds of thousands of volumes.

After al-Hakam's death, effective power passed to the chamberlain al-Mansur (Almanzor), whose military campaigns against the Christian north kept the caliphate dominant but hollowed out its institutions. Following al-Mansur's death in 1002, succession struggles erupted into civil war (the fitna of al-Andalus). The caliphate was formally abolished in 1031, fragmenting into competing taifa kingdoms.

Culture and society

Córdoba under the caliphs was renowned for its Great Mosque (La Mezquita), the palace-city of Madinat al-Zahra, paved and lit streets, libraries, and scholars such as the surgeon al-Zahrawi (Albucasis). The state was religiously plural: Muslims ruled over large Christian (Mozarab) and Jewish communities, and figures like the Jewish courtier Hasdai ibn Shaprut rose to high office. The caliphate's wealth rested on irrigated agriculture, trade, and tribute from the Christian kingdoms.
