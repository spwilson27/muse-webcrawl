MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are small, single-stranded, non-coding RNA molecules, about 22 nucleotides in length, that regulate gene expression post-transcriptionally. Found in plants, animals, and some viruses, miRNAs function by base-pairing with complementary sequences within messenger RNA (mRNA) targets — typically in the 3′ untranslated region — leading to mRNA cleavage, destabilization, or repression of translation.

In animals, a single miRNA may regulate hundreds of target genes, and it is estimated that miRNAs influence a large fraction of the human transcriptome. They play central roles in development, cell differentiation, proliferation, apoptosis, and metabolism.

History

The first miRNA, lin-4, was discovered in 1993 in the laboratory of Victor Ambros during studies of the nematode Caenorhabditis elegans: the lin-4 gene produced a tiny RNA that repressed the lin-14 protein by binding its mRNA. For several years this was regarded as a worm oddity. In 2000, Gary Ruvkun's group discovered let-7, a second small regulatory RNA that proved to be conserved across animals, including humans — revealing miRNAs as a vast, general class of regulators. Ambros and Ruvkun received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 2024 for the discovery of microRNA and its role in post-transcriptional gene regulation.

Biogenesis

Most miRNA genes are transcribed by RNA polymerase II into long primary transcripts (pri-miRNAs) that contain hairpin structures. In the nucleus, the Microprocessor complex — the RNase III enzyme Drosha together with DGCR8 — crops the hairpin to release a precursor miRNA (pre-miRNA) of about 70 nucleotides. Exportin-5 carries the pre-miRNA to the cytoplasm, where a second RNase III enzyme, Dicer, cuts it into a short RNA duplex. One strand (the mature miRNA) is loaded into an Argonaute protein to form the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC), which scans mRNAs for complementary targets; the other strand is usually discarded.

Mechanism

Target recognition depends principally on the miRNA "seed" region, nucleotides 2–8 at the 5′ end. In animals, seed pairing usually produces imperfect complementarity, and repression occurs mainly through recruitment of deadenylation and decapping machinery, destabilizing the mRNA, together with translational inhibition. In plants, pairing is usually near-perfect and Argonaute cleaves the target directly, as in RNA interference.

Clinical significance

Dysregulation of miRNAs is implicated in cancer (where some act as oncogenes, "oncomiRs", and others as tumour suppressors), cardiovascular disease, neurological disorders, and immune dysfunction. Circulating miRNAs in blood are investigated as biomarkers, and miRNA mimics and inhibitors (antagomirs) are under development as therapeutics; the first small-RNA drugs to reach the clinic, however, were siRNA-based.
