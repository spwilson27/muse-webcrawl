The Canterbury Tales

The Canterbury Tales is a collection of twenty-four stories, mostly in verse, written in Middle English by Geoffrey Chaucer between 1387 and 1400. It is considered one of the founding works of English literature.

Frame and pilgrims

The tales are presented as a storytelling contest held by a group of pilgrims travelling from the Tabard Inn in Southwark, London, to the shrine of Saint Thomas Becket at Canterbury Cathedral. The host, Harry Bailly, proposes that each pilgrim tell two tales on the way there and two on the return; most completed only one. The company spans medieval society: the Knight, the Miller, the Reeve, the Prioress, the Monk, the Wife of Bath, the Pardoner, the Summoner and others, each introduced in the famous General Prologue.

Contents

The tales range across romance (the Knight's Tale), fabliau (the Miller's Tale), beast fable (the Nun's Priest's Tale), saint's life, sermon and treatise. The Wife of Bath's Prologue and Tale, the Pardoner's Tale of three rioters who seek Death, and the Franklin's Tale are among the most studied. Chaucer left the work unfinished at his death; no authoritative order of the fragments survives, though the Ellesmere manuscript arrangement is widely used.

Significance

Written in the London dialect of Middle English, the Tales helped establish English as a literary language alongside Latin and French. Caxton printed the work in 1476, among the first books printed in England.
