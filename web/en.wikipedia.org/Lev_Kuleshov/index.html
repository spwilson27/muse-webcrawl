Lev Kuleshov

Lev Vladimirovich Kuleshov (Russian: Лев Владимирович Кулешов; 13 January 1899 – 29 March 1970) was a Russian and Soviet filmmaker and film theorist, one of the founders of the world's first film school, the Moscow Film School. He was a pioneer of Soviet montage theory, in which the primacy of editing — the juxtaposition of shots — over the content of any single shot was demonstrated in his famous Kuleshov effect experiments. His students included Vsevolod Pudovkin, Boris Barnet and the young Sergei Eisenstein briefly attended his circle; his workshop shaped Soviet cinema of the 1920s.

Early career

Kuleshov began as a set designer in pre-revolutionary Russian cinema under Yevgeni Bauer. After the Revolution he headed a workshop at the newly founded State Film School (VGIK) in Moscow. Because raw film stock was scarce, his "Kuleshov Workshop" practised "films without film" — rehearsing and staging scenes and re-editing existing prints, including D. W. Griffith's Intolerance, to study how meaning was constructed.

The Kuleshov effect

In his best-known experiment, Kuleshov intercut a single neutral close-up of the actor Ivan Mozzhukhin with shots of a bowl of soup, a dead woman in a coffin, and a little girl playing. Audiences praised the actor's subtle expressions of hunger, grief and affection — although the face shot was identical each time. The experiment showed that viewers derive meaning and emotion from the sequence of shots rather than from the shots alone, a foundational principle of montage and of film grammar generally.

Films and later life

Kuleshov's feature films include the comedy The Extraordinary Adventures of Mr. West in the Land of the Bolsheviks (1924), the Jack London adaptation By the Law (1926), and The Great Consoler (1933). From the 1930s, under the doctrine of socialist realism, his formalist approach fell out of official favour and he directed few films; he devoted himself to teaching at VGIK, where he remained a revered professor until his death in 1970.

Legacy

Kuleshov's writings, collected in translation as Kuleshov on Film, influenced film theory internationally. The effect bearing his name remains a standard demonstration in film schools, and his insistence that editing is the essence of cinema underpins modern editing practice.
