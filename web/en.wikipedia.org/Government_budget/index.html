Government budget

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A government budget is a projection of the government's revenues and expenditures for a certain period of time, often referred to as a financial or fiscal year, which may or may not correspond to the calendar year. Government budgets are prepared by the executive branch and, in democratic systems, are approved by the legislature before taking effect.

Contents

A budget sets out expected revenue — from taxes, duties, fees, state enterprises, and borrowing — and planned spending on public services, defence, infrastructure, welfare, and debt interest. The difference between revenue and expenditure is the budget balance: a surplus when revenue exceeds spending, a deficit when spending exceeds revenue.

The budget process

Typically, a finance ministry drafts the budget, the cabinet approves it, and the legislature debates, amends, and passes it into law as an appropriation or budget act. Execution is monitored through treasury reporting and independent audit. Many governments supplement annual budgets with medium-term frameworks and fiscal rules.

Budgeting methods

Common approaches include line-item budgeting, programme and performance budgeting, and zero-based budgeting, in which every expense must be justified anew for each period. Deficits may be financed by issuing government debt, which links the budget to public debt sustainability.
