Hesiod (Greek: Ἡσίοδος, Hēsíodos) was an ancient Greek poet generally thought to have been active between 750 and 650 BC, around the same time as Homer. He is generally regarded by Western authors as the first written poet in the Western tradition to regard himself as an individual persona with an active role to play in his subject. Ancient authors credited Hesiod and Homer with establishing Greek religious customs.

Life

Unlike Homer, Hesiod speaks of himself in his poems. He was a farmer and shepherd from Ascra in Boeotia, near Mount Helicon, where, he says, the Muses appeared to him while he tended sheep and gave him a staff of laurel and the gift of song. He quarrelled with his brother Perses over their inheritance, an injustice that prompted the moral reflections of the Works and Days. He won a tripod in a poetry contest at Chalcis, at the funeral games of Amphidamas.

Works

Two epic poems survive complete. The Theogony (c. 730–700 BC) narrates the origin of the cosmos and the genealogy of the Greek gods, culminating in the reign of Zeus. The Works and Days is a didactic poem addressed to Perses combining farming advice, a moral exhortation to justice and labour, the myth of the Five Ages of Man, the fable of the hawk and the nightingale, and the story of Pandora. Other poems were attributed to Hesiod in antiquity, notably the Catalogue of Women and the Shield of Heracles, of which fragments survive.

Influence

Hesiod's ordering of the Greek pantheon became standard, and his works are fundamental sources for Greek mythology, archaic Greek society, and early ideas about justice, work and the ages of humankind.
