Peach palm


Fruit of the peach palm (Bactris gasipaes)

Peach palm (Bactris gasipaes) is a species of palm native to the tropical forests of Central and South America. It is the only fully domesticated palm species in the Americas, cultivated for centuries — perhaps millennia — by Indigenous peoples, and has long been a staple food in the Amazon basin and elsewhere. It is known as pejibaye in Costa Rica, pijuayo in Peru, pupunha in Brazil, and chontaduro in Colombia and Ecuador.

Description

The tree grows rapidly to 20 metres or more and is armed with black spines on the trunk and leaf stems — a serious obstacle to harvesters, though spineless varieties have been bred. The fruit is a drupe, egg-sized, ripening red, orange, or yellow, with a starchy, mealy flesh surrounding a single seed. The fruit must be cooked (usually boiled in salted water) before eating; cooked flesh resembles chestnut or squash and is rich in starch, vitamin A, and oils.

Uses

Peach palm is grown for two products: the fruit, widely sold cooked in markets across Latin America, and heart of palm, harvested from the growing tip (killing the stem, so multi-stemmed cultivation is preferred). Costa Rica and Ecuador are major exporters of canned peach-palm hearts. The tree also yields durable wood for tool handles and bows, and the fruit is fermented into a traditional chicha. Because it produces on poor soils and yields abundantly with little input, the peach palm is promoted as a sustainable crop for the humid tropics.
