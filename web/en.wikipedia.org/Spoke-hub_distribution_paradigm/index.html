Spoke–hub distribution paradigm

The spoke–hub distribution paradigm (or hub-and-spoke model) is a form of transport network topology in which traffic, freight, or data moves through a central node (the hub) rather than directly between pairs of points (spokes). Named by analogy with a bicycle wheel, where the hub is at the centre and spokes run out to the rim, the model is used in air transport, freight, telecommunications, computer networks and supply chains.

Advantages and disadvantages

Consolidating flows through hubs lets carriers fill vehicles, serve many city pairs with fewer routes, and offer frequent service and lower unit costs through economies of scale. The classic illustration is airlines: instead of flying direct between every pair of cities, carriers route passengers through hubs such as Atlanta, Chicago O'Hare, Frankfurt or Dubai, so a network of n spokes needs only n routes rather than n(n−1)/2 point-to-point links. The disadvantages include longer travel times and detours, congestion and vulnerability at the hub, and delays that propagate across the network.

History and applications

The model was pioneered in freight by Federal Express, which from 1973 sorted packages overnight at Memphis, and in passenger aviation it spread rapidly after United States airline deregulation in 1978. Variants include multi-hub networks and "rolling hubs". The concept also appears in parcel delivery, less-than-truckload trucking, postal systems, military logistics, and even neuroscience and political organisation as a structural metaphor.
