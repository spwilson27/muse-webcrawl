Gamma-ray burst

In gamma-ray astronomy, gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) are extremely energetic explosions that emit brief, intense flashes of gamma radiation. They are the most luminous electromagnetic events known, releasing in seconds as much energy as the Sun will emit in its entire lifetime, concentrated into narrow relativistic jets.

Discovery and history

GRBs were discovered serendipitously in 1967 by the US Vela satellites, which monitored compliance with the nuclear test ban treaty; the discovery was announced in 1973. For decades their distances were unknown. In 1997 the BeppoSAX satellite localised the X-ray afterglow of GRB 970228, enabling redshift measurements that proved the bursts occur at cosmological distances.

Classification

Bursts are divided into short bursts (under about two seconds, harder spectra), thought to arise from mergers of neutron stars — confirmed by the gravitational-wave event GW170817 and its associated short burst GRB 170817A — and long bursts (over two seconds), associated with the collapse of massive stars (collapsars) and broad-lined Type Ic supernovae.

Afterglows and hosts

The fading afterglow, produced by the jet sweeping up surrounding matter, is observed from X-rays to radio and permits precise localisation. Long bursts occur in star-forming galaxies; short bursts in a wider range of hosts. The brightest burst recorded, GRB 221009A (the "BOAT"), occurred in October 2022.

Effects on Earth

A nearby GRB directed at Earth could damage the ozone layer; such events have been speculatively linked to mass extinctions such as the Late Ordovician, though no firm evidence exists.
