Tōdai-ji

Tōdai-ji — Buddhist temple complex in Nara, Japan. Built in the 8th century under Emperor Shōmu; houses the Great Buddha (Daibutsu). A UNESCO World Heritage Site as part of the "Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara".

Tōdai-ji (Japanese: 東大寺, "Great Eastern Temple") is a Buddhist temple complex that was once one of the powerful Seven Great Temples, located in the city of Nara, Japan. Its Great Buddha Hall (Daibutsuden) holds the world's largest bronze statue of the Buddha Vairocana, known in Japanese as Daibutsu (大仏, "Giant Buddha"). The temple is a listed UNESCO World Heritage Site as one of the "Historic Monuments of Ancient Nara", together with seven other sites.

History

The temple was founded in the first half of the 8th century, during the Nara period. In 741, when Emperor Shōmu issued an edict ordered that the building of provincial monasteries and convents be established throughout Japan, the government temple Kokubun-ji of Yamato Province was established in Nara. Construction of the great statue began around 747 and the eye-opening ceremony took place in 752. The temple grew in power over the centuries, becoming so influential that the capital was moved away from Nara in 784 partly in order to lower the temple's influence in government affairs.

The Great Buddha

The bronze statue is 14.98 metres (49.1 ft) tall and weighs about 500 tonnes. The current statue was recast several times for various reasons, including earthquake damage; the hands date from the Momoyama period and the head from the Edo period.

Approach and grounds

The approach to the hall is through the Nandaimon (Great South Gate), a large wooden gate watched over by two fierce statues. Deer, regarded as messengers of the gods in the Shinto tradition, roam the grounds freely. The Daibutsuden has been rebuilt twice after fire; the current building of 1709 is two-thirds the width of the original.
