The Deccan Traps are a large igneous province of west-central India, one of the largest volcanic features on Earth. They consist of multiple layers of solidified flood basalt that together form the stepped terrain (the word "traps" derives from the Swedish trappa, meaning stairs) of the Deccan Plateau. The eruptions that produced them occurred around 66 million years ago, near the end of the Cretaceous period.

Formation

The Deccan Traps were formed by massive flood basalt eruptions, widely attributed to a deep mantle plume — often identified with the Réunion hotspot — that impinged on the base of the Indian lithosphere as the Indian plate moved northward. Successive lava flows, each a few metres to tens of metres thick, stacked up to a composite thickness exceeding 2,000 metres near the western margin. The original volume of lava is estimated at well over one million cubic kilometres.

Extent

Today the traps cover roughly 500,000 square kilometres of the Deccan Plateau across the states of Maharashtra, Gujarat, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka, Telangana, and Andhra Pradesh. The greatest thickness is in the west, where the lava pile forms the Western Ghats escarpment; the flows thin progressively eastward and northward.

Relation to the end-Cretaceous extinction

High-precision argon–argon dating places the main phase of Deccan volcanism within a few hundred thousand years of the Cretaceous–Paleogene (K–Pg) boundary at 66 million years ago, the same interval as the Chicxulub impact in Mexico. Volcanic outgassing of sulphur dioxide and carbon dioxide from the eruptions would have caused severe climate perturbation, and the Deccan Traps are therefore considered a possible contributor to — or alternative cause of — the mass extinction that ended the reign of the non-avian dinosaurs. The relative roles of the impact and the volcanism remain actively debated.

Stratigraphy and chemistry

The lava pile is divided into subgroups and formations based on geochemistry and magnetic polarity. The bulk of the volume belongs to the Wai Subgroup, including the Poladpur, Ambenali, and Mahabaleshwar formations. Most Deccan lavas are tholeiitic basalts.
