Praetor

Praetor (Latin: praetor) was a title granted by the government of ancient Rome to a man acting in one of two official capacities: as a commander of an army, or as an elected magistrate (higher in rank than a quaestor and below the consul) assigned to perform various duties. In the Roman Republic the praetorship (praetura) was a key step in the cursus honorum.

History

The office was created in 367 BC under the Licinio-Sextian laws, initially as the holder of jurisdiction in the city. In 242 BC a second praetor was added: the praetor urbanus administered justice between Roman citizens, while the praetor peregrinus handled cases involving foreigners (peregrini). Through the praetor peregrinus's edicts the Romans developed the ius gentium. As Rome acquired provinces, additional praetors were elected to govern them — their number rose to eight under Sulla and sixteen under Julius Caesar. Each praetor issued an edict at the start of his term stating the rules he would follow, and these edicts became a major source of Roman law.

Duties and status

Praetors held imperium, could command legions, presided over courts, and governed provinces as propraetors after their term. They were attended by six lictors (two within the city). Under the Empire the office lost political power, though praetors retained judicial functions and funded public games; the title also survives in terms such as praetorium and Praetorian Guard, the emperor's bodyguard, whose prefects became powerful figures in their own right.
