Firebird (Slavic folklore)

The Firebird, as illustrated by Ivan Bilibin for the tale of Ivan Tsarevich.

In Slavic folklore, the Firebird (Russian: жар-пти́ца, zhar-ptitsa) is a magical and prophetic glowing or radiant bird from a faraway land which is both a blessing and a harbinger of doom to its captor.

Description

The Firebird is described as a large bird with majestic plumage that glows brightly with red, orange, and yellow light, like a bonfire that is past its turbulent flame phase. The feathers do not cease glowing if removed, and one feather can light a large room if not concealed. In later iconography, the bird's appearance is usually that of a fiery falcon or peacock-like bird, about the size of a peacock.

Role in fairy tales

A typical role of the Firebird in fairy tales is as an object of a difficult quest. The quest usually begins when a hero discovers a lost feather of the Firebird and sets out to capture the bird alive, often at the command of a tsar. In the best-known tale, Ivan Tsarevich and the Firebird, the youngest son of a tsar, aided by a magical grey wolf, captures the bird after it steals golden apples from his father's garden, and wins the hand of a princess. The bird is usually a symbol of beauty and desire that brings both fortune and peril.

The Firebird has parallels in other mythologies, such as the Persian Simurgh and the phoenix, and related figures appear throughout Slavic and Eurasian folklore.

In the arts

The Firebird is the subject of Igor Stravinsky's ballet The Firebird (1910), composed for Sergei Diaghilev's Ballets Russes, which launched Stravinsky's career. The tale was also famously illustrated by Ivan Bilibin, and the bird remains a popular motif in Russian art, lacquer miniatures from Palekh, and modern fantasy.
