Free flap

A free flap is a unit of tissue — skin, fat, muscle, bone, or a combination — that is completely detached from its donor site along with its artery and vein (the vascular pedicle) and transferred to a distant recipient site, where the vessels are reconnected to local vessels using microsurgical techniques. Free tissue transfer is a cornerstone of modern reconstructive surgery, used when local tissue options are inadequate because of the size, location, or complexity of a defect.

Technique

The surgeon designs the flap around a known perforating vessel, harvests it with its pedicle, and prepares recipient vessels near the defect. Under the operating microscope, the donor artery and vein are anastomosed to recipient vessels with sutures finer than a human hair. Blood flow is restored and flap perfusion monitored post-operatively by clinical observation, Doppler signals, or implantable probes, since thrombosis at the anastomosis — most likely in the first 48–72 hours — can cause flap loss if not promptly revised.

Common flaps and applications

Widely used free flaps include the radial forearm flap, the anterolateral thigh (ALT) flap, the fibula osteocutaneous flap for mandible reconstruction, the latissimus dorsi flap, and the DIEP flap, which uses abdominal skin and fat while sparing the rectus muscle for breast reconstruction after mastectomy. Free flaps are used to reconstruct defects after cancer ablation (especially head and neck tumors), trauma, burns, and infections, and to restore form and function in congenital anomalies.

History

Free tissue transfer became practical in the 1970s following advances in microvascular technique; Daniel and Taylor reported the first successful free flap (a groin flap) in 1973. Success rates in experienced centers now exceed 95 percent.
