Oriental Orthodoxy

Oriental Orthodoxy is the communion of Eastern Christian churches that recognise only the first three ecumenical councils — Nicaea (325), Constantinople (381) and Ephesus (431) — and reject the christological definition of the Council of Chalcedon (451). The communion's adherents number roughly 60 million worldwide.

Member churches

The Oriental Orthodox communion comprises six autocephalous churches: the Coptic Orthodox Church, the Syriac Orthodox Church, the Armenian Apostolic Church, the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, the Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church, and the Malankara Orthodox Syrian Church of India. Several autonomous churches are in communion with them.

History

The schism followed the Council of Chalcedon, whose definition of Christ as "in two natures" was rejected by churches in Egypt, Syria, Armenia and later Ethiopia, which adhered to the miaphysite formula of Cyril of Alexandria: "one incarnate nature of God the Word". Long labelled "Monophysite" by their opponents, Oriental Orthodox theologians reject that term, since they do not teach the single-nature doctrine of Eutyches. In the 20th and 21st centuries, official dialogues with the Eastern Orthodox, Roman Catholic and other churches have produced agreed christological statements acknowledging that the historic division rested partly on terminology and politics.

Worship

The churches celebrate ancient liturgies — the Liturgy of St Mark in Egypt, St James in the Syriac tradition, and St Basil and others — in languages including Coptic, Syriac, Armenian, Ge'ez and Malayalam, alongside local vernaculars.
