Tokugawa shogunate

The Tokugawa shogunate (Japanese: 徳川幕府, Tokugawa bakufu), also known as the Edo shogunate, was the military government of Japan during the Edo period from 1603 to 1868. Founded by Tokugawa Ieyasu after his victory at the Battle of Sekigahara (1600), it ruled from Edo (modern Tokyo) in the name of the Tokugawa shoguns while the emperor reigned, largely ceremonially, in Kyoto. It was the last of Japan's shogunates and presided over more than two and a half centuries of internal peace and stability.

Structure and control

The shogunate ruled directly over about a quarter of Japan's land, including the great cities and mines, while some 260 regional lords (daimyo) governed semi-autonomous domains (han). The bakufu controlled the daimyo through the sankin-kōtai system, which required them to spend alternate years at Edo and leave their families there as hostages, and through laws governing marriages, castle building and succession. Society was formally ordered into the four classes of the shi-nō-kō-shō hierarchy: samurai, peasants, artisans and merchants, with the samurai monopolising arms and office.

Foreign relations

From the 1630s the shogunate enforced the sakoku policy of restricted foreign contact: Japanese were forbidden to leave, Christianity was suppressed, and trade was confined to the Dutch and Chinese at Nagasaki, plus regulated relations through Tsushima (with Korea), Satsuma (with the Ryukyus) and Matsumae (with the Ainu). The isolation ended when Commodore Matthew Perry's American squadron arrived in 1853, forcing the opening of ports and unequal treaties that badly damaged the shogunate's prestige.

Fall

Economic strain, samurai discontent and the humiliation of the foreign treaties fed the sonnō jōi ("revere the emperor, expel the barbarians") movement. The domains of Satsuma and Chōshū allied against the bakufu, and after the brief Boshin War the last shogun, Tokugawa Yoshinobu, returned political authority to the emperor in 1867–68. The Meiji Restoration that followed abolished the shogunate, the domains and eventually the samurai class itself.
