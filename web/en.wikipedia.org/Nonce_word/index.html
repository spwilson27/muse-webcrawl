Nonce word

Nonce word
FieldLinguistics, lexicography
EtymologyMiddle English "for the nonce" — for the one occasion
SynonymOccasionalism

A nonce word (also called an occasionalism) is a word created or used for a single occasion — coined to fill a momentary expressive need, with no expectation that it will enter the general vocabulary. The term derives from the Middle English phrase for þe naness ("for the one [purpose/occasion]"), a rebracketing of for then anes. Nonce formations are typically transparent compounds, blends, or derivations produced by the normal word-formation rules of a language, and they illustrate the productivity of those rules.

Literary authors are prolific creators of nonce words

Examples

Literature is rich in nonce words. James Joyce coined many in Finnegans Wake, including "quark" — which the physicist Murray Gell-Mann later borrowed for the elementary particle, transforming it into a permanent scientific term. Lewis Carroll's portmanteaus in "Jabberwocky" ("slithy," "galumphing," "chortle") were nonce creations, though "chortle" and "galumph" became established words. Shakespeare is credited with countless one-off formations, and Dr. Seuss popularized whimsical coinages for children.

Nonce words in linguistics

Linguists use nonce words experimentally to test morphological productivity: in Jean Berko Gleason's famous 1958 "wug test," children shown a drawing of an imaginary creature called a "wug" readily supplied the plural "wugs," demonstrating that speakers command general rules rather than memorized forms. Invented nonce words that respect a language's phonotactics are called phonesthetically possible pseudowords, and they are standard probes in psycholinguistics.

From nonce to established

Most nonce words vanish immediately, but some are taken up by other speakers and cease to be nonce words, becoming neologisms and eventually ordinary vocabulary — a path followed by words such as "quark" and "chortle." Lexicographers treat a hapax legomenon, a word attested only once in a corpus, as a related but distinct concept concerning attestation rather than coinage.
