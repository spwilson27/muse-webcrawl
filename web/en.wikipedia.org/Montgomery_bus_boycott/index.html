Montgomery bus boycott

DateDecember 5, 1955 – December 20, 1956
LocationMontgomery, Alabama, US
Caused byRacial segregation on public buses; arrest of Rosa Parks
ResultBus segregation ruled unconstitutional (Browder v. Gayle)

The Montgomery bus boycott was a political and social protest campaign against the policy of racial segregation on the public transit system of Montgomery, Alabama. It was a foundational event in the civil rights movement in the United States. The campaign lasted from December 5, 1955 — the Monday after Rosa Parks, an African-American woman, was arrested for her refusal to surrender her seat to a white person — to December 20, 1956.

Background and course

Black activists, including the Women's Political Council led by Jo Ann Robinson and the minister Martin Luther King Jr., newly arrived at Dexter Avenue Baptist Church, organized the boycott through the Montgomery Improvement Association. Black residents, who made up the large majority of bus riders, instead walked, cycled, or used an elaborate carpool system. Participants faced harassment, arrests, and violence, including the bombing of King's home.

Resolution

In Browder v. Gayle, a federal district court ruled in June 1956 that bus segregation was unconstitutional under the Fourteenth Amendment; the U.S. Supreme Court upheld the decision in November 1956. The boycott ended after 381 days, when the court order was served and Montgomery's buses were integrated. The protest launched King to national prominence and demonstrated the power of nonviolent mass direct action.
