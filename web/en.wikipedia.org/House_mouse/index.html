House mouse

The house mouse (Mus musculus) is a small mammal of the order Rodentia, characteristically having a pointed snout, large rounded ears, and a long and almost hairless tail. It is one of the most abundant species of the genus Mus. Although a wild animal, the house mouse has benefited significantly from associating with human habitation to the point that truly wild populations are significantly less common than the semi-tame populations near human activity.

Behaviour and ecology

House mice are primarily nocturnal and social, living in hierarchical groups. They are opportunistic omnivores, feeding on grain, seeds, insects and human food scraps. Females can produce litters of 3–14 pups every three weeks in favourable conditions, which makes the species a significant agricultural and domestic pest and a fast-spreading invasive species on islands.

Relationship with humans

The house mouse spread from Asia alongside farming and trade and now occurs on every continent except Antarctica. It damages stored food and structures, and can transmit diseases such as leptospirosis and salmonellosis. At the same time, the laboratory mouse — bred from M. musculus — is the most widely used mammalian model organism in biomedical research, and house mice are also kept as pets ("fancy mice").
