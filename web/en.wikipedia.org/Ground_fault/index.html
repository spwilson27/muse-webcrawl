Ground fault

Ground fault
FieldElectrical engineering
CauseUnintended path between a live conductor and ground
ProtectionGFCI / RCD, ground-fault relays, fuses, breakers
HazardsElectric shock, arc flash, fire

A ground fault is an unintended electrical path between a power source and a grounded surface or the earth. It occurs when current leaks from an energised conductor to ground, for example through damaged insulation, moisture, or the body of a person touching a live part. Ground faults can cause electric shock, equipment damage, arc flashes and fires.

Detection and protection

A ground fault circuit interrupter (GFCI), called a residual-current device (RCD) in many countries, continuously compares current in the hot and neutral conductors; an imbalance as small as 5 milliamperes in North American GFCIs indicates leakage and causes the device to trip within milliseconds, protecting people from shock. Industrial systems use ground-fault relays and sensors, particularly on ungrounded or high-resistance-grounded systems where a first fault may only raise an alarm.

In power systems

On utility networks, single line-to-ground faults are the most common type of fault. Their current magnitude depends on the system grounding method: solidly grounded systems produce large fault currents that trip protective devices quickly, while resonant or high-resistance grounding limits current and allows continued operation.

Causes

Typical causes include deteriorated insulation, water ingress, rodent damage, insulation failure in motors and appliances, and accidental contact during digging or construction.
