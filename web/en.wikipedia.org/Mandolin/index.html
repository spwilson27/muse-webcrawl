Mandolin

A mandolin is a stringed musical instrument in the lute family, typically with eight strings in four courses of two strings each, tuned in perfect fifths like a violin (G–D–A–E). It is played with a plectrum (pick), often using rapid repeated picking — tremolo — to sustain notes on the short-decaying strings.

History and types

The mandolin developed in 18th-century Italy from earlier mandore-like instruments; the Neapolitan type, with its deep bowl back of ribs, became standard, and makers such as the Vinaccia family refined it. In the late 19th century mandolin orchestras became popular across Europe and America. In the United States, Orville Gibson and later Lloyd Loar of the Gibson company developed carved-top, flat-back mandolins (the F-style and A-style), suited to louder ensemble playing.

Musical roles

The mandolin appears in classical music (Vivaldi wrote concertos for it; Beethoven and Mozart used it, the latter in Don Giovanni), Italian folk music, and prominently in American bluegrass, where Bill Monroe's virtuosic playing made it a lead instrument, as well as in country, folk, and rock. Larger relatives include the mandola, octave mandolin and mandocello.
