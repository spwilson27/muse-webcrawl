Scale invariance

In physics, mathematics and statistics, scale invariance is a feature of objects or laws that does not change when scales of length, energy or other variables are multiplied by a common factor. A function is scale-invariant (homogeneous) if rescaling its argument rescales the function by a factor: f(λx) = λΔ f(x) for some exponent Δ. Scale invariance implies the absence of a characteristic scale and is closely connected with power laws and self-similarity.

Examples

Fractals such as the Koch snowflake and Sierpiński triangle are scale-invariant in a geometrical sense. In physics, scale invariance appears at critical points of phase transitions, where fluctuations occur on all length scales and correlations decay as power laws; the renormalisation group explains how effective theories flow toward scale-invariant fixed points. Quantum field theories that are scale-invariant are usually also conformally invariant, and conformal field theories play a central role in statistical mechanics and string theory. Unbroken scale symmetry is, however, typically broken by quantum effects, giving rise to trace anomalies.

Applications

Scale-free distributions arise in many natural and social systems: the Gutenberg–Richter law for earthquakes, city-size distributions (Zipf's law), critical phenomena, turbulence, and scale-free networks in biology and the internet. In probability, the Gaussian, Cauchy and Lévy stable distributions are connected with scaling limits, and random walks display exact statistical self-similarity: a Brownian path magnified appropriately is statistically identical to the original.
