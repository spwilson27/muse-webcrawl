Four color theorem

FieldGraph theory, topology
Conjectured1852 (Francis Guthrie)
Proved1976 (Kenneth Appel, Wolfgang Haken)

In mathematics, the four color theorem, or the four color map theorem, states that no more than four colors are required to color the regions of any map so that no two adjacent regions have the same color. Adjacent means that two regions share a segment of their respective boundaries, not merely a point. It was the first major theorem to be proved using a computer.

History

The conjecture was first proposed in 1852 when Francis Guthrie, while trying to color a map of the counties of England, noticed that four colors sufficed. It circulated through his brother to Augustus De Morgan. False proofs were published by Alfred Kempe (1879) and Peter Guthrie Tait (1880); Kempe's argument stood for eleven years before Percy Heawood found its flaw — though Heawood salvaged from it a proof that five colors always suffice. Many equivalent formulations recast the problem in terms of graph coloring and planar graphs.

The Appel–Haken proof

In 1976 Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken at the University of Illinois announced a proof: they showed that any counterexample must contain one of 1,936 (later 1,476) reducible configurations, each checked by more than a thousand hours of computer time. The reliance on computation provoked philosophical debate about whether the result had truly been "proved".

Later developments

A simpler proof by Robertson, Sanders, Seymour and Thomas (1997) reduced the case load to 633 configurations. In 2005 Georges Gonthier produced a fully machine-verified formal proof in the Coq proof assistant, removing lingering doubts about correctness of both the argument and the software.
