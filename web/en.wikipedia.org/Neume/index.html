A neume is the basic element in the Western and Eastern systems of musical notation prior to the invention of five-line staff notation, and remains the notation of Gregorian chant today. Neumes are signs written above the words of a chant text indicating melodic gestures — a single note or a group of two to four (or more) notes sung to one syllable — though in their earliest forms they conveyed the shape of a melody rather than exact pitches.

History

Neumatic notation appeared in the West in the 9th century, probably derived from accent marks, cheironomic (hand-gesture) signs, or Byzantine notation. The earliest surviving chant manuscripts with neumes, such as the St. Gallen Cantatorium (c. 920), use "unheighted" neumes written in open space (in campo aperto): they reminded singers who already knew the melodies how each phrase rose and fell, but could not be read at sight. During the 10th and 11th centuries scribes increasingly placed neumes at relative heights, and the development of ruled lines — culminating in the four-line staff associated with Guido d'Arezzo — made pitch exact ("diastematic" notation). Regional families of neumes (St. Gallen, Beneventan, Mozarabic, Aquitanian, and others) differed in their signs.

In the 12th and 13th centuries, square notation on four-line staves — the ancestor of the notation still printed in the Liber Usualis and Graduale Romanum — became standard for chant in the Latin West. Byzantine chant developed its own neumatic systems, the medieval forms of which were reformed in the early 19th century into the notation still used in the Greek Orthodox Church.

Basic neumes

Single notes are the punctum (low) and virga (high). Two-note neumes include the ascending podatus and descending clivis; three-note forms include the torculus (up-down), porrectus (down-up), and climacus (descending steps). Special signs such as the quilisma and oriscus, and the use of liquescent forms, add nuance whose precise rhythmic and vocal interpretation — studied by the semiology school centered on Solesmes Abbey — remains a field of scholarly debate.
