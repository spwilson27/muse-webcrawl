Ferret

The ferret (Mustela furo) is a small domesticated mammal belonging to the family Mustelidae, which also includes weasels, otters, and wolverines. It is the domesticated form of the European polecat (Mustela putorius), from which it was bred more than two thousand years ago, traditionally for hunting rabbits and rodents. Ferrets are now popular household pets in many countries, though their keeping is restricted in some jurisdictions.

Description

Ferrets have long, slender bodies typically 38–50 cm in length including the tail, with males ("hobs") noticeably larger than females ("jills"). They are obligate carnivores with a short digestive tract and a high metabolism, needing frequent meals rich in animal protein. Their colour varieties include the dark-masked sable, albino, and silver. Ferrets sleep for long periods — often 14 to 18 hours a day — and are most active at dawn and dusk.

History

Ferrets were probably domesticated by the Romans or earlier Mediterranean peoples for rabbit hunting, a practice called ferreting. They spread through Europe in the Middle Ages and were later used to run cables through pipes and aircraft conduits. In the 19th and 20th centuries they became laboratory animals, notably in research on influenza, to which they are susceptible in ways similar to humans.

Behaviour and care

Highly social and playful, ferrets perform a hopping, twisting display of excitement known as the "weasel war dance". They are intelligent and curious but prone to health problems including adrenal gland disease and insulinoma, particularly in North American pet populations. Ferrets are illegal as pets in California and Hawaii in the United States and are heavily regulated in places such as New Zealand, where escaped ferrets threaten native wildlife.
