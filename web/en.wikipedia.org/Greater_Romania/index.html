Greater Romania

The Kingdom of Romania, 1918–1940, at its greatest territorial extent.

Greater Romania (Romanian: România Mare) refers to the territory of the Kingdom of Romania in the interwar period, when the country reached its largest extent, and more generally to the ideal of uniting all Romanian-inhabited lands in a single state. Between 1918 and 1940 Romania covered about 295,049 km² (113,919 sq mi) and had a population of roughly 18–20 million.

Formation

Greater Romania was created in the aftermath of the First World War. In 1918 Bessarabia, Bukovina, and Transylvania (with the Banat, Crișana, and Maramureș) united with the Old Kingdom, unions confirmed by the treaties of Versailles, Saint-Germain, Neuilly, and Trianon (1919–1920). King Ferdinand I and Queen Marie, crowned at Alba Iulia in 1922, presided over the enlarged state.

Interwar period and loss

The enlarged Romania was a multi-ethnic state in which ethnic Romanians formed about 72% of the population, alongside significant Hungarian, German, Jewish, Ukrainian, and other minorities. In 1940, under pressure from Nazi Germany and the Soviet Union, Romania lost Bessarabia and northern Bukovina to the USSR, northern Transylvania to Hungary (Second Vienna Award), and southern Dobruja to Bulgaria (Treaty of Craiova). After the Second World War, Romania recovered northern Transylvania but not the Soviet-annexed territories; Bessarabia's successor state is the modern Republic of Moldova, and the term "Greater Romania" survives in nationalist politics and in the name of a Romanian political party.
