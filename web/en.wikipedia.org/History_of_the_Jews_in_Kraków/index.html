History of the Jews in Kraków


The Old Synagogue (Stara Synagoga) in Kazimierz, built in the 15th century

The history of the Jews in Kraków dates from the 13th century, when Kraków was the capital of the Kingdom of Poland. For centuries the adjacent town of Kazimierz was one of the great centres of Jewish scholarship and culture in Europe, home to the rabbi Moses Isserles (the Rema). Before the Second World War about 56,000–60,000 Jews — roughly a quarter of the city's population — lived in Kraków. The community was almost entirely destroyed in the Holocaust; today a small but revived Jewish community exists, centred again on Kazimierz.

Early settlement

Jewish merchants are recorded in Kraków from the 13th century; a Jewish street (ulica Żydowska) existed near the main square. Persecutions and restrictions prompted movement across the Vistula to Kazimierz, founded in 1335 by Casimir III the Great, who extended royal protection to Polish Jews. In 1495 the Jews of Kraków were formally expelled to Kazimierz, whose Jewish quarter (Oppidum Judaeorum) was walled and self-governing, with synagogues, a mikveh, cemetery and market.

Golden age

In the 16th century Kazimierz became a renowned centre of learning. Moses Isserles (1520–1572), rabbi of Kraków, wrote the Mappah, the glosses on the Shulchan Aruch that shaped Ashkenazi practice; his synagogue and the Remuh Cemetery remain landmarks. Hebrew printing presses operated from the 1530s. The community grew rich on trade with Silesia, Hungary and the Levant, and the Old Synagogue, rebuilt in Renaissance style after a fire, hosted royal visits. The era ended with the Swedish invasions (1655–57) and subsequent economic decline.

Partitions and emancipation

After the Third Partition of Poland (1795), Kraków fell to Austria; Joseph II's reforms imposed German schooling and surnames. The Free City of Kraków (1815–46) preceded direct Austrian rule. Emancipation came in 1867–68, and Jews entered the professions, city council and Polish cultural life, while many remained traditional in Kazimierz. A progressive synagogue (Tempel) served the acculturated community. By 1910 Jews numbered about 32,000 in Kraków; by 1939, around 56,000–60,000, with a dense network of parties (Zionist, Bundist, Agudist), schools, newspapers and sports clubs.

The Holocaust

Germany occupied Kraków on 6 September 1939 and made it capital of the General Government under Hans Frank. Most Jews were expelled to the countryside in 1940; the remaining ~15,000–17,000 were confined to a ghetto in Podgórze (March 1941). Deportations to Bełżec began in 1942; the ghetto was liquidated on 13–14 March 1943, with thousands murdered on the spot or sent to Auschwitz, and survivors transferred to the Płaszów labour camp, notorious under commandant Amon Göth. The businessman Oskar Schindler, whose enamel factory employed Jewish workers, saved some 1,100–1,200 of them — the story later told in Schindler's List. Only a few thousand Kraków Jews survived the war.

Post-war and revival

Some survivors returned after 1945, but anti-Jewish violence — notably the Kraków pogrom of August 1945 and the Kielce pogrom of 1946 — and the antisemitic campaign of 1968 drove most to emigrate. Under communism, Jewish institutions languished. After 1989, Kazimierz was restored: the annual Jewish Culture Festival (founded 1988) became one of the world's largest; a Jewish Community Centre opened in 2008; and seven historic synagogues survive, including the Old Synagogue museum and the still-active Remuh and Izaak synagogues. Thousands of descendants of Kraków Jews live in Israel and the diaspora.
