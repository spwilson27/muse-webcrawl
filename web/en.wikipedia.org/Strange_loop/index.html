Strange loop

A strange loop is a cyclic structure that goes through several levels in a hierarchical system, in which moving upward (or downward) through the levels eventually returns one to the starting point — a "tangled hierarchy". The concept was developed by Douglas Hofstadter in his books Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979) and I Am a Strange Loop (2007), where it is central to his account of self-reference and consciousness.

Examples

Classic examples include M. C. Escher's lithograph Drawing Hands, in which two hands draw each other into existence; the Penrose stairs, an impossible staircase that perpetually ascends; Gödel's incompleteness theorems, where a formal system encodes statements about itself; and the liar paradox ("this sentence is false").

Consciousness

Hofstadter argues that the human sense of self — the "I" — is itself a strange loop: the brain's symbolic system becomes rich enough to represent itself, and the resulting self-referential loop is what consciousness consists in. The idea has influenced philosophy of mind, cognitive science, and discussions of self-reference in logic and computer science, including quines (programs that print their own source code).
