Estates of the realm

The estates of the realm, or three estates, were the broad orders of social hierarchy used in Christian Europe from the Middle Ages to the early modern period. A polity organised on this model divided society into the clergy (First Estate), the nobility (Second Estate), and the commoners (Third Estate), each regarded as having a distinct God-given function: to pray, to fight, and to work.

The three estates

The classic formulation, found in the writings of Adalbero of Laon around the year 1000, described oratores (those who pray), bellatores (those who fight), and laboratores (those who labour). In France the three estates were represented separately in the Estates General; in Sweden a fourth estate, the peasantry, sat in the Riksdag of the Estates.

Decline

The estate system eroded with the growth of towns, the rise of royal bureaucracies, and Enlightenment criticism of privilege. In France, the Abbé Sieyès' pamphlet What Is the Third Estate? (1789) attacked the system months before the Estates General of 1789 voted its own abolition; legal privileges of the first two estates were swept away on the night of 4 August 1789.

Legacy

The phrase "Fourth Estate" was later applied to the press, and "estate" survives in the names of assemblies such as the States General of the Netherlands.
