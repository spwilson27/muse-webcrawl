Anglo-Saxon art

Anglo-Saxon art covers art produced within the Anglo-Saxon period of English history, beginning with the migration period style that the Anglo-Saxons brought with them from the continent in the 5th century, and ending in 1066 with the Norman Conquest. It encompasses metalwork, sculpture, illuminated manuscripts, ivory carving, embroidery, and architecture.

Early period

Early Anglo-Saxon art is dominated by metalwork from pagan burials, notably the ship burial at Sutton Hoo (c. 620s), whose gold and garnet cloisonné jewellery, helmet, and shield reveal a sophisticated Germanic animal style of interlacing beasts. Brooches, buckles, and weapon fittings display the Salin Style I and II animal ornament.

Christian art and manuscripts

Conversion to Christianity from 597 brought Mediterranean and Insular influences. Northumbrian monasteries produced the Lindisfarne Gospels (c. 700–715), a masterpiece of Insular illumination combining Celtic, Germanic, and Mediterranean motifs. Stone high crosses such as those at Ruthwell and Bewcastle carried carved biblical scenes and runic inscriptions, including verses of The Dream of the Rood.

Later period

After Viking disruption, a revival under Alfred the Great and the 10th-century Benedictine Reform produced the Winchester School of manuscript illumination, characterised by swirling acanthus borders and expressive drapery, seen in the Benedictional of St Æthelwold. Late Anglo-Saxon art includes the Bayeux Tapestry — an embroidery probably made in England — and fine metalwork, ivories, and stone sculpture.

Survival

Much Anglo-Saxon art was destroyed at the Reformation and in the melting down of church treasures; surviving works are concentrated in the British Museum, the British Library, and cathedral collections.
