Recovered-memory therapy

Recovered-memory therapy sought to retrieve supposedly repressed memories of childhood trauma.

Recovered-memory therapy is a discredited form of psychotherapy that aims to recover supposedly repressed memories of childhood trauma — typically sexual abuse — believed to be the hidden cause of a patient's current psychological symptoms. Techniques associated with it included hypnosis, guided imagery, age regression, dream interpretation and the use of suggestive questioning. The approach flourished in the 1980s and early 1990s and became the subject of intense scientific, legal and public controversy.

The memory wars

Patients in recovered-memory therapy sometimes came to report elaborate memories of abuse, including, in some cases, allegations connected with the satanic ritual abuse panic. Families were fractured by accusations, and some accused parents sued therapists. In 1992 the False Memory Syndrome Foundation was founded to support accused families and to publicise research showing that detailed, emotionally vivid memories can be created by suggestion.

Memory researchers such as Elizabeth Loftus demonstrated that false memories of entire events could be implanted in a substantial minority of experimental participants using suggestive techniques — the "lost in the mall" paradigm — and argued that therapy-recovered memories were unreliable unless independently corroborated. Critics of the recovered-memory movement replied that traumatic amnesia and later recall do occur, and that dismissing all such reports harmed genuine survivors.

Legal and professional consequences

Several high-profile cases reshaped the landscape. Some patients retracted their recovered memories and sued their former therapists, winning substantial damages; third-party suits by accused parents also succeeded. Professional bodies, including the American Psychological Association and the British Psychological Society, issued statements urging caution: memories recovered in therapy should be treated as unverified unless corroborated, and techniques known to heighten suggestibility should be avoided. The use of recovered memories in criminal prosecutions declined sharply, and the practices associated with recovered-memory therapy have largely been abandoned by mainstream practitioners, though debates about dissociative amnesia continue in the scientific literature.
