Adherens junction

Adherens junction
TypeCell–cell junction
Key proteinsCadherins (E-cadherin), β-catenin, α-catenin, p120-catenin
Cytoskeletal linkActin filaments
LocationEpithelia (zonula adherens belt), cardiomyocytes, synapses
FunctionMechanical adhesion, tissue integrity, signalling

Adherens junctions (or zonula adherens when forming a continuous belt) are protein complexes that occur at cell–cell junctions in epithelial and endothelial tissues, usually just below tight junctions. They connect the actin cytoskeletons of adjacent cells, providing mechanical strength to tissues while also participating in intracellular signalling.

Structure

The core adhesion molecule is a classical cadherin — E-cadherin in epithelia, N-cadherin in neural and muscle tissue, VE-cadherin in endothelium. Cadherin extracellular domains bind homophilically in a calcium-dependent manner across the intercellular gap. Inside the cell, the cadherin tail binds p120-catenin and β-catenin; β-catenin binds α-catenin, which links the complex dynamically to actin filaments. This coupling lets junctions bear and transmit tension across the tissue.

Functions

Adherens junctions initiate and stabilise cell–cell adhesion; their assembly precedes and enables formation of tight junctions and desmosomes. They sense and respond to mechanical forces (mechanotransduction), regulate cell proliferation through the Hippo/YAP pathway, and sequester β-catenin, tying adhesion to Wnt signalling. During development they are remodelled in processes such as epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and collective cell migration.

Clinical significance

Loss of E-cadherin is a hallmark of invasive carcinoma and EMT; germline mutations in its gene (CDH1) cause hereditary diffuse gastric cancer syndrome. Mutations in junctional proteins also underlie certain skin-blistering and cardiac disorders (e.g. Naxos disease involves the related desmosomal proteins).
