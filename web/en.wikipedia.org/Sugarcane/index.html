Sugarcane

Sugarcane or sugar cane is a species of tall, perennial grass (genus Saccharum, tribe Andropogoneae) native to the warm temperate and tropical regions of South Asia and Melanesia. It is used for sugar production. Sugarcane has stout, jointed, fibrous stalks that are rich in the sugar sucrose, which accumulates in the stalk internodes. The plant is two to six metres (six to twenty feet) tall.

History

Sugarcane was domesticated in New Guinea around 6000 BC. From there it spread westward to Island Southeast Asia and India, where it hybridised with wild species. Crystallised sugar was reported in India by the time of the Gupta Empire. The crop was carried to the Americas by Europeans; Christopher Columbus brought sugarcane to the Caribbean on his second voyage in 1493. Plantation production, historically reliant on enslaved labour, transformed the economies and societies of the Caribbean and Brazil.

Production

Sugarcane is the world's largest crop by production quantity. Brazil is the largest producer, followed by India, China, and Thailand. About 70% of the world's sugar is produced from sugarcane, the remainder mostly from sugar beet. Sugarcane is propagated from stem cuttings (setts) and is harvested either by hand or by mechanical harvesters; the same planting can be ratooned — regrown — for several harvests.

Uses

Besides table sugar, sugarcane yields molasses, bagasse (the fibrous residue, burned for energy or used in paper and board), and juice. Fermentation and distillation produce rum and the Brazilian spirit cachaça. In Brazil, sugarcane ethanol is a major vehicle fuel. Sugarcane wax, jaggery, and falernum are also derived from the plant.

Environmental impact

Sugarcane cultivation requires large quantities of water and land, and has been associated with deforestation, soil erosion, and water pollution, though bagasse-fuelled cogeneration can make sugar mills energy self-sufficient or even net exporters of electricity.
