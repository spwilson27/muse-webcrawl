In moral and political philosophy, the social contract is a theory or model that originated during the Age of Enlightenment and usually, although not always, addresses the legitimacy of the authority of the state over the individual. Social contract arguments typically are postulated that individuals have consented, either explicitly or tacitly, to surrender some of their freedoms and submit to the authority (of the ruler, or to the decision of a majority) in exchange for protection of their remaining rights or maintenance of the social order.

Hobbes

Thomas Hobbes, in Leviathan (1651), described life in the state of nature as "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." To escape the war of all against all, individuals covenant with one another to transfer their rights to an absolute sovereign. For Hobbes the contract is irrevocable and the sovereign is not himself a party to it.

Locke

John Locke's Two Treatises of Government (1689) offered a more limited account: in the state of nature people possess natural rights to life, liberty, and property, and government arises by consent to protect those rights. A government that violates its trust may be resisted and replaced—an argument that profoundly influenced the American and French revolutions.

Rousseau

Jean-Jacques Rousseau's The Social Contract (1762) opens with the famous line "Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains." Rousseau argued that legitimate political authority must express the general will, and that citizens remain free by obeying laws they have prescribed to themselves as members of the sovereign people.

Later developments

Immanuel Kant treated the contract as an idea of reason by which to test laws. In the twentieth century, John Rawls revived contract theory in A Theory of Justice (1971), asking which principles parties would choose behind a "veil of ignorance" in the original position. Critics, including feminist and critical-race theorists such as Carole Pateman and Charles Mills, have argued that historical contracts excluded women and non-white peoples, embedding domination within the supposedly universal agreement.
