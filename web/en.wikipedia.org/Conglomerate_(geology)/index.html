Conglomerate (geology)

Rounded clasts cemented in a conglomerate.

Conglomerate is a clastic sedimentary rock composed of a substantial fraction of rounded to subangular gravel-sized clasts, larger than 2 mm in diameter, set in a finer-grained matrix of sand, silt, or natural cement. It contrasts with breccia, in which the large clasts are angular, indicating little transport. Conglomerates form where strong currents or waves move and round coarse debris, and their composition reflects the rocks exposed in the source area.

Classification

Geologists classify conglomerates by the diversity of their clasts and by their support fabric. Monomict conglomerates contain clasts of one rock type, while polymict (oligomict and petromict) varieties mix several. In clast-supported (orthoconglomerate) rocks the gravels touch one another, typical of river and beach deposits; in matrix-supported paraconglomerates the clasts float in mud, as in glacial tillites and debris-flow deposits.

Formation and environments

Conglomerates accumulate in high-energy environments: alluvial fans at mountain fronts, braided rivers, rocky beaches, and submarine fans fed by turbidity currents. Because clasts round and shrink with transport, thick conglomerate sequences often record nearby uplift and erosion, as in the molasse deposits fringing young mountain ranges.

Significance

Some conglomerates are economically important: the gold- and uranium-bearing conglomerates of South Africa's Witwatersrand Basin are the world's greatest source of gold, interpreted as ancient placer deposits, and diamond-bearing examples occur elsewhere. Conglomerates are also used as dimension and decorative stone, the polished rock known historically as puddingstone.
