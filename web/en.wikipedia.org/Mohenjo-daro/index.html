Mohenjo-daro (Sindhi: "Mound of the Dead Men") was an ancient city of the Indus Valley Civilisation, built around 2600 BCE on the west bank of the Indus River in present-day Sindh, Pakistan. One of the largest settlements of the ancient world and among the earliest major cities anywhere, it was contemporary with the civilisations of ancient Egypt, Mesopotamia, and Minoan Crete. At its peak it may have housed some 40,000 people. It was abandoned around 1900 BCE and rediscovered in 1922.

Discovery and excavation

The site was identified in 1922 by R. D. Banerji of the Archaeological Survey of India and excavated on a large scale in the 1920s and 1930s under John Marshall, Ernest Mackay, and others; later campaigns were led by Mortimer Wheeler (1950) and George F. Dales (1964–65). The finds established that the Indus civilisation was a third great early civilisation alongside Egypt and Mesopotamia. In 1980 Mohenjo-daro became the first site in South Asia inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.

Layout and architecture

Mohenjo-daro was built on a grid plan of streets aligned to the cardinal directions, among the earliest examples of deliberate urban planning. The city divides into a raised western Citadel mound and a Lower Town. On the Citadel stand the Great Bath — a watertight brick tank, about 12 × 7 metres, probably used for ritual bathing — a large granary or assembly structure, and a later Buddhist stupa. The Lower Town's houses of fired brick, often two storeys around courtyards, were served by an extraordinary drainage system: covered street drains, household bathrooms, soak pits, and nearly 700 wells — roughly one per three houses.

Remarkably, the city shows no obvious palace, temple, or royal tombs, leading scholars to debate whether Indus society was governed by kings, priests, merchants, or some more collective arrangement.

Artefacts

Famous finds include the bronze "Dancing Girl" statuette, the steatite bust known as the "Priest-King", carved steatite seals bearing animal motifs and the still-undeciphered Indus script, and standardised weights and measures. Finds of Indus goods in Mesopotamia attest to maritime trade between the two civilisations.

Decline

From around 1900 BCE Mohenjo-daro was abandoned as the Indus civilisation deurbanised. Proposed causes include shifts in the Indus river system, flooding and silting, climate change weakening the monsoon, and declining trade — a gradual transformation rather than the violent invasion once hypothesised. The site today faces conservation threats from groundwater salinity, weathering, and extreme flooding, including damage during the 2022 Pakistan floods.
