Sukuk

Sukuk (Arabic: صكوك, plural of صك, ṣakk, "legal instrument, deed, cheque") is the Arabic name for financial certificates, similar to bonds, that comply with Islamic law (Sharia). Because fixed-income interest-bearing bonds are not permissible in Islam, sukuk securities are structured to generate returns to investors without infringing Islamic law, typically by linking returns to the performance of a real underlying asset.

Structure

Unlike a conventional bond, which represents a debt obligation, a sukuk represents an undivided beneficial ownership interest in an underlying asset or pool of assets. Common structures include ijara (lease-based), mudaraba (profit-sharing), musharaka (joint venture) and murabaha (cost-plus sale). Holders receive periodic distributions derived from the asset's cash flows and bear a share of asset-related risk.

History

The word sakk was used in the medieval Islamic world for documents acknowledging financial obligations, and is a possible origin of the English word "cheque". The modern sukuk market developed in Malaysia, which issued pioneering corporate and sovereign sukuk around the turn of the 21st century, and spread rapidly to the Gulf states. Standard-setting bodies such as AAOIFI in Bahrain publish Sharia standards for sukuk issuance.

Market

Global sukuk issuance has reached hundreds of billions of US dollars outstanding, with Malaysia and Saudi Arabia the largest markets. Sovereign sukuk are used by governments to fund infrastructure and budgets, while corporations and financial institutions issue them to diversify funding. Sukuk are traded on exchanges including Bursa Malaysia, Nasdaq Dubai and the London Stock Exchange.

Differences from conventional bonds

Key distinctions include the requirement of an underlying tangible asset, the prohibition of pure debt trading at other than par in many structures, the sharing of risk between issuer and investors, and certification by a Sharia board that the instrument complies with Islamic principles.
