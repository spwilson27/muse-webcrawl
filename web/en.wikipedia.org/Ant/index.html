Ant

Family: Formicidae
Order: Hymenoptera
Described species: over 13,800

Ants are eusocial insects of the family Formicidae, in the order Hymenoptera alongside bees and wasps, from which they evolved over 100 million years ago. More than 13,800 species are described, and their combined biomass rivals that of humanity. Ants colonise nearly every terrestrial habitat and are distinguished by their colonies — cooperative societies ranging from a few dozen individuals to millions, organised into castes of queens, workers, and males.

Biology and social organisation

An ant colony is founded by a mated queen, who lays eggs tended by sterile female workers. Workers forage, nurse brood, defend the nest, and in many species divide labour by age or body size; some species have soldier castes with enlarged heads and jaws. Communication is largely chemical, via pheromones that lay trails, raise alarms, and identify nestmates. Colonies of some species form vast supercolonies — the Argentine ant maintains interconnected populations spanning continents.

Ecology

Ants are major predators, scavengers, seed dispersers, and soil engineers. Leafcutter ants cultivate fungus gardens on harvested vegetation; weaver ants build nests from living leaves; army ants hunt in swarm raids. Many ants tend aphids and other insects for honeydew, and some plants host ants that protect them from herbivores. A few species, such as fire ants and Argentine ants, are serious invasive pests, while others, including carpenter and pharaoh ants, infest buildings.

Ants and humans

Ants feature in fable and folklore worldwide, are eaten in some cuisines, and inspire algorithms in computer science (ant colony optimisation). The scientific study of ants is myrmecology.
