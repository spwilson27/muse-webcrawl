Alkene

General formulaCnH2n (acyclic, one double bond)
Functional groupCarbon–carbon double bond (C=C)
Simplest memberEthylene (ethene)
Suffix-ene

In organic chemistry, an alkene, or olefin, is a hydrocarbon containing a carbon–carbon double bond. The double bond comprises one strong σ bond and one weaker π bond. Alkenes are generally colorless and nonpolar, and are similar to alkanes in many physical properties, but are more reactive because of the π bond.

Acyclic alkenes with only one double bond and no other functional groups (monofunctional alkenes) form a homologous series of hydrocarbons with the general formula CnH2n with n ≥ 2, the same formula as cycloalkanes. The simplest alkene, ethylene (C2H4), is the organic compound produced on the largest scale industrially; it is also a plant hormone involved in fruit ripening.

Structure and bonding

Each carbon atom of the double bond uses three sp² hybrid orbitals to form sigma bonds to three atoms, and the unhybridized p orbital on each carbon overlaps to form the π bond. The double bond is planar, with bond angles of approximately 120°, and the C=C bond length is about 1.34 Å, shorter than a C−C single bond (1.54 Å). Rotation about the double bond is restricted, which gives rise to cis–trans (E/Z) isomerism when each carbon bears two different substituents.

Nomenclature

In IUPAC nomenclature, alkenes are named by changing the -ane suffix of the corresponding alkane to -ene, with a locant indicating the position of the double bond: for example, propene, 2-butene (but-2-ene), and 1,3-butadiene. Older common names such as ethylene and propylene are retained for the simplest members.

Reactions

Alkenes are relatively stable compounds, but are more reactive than alkanes because of the reactivity of the carbon–carbon π-bond. Most reactions of alkenes involve additions to this π bond, forming new single bonds. Characteristic reactions include:

  • Hydrogenation — addition of H2 over a metal catalyst to give the alkane.
  • Halogenation — addition of halogens such as Br2 to give vicinal dihalides.
  • Hydrohalogenation — addition of HX, generally following Markovnikov's rule.
  • Hydration — acid-catalysed addition of water to give alcohols.
  • Ozonolysis and oxidation with permanganate — cleavage of the double bond.
  • Polymerization — formation of polyolefins such as polyethylene and polypropylene.

Occurrence and uses

The simplest alkenes occur in natural gas and petroleum, but ethyl­ene and propylene are manufactured on a huge scale by steam cracking of hydrocarbons. Alkenes are central feedstocks of the petrochemical industry, used to make plastics, detergents, and many other products. Many natural products — terpenes, carotenoids, and pheromones — contain alkene double bonds.
