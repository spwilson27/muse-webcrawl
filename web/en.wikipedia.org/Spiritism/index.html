Spiritism

Spiritism (French: spiritisme), also known as Kardecism, is a spiritualist philosophy codified in the 19th century by the French educator Hippolyte Léon Denizard Rivail, who wrote under the pen name Allan Kardec. It explores the nature, origin, and destiny of spirits, and their relation with the corporeal world.

Spiritism holds that humans are essentially immortal spirits temporarily inhabiting physical bodies, progressing through reincarnation toward moral and intellectual perfection. Communication with spirits of the dead through mediums is a central practice, and moral teachings — particularly charity — are emphasised.

Core works

Kardec's codification comprises five books: The Spirits' Book (1857), The Mediums' Book (1861), The Gospel According to Spiritism (1864), Heaven and Hell (1865), and The Genesis (1868). Kardec described his work as an effort to systematise the teachings he attributed to spirit communications received through mediums.

Distribution

Spiritism found its largest following in Brazil, where it arrived in the 19th century and grew through the work of figures such as the medium Chico Xavier. The Brazilian Spiritist Federation estimates millions of adherents, and Spiritist centres also exist across Latin America and Europe. The movement is distinct from Anglo-American Spiritualism, though the two share historical roots.
