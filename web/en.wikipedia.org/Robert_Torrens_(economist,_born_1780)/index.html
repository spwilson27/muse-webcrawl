Robert Torrens (economist, born 1780)

Born: 1780, Hervey Hill, County Londonderry, Ireland
Died: 27 May 1864, London, England
Occupation: Soldier, economist, politician, colonial promoter

Robert Torrens (1780 – 27 May 1864) was an Anglo-Irish soldier, political economist, politician and proponent of systematic colonisation. He served with distinction in the Peninsular War, sat in the House of Commons, and became one of the most prolific British economic writers of the early nineteenth century, contributing to debates on trade, currency, and the Corn Laws.

Military career

Torrens joined the Royal Marines in 1796 and rose through the ranks during the Napoleonic Wars. He was severely wounded at the Battle of Copenhagen in 1807 and later served in the Peninsula, where he was wounded again at the siege of Cádiz. He attained the rank of lieutenant-colonel in 1811.

Economic writings

Torrens wrote extensively on political economy. His Essay on the External Corn Trade (1815) introduced an early statement of the gains from trade that later writers associated with comparative advantage. He defended protectionist positions at times and liberal ones at others, and engaged in controversy with David Ricardo and other classical economists over rent, profits, and the currency question. His works include An Essay on the Production of Wealth (1821) and The Budget (1841–42).

Colonisation and South Australia

Torrens was a leading figure in the systematic colonisation movement associated with Edward Gibbon Wakefield. He chaired the South Australian Colonization Commission and was closely involved in the founding of the colony of South Australia. His son, Robert Richard Torrens, later premier of South Australia, introduced the Torrens title system of land registration.

Later life

Torrens represented Ipswich and later Bolton in Parliament. He died in London in 1864. Though criticised for inconsistencies in his economic thought, historians of economics regard him as a significant, if underrated, contributor to classical economic theory.
