The Soap Box Derby is a youth-oriented programme of gravity-powered car racing, founded in the United States in 1933. Participants build unpowered cars — originally from wooden soap crates and scrap materials — and race them down a hill, steering with a simple mechanism and braking at the finish. The world championship finals have been held in Akron, Ohio, since 1935.

History

The Derby was created by Dayton newspaperman Myron Scott, who photographed boys racing homemade cars in 1933 and organised a race that attracted 19 drivers. With Chevrolet sponsorship the event moved to Akron in 1935, where the purpose-built Derby Downs track opened in 1936. Races for girls were formally opened in the 1970s.

Divisions

Modern racing is organised in Stock, Super Stock, and Masters divisions, with kit cars of increasingly sophisticated design. Local race winners advance to the FirstEnergy All-American Soap Box Derby world championship each July.

In culture

The Derby became a celebrated American institution, drawing celebrities and large crowds in its mid-twentieth-century heyday. A 1973 cheating scandal, in which the winning car was found to contain a concealed electromagnet, led to tighter inspection rules and heightened scrutiny of adult involvement.
