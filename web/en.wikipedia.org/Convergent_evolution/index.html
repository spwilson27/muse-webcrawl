Convergent evolution

Convergent evolution
DefinitionIndependent evolution of similar traits in unrelated lineages
CauseSimilar selective pressures in similar environments
Classic examplesWings of birds, bats, insects; camera eyes of vertebrates and cephalopods
Contrasts withDivergent evolution; parallel evolution

Convergent evolution is the independent evolution of similar features in species of different lineages. Convergent evolution creates analogous structures that have similar form or function but were not present in the last common ancestor of those groups. The cladistic term for the same phenomenon is homoplasy.

Examples

Flight evolved independently in insects, pterosaurs, birds, and bats. The streamlined, fusiform bodies of sharks, ichthyosaurs, tuna, and dolphins converged under the demands of fast swimming. Camera-like eyes with a lens, iris, and retina arose independently in vertebrates and cephalopods, though built from different tissues. Echolocation evolved in bats and toothed whales, down to convergent changes in the same hearing genes. Marsupial mammals in Australia evolved counterparts of placental wolves, moles, and gliders elsewhere — the thylacine closely resembled a wolf despite distant kinship. Cacti in the Americas and succulent euphorbias in Africa converged on water-storing, spiny forms.

Causes

Convergence typically results from similar selective pressures: organisms adapting to similar niches face similar physical constraints, and natural selection finds similar solutions. It demonstrates that evolution is to a degree predictable — a point emphasized by paleontologist Simon Conway Morris — though historical contingency also matters, as Stephen Jay Gould argued.

Distinctions

In parallel evolution, similar traits evolve independently in closely related lineages from a similar ancestral condition, whereas convergence involves more distantly related groups. Both differ from divergent evolution, in which related populations accumulate differences. Molecular convergence — identical amino-acid substitutions in unrelated lineages — has been documented in proteins such as prestin (echolocation) and stomach lysozymes of foregut-fermenting mammals.
