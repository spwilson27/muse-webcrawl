Insulin-like growth factor 2 (IGF-2) is a peptide hormone of the insulin family that is a major growth factor in fetal development. Structurally similar to IGF-1 and proinsulin, IGF-2 is secreted principally by the liver and many other tissues, circulates bound to IGF-binding proteins, and signals mainly through the IGF-1 receptor (and the fetal insulin-receptor isoform A). Its dedicated second receptor, the IGF-2/mannose-6-phosphate receptor, does not signal for growth but internalises IGF-2 for degradation, acting as a "clearance" brake on its action.

Imprinting and genetics

IGF2 lies in the imprinted gene cluster at human chromosome 11p15.5, adjacent to the H19 non-coding RNA gene. In most tissues only the paternal copy of IGF2 is expressed while the maternal copy is silenced — one of the classic examples of genomic imprinting, controlled by an imprinting control region whose methylation state differs between the parental chromosomes. Loss of imprinting (biallelic expression) is one of the commonest epigenetic abnormalities in human cancer.

Physiology

IGF-2 is abundant in fetal blood and essential for embryonic growth: mouse knockouts are born at about 60% of normal size. In adults it continues to circulate at high concentrations (higher than IGF-1), though its postnatal roles are less growth-hormone-dependent and include functions in the brain, where it is implicated in memory consolidation, and in stem-cell niches.

Clinical significance

Overexpression of IGF-2 due to imprinting disorders of 11p15 causes the overgrowth syndrome Beckwith–Wiedemann syndrome, with increased risk of childhood tumours such as Wilms tumour; conversely, hypomethylation affecting the same locus contributes to the growth-restriction disorder Silver–Russell syndrome. Some tumours (e.g. solitary fibrous tumours and certain sarcomas and hepatocellular carcinomas) secrete incompletely processed "big" IGF-2, which can cause non-islet-cell tumour hypoglycaemia (Doege–Potter syndrome) by suppressing insulin and growth hormone.
