Hepatic portal system

System: Circulatory
Vein: Hepatic portal vein
Drains to: Liver sinusoids

The hepatic portal system is the system of veins that comprises the hepatic portal vein and its tributaries. It is responsible for directing blood from parts of the gastrointestinal tract to the liver. Substances absorbed in the small intestine travel first to the liver for processing before continuing to the heart. Not all of the gastrointestinal tract is part of this system: it extends from about the lower portion of the esophagus to the upper part of the anal canal, and also includes venous drainage from the spleen, pancreas, and gallbladder.

Structure

The hepatic portal vein is formed by the confluence of the superior mesenteric vein and the splenic vein behind the neck of the pancreas. The inferior mesenteric vein usually joins the splenic vein. The portal vein is about 8 cm long in adults and carries roughly 75 percent of the blood supplied to the liver; the remainder arrives via the hepatic artery proper. Blood leaves the liver through the hepatic veins, which drain into the inferior vena cava.

Function

The system performs a first-pass function: nutrients absorbed from the gut, such as glucose and amino acids, are delivered directly to hepatocytes, which store, modify, or detoxify them. The liver also removes bacteria and worn-out red blood cells (via Kupffer cells and splenic drainage) before the blood re-enters the general circulation. Many orally administered drugs are substantially metabolised during this first pass.

Clinical significance

Portal hypertension — increased pressure in the portal system, most often caused by cirrhosis — can lead to portosystemic collaterals, esophageal varices, splenomegaly, and ascites. Where the portal and systemic circulations anastomose (esophagus, rectum, umbilicus, retroperitoneum), dilated collaterals may bleed life-threateningly.
