Egocentrism

Egocentrism is the inability to differentiate between one's own perspective and that of another person, or to understand that another person's viewpoint may differ from one's own. In developmental psychology the term was introduced by Jean Piaget to describe a characteristic of young children's thinking in the preoperational stage (roughly ages two to seven), in which the child assumes that others see, hear and feel exactly as the child does.

Piaget's account

Piaget demonstrated egocentrism with tasks such as the "three mountains" experiment, in which a child seated before a model of three mountains is asked to choose the photograph matching what a doll on the other side sees; preoperational children typically choose their own view. Egocentric speech — children's monologues and collective monologues not adapted to a listener — was likewise interpreted by Piaget as evidence of this cognitive limitation. Lev Vygotsky reinterpreted such speech as "private speech" serving self-regulation, a view supported by later research.

Adolescent egocentrism

David Elkind extended the concept to adolescence, describing the imaginary audience (the belief that one is the constant focus of others' attention) and the personal fable (a sense of uniqueness and invulnerability), which he linked to adolescent risk-taking and self-consciousness.

Adult egocentrism and related concepts

Although egocentrism declines with the development of theory of mind, studies show that adults retain measurable egocentric biases — for example the curse of knowledge, in which people overestimate how much others share their knowledge, and egocentric anchoring in perspective-taking tasks, where adults must effortfully correct an initial self-centred interpretation. Egocentrism in this technical sense is distinct from selfishness, egotism and narcissism, though the terms are often confused in everyday usage.
