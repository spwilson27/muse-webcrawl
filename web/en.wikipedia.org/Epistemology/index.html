Epistemology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Epistemology is the branch of philosophy that studies the nature, origin and scope of knowledge, epistemic justification, the rationality of belief, and related issues. It examines what knowledge is, how it can be acquired, and how we can know that we know something. An epistemologist is a philosopher who specialises in the theory of knowledge. Epistemology is considered a major subfield of philosophy, together with other major subfields such as ethics, logic, and metaphysics.

The word derives from the Greek epistēmē ("knowledge") and logos ("account"). Debates in epistemology are generally clustered around four core areas: the philosophical analysis of the nature of knowledge and how it relates to concepts such as truth, belief and justification; various problems of skepticism; the sources and scope of knowledge and justified belief; and the criteria for knowledge and justification.

The nature of knowledge

Since Plato, knowledge has traditionally been analysed as "justified true belief": to know that p, one must believe p, p must be true, and one's belief must be justified. In 1963, Edmund Gettier published a famous three-page paper presenting counterexamples — cases of justified true belief that seem to be mere luck rather than knowledge — launching decades of attempts to repair the analysis by adding a fourth condition or replacing justification with reliability or truth-tracking. Epistemologists also distinguish propositional knowledge (knowledge-that) from procedural knowledge (know-how) and knowledge by acquaintance.

Sources of knowledge

Perception, memory, introspection, testimony and reason are the standard candidate sources. Empiricists hold that all substantive knowledge derives from experience, while rationalists maintain that some knowledge is innate or justified independently of experience (a priori). The debate over induction — how observations justify general conclusions — was posed sharply by Hume and reworked by philosophers of science such as Karl Popper, who proposed falsification instead of inductive verification.

Skepticism

Skeptical challenges ask whether any of our beliefs are genuinely justified or known. Ancient Pyrrhonists suspended judgment to achieve tranquillity; Descartes used methodical doubt — the evil demon and dreaming arguments — to search for certainty, concluding with the cogito. Modern responses include foundationalism (some beliefs are basic and need no support), coherentism (beliefs are justified by mutual fit), reliabilism (beliefs are justified if produced by reliable processes), and contextualism. Epistemologists also debate internalism versus externalism about justification, the structure of epistemic justification, and the value of knowledge.

Applied and social epistemology

Contemporary epistemology extends to social questions: how knowledge is distributed in groups, the epistemology of testimony and disagreement, epistemic injustice (Miranda Fricker's term for wrongs done to people in their capacity as knowers), and the epistemic health of public discourse and the internet.
