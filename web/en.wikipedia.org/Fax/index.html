Fax

Full nameFacsimile transmission
Invented1843 (Bain's electric printing telegraph); commercialised 1960s–80s
StandardITU-T Group 3 (T.30/T.4)
StatusLargely superseded by email and e-fax services

Fax (short for facsimile), sometimes called telecopying or telefax, is the telephonic transmission of scanned printed material (both text and images), normally to a telephone number connected to a printer or other output device. The document is scanned, converted into an electrical signal, sent over the telephone network, and reproduced as a paper copy at the receiving end.

History

Scottish inventor Alexander Bain patented an image-scanning telegraph in 1843, and Frederick Bakewell demonstrated an improved version in 1851. Wire services transmitted photographs by wirephoto in the early 20th century. Fax became a mass business technology in the 1980s after the ITU's Group 3 standard (1980) let machines from different makers interoperate and transmit a page in about a minute over ordinary phone lines. In Japan, fax use became especially widespread, partly due to the demands of handwritten Japanese text.

Decline and persistence

From the late 1990s, email, PDFs, and the web displaced fax for most purposes. Fax nevertheless persists in healthcare, law, government, and parts of Japanese business life, where signatures, established workflows, and regulatory expectations have slowed its disappearance. Internet fax services (FoIP, T.38, and email-to-fax gateways) now carry much of the remaining traffic.
