Series A round

A Series A round is typically a startup's first significant institutional financing

A Series A round is the first round of financing that follows seed funding in a startup company, and is generally the name given to the company's first significant round of venture capital financing. The name refers to the class of preferred stock sold to investors in exchange for their investment. Series A rounds are typically raised once a company has developed a track record — an established user base, consistent revenue figures, or some other key performance indicator — and are used to further optimize its product and business model.

Process

In a Series A round, investors receive newly issued preferred shares (Series A preferred stock) which carry rights senior to common stock, often including liquidation preferences and board representation. Valuations are negotiated between the company and lead investors, and the round commonly dilutes existing shareholders by roughly 10 to 30 percent. In the United States, Series A rounds in recent decades have typically ranged from about $2 million to $15 million, though amounts have grown over time.

Participants

Series A investors are usually venture capital firms, sometimes joined by corporate venture arms or returning seed investors. Increasingly, the round is led by a single firm that sets the terms in a term sheet, with other investors participating on the same terms.

Subsequent rounds

As the company matures, further rounds are conventionally labelled Series B, Series C and so on, each funding successive stages of growth, until an exit through an initial public offering or acquisition. Companies that cannot progress may raise extension or "bridge" rounds on earlier terms.
