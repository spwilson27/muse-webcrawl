Sutton Hoo

Sutton Hoo
LocationWoodbridge, Suffolk, England
PeriodEarly medieval (6th–7th centuries AD)
CultureAnglo-Saxon

Sutton Hoo is an early medieval burial site near Woodbridge in Suffolk, England. Excavations in 1939 revealed the undisturbed burial of a 27-metre-long ship under Mound 1, containing a wealth of Anglo-Saxon artefacts of outstanding art-historical and archaeological significance — often described as one of the greatest archaeological discoveries of all time.

The great ship burial

Local archaeologist Basil Brown, working for the landowner Edith Pretty, uncovered the imprint of the ship's rivets in the sand in 1939. The burial chamber contained a spectacular assemblage: an ornate iron helmet with a face mask, a pattern-welded sword, a shield, a lyre, gold and garnet jewellery of superb craftsmanship, a great gold buckle, Byzantine silverware, and the ceremonial whetstone sceptre. Because the acidic soil had dissolved all bone, no body was found, but the burial is widely believed to be that of Rædwald, king of East Anglia, who died around 625.

Significance

Sutton Hoo transformed understanding of the Anglo-Saxon period, revealing the wealth, craftsmanship and international connections of the early English kingdoms and illuminating the world depicted in the poem Beowulf, which also describes a ship burial. The treasures were donated to the British Museum by Edith Pretty and remain its most famous early medieval holdings. The site, with its other royal burial mounds and execution cemetery, is managed by the National Trust.
