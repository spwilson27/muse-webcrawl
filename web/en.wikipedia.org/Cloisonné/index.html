Cloisonné

Cloisonné is an ancient technique for decorating metalwork objects with coloured material held in place, or separated, by metal strips or wire, usually of gold or silver. The name comes from the French cloison, a partition or compartment. Designs are formed by soldering thin wires edge-on to a metal body to create cells (cloisons), which are then filled with enamel paste, gemstones, or glass, and the whole piece is fired and polished.

Early history

Some of the earliest examples come from the ancient Near East and Egypt, and the technique was perfected in the Byzantine Empire from the 6th century onwards, where cloisonné enamel icons and jewellery of gold achieved remarkable delicacy. Byzantine work spread to Western Europe, where it was used for reliquaries and liturgical objects, and eastward along trade routes.

Chinese cloisonné

In China, cloisonné enamel on bronze or copper — known as jingtailan — flourished from the Ming dynasty, especially under the Jingtai Emperor (r. 1449–1457) from whom it takes its Chinese name. Artisans filled the cells between soldered brass wires with bright mineral colours; imperial workshops produced vases, incense burners, and vessels in vivid turquoise, blue, and polychrome designs. The art reached a further peak in the Qing dynasty.

Japanese cloisonné

Japanese shippō enamel reached its golden age in the later nineteenth century, when masters such as Namikawa Yasuyuki developed wireless techniques (musen shippō) that eliminated visible wires for painterly effects, winning prizes at international exhibitions. Cloisonné remains a practised craft in China, Japan, and elsewhere.
