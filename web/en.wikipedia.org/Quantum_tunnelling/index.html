Quantum tunnelling

Quantum tunnelling is a quantum mechanical phenomenon in which an object such as an electron or atom passes through a potential energy barrier that, according to classical mechanics, should not be passable due to the object not having sufficient energy to pass or surmount the barrier. Tunnelling is a consequence of the wave nature of matter: the wave function of a particle decays exponentially inside a barrier but does not vanish, so there is a finite probability of finding the particle on the far side.

History

The effect was applied by George Gamow in 1928 to explain alpha decay, in which an alpha particle escapes the nucleus by tunnelling through the Coulomb barrier. Friedrich Hund had used tunnelling in 1927 in calculations on the double-well potential. Tunnelling became central to the understanding of nuclear fusion in stars, where it allows fusion to occur at far lower temperatures than classical estimates require.

Applications

Quantum tunnelling is exploited in the tunnel diode, in flash memory, and in the scanning tunnelling microscope, invented in 1981, which images surfaces at atomic resolution by measuring a tunnelling current between a sharp tip and the sample. Tunnelling also plays roles in enzyme catalysis and hydrogen transfer in chemistry, and it sets limits on the miniaturisation of transistors, since electrons can leak through ever-thinner insulating layers.

Related concepts

The Hartman effect concerns the saturation of tunnelling time with barrier width. Tunnelling between two superconductors separated by a thin insulator gives rise to the Josephson effect, the basis of highly sensitive magnetic sensors and of many superconducting qubit designs.
