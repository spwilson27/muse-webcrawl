IEEE 754

IEEE 754 is a technical standard for floating-point arithmetic established in 1985 by the Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE). The standard addressed many problems found in the diverse floating-point implementations that made them difficult to use reliably and portably. Many hardware floating-point units use the IEEE 754 standard. The current version is IEEE 754-2019, published in July 2019, a minor revision of the previous version, IEEE 754-2008, published in 2008.

A 32-bit IEEE 754 floating-point number: 1 sign bit, 8 exponent bits, 23 fraction bits.

Representation

The standard specifies binary and decimal floating-point data, including binary16 (half precision), binary32 (single precision), binary64 (double precision) and binary128 (quadruple precision), as well as decimal formats. A binary floating-point number is stored as a sign bit, an exponent field, and a fraction (significand or mantissa) field. The value is computed as (−1)sign × 1.fraction × 2exponent − bias, with the implicit leading bit giving normalized numbers an extra bit of precision.

Special values

The standard reserves exponent patterns for special values: signed zeros (+0 and −0), subnormal numbers (which fill the gap around zero and provide gradual underflow), infinities (+∞ and −∞), and NaNs (not-a-number values), which come in quiet and signaling varieties. NaN is produced by invalid operations such as 0/0 or the square root of a negative number. Comparisons involving NaN are unordered, so NaN compares unequal to everything, including itself.

Rounding and operations

IEEE 754 requires that addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, and square root be correctly rounded: the result must be as if computed to infinite precision and then rounded to the destination format. The default rounding mode is round-to-nearest, ties-to-even; directed rounding modes (toward zero, toward +∞, toward −∞) are also specified. The standard also describes exception flags — invalid operation, division by zero, overflow, underflow, and inexact — and recommends extended and extendable precision formats. See also IEEE 802.11 for another widely used IEEE standard, and IP address for a related area of computing standards.

History

Work on the standard began in the 1970s, driven in large part by William Kahan of the University of California, Berkeley, who received the Turing Award in 1989 partly for this work. The Intel 8087 coprocessor, designed in consultation with the committee, helped demonstrate the design's practicality. IEEE 754-2008 merged the original binary standard with IEEE 854 (radix-independent floating point), adding decimal arithmetic and the fused multiply-add operation. The 2019 revision made mostly clarifications and recommended additional operations such as augmented arithmetic.
