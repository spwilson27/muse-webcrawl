Slavic languages

Distribution of the Slavic languages in Europe

The Slavic languages are a branch of the Indo-European language family spoken natively by about 315 million people, principally in Central, Eastern and Southeastern Europe. They descend from Proto-Slavic, the common ancestor spoken until roughly the early second millennium AD, and are conventionally divided into East, West and South Slavic subgroups.

Classification

  • East Slavic: Russian, Ukrainian, Belarusian and Rusyn. Russian is by far the largest Slavic language.
  • West Slavic: Polish, Czech, Slovak, Kashubian, Silesian and the Sorbian languages.
  • South Slavic: Bulgarian, Macedonian, Serbo-Croatian (Serbian, Croatian, Bosnian, Montenegrin) and Slovene, plus the liturgical Old Church Slavonic.

Features

Slavic languages are written in both the Cyrillic and Latin alphabets, a division reflecting religious history between Orthodox and Roman Catholic regions. They share features such as a rich system of nominal cases (up to seven), a distinction between perfective and imperfective verb aspect, free word order and a system of palatalised consonants. The earliest written Slavic language is Old Church Slavonic, created in the 9th century by the missionaries Cyril and Methodius, who also devised the Glagolitic alphabet from which Cyrillic developed.
