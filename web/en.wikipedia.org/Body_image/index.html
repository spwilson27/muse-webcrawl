Body image

Body image is a person's thoughts, perceptions, and feelings about their physical appearance. It encompasses how one sees oneself, how one believes others see them, and how one feels about that perception. Body image can be positive or negative and is influenced by individual psychology, family, culture, media, and social pressures. The concept was first defined by the Austrian neurologist and psychoanalyst Paul Schilder in his 1935 book The Image and Appearance of the Human Body.

Body image concerns perception and feeling, not appearance alone.

Components

Psychologists commonly distinguish four aspects of body image: the perceptual (how accurately a person judges their size and shape), the affective (how they feel about their appearance), the cognitive (the thoughts and beliefs they hold about their body), and the behavioural (what they do in response—such as avoidance, checking, dieting, or exercise). A person may have accurate perception yet strong dissatisfaction, or markedly distorted perception.

Negative body image

Body dissatisfaction is widespread in industrialised societies and is a risk factor for eating disorders such as anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, and binge eating disorder. Extreme preoccupation with perceived defects is classified as body dysmorphic disorder in the DSM-5. In men, muscle dysmorphia—perceiving oneself as small and weak despite being muscular—has been increasingly studied. Research links body dissatisfaction to exposure to idealised media images, social comparison, weight stigma, and teasing; the rise of social media and image filters has intensified scholarly and public concern, particularly for adolescents.

Positive body image and movements

The body positivity movement, with roots in the fat acceptance movement of the 1960s, promotes acceptance of all body types, while the related concept of "body neutrality" emphasises appreciating the body's function rather than its appearance. Clinical interventions include cognitive behavioural therapy, media literacy programmes, and dissonance-based prevention interventions such as the Body Project, which have shown evidence of reducing eating disorder risk.

History of research

Schilder built on earlier neurology of the "body schema," such as phantom limb phenomena. Systematic research into body image disturbance grew with studies of anorexia nervosa in the 1960s–70s, and the field expanded in the 1990s with validated questionnaires and the application of social comparison theory to fashion and advertising imagery.
