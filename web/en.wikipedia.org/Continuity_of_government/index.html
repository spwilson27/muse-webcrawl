Continuity of government

Continuity of government (COG) is the set of plans and procedures by which a government maintains its essential functions and constitutional leadership during and after a catastrophic emergency — such as nuclear war, a major terrorist attack, or a natural disaster — that could incapacitate its normal seat of power.

United States plans

American COG planning dates to the Cold War. Presidential directives established alternate relocation sites — including the Raven Rock Mountain Complex, Mount Weather, and the former congressional bunker at The Greenbrier — along with airborne command posts and rules for devolution of authority. The presidential line of succession, codified in the Presidential Succession Act, works together with these plans. After the September 11 attacks, a “shadow government” of officials was activated, and COG was formally reissued through directives such as NSPD-51/HSPD-20 (2007).

Other countries

The United Kingdom maintained the Central Government War Headquarters at Corsham and regional seats of government during the Cold War; Canada built the Diefenbunker network; many other states keep analogous, usually classified, arrangements.

Criticism and debate

Critics have questioned whether extra-constitutional emergency arrangements could undermine democratic legitimacy, while defenders argue that preserving lawful authority is itself essential to constitutional survival.
