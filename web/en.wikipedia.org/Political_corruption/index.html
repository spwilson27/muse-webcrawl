Political corruption

Political corruption is the use of powers by government officials, or their network contacts, for illegitimate private gain. Forms of corruption vary but can include bribery, lobbying, extortion, cronyism, nepotism, parochialism, patronage, influence peddling, graft, and embezzlement. Corruption may facilitate criminal enterprise, such as drug trafficking, money laundering, and human trafficking, though it is not restricted to these activities.

Forms

  • Bribery — offering or accepting something of value to influence an official act.
  • Embezzlement — theft of public funds by those entrusted with them.
  • Nepotism and cronyism — favouring relatives or associates in appointments and contracts.
  • Electoral fraud — manipulating votes, boundaries (gerrymandering), or campaign finance.
  • State capture — private interests shaping laws and regulations themselves for their own benefit.
  • Kleptocracy — rule by those who systematically loot public resources.

Measurement and effects

Transparency International's annual Corruption Perceptions Index ranks countries by perceived public-sector corruption. Research associates high corruption with slower economic growth, distorted public investment, reduced foreign investment, weaker public services, and erosion of trust in institutions. Estimating the real scale of corruption is difficult because it is covert; estimates by the World Bank and others place annual global bribery in the hundreds of billions to trillions of dollars.

Combating corruption

Measures to reduce corruption include transparency laws and open procurement, independent anti-corruption agencies (such as Hong Kong's ICAC and Singapore's CPIB), asset declarations, whistleblower protection, judicial independence, free media, and international instruments such as the United Nations Convention against Corruption (2003) and the OECD Anti-Bribery Convention (1997).
