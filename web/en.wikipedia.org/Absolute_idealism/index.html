Absolute idealism

Key figureGeorg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel
RegionGerman philosophy, 19th century
Related schoolsGerman idealism, British idealism

Absolute idealism is a philosophical theory associated principally with Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel (1770–1831) and, in modified forms, with Friedrich Schelling and later British and American idealists. It holds that reality as a whole — the Absolute — is fundamentally spiritual or mental in character, and that finite things and minds are intelligible only as parts of this self-developing whole.

Doctrine

For Hegel, the familiar opposition between thought and being, subject and object, is overcome in the Absolute: rational structure and reality are ultimately identical ("the rational is the actual, and the actual is the rational"). The Absolute is not a static substance but Geist (spirit or mind) coming to self-knowledge through a dialectical process in nature, history, art, religion and philosophy. Knowledge of isolated finite things is therefore always partial; truth belongs to the whole.

Development and influence

Absolute idealism grew out of Kant's transcendental idealism and the work of Fichte and Schelling. In the later nineteenth century it dominated British philosophy through thinkers such as T. H. Green, F. H. Bradley and Bernard Bosanquet, and influenced American philosophy through Josiah Royce. In the early twentieth century it was sharply attacked by G. E. Moore and Bertrand Russell, whose criticisms helped launch analytic philosophy, though Hegel scholarship and interest in absolute idealism revived later in the century.
