Mental representation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A mental representation (or cognitive representation) is a hypothetical internal cognitive symbol or state that represents external reality, or a mental process that makes use of such a symbol. It is a central concept in philosophy of mind, cognitive psychology, and cognitive science: thoughts, beliefs, percepts, and memories are commonly explained as involving internal representations that stand for objects, properties, and states of affairs in the world.

Theories

Jerry Fodor's representational theory of mind holds that thinking occurs in an internal "language of thought" (mentalese) whose symbols are combined according to syntactic rules — the basis of classical computational theories of cognition. Alternative accounts include connectionism, which distributes representations across networks of units, and embodied or enactive approaches that question how far cognition depends on amodal symbols at all.

Forms

Proposed representational formats include propositional representations, mental images (the subject of the imagery debate between proponents of pictorial and descriptive formats), mental models used in reasoning, and cognitive maps of space.
