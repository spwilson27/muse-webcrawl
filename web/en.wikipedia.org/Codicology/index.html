Codicology is the study of codices or manuscript books as physical objects, especially the materials, structure, and production processes of manuscripts produced before the invention of printing. It is often described as the "archaeology of the book". Where palaeography studies the handwriting and textual criticism studies the text, codicology studies the book itself: its parchment or paper, quire structure, ruling and layout, binding, decoration, and signs of ownership and use.

Scope and methods

Codicologists examine how a manuscript was planned and assembled — the collation of its gatherings, the preparation and pricking of leaves, the sewing and binding — in order to date and localise production, reconstruct lost portions, and understand how a text was read and used. Quantitative codicology, developed by scholars such as Carla Bozzolo and Ezio Ornato, applies statistical methods to large corpora of manuscripts to study the economics and geography of book production.

The term was popularised in French scholarship (codicologie) in the mid-20th century, notably by Alphonse Dain, and the discipline has expanded to cover manuscripts from many traditions, including Islamic, Hebrew, and Ethiopic books.

Applications

Codicological evidence can distinguish original production from later additions, identify workshops and scribes, and trace a manuscript's provenance. It underpins modern manuscript cataloguing and conservation, and increasingly uses scientific techniques — multispectral imaging, ink and pigment analysis, and DNA analysis of parchment ("biocodicology") — to recover erased texts and the origins of animal skins used in books.
