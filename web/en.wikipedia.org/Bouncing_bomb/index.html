Bouncing bomb

A bouncing bomb is a bomb designed to bounce across the surface of water toward its target, avoiding torpedo nets and other underwater defences before sinking and detonating beside or beneath it. The best-known example is the Upkeep bomb developed by British engineer Barnes Wallis for the Royal Air Force during the Second World War.

Design and use in the Dams Raid

Upkeep was a cylindrical mine weighing about 9,250 lb, given back-spin of around 500 rpm by a motor in the modified Lancaster bomber before release. Dropped at precisely 60 feet and about 220 mph, it skipped over the water, struck the dam wall, and rolled down it before a hydrostatic pistol detonated it at depth — maximising destructive effect through contact with the structure. On the night of 16–17 May 1943, No. 617 Squadron under Wing Commander Guy Gibson used Upkeep in Operation Chastise, breaching the Möhne and Eder dams in Germany's Ruhr valley.

Related weapons

Wallis also designed the smaller Highball spherical bouncing bomb for use against ships, tested but never used operationally, and bouncing bombs were considered by other nations. The principle was later immortalised in the 1955 film The Dam Busters.
