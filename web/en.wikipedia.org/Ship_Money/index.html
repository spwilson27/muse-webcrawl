Ship money

Ship money was a tax of medieval origin in England, levied intermittently on coastal towns and counties to fund the Royal Navy in times of war or emergency. It became notorious in the 1630s when King Charles I, ruling without Parliament during the "Personal Rule", revived and greatly extended the levy — from the ports to inland counties, and from wartime to annual peacetime collection. Ship money became the emblem of arbitrary taxation and a central grievance leading to the English Civil War.

History of the levy

From Anglo-Saxon and medieval times, maritime communities owed the Crown ships or money in lieu for coastal defence; Elizabeth I and James I had levied ship money or requisitioned ships during war with Spain and other emergencies. Charles I's first writ (October 1634) was confined to ports and maritime counties, but the writ of August 1635 extended the charge to inland counties, and further writs made it effectively an annual national tax — without parliamentary consent, contrary to the principle asserted in the Petition of Right (1628) that taxation required Parliament's approval.

Hampden's case

The Buckinghamshire landowner John Hampden refused to pay his assessment of 20 shillings and was prosecuted in the Court of Exchequer Chamber in 1637. The case — R v Hampden, the "Case of Ship-Money" — was argued before all twelve common-law judges. In 1638 they found for the King by the narrow margin of 7 to 5, holding that in cases of danger the King was sole judge of the emergency and could charge his subjects accordingly. The closeness of the decision and the dissenting judgments (notably of Sir George Croke and Sir Richard Hutton) were widely publicised and turned Hampden into a popular hero; collection rates, initially high, collapsed by 1639–40 as resistance grew.

Abolition and legacy

The Long Parliament declared ship money illegal by the Ship Money Act 1640, voided the judgment against Hampden, and impeached the judges who had supported the Crown. The affair is a landmark in the constitutional history of taxation: it established in political practice — later entrenched by the Bill of Rights 1689 — that the Crown may not levy taxes without Parliament. The navy the tax funded was real enough: its proceeds built ships including the great Sovereign of the Seas (1637).
