Incense

Incense is an aromatic biotic material that releases fragrant smoke when burned. The term covers a wide range of substances, including resins such as frankincense and myrrh, aromatic woods such as sandalwood and agarwood, dried plant materials, and manufactured sticks, cones and coils in which powdered aromatics are bound with a combustible binder.

History

The use of incense is ancient. It was burned in ancient Egypt, where resin pellets were used in religious ritual and perfumery; in the Indus Valley and China, where aromatic woods were used in ceremony; and across the ancient Near East, where frankincense and myrrh were valuable trade goods carried along the Incense trade route. Incense spread along the Silk Road and became embedded in Hindu, Buddhist, Daoist and Shinto practice, as well as in the liturgy of the Roman Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and some other Christian churches.

Production and forms

Incense may be burned directly, as with resin grains placed on hot charcoal, or indirectly, in manufactured forms such as sticks (with or without a bamboo core), cones, coils and ropes. Japanese incense is notable for coreless sticks of finely ground ingredients, while Indian agarbatti typically use a bamboo core coated with a paste of charcoal and aromatic materials.

Uses

Incense is used in religious worship, ancestor veneration, meditation, aromatherapy, masking odours and insect repellence. Its smoke is also a component of certain aesthetic and ceremonial practices, such as the Japanese incense ceremony known as kōdō.

Health effects

Incense smoke contains particulate matter and volatile compounds; prolonged exposure in enclosed spaces has been associated in studies with respiratory irritation and, in some populations, elevated risks of respiratory disease.
