Protist

Protists
DomainEukaryota
OrganisationMostly unicellular; some colonial or multicellular (e.g. kelps)

A protist is any eukaryotic organism that is not an animal, plant, or fungus. Protists are mostly single-celled microorganisms, though some, such as the giant kelps, are large multicellular organisms. The term was introduced by Ernst Haeckel in 1866 for a proposed third kingdom, Protista. Modern phylogenetics shows that "protists" do not form a natural, single lineage: they are a paraphyletic convenience grouping spanning most of the diversity of eukaryotes.

Diversity and ecology

Protists include photosynthetic algae and diatoms, which account for a large fraction of oceanic primary production; heterotrophic "protozoa" such as amoebae, flagellates, and ciliates that graze on bacteria; fungus-like slime moulds and water moulds; and many parasites. Reproduction may be asexual, sexual, or both, and life cycles are often complex.

Pathogens

Several protists cause major human diseases: Plasmodium species cause malaria; Entamoeba histolytica causes amoebic dysentery; Giardia causes giardiasis; and Trypanosoma brucei causes African sleeping sickness. Others devastate crops, notably the water mould Phytophthora infestans, responsible for the Irish potato famine of the 1840s.

Classification

Contemporary systematics replaces the old kingdom Protista with supergroups such as Amoebozoa, Archaeplastida, the SAR supergroup (stramenopiles, alveolates, rhizarians), Excavata, and Opisthokonta (the group that also contains animals and fungi). The word "protist" survives as a useful informal label for the predominantly microscopic eukaryotes outside the three familiar kingdoms.
