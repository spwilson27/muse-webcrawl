Grid plan

In urban planning, a grid plan, also called a grid street plan or gridiron plan, is a type of city plan in which streets run at right angles to each other, forming a grid. Two inherent characteristics of the grid plan are frequent intersections and orthogonal geometry. The grid creates a regular, easily divisible pattern of blocks and lots, simplifying land measurement, navigation and the extension of the city.

History

The grid is one of the oldest city plans: it appears in the Indus Valley cities of Mohenjo-daro and Harappa, in ancient Egyptian and Chinese planning, and throughout the Greek world, where Hippodamus of Miletus gave his name to planned grids. Rome imposed grids on its colonies and military camps. The Spanish Laws of the Indies (1573) prescribed grids for colonial towns in the Americas, and grid plans dominate newer cities in the United States and Canada, exemplified by William Penn's Philadelphia (1682) and the Manhattan Commissioners' Plan of 1811. Critics such as Camillo Sitte faulted grids for monotony, and 20th-century suburban planning often favoured curved streets and cul-de-sacs, but the grid has returned to favour for its walkability, connectivity and efficient traffic distribution.
