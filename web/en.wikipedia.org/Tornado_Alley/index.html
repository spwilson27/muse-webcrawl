Tornado Alley
Region: Central United States
Core states: Texas, Oklahoma, Kansas, Nebraska
Phenomenon: Highest tornado frequency in the world

Tornado Alley is a loosely defined region of the central United States where tornadoes are most frequent. Although no official boundary exists, the term generally covers northern Texas, Oklahoma, Kansas, Nebraska and parts of surrounding states; recent research suggests the zone of greatest tornado activity may be shifting eastward toward the Mississippi Valley, sometimes called Dixie Alley.

Meteorology

The region's tornadoes arise from its unique geography: warm, moist air from the Gulf of Mexico collides with cold, dry air descending from the Canadian Rockies and hot, dry air from the southwestern deserts, all across flat terrain with no barriers to interrupt the flow. In spring, these ingredients combine under strong jet-stream winds to produce supercell thunderstorms capable of spawning violent tornadoes.

Notable tornadoes

Tornado Alley has experienced many of the most destructive tornadoes on record, including the 1999 Bridge Creek–Moore tornado, whose winds of 321 mph (517 km/h) are the highest ever measured on Earth, and the El Reno tornado of 2013, the widest ever recorded at 2.6 miles (4.2 km) across. The National Weather Center in Norman, Oklahoma, anchors the region's forecasting and research, including the Storm Prediction Center.

Preparedness

Communities in the region maintain dense networks of storm shelters, sirens and spotter programmes, and the local culture of storm chasing — both scientific and recreational — originated here.
