Fianna Fáil

Fianna Fáil (Irish: [ˈfʲiən̪ˠə ˈfˠaːlʲ]; meaning "Soldiers of Destiny" or "Warriors of Fál"), officially Fianna Fáil – The Republican Party, is a conservative and Christian democratic political party in Ireland. Founded in 1926 by Éamon de Valera, it has been the largest party in Ireland for most of the state's history and has governed for roughly 61 of the years from 1932 onwards.

History

Fianna Fáil emerged from the losing anti-Treaty side of the Irish Civil War. De Valera and his followers split from Sinn Féin over the policy of abstention from the Irish Free State parliament, Dáil Éireann. In 1927 the party's TDs took their seats, describing the oath of allegiance to the British Crown as an "empty formula". Fianna Fáil won the 1932 general election and de Valera became head of government, beginning a 16-year tenure. The party dominated Irish politics through the 20th century under leaders including Seán Lemass, Jack Lynch, Charles Haughey, and Albert Reynolds.

Modern era

Under Bertie Ahern (leader 1994–2008) the party won three consecutive elections and helped negotiate the Good Friday Agreement of 1998. The 2008 financial crisis devastated its support: in the 2011 general election it fell to third place with its worst result ever, widely blamed for the banking collapse under Taoiseach Brian Cowen. Under Micheál Martin it gradually recovered, finishing as the largest party in the closely divided 2020 election and entering a coalition with Fine Gael and the Green Party, with Martin and Leo Varadkar rotating as Taoiseach.

Ideology

Fianna Fáil has traditionally been a broad, catch-all party of the centre to centre-right, historically tied to the goal of a united Ireland, protectionist economics in its early decades, and later to social partnership and pro-European integration. It sits with the Renew Europe group in the European Parliament.
