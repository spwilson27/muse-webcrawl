Latent heat

Heating curve showing plateaus where latent heat is absorbed

Latent heat (also known as latent energy or heat of transformation) is energy released or absorbed by a body or a thermodynamic system during a constant-temperature process, usually a first-order phase transition, like melting or condensation. During such a process the absorbed energy changes the state of the substance without changing its temperature.

Types

The two main kinds of latent heat are the latent heat of fusion (melting or freezing) and the latent heat of vaporization (boiling or condensation). A third, the latent heat of sublimation, covers the direct transition between solid and gas. For water, the latent heat of vaporization is about 2,260 kJ/kg, much larger than the latent heat of fusion of about 334 kJ/kg.

History of the term

The term was introduced around 1762 by the Scottish chemist Joseph Black, whose calorimetric experiments showed that ice absorbs heat while melting without any change in temperature. The word "latent" refers to the heat being hidden, producing no rise on a thermometer.

Role in the atmosphere

The release of latent heat when water vapour condenses is the principal energy source for thunderstorms, hurricanes, and much of the Earth's weather, transferring heat from the surface into the atmosphere.
