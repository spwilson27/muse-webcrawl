Electron affinity

The electron affinity of an atom or molecule is defined as the amount of energy released when an electron attaches to a neutral atom or molecule in the gaseous state to form an anion.

Definition and sign convention

Expressed as X(g) + e− → X−(g) + energy, electron affinities are usually given in kilojoules per mole. Chlorine has the largest electron affinity of any element, about 349 kJ/mol, exceeding even fluorine because fluorine's compact 2p subshell produces strong electron–electron repulsion.

Periodic trends

Electron affinity generally increases across a period as effective nuclear charge rises, and decreases down a group as the added electron enters a more distant shell. Noble gases have near-zero or negative affinities because their shells are complete.

Second electron affinity

Adding a second electron to an already negative ion always requires energy, so second electron affinities are endothermic; the formation of oxide ions, for example, is driven overall by lattice energy in ionic solids.
