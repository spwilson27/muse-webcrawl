The Last Glacial Maximum (LGM), also referred to as the Last Glacial Coldest Period, was the most recent time during the Last Glacial Period when ice sheets were at their largest volume and extent. It is conventionally dated to between about 26,500 and 19,000 years ago, when the Laurentide Ice Sheet covered most of Canada and the northern United States, the Fennoscandian Ice Sheet covered northern Europe, and glaciers expanded across Siberia, the Southern Alps, and Patagonia.

Climate

Global average temperatures were roughly 6 °C lower than today, ice sheets locked up so much water that sea level stood about 120–135 metres below present, and atmospheric carbon dioxide concentrations fell to around 180–190 parts per million. The exposed continental shelves created land bridges, most famously Beringia between Siberia and Alaska, along which humans and other species migrated.

Regional summary

In North America, the Laurentide and Cordilleran ice sheets sometimes merged; south of the ice, periglacial tundra and loess plains stretched across the continent. In Eurasia, an integrated ice sheet covered Scandinavia, the Barents and Kara seas, and the British Isles as far south as the Midlands. Much of the tropics was drier, with reduced monsoons, expanded deserts, and lowered lake levels, though rainforest refuges persisted.

Deglaciation

After about 19,000 years ago, rising Northern Hemisphere summer insolation and feedbacks involving greenhouse gases drove the Last Glacial Termination: ice sheets retreated, sea level rose rapidly (including meltwater pulse 1A around 14,600 years ago), and the Earth entered the Holocene interglacial by 11,700 years ago.
