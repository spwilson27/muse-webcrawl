Reconciliation (United States Congress)

Budget reconciliation is a special parliamentary procedure of the United States Congress set out in the Congressional Budget Act of 1974. It allows expedited passage of certain budgetary legislation in the Senate: debate is limited to 20 hours, amendments must be germane, and the bill cannot be filibustered, so it requires only a simple majority of 51 votes (or 50 plus the Vice President) rather than the 60 votes usually needed to invoke cloture.

History

Reconciliation was first used in 1980 and has since been used to enact major legislation, including tax cuts under Presidents Ronald Reagan, George W. Bush, and Donald Trump, welfare reform in 1996, portions of the Affordable Care Act in 2010, and the Inflation Reduction Act of 2022.

The Byrd Rule

The Byrd Rule, named after Senator Robert Byrd, restricts reconciliation bills in the Senate. Provisions that are "extraneous" to the budget — including those with no budgetary effect, that increase the deficit beyond the budget window, or that change Social Security — may be struck on a point of order.

Process

The process begins when a concurrent budget resolution includes reconciliation instructions directing committees to produce legislation changing spending, revenue, or the debt limit by specified amounts. The resulting bill is packaged by the Budget Committee and considered under the expedited rules.
