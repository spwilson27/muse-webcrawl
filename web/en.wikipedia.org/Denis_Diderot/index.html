Denis Diderot (5 October 1713 – 31 July 1784) was a French philosopher, art critic and writer, best known for serving as co-founder, chief editor and a contributor to the Encyclopédie, one of the emblematic works of the Enlightenment.

Life

Born in Langres, the son of a master cutler, Diderot was educated by Jesuits and at the University of Paris, then lived a precarious literary life in Paris, writing sermons, translations and hack work. His Letter on the Blind (1749), with its materialist implications, led to his imprisonment at Vincennes. Catherine the Great of Russia became his patron, purchasing his library in 1765 while leaving it in his custody, and he visited St Petersburg in 1773–74.

The Encyclopédie

From 1747 Diderot, with Jean le Rond d'Alembert, edited the Encyclopédie, ou dictionnaire raisonné des sciences, des arts et des métiers, published in 17 volumes of text (1751–1765) and 11 of plates. Battling censorship and the withdrawal of its royal privilege in 1759, Diderot secretly completed the work, which spread Enlightenment ideas and detailed knowledge of crafts and trades to a wide readership.

Other works

His philosophical dialogues — Jacques the Fatalist, Rameau's Nephew and D'Alembert's Dream — were mostly published after his death. His Salons, reviews of the Paris art exhibitions, founded modern art criticism. Diderot's materialist philosophy anticipated ideas in evolution and the philosophy of mind.
