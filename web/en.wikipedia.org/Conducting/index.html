Conducting

Conducting is the art of directing a musical performance, such as an orchestral or choral concert. It has been defined as "the art of directing the simultaneous performance of several players or singers by the use of gesture". The primary duties of the conductor are to interpret the score in a way that reflects the specific indications in that score, set the tempo, ensure correct entries by ensemble members, and "shape" the phrasing where appropriate.

A conductor directing an orchestra

History

Time-keeping leadership existed in ancient and medieval music, often by tapping a staff or moving the hand. In the Baroque era the ensemble was led from the harpsichord or by the first violinist. Modern conducting emerged in the 19th century with figures such as Louis Spohr, Hector Berlioz and Felix Mendelssohn, as orchestras grew and scores became more complex. Hans von Bülow, Arthur Nikisch and Gustav Mahler established the conductor as interpreter-in-chief.

Technique

The conductor's right hand, usually holding a baton, marks the beat pattern — standard patterns exist for two, three, four and compound beats per bar — while the left hand shapes dynamics, tone and expression. Rehearsal technique, score study, and communication with players are as important as podium gesture. Choral conductors use similar techniques adapted to singers.

The conductor's role

Beyond performance, music directors select repertoire, audition and hire musicians, plan seasons, and represent the ensemble publicly. Notable conductors include Herbert von Karajan, Leonard Bernstein, Georg Solti, Claudio Abbado, and, among today's leading figures, Simon Rattle and Gustavo Dudamel.
