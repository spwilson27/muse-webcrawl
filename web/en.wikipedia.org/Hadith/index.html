Hadith is the Arabic word for a report, account or narrative. In Islamic terminology, a hadith is a record of the words, actions, and silent approvals of the Prophet Muhammad, transmitted through chains of narrators (isnad). Together with the Qur'an, the hadith corpus — embodying the sunnah, Muhammad's exemplary practice — forms the primary source of Islamic law (sharia) and moral guidance for most Muslims.

Compilation and criticism

Hadith were transmitted orally for generations before being systematically compiled in the 8th and 9th centuries CE. Scholars developed an elaborate science of hadith criticism (ilm al-rijal), evaluating the reliability of each narrator and grading reports as sound (sahih), good (hasan) or weak (da'if). The most authoritative Sunni collections are the Sahih of al-Bukhari (d. 870) and of Muslim ibn al-Hajjaj (d. 875), together with the four Sunan works; Twelver Shia Islam relies chiefly on the Four Books, including al-Kulayni's Al-Kafi.

Role and scholarly debate

Hadith govern matters from ritual prayer and fasting to commerce and family law. Since the 19th century, Western scholars such as Ignaz Goldziher and Joseph Schacht questioned the authenticity of many attributions, arguing that many hadith were back-projected to Muhammad; traditional Muslim scholarship and many later historians defend more nuanced positions. A minority movement, Quranism, rejects hadith authority altogether.
