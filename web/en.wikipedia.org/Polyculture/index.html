Polyculture

Polyculture
DefinitionGrowing multiple crops (or livestock species) in the same space
OppositeMonoculture
FormsIntercropping, companion planting, multi-species pasture, integrated aquaculture
Famous systemThree Sisters (maize, beans, squash)

Polyculture is the practice of growing more than one crop species in the same space at the same time, in imitation of the diversity of natural ecosystems. It is the opposite of monoculture, in which only a single species is grown over a wide area. Polyculture was the norm in agriculture for most of human history and remains widespread in smallholder and subsistence farming, particularly in the tropics.

Methods

Common techniques include intercropping — growing two or more crops in proximity, in rows or mixed stands; companion planting, in which species are chosen for mutual benefit; cover cropping; multi-species grazing and pasture mixtures; and integrated systems such as rice–fish culture and agroforestry. The classic Native American "Three Sisters" system interplants maize (which provides a trellis), beans (which fix nitrogen) and squash (whose leaves shade the soil and suppress weeds).

Advantages and disadvantages

Polycultures can use nutrients, water and light more completely than monocultures (producing higher combined yields per unit area, measured by the land equivalent ratio), reduce pest and disease spread, improve soil health and stability of production, and support biodiversity. Yields of any single commodity are typically lower and management, planting and especially mechanised harvesting are more complex, which is why industrial agriculture favours monocultures. Modern research in agroecology and permaculture seeks to design high-diversity systems that recover polyculture's ecological benefits at larger scales.
