Airline hub

An airline hub is an airport used by an airline as a transfer point where passengers and cargo are routed between flights, enabling the airline to serve many city pairs through connections rather than direct services. The hub-and-spoke system contrasts with point-to-point networks favoured by many low-cost carriers. Hubs concentrate an airline's operations — gates, maintenance, crew bases, and lounges — at a small number of airports.

Operation

Hubs are typically scheduled in "banks": waves of arriving flights feed a coordinated wave of departures, maximizing the number of possible connections within a short ground interval. A "fortress hub" is an airport dominated by a single carrier, which can confer pricing power but also attract regulatory scrutiny. Hartsfield–Jackson Atlanta International Airport, Delta Air Lines' largest hub, has long been the world's busiest passenger airport; other major hubs include Frankfurt for Lufthansa, Dubai for Emirates, and Istanbul for Turkish Airlines.

History

The hub concept expanded rapidly after U.S. airline deregulation in 1978, when carriers such as Delta (Atlanta) and American (Dallas/Fort Worth) built connecting complexes; the model was adapted from FedEx's Memphis cargo hub, opened in 1973. Airline bankruptcies and mergers have repeatedly reshaped hub maps, with airports such as Cincinnati, Memphis, and St. Louis losing hub status. Long-range narrowbody aircraft and shifting demand have since encouraged some de-hubbing and more point-to-point flying.
