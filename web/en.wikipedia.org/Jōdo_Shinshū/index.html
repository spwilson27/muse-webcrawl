Jōdo Shinshū (浄土真宗, "The True Essence of the Pure Land Teaching"), also known as Shin Buddhism, is a school of Pure Land Buddhism founded by the Japanese monk Shinran (1173–1263), a disciple of Hōnen. It is the most widely practised branch of Buddhism in Japan and has significant communities overseas, including the Buddhist Churches of America.

Teachings

Shinran taught that in the present degenerate age (mappō), human beings cannot attain enlightenment by their own efforts ("self-power", jiriki). Salvation comes solely through the "other-power" (tariki) of Amida Buddha's Primal Vow: by entrusting oneself to Amida (shinjin) and reciting the nembutsu — "Namu Amida Butsu" — one is assured birth in the Pure Land at death. Unusually, Shin clergy may marry and eat meat, a practice begun by Shinran himself.

History

Shinran's successor communities coalesced around the Hongan-ji in Kyoto. Under the eighth monshu Rennyo (1415–1499) the school expanded dramatically among peasants and provincial warriors, spawning the ikkō-ikki leagues. In 1602 the shogun Tokugawa Ieyasu split the Hongan-ji into eastern (Higashi) and western (Nishi) branches, which remain the school's two largest organisations.
