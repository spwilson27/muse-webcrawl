Rohonc Codex

TypeIlluminated manuscript
Pages448
Present locationLibrary of the Hungarian Academy of Sciences, Budapest
StatusUndeciphered

The Rohonc Codex (Hungarian: Rohonci kódex) is an illustrated manuscript book containing writing in an unknown script and language. The codex surfaced in Hungary in the 19th century, when Count Gusztáv Batthyány donated his library at Rohonc (now Rechnitz, Austria) to the Hungarian Academy of Sciences in 1838. Despite nearly two centuries of study, neither the script nor the language of the codex has been convincingly deciphered, and it is frequently compared with the Voynich manuscript.

Description

The codex consists of 448 paper pages measuring roughly 12 by 10 centimetres, each containing between nine and fourteen rows of glyphs. The paper bears a watermark of an anchor within a circle surmounted by a six-pointed star, a mark associated with Italian paper mills of the 16th century, though the dating of the manuscript itself is debated. Interspersed with the text are 87 illustrations depicting religious scenes — including episodes from the life of Christ — as well as military scenes, landscapes, and heraldic emblems. The imagery blends elements suggestive of Christian, Islamic, and possibly Hindu iconography.

Script and language

The script has an inventory of roughly 800 distinct symbols — far more than a typical alphabet — and the text appears to run from right to left, with pages bound in reverse order relative to Western convention. Proposed solutions have claimed the codex is written in early Hungarian, Dacian, Romanian, Cuman, or even an Indian language, but none has won scholarly acceptance. Because the manuscript is associated with the collection of the notorious forger Sámuel Literáti Nemes, some researchers suspect it may be an elaborate hoax, while others regard it as a genuine but still-undeciphered historical document.

Recent research

In 2018, Hungarian researchers Levente Király and Gábor Tokai published a claimed partial decipherment arguing that the text is written in a ciphered shorthand-like system, contains references to biblical passages, and was composed around the 16th or 17th century. Their proposed solution, based on finding repeated structured references and a small deciphered signary, has attracted attention but not consensus.
