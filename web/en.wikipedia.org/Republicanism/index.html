Republicanism

Republicanism is a political ideology centred on citizenship in a state organized as a republic, under which the people hold popular sovereignty. Historically, it ranges from the rule of a representative minority or oligarchy to popular sovereignty, and it emphasizes the rule of law, civic virtue, and opposition to corruption, monarchy, and arbitrary power. In the modern philosophical formulation associated with Philip Pettit, republican liberty means freedom as non-domination — not living at the mercy of another's arbitrary will.

Historical development

Republican thought descends from the Roman Republic and was revived by Renaissance civic humanists such as Machiavelli, who analysed the preservation of liberty in a republic in his Discourses on Livy. In seventeenth-century England, writers including James Harrington and John Milton defended the Commonwealth against monarchy; their ideas travelled to the American colonies, where republicanism — blended with liberalism and Protestant thought — underlay the American Revolution and the United States Constitution. The French Revolution later radicalized republican ideals of citizenship and the nation.

Key concepts

Classical republican themes include mixed government and separation of powers to prevent tyranny; the citizen militia; suspicion of standing armies, luxury, and faction; and the dependence of free government on an economically independent, virtuous citizenry. Modern republicanism adds an emphasis on public deliberation, checks on private as well as state power, and freedom as non-domination.

Republicanism by country

Movements described as republican include Irish republicanism, Australian republicanism (which seeks to replace the monarchy with a resident head of state), and the historical republican traditions of the United States, France, and Turkey. The term is also used by political parties, including the Republican Party in the United States, though party names do not always align with the ideology in its scholarly sense.
