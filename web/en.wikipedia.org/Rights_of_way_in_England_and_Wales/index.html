Rights of way in England and Wales

In England and Wales, a public right of way is a path on which the public has a legally protected right to pass and repass. Rights of way are recorded on the definitive map maintained by each local highway authority, and the network totals roughly 140,000 miles (225,000 km) of routes.

Categories

  • Public footpath — for pedestrians only.
  • Public bridleway — for pedestrians, horse riders and cyclists.
  • Restricted byway — for all non-motorised traffic, including horse-drawn vehicles.
  • Byway open to all traffic (BOAT) — usable by vehicles but mainly used for walking and riding.

History and law

Many routes are ancient, originating as trade, church or funeral paths. The Rights of Way Act 1932, National Parks and Access to the Countryside Act 1949, Countryside Act 1968, Wildlife and Countryside Act 1981 and the Countryside and Rights of Way Act 2000 progressively established the modern legal framework, including definitive maps and, in 2000, a right to roam over mapped access land. Landowners must keep rights of way unobstructed and are restricted in ploughing or fencing across them.

Access campaigns

Organisations such as the Ramblers campaign for the protection and completion of the network; disputes over unrecorded historic paths and deadlines for registering them have been politically contentious.
