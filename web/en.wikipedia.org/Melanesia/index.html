Melanesia is a subregion of Oceania in the southwestern Pacific Ocean, extending from New Guinea in the west to Fiji in the east. It comprises the modern states of Fiji, Papua New Guinea, Solomon Islands, and Vanuatu, together with the French collectivity of New Caledonia and the western part of the island of New Guinea administered by Indonesia. The name, coined by the French explorer Jules Dumont d'Urville in 1832, derives from Greek words for "black islands," one of his threefold division of the Pacific alongside Polynesia and Micronesia — a classification now recognized as flawed but still in general use.

Geography

Melanesia's islands are mostly large, mountainous, and volcanic, formed along the tectonically active boundary of the Australian and Pacific plates. New Guinea, the world's second-largest island, dominates the region with peaks approaching 5,000 metres. The islands lie within the tropics, with rainforest covering much of their interiors, and they include some of the most biologically diverse marine environments on Earth, notably in the Coral Triangle's eastern reaches.

Peoples and languages

Melanesia is among the most linguistically diverse regions on Earth: Papua New Guinea alone has over 800 living languages. The population descends both from some of the earliest modern human settlement outside Africa (over 40,000 years ago in Sahul) and from the Austronesian-speaking Lapita culture that arrived about 3,300 years ago. Cultural institutions famously studied by anthropologists include the big-man leadership system, ceremonial exchange networks such as the kula ring of the Trobriand Islands, and diverse cargo cults that emerged after contact with Europeans.

History and politics

European contact intensified in the 19th century with traders, missionaries, and the "blackbirding" labour trade; by 1914 the region was divided among Britain, France, Germany, and the Netherlands. After the Second World War — during which the islands were major battlegrounds, including Guadalcanal — the Melanesian states gained independence between 1962 and 1980. Regional cooperation is pursued through the Melanesian Spearhead Group (founded 1986), while live political issues include the Kanak independence movement in New Caledonia and the West Papuan conflict.
