Genre: Literary fiction
Related: Novel of ideas, roman à thèse

Philosophical novel

A philosophical novel is a work of fiction devoted to exploring philosophical questions and ideas through narrative. Rather than presenting arguments in treatise form, the philosophical novel dramatizes ideas through characters, plot, and dialogue, allowing abstract concepts to be tested against lived experience.

History

Early examples include Voltaire's Candide (1759), a satire of Leibnizian optimism, and Rousseau's Émile, partly presented in narrative form. The genre flourished in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries with works by Dostoevsky, Thomas Mann, Jean-Paul Sartre, Albert Camus, and Ayn Rand.

Characteristics

Philosophical novels typically feature extended dialogues or interior monologues in which characters articulate competing worldviews. A significant portion of the work is devoted to exploring a particular philosophical idea, and the plot often serves as a vehicle for that exploration rather than an end in itself.

Notable examples

Notable examples include Crime and Punishment and The Brothers Karamazov by Fyodor Dostoevsky, Thus Spoke Zarathustra by Friedrich Nietzsche, Nausea by Jean-Paul Sartre, The Stranger by Albert Camus, Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance by Robert M. Pirsig, and Sophie's World by Jostein Gaarder.
