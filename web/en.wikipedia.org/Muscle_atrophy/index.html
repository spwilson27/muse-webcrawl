Muscle atrophy

Muscle atrophy is the loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength and function. It can result from disuse, malnutrition, aging (sarcopenia), disease, or loss of nerve supply to the muscle. Atrophy reflects an imbalance in which protein degradation exceeds protein synthesis within muscle fibres.

Causes

Disuse atrophy occurs when a muscle is inactive for a prolonged period, as with bed rest, limb immobilization in a cast, or the microgravity of spaceflight. Neurogenic atrophy follows damage to the motor neurons or peripheral nerves supplying a muscle, as in poliomyelitis, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis or spinal muscular atrophy, and is typically more rapid and severe. Chronic diseases such as cancer, heart failure and chronic kidney disease can cause cachexia, a wasting syndrome that includes muscle loss. Corticosteroid use and certain endocrine disorders also promote atrophy.

Mechanisms and treatment

At the molecular level, the ubiquitin–proteasome system and autophagy pathways break down contractile proteins, regulated in part by atrogenes such as atrogin-1 and MuRF1, while signalling through the IGF-1/Akt/mTOR pathway promotes synthesis. Treatment focuses on the underlying cause, together with resistance exercise, adequate protein nutrition and physical rehabilitation, which can partially reverse disuse atrophy.
