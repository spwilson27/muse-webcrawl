Sipahi

Sipahi (from Persian sepāhī, "soldier"; Turkish: sipahi) were two types of Ottoman cavalry corps, including the fief-holding provincial timarli sipahi and the salaried household cavalry (kapıkulu sipahi) of the Sultan's court. The word also gives English the Indian "sepoy", applied to native infantry in European service.

The timar system

The provincial sipahis formed the backbone of the Ottoman army in its classical age. In return for military service — appearing on campaign with horses, arms, armour and retainers proportional to his income — a sipahi was granted a timar, the right to collect taxes from a defined area of state land. He did not own the land itself, and the grant could be revoked for failure to serve. Larger grants were called zeamet and has. The kapıkulu sipahis, by contrast, were paid cavalry regiments of the standing household army in the capital.

Decline

From the late 16th century the value of heavy cavalry fell as firearms and drilled infantry came to dominate battlefields, while inflation eroded the worth of timars. Sipahis resisted reforms and were periodically in revolt; the corps declined through the 17th and 18th centuries and was abolished in the 19th century as the Ottoman Empire built a modern army.
