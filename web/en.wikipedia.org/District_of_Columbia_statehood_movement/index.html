District of Columbia statehood movement

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The District of Columbia statehood movement is the movement to make the District of Columbia a U.S. state, giving its roughly 700,000 residents full voting representation in the United States Congress, including two senators and a voting member of the House of Representatives. At present the District elects only a non-voting delegate to the House, and, since the Twenty-third Amendment (1961), electoral votes in presidential elections, despite its residents paying federal taxes — a grievance reflected in the slogan "Taxation Without Representation" on D.C. licence plates.

History

Proposals for voting representation date back to the District's creation in 1790–1801. The District of Columbia Voting Rights Amendment, passed by Congress in 1978, would have granted full representation without statehood but failed ratification by the states. District voters approved a statehood referendum in 1982 and again, by about 86%, in 2016, endorsing the name "State of Washington, Douglass Commonwealth" in honour of Frederick Douglass. Bills such as the Washington, D.C. Admission Act have passed the House of Representatives (in 2020 and 2021) but stalled in the Senate.

Arguments

Supporters argue that denying statehood violates democratic principles, noting the District has more residents than Wyoming and Vermont. Opponents argue that the Constitution intended a neutral federal district outside any state, that statehood might require a constitutional amendment, and that the federal core should instead be shrunk while the residential areas are retroceded to Maryland — an alternative some residents and politicians have proposed instead.
