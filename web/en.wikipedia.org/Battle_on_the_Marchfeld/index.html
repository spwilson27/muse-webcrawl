The Battle on the Marchfeld, also known as the Battle of Dürnkrut and Jedenspeigen, was fought on 26 August 1278 between the Bohemian king Ottokar II and a combined army of Rudolf I of Habsburg, King of the Romans, and Ladislaus IV of Hungary. It is often called the largest knightly battle of the Middle Ages, and its outcome — the death of Ottokar and the Habsburg acquisition of Austria — laid the foundation for the rise of the House of Habsburg in Central Europe.

Background

During the Great Interregnum, Ottokar II of Bohemia had built a great central European empire, acquiring Austria, Styria, Carinthia and Carniola. When the imperial electors chose the relatively obscure Count Rudolf of Habsburg as King of the Romans in 1273, Rudolf demanded the return of the imperial lands Ottokar had seized. After being placed under the imperial ban and defeated in a brief campaign in 1276, Ottokar formally surrendered Austria but rebuilt his alliances and marched again in 1278 to recover his losses. Rudolf, reinforced by some 15,000 Cuman and Hungarian troops under King Ladislaus IV, met him on the Marchfeld plain northeast of Vienna.

Battle

Estimates of the armies vary widely, from about 15,000 to 30,000 men a side, with Bohemian heavy cavalry considered the finest in Europe. The battle began with a clash of the Hungarian and Cuman horse-archers against the Bohemian advance guard. The main bodies of knights then engaged in a grinding melee. A concealed Habsburg reserve of some 50–60 knights under Ulrich von Kapellen, hidden behind a rise, charged into the Bohemian flank at the crisis of the fight — an ambush that Bohemian sources condemned as dishonourable. The Bohemian army broke; Ottokar, refusing to flee, was killed in the aftermath, reportedly by men with private grievances, and his body was later displayed in Vienna.

Consequences

Bohemia lost its Austrian empire and was reduced in influence for a generation, while Rudolf installed his sons Albert and Rudolf as rulers of Austria and Styria in 1282. The Habsburgs would hold Austria almost continuously until 1918. The battle is still commemorated in Austria, with memorials near Dürnkrut and Jedenspeigen marking the site.
