Arachnids are a class (Arachnida) of joint-legged invertebrates in the subphylum Chelicerata. Almost all have eight walking legs, two main body segments — the cephalothorax (prosoma) and abdomen (opisthosoma) — and lack antennae and wings. The class includes spiders, scorpions, harvestmen (daddy longlegs), mites and ticks, solifuges, and several smaller orders; spiders alone account for roughly 50,000 described species.

Anatomy

In front of the walking legs, arachnids bear a pair of chelicerae (used in feeding, modified into fangs in spiders) and a pair of pedipalps, which serve as sensory organs, prey-handling limbs or, in scorpions, large pincers. Most arachnids are terrestrial and breathe through book lungs, tracheae, or both. Their mostly liquid diet is digested externally or filtered, as arachnids generally cannot ingest solid food.

Ecology and behaviour

Arachnids are predominantly predators of insects and other small arthropods, though mites include herbivores, detritivores and parasites — ticks feed on vertebrate blood and transmit diseases such as Lyme disease. Many arachnids produce venom for subduing prey, but only a small number of spiders and scorpions are dangerous to humans. Silk production is characteristic of spiders and some mites; spiders also use silk for webs, egg sacs and ballooning dispersal.

Evolution

Arachnids descend from aquatic chelicerate ancestors related to horseshoe crabs and sea spiders. The earliest known terrestrial arachnids, trigonotarbids and early scorpions, date to the Silurian and Devonian periods, more than 400 million years ago, making arachnids among the earliest animals to live on land.
