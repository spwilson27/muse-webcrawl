Hebron

Hebron (Arabic: الخليل, al-Khalīl; Hebrew: חֶבְרוֹן, Ḥevron) is a Palestinian city in the southern West Bank, about 30 kilometres south of Jerusalem. It is the largest city in the West Bank by population and the capital of the Hebron Governorate. Situated at around 930 metres above sea level in the Judaean Mountains, Hebron is one of the oldest continuously inhabited cities in the world.

Religious significance

Hebron is sacred to Judaism, Islam, and Christianity as the burial place of the patriarchs and matriarchs. The Cave of the Patriarchs (known to Muslims as the Ibrahimi Mosque) is traditionally identified as the tomb of Abraham, Sarah, Isaac, Rebecca, Jacob, and Leah. According to the Bible, Abraham purchased the cave as a family burial plot, and Hebron was where David was anointed king of Israel before ruling in Jerusalem.

History

The city has been ruled in turn by Canaanites, Israelites, Romans, Byzantines, Arab caliphates, Crusaders, Mamluks, and Ottomans. Herod the Great built the great enclosure wall around the Cave of the Patriarchs that still stands. In 1929, riots led to the massacre of 67 Jews and the evacuation of the ancient Jewish community. After 1967 Hebron came under Israeli occupation; Jewish settlers later established enclaves in and near the old city. Following the 1994 massacre of 29 Palestinian worshippers at the Ibrahimi Mosque by an Israeli settler, the 1997 Hebron Protocol divided the city: about 80 per cent (H1) under Palestinian Authority control and 20 per cent (H2), including the old city and the shrine, under Israeli military control. In 2017 UNESCO inscribed Hebron's Old Town as a World Heritage Site.
