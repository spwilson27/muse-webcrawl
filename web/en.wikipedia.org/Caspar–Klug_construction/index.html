Caspar–Klug construction

Icosahedral capsid lattice constructed by folding a hexagonal sheet according to (h,k) = (2,1)

The Caspar–Klug construction is a geometric scheme, introduced by Donald Caspar and Aaron Klug in 1962, for describing the organisation of proteins on the surfaces of icosahedral virus capsids. It extends the ideas found in Buckminster Fuller's geodesic domes to explain how identical protein subunits can assemble into a closed icosahedral shell larger than 60 subunits.

Triangulation number

The construction begins with a planar hexagonal (triangular) lattice of protein subunits. An equilateral triangle of the lattice is chosen whose edge is a vector defined by two non-negative integers h and k, and twenty such triangles are folded into an icosahedron. The resulting capsid contains 60T protein subunits, where the triangulation number is

T = h2 + hk + k2.

The allowed values are T = 1, 3, 4, 7, 9, 12, 13, … Subunits occupy "quasi-equivalent" environments: those at the twelve vertices sit in pentamers (pentons), while the remainder form hexamers (hexons). The theory of quasi-equivalence rationalised electron-microscope images of viruses such as tomato bushy stunt virus and poliovirus, and remains a standard framework in structural virology, although many viruses are now known to deviate from strict Caspar–Klug geometry (for example, "pseudo T" arrangements, in which hexamer positions are occupied by other oligomers).

Applications

Beyond natural viruses, the construction is used in the design of artificial protein cages, virus-like particles for vaccines, and bionanotechnology.
