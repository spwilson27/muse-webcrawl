Moral rights

Moral rights are rights of creators of copyrighted works that protect the personal and reputational bond between an author and their work, independently of the author's economic rights. The concept — French: droit moral — developed in civil-law countries, above all France, and is recognised internationally through Article 6bis of the Berne Convention.

The principal rights

  • Right of attribution (paternity): to be identified, or to remain anonymous or pseudonymous, as the author of the work.
  • Right of integrity: to object to distortion, mutilation, or other derogatory treatment of the work prejudicial to the author's honour or reputation.
  • Right of disclosure: to decide whether and when the work is made public (recognised in some jurisdictions).
  • Right of withdrawal: in a few systems, to retract the work from circulation, subject to indemnifying users.

Duration and transferability

In many civil-law systems moral rights are perpetual, inalienable, and imprescriptible — in France they pass to the author's heirs and can be exercised indefinitely — while copyright statutes elsewhere limit them to the author's lifetime or the term of copyright. Because they are personal, moral rights generally cannot be sold, though in some jurisdictions they may be waived by consent.

Common-law jurisdictions

Common-law countries adopted moral rights more narrowly and later. The United Kingdom introduced them with the Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, requiring attribution rights to be asserted. The United States recognises moral rights mainly for works of visual art under the Visual Artists Rights Act of 1990 (VARA), alongside patchwork protection through contract, trademark, and defamation law.
