The Provisional Irish Republican Army (Provisional IRA), officially known as the Irish Republican Army (IRA) and informally as the Provos, was an Irish republican paramilitary organisation that sought to end British rule in Northern Ireland, facilitate Irish reunification, and bring about an independent republic encompassing all of Ireland. It was the largest republican paramilitary group during the Troubles, the decades-long conflict in Northern Ireland.

Origins

The Provisional IRA emerged in December 1969 from a split in the Irish Republican Army over strategy and ideology. The older leadership in Dublin, influenced by Marxism, had moved toward political action and away from armed struggle, and was accused by Belfast members of failing to defend Catholic neighbourhoods during the communal violence of August 1969. The breakaway "Provisional" faction, led by figures such as Seán Mac Stíofáin and Ruairí Ó Brádaigh, insisted on retaining abstentionism and a commitment to military action, while the "Official" wing went on to become the Official IRA.

Campaign

From 1970 the Provisional IRA waged an armed campaign against the British Army, the Royal Ulster Constabulary, and loyalist paramilitaries, as well as economic and political targets in Northern Ireland and England. Its campaign included bombings, shootings, and punishment attacks; it also ran a political wing relationship with Sinn Féin, though the two organisations maintained distinct structures. Major episodes included Bloody Friday (1972), the Brighton hotel bombing (1984) targeting Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher, mortar attacks on Downing Street (1991), and the large City of London bombs at the Baltic Exchange (1992) and Bishopsgate (1993). The IRA declared ceasefires in 1975 and again in 1994; the latter broke down with the Canary Wharf bombing of 1996 before being restored in 1997.

Peace process and decommissioning

The restored ceasefire underpinned the negotiations leading to the Good Friday Agreement of 1998, which established a power-sharing settlement in Northern Ireland. After years of dispute over arms decommissioning, on 28 July 2005 the IRA announced the formal end of its armed campaign, ordering units to dump arms and pursue exclusively peaceful means. In September 2005 the Independent International Commission on Decommissioning reported that the IRA had put its entire arsenal beyond use. Dissident groups that rejected the peace process, such as the Real IRA and Continuity IRA, continued sporadic violence.

Casualties and legacy

Security-force and academic tallies attribute roughly 1,700 deaths to the Provisional IRA during the Troubles, including about 1,000 members of the security forces and several hundred civilians; around 300 IRA volunteers also died on active service. The organisation's campaign, and the wider conflict, remain the subject of intense historical and political debate in Ireland and Britain.
