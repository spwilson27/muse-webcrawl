Senatus consultum ultimum

The senatus consultum ultimum ("final decree of the Senate"), more fully senatus consultum de re publica defendenda ("decree of the Senate on defending the republic"), is a term generally applied to a special emergency decree passed by the Roman Senate during the late Roman Republic. The form of the decree instructed the magistrates (chiefly the consuls) to take measures "that the republic take no harm" (videant consules ne quid res publica detrimenti capiat), authorizing extraordinary action against citizens deemed enemies of the state.

History

The first undisputed use of the decree was in 121 BC against Gaius Gracchus and his supporters, when the consul Lucius Opimius used it to justify killing Roman citizens without trial; Gracchus died and some 3,000 of his followers were executed. Opimius was later prosecuted but acquitted, setting a contested precedent.

Subsequent uses included the decrees against Saturninus in 100 BC, against the Catilinarian conspiracy in 63 BC—when Cicero as consul executed five conspirators under its authority, an act for which he was later exiled—and against Julius Caesar in January 49 BC, a precipitating act of the civil war.

Legality and debate

The decree's legality was debated in antiquity and remains so among scholars. It did not create new legal powers and did not formally suspend the citizens' right of appeal (provocatio), but it expressed the Senate's opinion that the consuls were justified in acting, protecting them politically—and, in practice, often in law. Critics such as Caesar argued in his Commentarii de Bello Civili that the decree was being abused against tribunes and lawful political opposition.
