Edible birds nest

Edible birds nest refers to the nests of certain swiftlets that are harvested and consumed, most famously in Chinese cuisine, where they are the principal ingredient of bird's nest soup. The nests are made almost entirely of solidified saliva produced by the birds, and when dissolved in water they take on a gelatinous texture prized for both culinary and medicinal purposes.

Source birds

The most commercially important species are the edible-nest swiftlet (Aerodramus fuciphagus) and the black-nest swiftlet (Aerodramus maximus). These small birds, found throughout Southeast Asia, traditionally nested in large limestone caves, though purpose-built "swiftlet houses" are now widespread. During the breeding season the male swiftlet produces copious saliva from enlarged sublingual glands, which it uses to construct a shallow cup-shaped nest attached to a cave wall.

Harvesting

Nest collection is a hazardous occupation: harvesters climb tall bamboo scaffolding or are lowered on ropes inside dark caves to reach nests high on the walls. Major producing regions include Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, Vietnam, and parts of the Philippines. Because a single kilogram of cleaned white nests can contain hundreds of individual nests and fetch very high prices, the trade is tightly regulated in several countries to prevent over-harvesting and disturbance of breeding colonies.

Culinary use

After cleaning — a laborious process of soaking and picking out feathers and debris — the nests are simmered, often with rock sugar, to make the classic sweet soup. The nests themselves have little flavour; their appeal lies in their delicate, strand-like gelatinous texture. In savoury preparations they may be cooked in chicken broth or served with minced meat and egg white.

Traditional medicine and research

In traditional Chinese medicine, bird's nest is believed to nourish the lungs, improve complexion, and support the immune system. Modern analysis shows the nests are rich in glycoproteins and contain epidermal growth factor-like compounds and sialic acid, though clinical evidence for health claims remains limited.
