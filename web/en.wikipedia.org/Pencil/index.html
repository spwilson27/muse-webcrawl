Pencil

A pencil is a writing or drawing implement with a solid pigment core inside a protective casing that reduces the risk of core breakage and keeps the user's hand from becoming soiled. Most pencil cores are made of graphite powder mixed with a clay binder—the word "lead pencil" is a historical misnomer, as the core contains no lead. Unlike ink, pencil marks can usually be erased.

History

The modern pencil followed the discovery of a large deposit of pure graphite at Borrowdale, England, around 1564. Initially graphite sticks were wrapped in string or wood; the graphite-clay mixture was developed independently by Nicolas-Jacques Conté in France in 1795. Mass production flourished in the 19th century in Germany (Faber-Castell), Britain (Cumberland) and the United States, where the familiar yellow-painted hexagonal pencil became standard.

Grading and types

Pencils are graded by the hardness of their cores: H grades are harder and lighter, B grades softer and darker, with HB in the middle; F denotes a fine point. Other types include mechanical pencils with refillable leads, coloured pencils with pigment cores, carpenter's pencils with flat cores, and charcoal and grease pencils.

Manufacture

Wooden pencils are typically made by milling grooves into slats of incense cedar, inserting the graphite-clay cores, gluing a second slat on top, and cutting and lacquering the individual pencils—many of which are tipped with an eraser held by a metal ferrule.
