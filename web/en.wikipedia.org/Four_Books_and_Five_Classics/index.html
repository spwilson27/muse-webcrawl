The Four Books and Five Classics are authoritative writings that form the core of the Confucian canon in China. They were the subject of the civil examination for most of the period from the Song dynasty onward, and formed the foundation of orthodox education across East Asia, influencing Korea, Japan and Vietnam.

The Four Books

Selected and published as a set by the Neo-Confucian scholar Zhu Xi in 1190:

  • Great Learning (Daxue) — on self-cultivation as the root of social order
  • Doctrine of the Mean (Zhongyong) — on harmony and sincerity
  • Analects (Lunyu) — sayings of Confucius and his disciples
  • Mencius (Mengzi) — teachings of the philosopher Mencius

The Five Classics

  • Classic of Poetry (Shijing) — 305 poems from the early Zhou
  • Book of Documents (Shujing) — speeches and records of early rulers
  • Book of Rites (Liji) — ritual and social norms
  • I Ching (Yijing, Book of Changes) — divination and cosmology
  • Spring and Autumn Annals (Chunqiu) — chronicle of the state of Lu, attributed to Confucius

Role in examinations

From 1313 until the system's abolition in 1905, candidates for imperial office were examined chiefly on the Four Books with Zhu Xi's commentaries as the orthodox interpretation. Mastery of the canon was the gateway to bureaucratic careers, creating a shared intellectual culture among China's literati for centuries.

Legacy

The canon shaped ethics, historiography, and literary style throughout the Sinosphere. After 1905 the texts lost their institutional role but remain central to the study of Chinese philosophy and are again taught in modern Chinese curricula.
