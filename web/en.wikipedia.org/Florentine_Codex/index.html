Florentine Codex

The Florentine Codex is a 16th-century ethnographic research study in Mesoamerica by the Spanish Franciscan friar Bernardino de Sahagún. Sahagún originally titled it La Historia General de las Cosas de Nueva España (The General History of the Things of New Spain). After a translation mistake, it was given the name Historia general de las cosas de Nueva España. The best-preserved manuscript is commonly referred to as the Florentine Codex, as it is held in the Laurentian Library of Florence, Italy.

Contents

The work consists of twelve books covering Aztec religion and cosmology, the calendar, rhetoric and moral philosophy, medicine, natural history, crafts, and, in Book 12, an account of the Spanish conquest of the Aztec Empire told from the perspective of the Nahua of Tlatelolco. It is written in parallel columns of Nahuatl and Spanish, with some 2,500 illustrations drawn by Nahua artists trained in European techniques.

Creation and suppression

Sahagún worked from about 1545 until 1590, questioning Nahua elders and scribes at Tepepulco, Tlatelolco, and Mexico City. In 1577, concerned that the work preserved "idolatrous" knowledge, the Spanish Crown ordered his manuscripts confiscated and sent to Spain; copies nevertheless survived, and the principal manuscript reached Florence, where it was rediscovered in the late 19th century.

Significance

The Florentine Codex is regarded as one of the most complete sources on Aztec culture and the Nahuatl language, and has been called the "first ethnography." A full digital edition was published by the World Digital Library.
