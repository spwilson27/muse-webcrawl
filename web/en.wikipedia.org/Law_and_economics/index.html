Law and economics

Law and economics applies economic theory to the analysis of law.

Law and economics, or economic analysis of law, is the application of microeconomic theory to the analysis of law. The field emerged in the United States in the 1960s, principally at the University of Chicago, from the work of scholars such as Ronald Coase, whose 1960 article "The Problem of Social Cost" introduced the Coase theorem, and Guido Calabresi, whose work on torts and risk distribution appeared shortly after. Richard Posner's treatise Economic Analysis of Law (1973) systematised the field for lawyers.

Origins and development

Although earlier writers — including Adam Smith on the economic effects of legislation and Jeremy Bentham on utilitarian law reform — anticipated the field, law and economics as a self-conscious movement began around 1960 with the founding of the Journal of Law and Economics (1958), Coase's analysis of transaction costs, Gary Becker's economic analysis of crime and punishment, and Calabresi's work on accidents. The movement became highly influential in American legal education, judicial reasoning (notably through judges such as Posner, Easterbrook and Bork) and regulatory policy, including cost-benefit analysis of regulation.

Method and key ideas

Law and economics analyses legal rules as systems of incentives: sanctions function as prices, and individuals respond to changes in them. Central concepts include efficiency (notably Pareto and Kaldor–Hicks efficiency), transaction costs, externalities, property rights, and the economic models of contracts, torts, crime, litigation and legal process. A celebrated positive claim is the efficiency of the common law hypothesis — that judge-made law tends to evolve toward efficient rules — while the normative branch argues that efficiency should be a goal of legal policy.

Criticism

Critics question the field's assumptions of rational, self-interested actors; behavioural law and economics has incorporated findings from psychology to address these concerns. Others challenge efficiency as the proper criterion for law, arguing that distributional justice and rights should not be subordinated to wealth maximisation. The movement's influence has been greatest in the United States, though it has spread to Europe, Latin America and Asia.
