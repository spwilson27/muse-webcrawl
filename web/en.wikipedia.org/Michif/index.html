Michif (also spelled Mitchif, from a Cree pronunciation of French métif, "mixed") is the language of the Métis people of Canada and the northern United States, who are the descendants of Indigenous women — chiefly Cree — and French (and other European) fur traders. It is a classic mixed language, combining French noun phrases with Cree verb phrases in a stable, rule-governed way.

Structure

Michif is unusual among the world's mixed languages for the complexity of both its components. Nouns, adjectives, articles, and numerals are overwhelmingly of French origin and retain French gender and phonology, including liaison. Verbs, by contrast, are almost entirely Plains Cree, with the full polysynthetic Algonquian verbal morphology — animate/inanimate distinctions, obviation, and complex inflection — intact. Linguist Peter Bakker's A Language of Our Own (1997) is the standard description. Some dialects incorporate elements of Ojibwe and Assiniboine.

History and status

The language crystallized in early 19th-century Métis communities of the Red River region and the buffalo-hunting brigades of the northern plains. Suppression of Métis identity after the Red River and North-West Resistances, together with residential schooling and stigma, drove steep decline; by the early 21st century fluent speakers numbered under a hundred, most of them elderly. Revitalization efforts by Métis governments and language organizations include dictionaries, teaching materials, and immersion programs, and Michif gained new visibility when it was spoken ceremonially in Canada's Parliament.
