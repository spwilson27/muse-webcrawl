Jacob Riis

Jacob August Riis
Born: 3 May 1849, Ribe, Denmark
Died: 26 May 1914, Barre, Massachusetts
Occupation: Journalist, photographer, social reformer

Jacob August Riis (May 3, 1849 – May 26, 1914) was a Danish-American social reformer, "muckraking" journalist and social documentary photographer. He is best known for How the Other Half Lives (1890), which used flash photography to expose the squalid living conditions in New York City slums and helped spur tenement reform.

Early life

Riis emigrated from Ribe, Denmark, to the United States in 1870, arriving nearly penniless and experiencing homelessness himself. He worked various jobs before becoming a police reporter for the New-York Tribune, covering crime in the Lower East Side, where he saw first-hand the overcrowded tenements of the poor.

How the Other Half Lives

Dissatisfied that words alone could not convey what he witnessed, Riis took up photography, using the new flash powder technique to photograph dark interiors. His lantern-slide lectures and his 1890 book combined text and images to reveal the conditions of the "other half". The book influenced reformers including Theodore Roosevelt, then a police commissioner, who called Riis "the most useful citizen of New York."

Legacy

Riis campaigned for the demolition of the worst slums such as Mulberry Bend, for parks, schools and cleaner water. Jacob Riis Park in Queens and schools and a housing project in New York bear his name. His photographs, preserved at the Library of Congress and the Museum of the City of New York, are landmarks of documentary photography.
