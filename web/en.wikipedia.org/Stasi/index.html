Stasi

The Stasi headquarters complex in Berlin-Lichtenberg, now a museum.

The Stasi (German: Staatssicherheit), officially the Ministry for State Security (Ministerium für Staatssicherheit, MfS), was the state security service of East Germany (the German Democratic Republic) from 1950 to 1990. It functioned as both an intelligence and secret police agency, devoted to surveillance and repression of the population to protect the ruling Socialist Unity Party regime.

Surveillance network

The Stasi maintained one of the most extensive surveillance networks in history. By 1989 it employed about 91,000 full-time staff and relied on roughly 170,000 unofficial collaborators (Inoffizielle Mitarbeiter) who reported on colleagues, neighbours, and family members. Its long-serving head, Erich Mielke (1957–1989), directed operations including mail and telephone interception, and Zersetzung ("decomposition") — psychological harassment designed to undermine dissidents' careers, relationships, and mental health without overt arrest.

After reunification

Following the Peaceful Revolution, citizens occupied Stasi offices in January 1990 to stop the destruction of files. The agency was dissolved, and its archives — some 111 kilometres of files — were opened to the public under the Stasi Records Act, administered by a federal records agency. Victims gained the right to see their files, and the records have been extensively used in research and in vetting public officials.
