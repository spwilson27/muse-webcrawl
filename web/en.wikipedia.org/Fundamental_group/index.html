Fundamental group

In the mathematical field of algebraic topology, the fundamental group of a topological space is a group that encodes information about the shape, or topology, of the space. It is formed by equivalence classes of loops — continuous paths that start and end at a fixed base point — where two loops are equivalent if one can be continuously deformed into the other within the space. The fundamental group is usually denoted π1(X, x0).

Definition

Given a topological space X and a base point x0, a loop is a continuous map from the unit interval [0, 1] to X sending 0 and 1 to x0. Loops are combined by concatenation: traverse the first loop, then the second. Up to homotopy (continuous deformation relative to the endpoints), this operation makes the set of equivalence classes into a group, with the constant loop as identity and the reversed loop as inverse.

For a path-connected space, the fundamental group does not depend (up to isomorphism) on the choice of base point. A space whose fundamental group is trivial — consisting of a single element — is called simply connected, provided it is also path-connected.

Examples

The fundamental group of the circle is the group of integers ℤ: loops are classified by their winding number. The fundamental group of the n-sphere is trivial for n ≥ 2. The torus has fundamental group ℤ × ℤ, generated by loops around its two independent cycles. The real projective plane has fundamental group of order two, and the figure-eight (wedge of two circles) has the free group on two generators. By contrast, the fundamental group of a knot complement is a subtle invariant used in knot theory.

Functoriality and covering spaces

Continuous maps between spaces induce group homomorphisms between their fundamental groups, making π1 a functor from pointed topological spaces to groups — historically one of the motivating examples of functorial thinking. There is a deep correspondence (the Galois correspondence of covering spaces) between subgroups of the fundamental group and covering spaces of the space; the universal cover corresponds to the trivial subgroup. The van Kampen theorem provides a practical method for computing fundamental groups by decomposing a space into overlapping pieces.
