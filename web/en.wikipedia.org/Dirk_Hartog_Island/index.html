Dirk Hartog Island is an island off the Gascoyne coast of Western Australia, at the westernmost point of the Australian continent, within the Shark Bay World Heritage Area. At about 80 km long and up to 15 km wide, it is the largest island in Western Australia. It is named after Dutch sea captain Dirk Hartog, who landed there in 1616.

History

On 25 October 1616, Dirk Hartog, captain of the Dutch East India Company ship Eendracht, made the first recorded European landing on the western coast of Australia, leaving an inscribed pewter plate nailed to a post at the island's northern end — now Cape Inscription. The plate, replaced by Willem de Vlamingh in 1697 and later removed by Louis de Freycinet in 1818, is now held in the Rijksmuseum in Amsterdam; it is the oldest known European artifact from Australia. The island was later used as a pastoral station for sheep from the 1860s, and for pearling and guano mining at various times.

Ecology and conservation

In 2009 the island was purchased by the Western Australian government and converted into Dirk Hartog Island National Park. Under the "Return to 1616" ecological restoration project, sheep and feral goats were removed and feral cats eradicated, and native species extinct on the island — including rufous and banded hare-wallabies, dibblers, Shark Bay mice, and western barred bandicoots — have been reintroduced. The island's arid shrublands, seagrass surroundings, and loggerhead turtle nesting beaches are of high conservation value within the Shark Bay World Heritage Site.
