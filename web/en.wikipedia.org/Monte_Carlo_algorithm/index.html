Monte Carlo algorithm

In computational complexity theory, a Monte Carlo algorithm is a randomized algorithm whose output may be incorrect with some small probability. The name comes from the Monte Carlo method of using repeated random sampling, itself named after the casino district of Monaco. Such algorithms trade certainty for speed: they answer a decision problem correctly with probability at least some constant greater than one half, and the error can be reduced exponentially by repeating the computation and taking a majority vote.

Randomized algorithms use chance as a computational resource.

One-sided and two-sided error

Monte Carlo algorithms may have one-sided error — for example, always answering correctly on "yes" instances while sometimes erring on "no" instances — or two-sided error, erring on either. A classic example is the Solovay–Strassen primality test, and its successor the Miller–Rabin test, which can declare a composite number prime with a small, controllable probability. The complexity class BPP (bounded-error probabilistic polynomial time) consists of problems solvable by Monte Carlo algorithms in polynomial time with error probability at most 1/3.

Amplification

If an algorithm errs with probability at most 1/2 − ε, running it independently k times and taking the majority answer reduces the error probability to a quantity that falls exponentially in k (a Chernoff bound argument). This amplification is what makes randomized algorithms practical despite their uncertainty.

Comparison with Las Vegas algorithms

Monte Carlo algorithms contrast with Las Vegas algorithms, whose runtime is random but whose output is always correct. A Las Vegas algorithm can be converted to a Monte Carlo algorithm by cutting it off after a fixed time and accepting a small chance of no answer.
