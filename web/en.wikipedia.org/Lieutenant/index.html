Lieutenant

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A lieutenant (UK: /lɛfˈtɛnənt/; US: /luːˈtɛnənt/) is a junior commissioned officer rank in many nations' armies, navies, and air forces, as well as in some police and fire services. The word comes from the French lieu tenant — literally "holding a place" — meaning a deputy who acts in the place of a superior. Historically, a lieutenant was the deputy of a captain; in modern usage it denotes the lower grades of commissioned officer.

Army rank

In most Commonwealth armies, lieutenant (pronounced "leftenant" in British and Canadian usage) is the rank above second lieutenant and below captain; the holder typically commands a platoon or troop of about 30 soldiers. In the United States Army, Marine Corps, Air Force, and Space Force, the rank is divided into second lieutenant (grade O-1) and first lieutenant (O-2); a first lieutenant may serve as a platoon leader, executive officer, or staff officer.

Naval rank

In the Royal Navy, a lieutenant is a commissioned officer senior to sub-lieutenant and junior to lieutenant commander, equivalent to an army captain. In the United States Navy and Coast Guard, lieutenant (O-3) is likewise equivalent to an army captain, with lieutenant (junior grade) (O-2) below it; the corresponding junior grade derives from the older naval structure in which lieutenants were the commissioned deputies of a ship's captain. "Lieutenant" also appears in compound appointments such as first lieutenant, originally denoting the senior lieutenant of a ship.

Other uses

Many police forces, particularly in the United States, use lieutenant as a mid-level supervisory rank above sergeant and below captain. Fire services in several countries use it for company officers. The term also appears in civil titles such as lieutenant governor and lord lieutenant, reflecting its original meaning of a deputy.

Pronunciation

The British "leftenant" pronunciation is also used in Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and India, while American English uses "loo-tenant". The divergence is centuries old; the British form is thought to derive from an early reading of the French lieu with a labialized vowel interpreted as "f".
