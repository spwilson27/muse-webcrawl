Cement

A cement is a binder, a chemical substance used for construction that sets, hardens, and adheres to other materials to bind them together. Cement is seldom used on its own, but rather to bind sand and gravel (aggregate) together. Cement mixed with fine aggregate produces mortar for masonry, or with sand and gravel, produces concrete. Concrete is the most widely used material in existence and is behind only water as the planet's most-consumed resource.

Types

Cements used in construction are usually inorganic, often lime- or calcium silicate-based. The most common is Portland cement, patented by Joseph Aspdin in 1824 and produced by heating limestone and clay in a kiln to form clinker, which is ground with gypsum. Other types include pozzolanic cements, slag cements, white cement and geopolymer cements.

Chemistry

Portland cement clinker consists mainly of alite (tricalcium silicate), belite, tricalcium aluminate and ferrite. On contact with water these phases hydrate exothermically to form calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) gel and calcium hydroxide, which give hardened cement its strength.

Production and environment

World production exceeds four billion tonnes per year, with China the largest producer. Cement manufacture accounts for roughly 8% of global carbon dioxide emissions, about half from the calcination of limestone and half from fuel combustion; decarbonisation strategies include clinker substitution with slag, fly ash and calcined clays, alternative fuels and carbon capture.

History

The Romans used hydraulic lime and volcanic pozzolana to make durable concrete for structures such as the Pantheon. The secret was lost in the Middle Ages and rediscovered through the work of John Smeaton (Eddystone Lighthouse, 1756) and Aspdin's Portland cement.
