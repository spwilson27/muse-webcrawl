Mistrial

A mistrial is a trial that has been terminated and declared void prior to the court's return of a verdict or some other legal resolution. A mistrial is not the same as an acquittal or a conviction; rather, the proceedings are invalidated, and the case may be retried before a new jury and a different judge, at the discretion of the prosecuting authority.

Grounds for a mistrial

A mistrial may be declared for a number of reasons, including:

  • A hung jury, in which the jurors cannot reach the unanimous or supermajority verdict required by law after extended deliberation.
  • Improper admission of prejudicial evidence or testimony that cannot be cured by a jury instruction.
  • Misconduct by a juror, attorney, or witness that compromises the fairness of the proceedings.
  • The death or serious illness of a juror, judge, or essential participant.
  • A fundamental error in procedure, such as an improperly constituted jury.

Effect and double jeopardy

When a mistrial is declared, the double jeopardy clause generally does not bar retrial where the mistrial was declared out of "manifest necessity" — the classic example being a genuinely deadlocked jury. However, if a mistrial is provoked by prosecutorial misconduct intended to goad the defendant into seeking it, retrial may be barred. If the mistrial results from the absence of evidence, the appellate court may treat the outcome as equivalent to an acquittal.

Hung juries

The most frequent cause of mistrials in jury trials is the hung jury. In some jurisdictions a substantial fraction of jury trials — estimates range from under 2 percent to over 20 percent depending on the court system — end in deadlock. After a mistrial for deadlock, prosecutors must decide whether to retry the case, offer a plea bargain, or dismiss the charges, a decision influenced by the reported split of the jury.
