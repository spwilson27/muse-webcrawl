Kalila and Dimna

Kalila and Dimna (Arabic: كليلة ودمنة) is a famous collection of animal fables framed as a mirror for princes, offering counsel on statecraft, friendship, and prudence. It derives from the Sanskrit Panchatantra, which was translated into Middle Persian in the 6th century by the physician Borzuya, and from Persian into Arabic around 750 CE by Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ, whose elegant version became a classic of Arabic prose.

Content

The frame story concerns a king who asks a philosopher to instruct him through animal tales. The title characters, Kalila and Dimna, are two jackals serving a lion king; Dimna's scheming against the lion's friend, the ox Shanzaba, drives the longest and most celebrated tale, a meditation on ambition, slander, and the corruption of courts. Nested stories within stories illustrate each moral from multiple angles.

Influence

From Arabic, the work was translated into Syriac, Persian, Hebrew, Castilian, and many other languages, spreading across Europe and Asia; it influenced later fabulists including Jean de La Fontaine. More than a hundred versions in over fifty languages are known, and illustrated manuscripts of Kalila and Dimna are among the masterpieces of Persian and Mughal painting.
