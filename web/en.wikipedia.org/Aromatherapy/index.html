Aromatherapy

Aromatherapy is a practice within alternative medicine that uses aromatic plant materials — principally essential oils — in the belief that inhaling or applying them can improve psychological or physical well-being. It is used in massage, baths, inhalation and topical preparations, often alongside other complementary therapies.

History

The use of aromatic plants and oils dates to ancient Egyptian, Chinese and Indian traditions. The modern term was coined by the French chemist René-Maurice Gattefossé, whose 1937 book Aromathérapie recounted the use of lavender oil on burns; the practice was further developed by Jean Valnet and the biochemist Marguerite Maury, who promoted its use in massage. Aromatherapy became widely popular in the late 20th century as part of the growth of complementary and alternative medicine.

Evidence and criticism

Scientific evidence for aromatherapy's effectiveness beyond possible short-term effects on mood, anxiety or nausea is limited: systematic reviews generally find small, inconsistent effects and note poor trial quality, and major medical bodies classify most therapeutic claims as unproven. Some essential oils can cause allergic reactions, skin irritation or toxicity if ingested, and regulators in several countries restrict health claims made for them. Aromatherapy is nonetheless widely offered in spas, hospices and some hospitals as a comfort measure.
