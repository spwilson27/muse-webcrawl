The Data Encryption Standard (DES) is a symmetric-key algorithm for the encryption of digital data. Developed at IBM in the early 1970s and adopted as a US federal standard in 1977, DES was for decades the dominant block cipher, used in banking, telecommunications, and government systems. Its short 56-bit key eventually made it vulnerable to brute-force attack, and it was superseded by the Advanced Encryption Standard (AES).

History

DES derived from IBM's Lucifer cipher, designed by a team including Horst Feistel and Don Coppersmith. The National Bureau of Standards solicited a standard cipher, and IBM's submission — modified in consultation with the NSA, which shortened the key from 128 to 56 bits and altered the S-boxes — was published in 1975 amid controversy over possible backdoors. Later research showed the S-box changes actually strengthened DES against differential cryptanalysis, a technique not publicly known until 1990.

Algorithm

DES encrypts 64-bit blocks through 16 Feistel rounds, each using a 48-bit subkey derived from the 56-bit key. Initial and final permutations and eight substitution boxes (S-boxes) provide confusion and diffusion. Decryption uses the same circuit with subkeys in reverse order.

Security

The 56-bit key space (256 possibilities) was criticised as too small from the outset; in 1977 Diffie and Hellman estimated a dedicated machine could break DES in a day. In 1998 the Electronic Frontier Foundation's "Deep Crack" machine recovered a DES key in 56 hours, and in 1999 in just over 22 hours with distributed help. Triple DES (3DES) extended the cipher's life in finance, but NIST withdrew DES as a standard in 2005 and deprecated 3DES thereafter.
