The Basilica Cistern (Turkish: Yerebatan Sarnıcı, "Cistern Sinking into the Ground") is the largest of several hundred ancient cisterns that lie beneath the city of Istanbul, Turkey. Built in the 6th century during the reign of the Byzantine emperor Justinian I, it stored water for the Great Palace of Constantinople and other buildings on the First Hill.

History

The cistern takes its name from a large public square, the Stoa Basilica, that once stood above it, beneath which an earlier cistern had been built. Justinian's engineers constructed the vast underground reservoir after the Nika riots of 532, employing thousands of workers. Water arrived by aqueducts from the Belgrade Forest north of the city. After the Ottoman conquest the cistern was largely forgotten by the authorities — residents drew water through holes in their floors — until it was publicized by the French scholar Petrus Gyllius in the 1540s.

Description

The cistern's vaulted ceiling is supported by a forest of 336 marble columns, each about 9 metres high, arranged in 12 rows of 28, many of them reused from earlier Roman buildings. Two column bases famously incorporate enormous carved Medusa heads, placed sideways and upside down. The brick walls are coated with waterproof mortar, and the reservoir could hold around 80,000 cubic metres of water.

Today

Cleaned and opened to the public in 1987, the Basilica Cistern is now one of Istanbul's most visited attractions, with raised walkways above the shallow water and atmospheric lighting and art installations.
