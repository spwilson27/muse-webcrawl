1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak

The 1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak was a severe outbreak of cholera that occurred in the Soho district of London, England, then part of a wider cholera pandemic. More than 600 people died in the immediate neighbourhood within days. The physician John Snow investigated the outbreak, mapped the cases, and traced the source to a public water pump on Broad Street (now Broadwick Street) — a landmark investigation in the founding of modern epidemiology.

The outbreak

On 31 August 1854 the neighbourhood around Broad Street (today Broadwick Street) in Soho was struck by a sudden, devastating epidemic. Within three days, 127 people living near the street had died, and within two weeks more than 500 had died in the district. Many residents fled; those who remained used the popular Broad Street pump for drinking water.

Snow's investigation

Snow, who had hypothesised that cholera spread through contaminated water rather than "miasma" (bad air), plotted the deaths on a map — the famous dot map — showing cases clustered around the pump. He identified anomalies that strengthened his case: workers at the nearby Lion Brewery, who drank malt liquor and rarely the pump water, largely escaped, while a widow in Hampstead who had Broad Street water delivered died along with her visiting niece. On 7 September Snow persuaded the parish Board of Guardians to remove the pump handle. The epidemic then subsided — although Snow himself noted it had already begun to decline.

Aftermath

Later investigation showed that a cesspool at 40 Broad Street, into which the soiled nappies of an infant who had contracted cholera elsewhere had been washed, leaked into the pump's well. Snow's findings, though resisted at the time amid the dominance of miasma theory, were vindicated as germ theory developed; the episode is a canonical case study in public health. A replica pump and a pub named after Snow now commemorate the site.
