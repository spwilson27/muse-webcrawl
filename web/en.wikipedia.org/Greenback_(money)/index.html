Greenbacks are United States paper currency issued by the federal government during and after the American Civil War — first the Demand Notes of 1861 and then the United States Notes authorised by the Legal Tender Acts of 1862 and 1863. The name comes from the distinctive green ink printed on the back of the notes, a feature later imitated by all US currency; "greenback" remains a general slang term for the US dollar.

Origins

Faced with the immense cost of the Civil War and dwindling gold reserves, and after banks suspended specie payments in December 1861, Secretary of the Treasury Salmon P. Chase proposed government paper money. The Legal Tender Act of 25 February 1862 authorised $150 million in United States Notes, declared legal tender for most debts; two further acts raised the total authorised to $450 million.

Value and controversy

Because they were not redeemable in gold on demand, greenbacks traded at a discount against gold that fluctuated with the Union's military fortunes, sinking lowest in mid-1864. Opponents denounced them as unconstitutional fiat money; the Supreme Court first struck down the legal tender provision as applied to earlier contracts in Hepburn v. Griswold (1870) and then upheld it in the Legal Tender Cases of 1871.

Later history

Debate over contracting or expanding the greenback supply dominated post-war monetary politics. The Greenback Party of the 1870s and 1880s campaigned for an expanded paper currency on behalf of farmers and labourers. The Resumption Act of 1875 led to the resumption of gold redemption on 1 January 1879, but Congress froze the outstanding amount at $346,681,016, and United States Notes in that fixed quantity continued to circulate alongside Federal Reserve Notes well into the twentieth century.
