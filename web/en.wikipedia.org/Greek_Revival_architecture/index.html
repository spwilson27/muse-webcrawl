Greek Revival architecture

Greek Revival is an architectural style that began in the middle of the 18th century but which particularly flourished in the late 18th and early 19th centuries, predominately in northern Europe, the United States, and Canada, as well as in Greece itself following its independence. It revived many aspects of the forms and styles of ancient Greek architecture, in particular the Greek temple with its orders, porticoes and pediments, by contrast with earlier revivals of Roman classical architecture.

Development

Renewed access to Greece under Ottoman rule, and publications such as James Stuart and Nicholas Revett's The Antiquities of Athens (1762), gave architects first-hand measurements of Greek buildings for the first time. Early examples include garden temples in England and the work of "Athenian" Stuart. In Germany, the Brandenburg Gate in Berlin (1788–91) and the Glyptothek in Munich exemplified the style's monumental use; Karl Friedrich Schinkel's Altes Museum in Berlin is a celebrated example.

In the United States

In the young United States, Greek Revival became the dominant national style from roughly 1825 to 1860. Identification with ancient Greek democracy — reinforced by sympathy for the Greek War of Independence against the Ottomans — made it the natural idiom for courthouses, capitols, banks, churches and houses. Benjamin Henry Latrobe introduced Greek forms in the Bank of Pennsylvania (1799), and William Strickland's Second Bank of the United States in Philadelphia (1818–24) modelled itself on the Parthenon. Pattern books by Asher Benjamin and Minard Lafever spread the style to carpenters across the country, producing the classic white-columned American house with a front-facing gable.

Characteristics

Typical features include a temple-front portico with Doric, Ionic or Corinthian columns; a low-pitched pedimented gable; heavy cornices; horizontal transoms; and plain, often white-painted, surfaces that evoke marble. Buildings are usually symmetrical, with six-over-six double-hung windows and a wide front door often flanked by sidelights.
