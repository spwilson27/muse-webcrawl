Bimetallism

Bimetallism is a monetary standard in which the value of the monetary unit is defined as equivalent to a certain quantity of two metals, typically gold and silver, with a fixed exchange ratio between them established by law. Under a bimetallic standard, both metals are used as legal tender and can be minted into coin at the fixed ratio.

History

Bimetallic systems were used in Europe and the United States during the 19th century. The United States adopted a bimetallic standard in 1792 with a silver-to-gold ratio of 15:1. France's 1803 standard, at a ratio of 15.5:1, helped stabilise the international system for decades.

Problems and decline

When the market ratio between the metals diverged from the legal ratio, Gresham's law operated: the undervalued metal disappeared from circulation. Large silver discoveries and Germany's adoption of the gold standard in the 1870s undermined bimetallism worldwide.

The free silver movement

In the United States, bimetallism became a major political issue in the late 19th century. Farmers and debtors supported the "free silver" movement, and William Jennings Bryan championed it in his famous "Cross of Gold" speech of 1896, but the gold standard ultimately prevailed.
