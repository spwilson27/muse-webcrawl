Intravenous therapy

Intravenous therapy (abbreviated IV therapy) is the administration of fluids, medications, blood products, or nutrients directly into a vein. Because it bypasses the digestive system, it acts rapidly and is used to correct fluid and electrolyte imbalances, deliver drugs with poor oral absorption, transfuse blood, and provide total parenteral nutrition.

Access

Peripheral intravenous catheters, placed in veins of the hand or arm, are the most common access device in medicine. For long-term or irritating infusions, central venous catheters are threaded into large central veins, and implantable ports may be placed under the skin. Intraosseous access into bone marrow is an emergency alternative.

Solutions and uses

Common crystalloid solutions include normal saline and lactated Ringer's solution; dextrose solutions provide free water and some energy. IV therapy also delivers antibiotics, chemotherapy, analgesia, and electrolytes, and supports patients who cannot eat — parenteral nutrition mixtures supply amino acids, glucose, lipids, vitamins, and minerals.

Complications

Risks include infection at the insertion site, phlebitis, infiltration of fluid into surrounding tissue, air embolism, and fluid overload; aseptic technique and monitoring reduce these risks.
