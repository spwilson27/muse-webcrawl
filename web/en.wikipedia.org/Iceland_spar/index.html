Iceland spar

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Iceland spar, formerly called Iceland crystal, is a transparent variety of calcite (calcium carbonate, CaCO3) notable for its pronounced birefringence: objects viewed through the crystal appear doubled. The classic source is the Helgustaðir quarry near Eskifjörður in eastern Iceland, from which large, clear rhombohedra were exported from the 17th century onward.

Optical properties and science

In 1669 the Danish scientist Rasmus Bartholin published the first account of double refraction in Iceland spar, and in 1690 Christiaan Huygens explained the phenomenon with his wave theory of light, correctly inferring that light travels more slowly in the crystal along one axis than another. Iceland spar polarisers became standard laboratory instruments in the 19th century, and large optical-grade crystals were of strategic importance for polarising microscopes and rangefinders.

Sunstone hypothesis

It has been proposed that the "sunstone" (sólarsteinn) mentioned in Icelandic sagas was a piece of Iceland spar used by Norse navigators to locate the sun in overcast skies by polarisation of skylight; experiments with calcite crystals have shown the method to be workable.

Mining

The Helgustaðir deposit, worked intermittently for about 300 years, is now a protected natural monument. Optical calcite was also mined in Mexico, the United States, and Russia.
