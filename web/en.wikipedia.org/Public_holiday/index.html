Public holiday
A national day celebration, typically a public holiday.
Also calledBank holiday (UK/Ireland), federal holiday (US), statutory holiday
Established byLegislation or proclamation

A public holiday is a holiday generally established by law and is usually a non-working day during the year. Sovereign nations and territories observe public holidays based on events of historical, religious or cultural significance — such as national days, saints' days, New Year, Christmas, Eid, Diwali or commemorations of independence and of the war dead. On a public holiday, government offices, schools and most businesses close, and employees are commonly entitled to a paid day off or to premium pay for working.

Types

Public holidays may be national (applying country-wide), regional (applying in states, provinces or municipalities) or sectoral (for example, days on which banks or public services close). Some holidays fall on fixed dates; others are defined relative to a weekday (e.g. "the first Monday in May") or by lunisolar calendars, so their Gregorian dates move each year. When a fixed-date holiday falls on a weekend, many countries designate a substitute weekday.

Variation between countries

The number of public holidays varies widely: Nepal and India are often cited among countries with the most, with around 20–30 days when national and regional observances are combined, while countries such as Mexico and the United Kingdom have comparatively few (England and Wales have eight bank holidays). The United States recognises eleven federal holidays, though private employers are not generally required to observe them.

Effects

Public holidays affect commerce, transport and public services: retail and tourism often peak, while financial markets close. Economists debate their net economic effect, weighing lost output against increased leisure spending. Additional one-off holidays are sometimes proclaimed for royal weddings, jubilees or major events.
