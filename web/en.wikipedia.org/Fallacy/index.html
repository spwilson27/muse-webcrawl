Fallacy

A fallacy is the use of invalid or otherwise faulty reasoning in the construction of an argument, which may appear stronger than it really is. A fallacious argument may be deceptive by appearing better than it is; some fallacies are committed intentionally to manipulate or persuade by deception, while others are committed unintentionally through carelessness or ignorance.

Classification

Fallacies are broadly divided into formal fallacies — errors in the logical structure of a deductive argument, such as affirming the consequent or denying the antecedent — and informal fallacies, which arise from errors in content, context or inductive reasoning. Famous informal fallacies include the straw man, ad hominem, appeal to authority, false dilemma, slippery slope, begging the question, and the naturalistic fallacy. Aristotle's Sophistical Refutations gave the first systematic catalogue, and the study of fallacies has been a staple of rhetoric and informal logic ever since.

Use

Since the mid-20th century, theorists such as C. L. Hamblin and the pragma-dialectical school have treated fallacies less as textbook logical errors and more as unreasonable moves in dialogue and argumentation. Identifying fallacies is central to critical thinking education, although charges of fallaciousness can themselves be misused to dismiss arguments.
