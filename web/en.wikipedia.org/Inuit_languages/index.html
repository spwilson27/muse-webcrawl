The Inuit languages are a closely related group of indigenous American languages traditionally spoken across the North American Arctic and parts of sub-Arctic Labrador, as well as in Greenland and a small area of the Russian Far East. They form the Inuit branch of the Eskimo–Aleut language family and are closely related to the Yupik languages and more distantly to Aleut (Unangam Tunuu).

Classification and distribution

The Inuit languages form a dialect continuum stretching from northwestern Alaska to Greenland. Western varieties include Iñupiaq in Alaska and Inuvialuktun in Canada's western Arctic. In the eastern Canadian Arctic and Quebec the language is generally called Inuktitut. In Greenland, Greenlandic (Kalaallisut) is the official language, alongside the Tunumiisut and Inuktun varieties.

Grammar

Inuit languages are polysynthetic and agglutinative: long words are built by stringing together many suffixes onto a root, so that a single word can express what would require a whole sentence in English. They are ergative–absolutive in alignment and typically distinguish three numbers — singular, dual and plural — in nouns and verbs.

Writing systems

In Canada, Inuktitut is written both in a syllabic script derived from Canadian Aboriginal syllabics, developed in the 19th century, and in Latin-based orthographies. Greenlandic and the Alaskan languages use Latin alphabets; Greenlandic underwent a spelling reform in 1973.

Status and revitalization

Greenlandic is vigorous and official in Greenland. In Canada, Inuktitut is an official language of Nunavut and has tens of thousands of speakers, though several western varieties are endangered. Language nest preschools, immersion schooling and media in Inuit languages support revitalization efforts across the Arctic.
