Grimm's law

Grimm's law, also known as the First Germanic Sound Shift, is a set of sound laws describing the consonant changes that took place in Proto-Germanic as it diverged from Proto-Indo-European (PIE), probably during the first millennium BCE. It was formulated in detail by Jacob Grimm in the second edition of his Deutsche Grammatik (1822), building on observations made by Rasmus Rask, and it is considered a founding result of historical linguistics because it showed that sound change could be regular and exceptionless.

The correspondences

The law describes a chain shift of the PIE stop consonants:

  • Voiceless stops (*p, *t, *k, *kʷ) became fricatives (f, þ (th), h/x, hw): compare Latin piscis with English fish, Latin trēs with English three, Latin canis with English hound.
  • Voiced stops (*b, *d, *g, *gʷ) became voiceless stops (p, t, k, kw): compare Latin decem with English ten.
  • Voiced aspirated stops (*bʰ, *dʰ, *gʰ, *gʷʰ) became plain voiced stops or fricatives (b, d, g, w): compare Sanskrit bhrā́tā with English brother.

Exceptions and refinements

Apparent exceptions were later explained by further laws. Verner's law (1875) showed that the fricatives produced by Grimm's law were voiced when the preceding PIE syllable had not carried the accent, explaining pairs such as English father versus brother. Other exceptions involve stops remaining unchanged after fricatives (e.g. *sp, *st, *sk) and the behaviour of consonant clusters. Grimm's law must also be distinguished from the later High German consonant shift, which affected only the High German dialects.
