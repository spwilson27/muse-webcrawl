Ocean current

Image placeholder: Map of major ocean surface currents
Major ocean currents of the world

An ocean current is a continuous, directed movement of ocean water generated by forces acting upon the water, including wind, the Coriolis effect, breaking waves, temperature and salinity differences, and tides. Depth contours, shoreline configurations, and interactions with other currents influence a current's direction and strength.

Types

Surface currents, driven mainly by wind stress and organized by the planetary wind patterns, occupy the upper ~400 metres and make up about 10% of the ocean's water. They form large rotating systems called gyres in each ocean basin, with intense western boundary currents such as the Gulf Stream and the Kuroshio.

Deep-water currents are driven by density differences arising from temperature and salinity — the thermohaline circulation — which slowly moves the remaining ~90% of ocean water around the globe, linking the ocean basins in a "global conveyor belt."

Major currents

  • Atlantic: Gulf Stream, North Atlantic Current, Canary Current, Brazil Current, Antarctic Circumpolar Current (shared).
  • Pacific: Kuroshio, California Current, Humboldt (Peru) Current, East Australian Current, North and South Equatorial Currents.
  • Indian: Agulhas Current, West Australian Current, monsoon-driven Somali Current.

Effects

Ocean currents redistribute heat from the tropics to higher latitudes, moderating climates — for example, the North Atlantic Current gives northwestern Europe a milder climate than other regions at similar latitudes. Currents also transport nutrients, drive upwelling zones that support major fisheries, influence weather and storm tracks, disperse larvae and pollutants, and are harnessed in measurement and forecasting for shipping and climate science.
