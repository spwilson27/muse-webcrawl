Gabriele Falloppio

Gabriele Falloppio (1523–1562), Italian anatomist

Gabriele Falloppio (1523 – 9 October 1562), often known by his Latin name Fallopius, was an Italian Catholic priest and anatomist often known as an early anatomist — one of the most important physicians of the sixteenth century, part of the generation of Vesalius that transformed anatomy through systematic human dissection.

Life

Falloppio was born in Modena and studied medicine at the University of Ferrara, where he later taught. In 1548 he became professor of anatomy at Pisa, and in 1551 he was invited to the prestigious chair of anatomy and surgery at the University of Padua, where he also supervised the botanical garden and lectured on medicinal plants. His students included the German botanist-physician Joachim Camerarius and the anatomist Volcher Coiter. He died in Padua in 1562, aged only 39.

Contributions

Falloppio's only work published in his lifetime was the Observationes anatomicae (1561), a series of corrections and additions to Vesalius's De humani corporis fabrica, offered in a respectful spirit. He described the structure of the inner ear, including the tympanic cavity and the semicircular canals, the chorda tympani nerve, and the aqueduct of the vestibule. He is best remembered for his description of the tubes connecting the ovaries to the uterus, which still bear his name as the Fallopian tubes. Several other structures — the Fallopian canal of the facial nerve and the Fallopian ligament (the inguinal ligament) — also commemorate him.

In writings published after his death, Falloppio also described and advocated the use of a linen sheath as protection against syphilis, an early account of a condom-like device, and he wrote on mineral waters, fossils, and the composition of medicines.
