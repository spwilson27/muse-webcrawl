Abolitionism in the United States

Abolitionism in the United States was the movement to end slavery in the United States. It grew from early antislavery sentiment in the colonial and revolutionary eras into a radical, organised campaign in the 1830s demanding the immediate, uncompensated emancipation of enslaved people. The movement finally achieved its goal with the ratification of the Thirteenth Amendment in 1865, at the close of the American Civil War.

Early antislavery

Opposition to slavery appeared early: Quakers such as John Woolman and Anthony Benezet denounced it in the 18th century, and the American Revolution's ideals of liberty prompted northern states to adopt gradual emancipation laws between 1777 and 1804. The American Colonization Society (founded 1816) promoted emigration of free Black people to Liberia, a programme many Black Americans and later abolitionists rejected as racist and impractical.

Immediatism

From about 1830 abolitionists such as William Lloyd Garrison, whose newspaper The Liberator began publication in 1831, demanded immediate emancipation and attacked the Constitution as a pro-slavery compact. The American Anti-Slavery Society (1833) organised petition campaigns, lectures, and publications. Black abolitionists — including Frederick Douglass, Sojourner Truth, and David Walker, whose Appeal appeared in 1829 — were central to the movement, and the Underground Railroad helped enslaved people escape to freedom. Abolitionism intersected with women's rights; the exclusion of women delegates in 1840 helped prompt the Seneca Falls Convention of 1848.

Politics and war

Political abolitionism produced the Liberty Party, the Free Soil Party, and ultimately the Republican Party. Southern resistance, the Fugitive Slave Act of 1850, Uncle Tom's Cabin (1852), and John Brown's raid on Harpers Ferry (1859) deepened sectional crisis. During the Civil War, abolitionists pressed Abraham Lincoln to make emancipation a war aim, achieved by the Emancipation Proclamation (1863) and completed by the Thirteenth Amendment.
