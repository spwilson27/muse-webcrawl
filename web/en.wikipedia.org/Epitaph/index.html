Epitaph

Etymology
Greek epitaphios, "over a tomb"
Forms
Inscription, short poem, prose tribute

An epitaph (from Ancient Greek ἐπιτάφιος, "over a tomb") is a short text honouring a deceased person, strictly an inscription on a tombstone or grave marker, though the term is also used for brief literary compositions in a dead person's honour, often in verse.

History

Epitaphs were common in ancient Egypt and Greece; the epigrams of the Greek Anthology include many, some attributed to Simonides, whose lines for the Spartans who fell at Thermopylae — "Go tell the Spartans, passerby, that here, obedient to their laws, we lie" — are among the most famous. Roman epitaphs tended to record the deceased's career and family in standardised form.

Famous epitaphs

Well-known examples include W. B. Yeats's self-composed "Cast a cold eye / On life, on death. / Horseman, pass by!", the playful "I told you I was ill" on Spike Milligan's headstone (inscribed in Irish), and the epitaph of Martin Luther King Jr., quoting the spiritual "Free at last". Writers from Shakespeare to Dorothy Parker composed their own epitaphs, and some — like Shakespeare's curse against moving his bones — were intended practically.
