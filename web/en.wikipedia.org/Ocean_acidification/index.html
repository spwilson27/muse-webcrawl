Ocean acidification

Ocean acidification is the ongoing decrease in the pH of the Earth's oceans, caused primarily by the uptake of carbon dioxide (CO₂) from the atmosphere. Between 1950 and 2020 the average surface ocean pH fell from about 8.15 to 8.06—a roughly 30% increase in hydrogen-ion concentration (acidity) since the Industrial Revolution. The oceans absorb about a quarter to a third of human-generated CO₂ emissions, slowing atmospheric warming but altering seawater chemistry.

Chemistry

Dissolved CO₂ reacts with water to form carbonic acid, which releases hydrogen ions and converts carbonate ions into bicarbonate. The falling carbonate concentration lowers the saturation state of calcium carbonate minerals (aragonite and calcite), making it harder—and energetically costlier—for calcifying organisms to build shells and skeletons, and in undersaturated water existing shells can begin to dissolve.

Impacts

Affected organisms include reef-building corals, oysters and other shellfish (larval oyster failures in Pacific Northwest hatcheries have been linked to acidified upwelled water), pteropods (a key food-web species in polar and subpolar seas), coccolithophores, and some fish, in which acidification can impair olfaction and behaviour. Impacts are compounded by ocean warming and deoxygenation—the "deadly trio" of climate stressors for marine life.

History and monitoring

The phenomenon was brought to wide scientific attention in the early 2000s, notably by Ken Caldeira and Michael Wickett (2003) and a 2005 Royal Society report. Long time series at Station ALOHA (Hawaii) and Bermuda document the pH decline tracking atmospheric CO₂. In Earth history, rapid acidification accompanied the Paleocene–Eocene Thermal Maximum and mass-extinction events, though current rates are believed to be unusually fast.
