Crux ansata

Crux ansata is the Latin name ("cross with a handle") for the ancient Egyptian symbol more commonly known by its Egyptian name, the ankh. The sign is a cross surmounted by a loop, and in hieroglyphic writing it stood for the word for "life". Gods in Egyptian art are frequently shown holding the ankh to the nose or mouth of the king, bestowing the breath of life.

Use and significance

The ankh was among the most ubiquitous symbols of ancient Egypt, appearing in temple reliefs, tomb paintings, amulets and mirrors. Its precise origin is debated — it has been interpreted as a sandal strap, a knot, or a stylised representation of fertility — but its association with life, vitality and the afterlife is clear.

Later adoption

Early Christians in Egypt, the Copts, adopted the looped cross as a Christian symbol, where it became known as the crux ansata, merging the cross of Christ with the Egyptian sign of life. In modern times the ankh has been widely used in jewellery and popular culture as a symbol of life and African heritage.
