In logic, negation, also called the logical complement, is an operation that takes a proposition p to another proposition "not p", written ¬p. It reverses truth value: if p is true, ¬p is false, and if p is false, ¬p is true. Negation is one of the most fundamental operations in logic, mathematics, linguistics, and computing.

In classical logic

In classical propositional logic, negation is a truth-functional unary connective defined by the truth table: ¬T = F and ¬F = T. It obeys the law of double negation (¬¬p is equivalent to p) and features in De Morgan's laws, which relate negation to conjunction and disjunction: ¬(p ∧ q) is equivalent to ¬p ∨ ¬q, and ¬(p ∨ q) is equivalent to ¬p ∧ ¬q. Together with material implication, negation allows elegant formulations of proof by contradiction and of principles such as the law of excluded middle.

In non-classical logics

In intuitionistic logic, ¬p is defined as p → ⊥ ("p implies absurdity"); here double-negation elimination fails, so from ¬¬p one cannot in general infer p. Paraconsistent and relevance logics modify negation to tolerate contradictions without explosion. In fuzzy logic, negation is commonly a function such as 1 − x on truth values in the unit interval.

In language

In natural language, negation is expressed by words such as "not", "no", "never", and by negative affixes (un-, in-, non-). Many languages mark negation with a negative particle or verb form; some, like French (ne … pas), use two markers — a development described by Jespersen's cycle. Double negatives vary: in standard English two negatives typically cancel, while in many dialects and languages they reinforce (negative concord).

In computing and mathematics

The NOT operation in Boolean algebra inverts a bit and underlies digital logic gates. In arithmetic, "negation" also denotes the additive inverse of a number. In set theory, complementation is the analogous operation on sets.
