The Niger River is the principal river of West Africa, extending about 4,180 kilometres (2,600 mi). It is the third-longest river in Africa, exceeded only by the Nile and the Congo. Its drainage basin covers roughly 2.1 million square kilometres across nine countries. The river takes an unusual boomerang-shaped course: it rises in the Guinea Highlands of southeastern Guinea, close to the Atlantic coast, flows northeast through Mali into the Sahara fringe, then turns southeast through Niger and Nigeria before emptying into the Gulf of Guinea through the vast Niger Delta.

Geography and course

The upper Niger flows through Guinea and Mali, where it spreads into the Inner Niger Delta, a vast seasonally flooded plain around Mopti that supports fishing, grazing and rice cultivation. Below the delta the river passes Timbuktu and Gao, bends southward, and forms part of the borders of Niger with Benin. In Nigeria it is joined at Lokoja by its largest tributary, the Benue River, and then flows south to the delta, one of the world's largest, where it branches into distributaries such as the Nun and Forcados before reaching the Atlantic.

History

The Niger sustained great West African empires, including the Ghana, Mali and Songhai empires, whose trading cities of Djenné and Timbuktu grew rich on trans-Saharan commerce in gold and salt. The river's course long puzzled European geographers; the Scottish explorer Mungo Park reached it in 1796, and its lower course and outlet were established by the Lander brothers in 1830.

Economy and environment

The river is vital for irrigation, fishing, hydroelectric power (notably at the Kainji and Jebba dams in Nigeria) and transport. Its basin faces pressures from population growth, climate change, dam construction and, in the delta, decades of oil extraction and pollution.
