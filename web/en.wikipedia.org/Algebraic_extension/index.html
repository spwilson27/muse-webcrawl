Algebraic extension

In abstract algebra, a field extension L/K is called an algebraic extension if every element of L is algebraic over K — that is, every element of L is a root of some non-zero polynomial with coefficients in K. A field extension that is not algebraic is called transcendental.

Field extensions form a hierarchy; algebraic extensions are central to Galois theory.

Definitions

An element α of L is algebraic over K if it satisfies a polynomial equation with coefficients in K; the lowest-degree monic such polynomial is its minimal polynomial. An extension is finite — finite-dimensional as a vector space over K — if and only if it is algebraic and finitely generated; its dimension is called the degree of the extension.

Examples

The field of complex numbers is an algebraic extension of the real numbers of degree 2, generated by the imaginary unit i. The field of algebraic numbers — all complex numbers algebraic over the rationals — is an algebraic extension of ℚ of infinite degree. By contrast, ℝ over ℚ is transcendental, since numbers such as π are not roots of any rational polynomial.

Properties

Algebraic extensions are transitive: if M/L and L/K are algebraic, so is M/K. Every field has an algebraic closure, an algebraically closed algebraic extension, whose existence is proved using Zorn's lemma. Algebraic extensions are the setting of Galois theory, which classifies them through their automorphism groups.
