Pressure suit

Pressure suits protect aviators and astronauts where ambient pressure is too low to sustain life.

A pressure suit is a protective suit worn by high-altitude pilots, aircrew and astronauts in low-pressure environments that are too thin to sustain human life without assistance. Above the Armstrong limit (about 19 km), the ambient pressure is so low that water boils at body temperature, so an unprotected person would quickly lose consciousness and die; a pressure suit maintains a survivable pressure and breathing-oxygen supply around the body.

Types

Partial-pressure suits use inflatable capstans and tight-fitting bladders to squeeze the body mechanically while the wearer breathes pressurised oxygen through a mask; they serve as emergency backup in military aircraft such as the U-2. Full-pressure suits enclose the entire body in a pressurised envelope with a sealed helmet — the "get-me-down" suits worn by U-2 and SR-71 crews, the ACES suit worn by Space Shuttle crews, and the Sokol and Crew Dragon suits used for launch and re-entry. A space suit such as NASA's EMU is a specialised full-pressure suit with its own life-support backpack for working in vacuum outside a spacecraft.

History

Early designs date to the 1930s, including Wiley Post's rubberised suit of 1934–35, built with the B.F. Goodrich Company for his record high-altitude flights. Wartime and Cold War high-altitude reconnaissance drove development of modern suits, whose principles carry over directly to astronaut intravehicular suits today.
