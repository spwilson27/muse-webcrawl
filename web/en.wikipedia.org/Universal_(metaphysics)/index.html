Universal (metaphysics)

In metaphysics, a universal is what particular things have in common — namely characteristics or qualities. In other words, universals are repeatable entities that can be instantiated, or exemplified, by many particular things. For example, the type dog and the property redness are universals if every particular dog shares the nature of being a dog, and every red thing shares the quality of redness.

The problem of universals

The problem asks whether universals exist, and if so in what manner. Plato's Theory of Forms held that universals exist independently in an abstract realm, with particulars "participating" in them. Aristotle placed forms within things themselves. In medieval philosophy, Boethius framed the debate taken up by scholastics: realists such as Aquinas granted universals a mode of existence; conceptualists like Peter Abelard located them in the mind; and nominalists, most famously William of Ockham, denied them any existence beyond the names applied to similar particulars.

Modern debates

Contemporary metaphysicians distinguish properties, kinds, and relations as candidate universals, and contrast realism with trope theory, resemblance nominalism, and class nominalism. The debate matters for philosophy of science (whether laws of nature govern by universals) and semantics (what general terms refer to). Realism about universals is often associated with David Armstrong, while nominalism has been defended by philosophers favoring parsimony and Ockham's razor.
