The Delhi Sultanate was a late medieval empire primarily based in Delhi that ruled large parts of the Indian subcontinent for more than three centuries, from 1206 to 1526. It was established by Qutb ud-Din Aibak, a former slave commander of Muhammad of Ghor, and was ruled by five successive dynasties: the Mamluk (Slave), Khalji, Tughlaq, Sayyid and Lodi dynasties.

History

Under the Khalji sultan Alauddin Khalji (r. 1296–1316) the empire expanded deep into the Deccan and resisted Mongol invasions. Muhammad bin Tughluq (r. 1325–1351) ruled the sultanate at its greatest extent but his ambitious projects — including a temporary move of the capital to Daulatabad and a token currency — strained the state. After Timur's sack of Delhi in 1398 the sultanate shrank, and the Afghan Lodi dynasty ruled a reduced realm until Ibrahim Lodi was defeated by Babur at the First Battle of Panipat in 1526.

Administration and culture

The sultanate governed through a Persianate court culture, the iqta land-revenue system, and powerful cavalry armies. Its rule spread Islam in northern India, though most subjects remained Hindu. The period produced landmark Indo-Islamic architecture, including the Qutb Minar complex, and saw the growth of Urdu and of Sufi orders such as the Chishtiyya.

Economy

Alauddin Khalji's market controls and land reforms are noted by chroniclers, and the sultanate's coinage — the silver tanka and copper jital — underpinned a monetised economy connected to trade across Central Asia and the Indian Ocean.
