Gleichschaltung

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Gleichschaltung (German pronunciation: [ˈɡlaɪçʃaltʊŋ]), roughly "coordination" or "bringing into line", is the term for the process by which the Nazi Party, after Adolf Hitler became Chancellor in January 1933, established totalitarian control over all aspects of German society: the state, the economy, culture, education, the professions and even leisure organisations.

Measures

The process unfolded rapidly in 1933–34. The Reichstag Fire Decree (February 1933) suspended civil liberties, and the Enabling Act (March 1933) allowed the cabinet to legislate without the Reichstag. State governments were subordinated to Reich governors under the Gleichschaltung laws of March and April 1933. The Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service purged Jews and political opponents from public employment. Trade unions were dissolved on 2 May 1933 and replaced by the German Labour Front; all other political parties were banned or dissolved by July 1933, making Germany a one-party state.

Coordination extended beyond government: professional associations, sports clubs, youth groups, charities and cultural bodies were merged into Nazi umbrella organisations or brought under party leadership, in many cases through "self-coordination" (Selbstgleichschaltung) by institutions eager to accommodate the new regime. The judiciary, universities, churches and press were likewise pressured into conformity.

Completion

The process was effectively completed in August 1934, when, following the Night of the Long Knives and the death of President Paul von Hindenburg, Hitler merged the offices of Chancellor and President as Führer und Reichskanzler, and the armed forces swore a personal oath of loyalty to him.
