The German Shepherd, also known in Britain as the Alsatian, is a breed of working dog of medium to large size that originated in Germany. It was developed in the late nineteenth century by Captain Max von Stephanitz from traditional German herding dogs, standardised for intelligence, strength, trainability, and obedience. The breed's versatility has made it one of the most widely used working dogs in the world, serving in police, military, search-and-rescue, and assistance roles.

History

In 1899 Max von Stephanitz purchased a dog named Horand von Grafrath at a show and founded the Verein für Deutsche Schäferhunde (Society for German Shepherd Dogs), establishing the breed standard. German Shepherds served extensively in both World Wars as messenger, scout, and sentry dogs. After the First World War, the breed's name was changed to Alsatian in Britain owing to anti-German sentiment; the name German Shepherd was officially restored by the Kennel Club in 1977.

Characteristics

German Shepherds are well-muscled dogs with a domed forehead, long square-cut muzzle, erect ears, and a long neck and bushy tail. The most common colouring is black and tan, though sable, all-black, and other colours occur. They are known for their intelligence — popularly ranked among the most intelligent breeds — loyalty, confidence, and willingness to work. The breed requires regular exercise and mental stimulation.

Uses

Because of their keen sense of smell, strength, and trainability, German Shepherds are used as police and military working dogs, explosives and narcotics detection dogs, search-and-rescue dogs, and guide and assistance dogs for people with disabilities. They are also popular family companions and competitors in obedience and protection sports such as Schutzhund/IGP.

Health

The breed is prone to hip and elbow dysplasia, degenerative myelopathy, and bloat (gastric dilatation-volvulus). Responsible breeding with health screening is recommended.
