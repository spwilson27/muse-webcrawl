Notre-Dame de Paris (French for "Our Lady of Paris"), formally the Cathedral of Notre-Dame de Paris, is a medieval Catholic cathedral on the Île de la Cité in the 4th arrondissement of Paris, France. Dedicated to the Virgin Mary, it is widely considered one of the finest examples of French Gothic architecture and among the most famous church buildings in the world, renowned for its flying buttresses, rose windows, twin towers, and gargoyles.

History

Construction began in 1163 under Bishop Maurice de Sully and was largely complete by 1260, though modifications continued until 1345. The cathedral witnessed numerous defining moments of French history: the coronation of Napoleon Bonaparte in 1804, the beatification of Joan of Arc in 1909, and the requiem masses of French presidents. During the French Revolution it was desecrated, rededicated to the Cult of Reason, and many of its treasures were destroyed or plundered.

The publication of Victor Hugo's novel Notre-Dame de Paris (1831; The Hunchback of Notre-Dame) drew attention to the cathedral's dilapidated state and spurred a major restoration led by architect Eugène Viollet-le-Duc between 1844 and 1864, which added the iconic spire and many of the chimeras on the gallery.

Architecture

Notre-Dame was among the first buildings in the world to use the flying buttress, allowing its walls to rise higher and hold larger windows. The western facade features three portals richly carved with biblical scenes, a circular rose window about 9.6 metres in diameter, and two towers that were intended, but never built, to carry spires. Inside, the nave rises to a vaulted ceiling 32 metres high. The cathedral's three great rose windows are masterpieces of 13th-century stained glass, and its great organ, with some 8,000 pipes, is the largest in France. The treasury preserves relics including the Crown of Thorns, traditionally held to have been worn by Christ.

2019 fire and restoration

On 15 April 2019, during renovation work, a fire broke out in the roof space. The oak-and-lead spire collapsed through the vaulted ceiling, and most of the roof was destroyed, though the main structure, facade, towers, and most artworks and relics were saved. President Emmanuel Macron pledged to rebuild within five years; donations approaching €850 million were pledged worldwide. The restoration, employing traditional techniques and an identical oak frame, culminated in the cathedral's reopening on 7–8 December 2024.
