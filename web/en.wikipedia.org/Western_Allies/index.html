Western Allies

Forces of the Western Allies landing in Normandy on D-Day, 6 June 1944.

The Western Allies were the countries of the Allies of World War II based in Western Europe and North America — principally the United Kingdom (with the British Commonwealth and Empire), the United States, and France — as distinct from the Soviet Union, whose forces fought on the Eastern Front. The term is often used retrospectively to contrast the Western democracies with the Soviet bloc, a division that hardened into the Cold War almost as soon as the war in Europe ended.

Usage in World War II

During the war, Western Allied strategy was coordinated through the Combined Chiefs of Staff and conferences such as Casablanca (1943), Tehran (1943) and Yalta (1945). Land campaigns conducted predominantly by the Western Allies included the North African campaign (1940–43), the invasions of Sicily and Italy (1943), the Normandy landings and the liberation of France (1944), and the advance into western Germany (1945). The Western Allies met Soviet forces at the Elbe in April 1945, and Germany was divided into four occupation zones — American, British, French and Soviet.

Tensions over Poland, the future of Germany and the composition of post-war governments appeared at the Yalta and Potsdam conferences and shaped the term's later meaning: "Western Allies" came to denote the three Western occupying powers in Germany and Berlin, whose sectors eventually formed West Berlin, in contrast to the Soviet sector (East Berlin).

Other uses

The phrase is also used for the Western members of the First World War Allies — chiefly France, Britain and (from 1917) the United States — as distinct from Russia. More loosely, it may refer to the Western Bloc during the Cold War and to the NATO alliance.
