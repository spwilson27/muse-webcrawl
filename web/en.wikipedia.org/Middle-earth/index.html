Middle-earth

Middle-earth is the fictional setting of much of the English writer J. R. R. Tolkien's fantasy writing. The term is taken from the Old English middangeard, the realm of Men in Norse mythology and Germanic folklore. Tolkien's works set there include The Hobbit (1937), The Lord of the Rings (1954–55), and the posthumously published The Silmarillion (1977).

Middle-earth is the central continent of the world of Arda, inhabited by Elves, Men, Dwarves, Hobbits, Orcs, Ents, and other peoples. Its history is divided into Ages: the First Age saw the wars against Morgoth in Beleriand, the Second Age the forging of the Rings of Power and the fall of Númenor, and the Third Age the War of the Ring against Sauron, narrated in The Lord of the Rings. Tolkien conceived the world in detail, with invented languages (notably the Elvish tongues Quenya and Sindarin), genealogies, calendars, and maps.

Influence

Middle-earth became the archetypal fantasy world, profoundly influencing modern fantasy literature, role-playing games, and film, including Peter Jackson's The Lord of the Rings and The Hobbit trilogies, filmed in New Zealand. The word "Middle-earth" derives from Old English translations of the Greek oikoumene, the inhabited world.
