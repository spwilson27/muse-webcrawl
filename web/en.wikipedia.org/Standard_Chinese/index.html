Standard Chinese

Native speakers: approx. 1 billion (incl. L2)
Based on: Beijing dialect of Mandarin
Official in: China, Taiwan, Singapore
Writing: Chinese characters; pinyin romanisation

Standard Chinese (simplified Chinese: 现代标准汉语; traditional Chinese: 現代標準漢語; pinyin: Xiàndài biāozhǔn hànyǔ; lit. 'modern standard Han language') is a modern standard form of Mandarin Chinese that was first codified during the republican era of the early 20th century.

It is designated as the official language of mainland China (where it is called Pǔtōnghuà, 普通话, 'common speech'), of Taiwan (Guóyǔ, 國語, 'national language'), and as one of the four official languages of Singapore (Huáyǔ, 华语). It is based on the Beijing dialect of Mandarin, with vocabulary drawn from Mandarin varieties generally and grammar based on modern vernacular written Chinese.

Phonology

Standard Chinese has four main tones plus a neutral tone, applied to a syllable inventory of about 400 base syllables (roughly 1,300 when tones are counted). The syllable structure allows no consonant clusters, and most syllables end in a vowel or a nasal consonant. The romanisation system Hanyu Pinyin is the standard means of transcribing the sounds.

History

Efforts to define a national standard began in the last years of the Qing dynasty and continued under the Republic of China, culminating in conferences on pronunciation unification in 1913 and definitions published in the 1920s and 1930s. The People's Republic of China promulgated the modern definition of Putonghua in 1955–56 and promoted it through education and media.
