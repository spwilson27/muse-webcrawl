Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome

Wernicke–Korsakoff syndrome (WKS) is a neurological disorder caused by deficiency of thiamine (vitamin B1). It combines Wernicke encephalopathy, an acute condition marked by confusion, abnormal eye movements (ophthalmoplegia or nystagmus), and unsteady gait (ataxia), with Korsakoff syndrome, a chronic amnesia in which patients cannot form new memories and often confabulate to fill gaps.

Pathophysiology

Thiamine is essential for carbohydrate metabolism in the brain. Deficiency damages periventricular structures including the mammillary bodies, dorsomedial thalamus, and periaqueductal grey matter. WKS is most often associated with heavy alcohol use, which impairs intake, absorption, and storage of thiamine, but it also occurs with malnutrition, prolonged vomiting, dialysis, and after bariatric surgery.

Diagnosis and treatment

The classic triad is present in only a minority of cases, so WKS is frequently underdiagnosed. Because untreated Wernicke encephalopathy can progress to irreversible Korsakoff amnesia or death, suspected cases are treated immediately with high-dose intravenous thiamine before glucose administration. Early treatment can reverse the acute symptoms; the memory disorder of Korsakoff syndrome improves fully in only a minority of patients.

History

Carl Wernicke described the acute syndrome in 1881, and Sergei Korsakoff described the associated amnesic psychosis in the 1880s; their connection through thiamine deficiency was established in the 20th century.
