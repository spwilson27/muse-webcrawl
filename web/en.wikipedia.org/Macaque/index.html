Macaque

The macaques constitute the genus Macaca of gregarious Old World monkeys of the subfamily Cercopithecinae. The roughly two dozen species of macaques inhabit ranges across Asia, North Africa, and (in the case of the Barbary macaque) Gibraltar in Europe; an introduced population also exists in Florida. Aside from humans, macaques are the most widespread primate genus.

Characteristics

Macaques are robust, medium-sized monkeys with cheek pouches for storing food, powerful limbs, and strongly social behaviour. They live in troops with matrilineal hierarchies ranging from a handful to hundreds of individuals, and are omnivorous, eating fruit, seeds, leaves, roots, insects, and small animals. Well-known species include the rhesus macaque (M. mulatta), the crab-eating or long-tailed macaque (M. fascicularis), the Japanese macaque (M. fuscata) famous for bathing in hot springs, the pig-tailed and lion-tailed macaques, and the stump-tailed macaque.

Relation to humans

Macaques have been extensively used in medical and scientific research — the Rh blood group system is named after the rhesus macaque — and they hold significance in the religions and folklore of Asia, where some populations are protected at temple sites. Conversely, crop-raiding brings them into conflict with farmers, and several species are threatened by habitat loss and the wildlife trade; the lion-tailed macaque of the Western Ghats is endangered.
