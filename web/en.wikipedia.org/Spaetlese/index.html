Spaetlese

A bottle of German Spaetlese Riesling

Spaetlese (German: Spätlese, literally "late harvest") is a German wine term for a late-harvest wine and the second level of the Prädikatswein classification in Germany and Austria. Grapes for Spaetlese wines are picked at least seven days after the normal harvest begins and have attained a specified minimum must weight, so they are riper and produce wines with more body and flavour than Kabinett wines.

Characteristics

Spaetlese wines are most commonly made from Riesling, though other varieties such as Müller-Thurgau, Silvaner and Pinot Gris are also used. They may be fermented dry (trocken) or made with noticeable residual sweetness; the riper fruit gives intense aromas of apple, peach and citrus balanced by high acidity. In Austria, Spaetlese is likewise a Prädikat level requiring a higher minimum must weight than in Germany.

History

The term is associated with a famous story from 1775, when a courier bringing permission for the harvest at Schloss Johannisberg in the Rheingau was delayed, and the grapes were picked late — by then affected by noble rot — yet produced an unexpectedly excellent wine. The "Spätlesereiter" (late-harvest courier) is commemorated by a statue at the estate. The category was formalised in the German wine law of 1971 within the Prädikat system, ranking above Kabinett and below Auslese.
