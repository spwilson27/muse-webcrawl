Hausdorff paradox

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Hausdorff paradox is a result in mathematics, named after Felix Hausdorff, which shows that a sphere can be partitioned into subsets with a counter-intuitive property: there is no assignment of "surface area" (a finitely additive, rotation-invariant measure) to all subsets of the sphere's surface. It was published by Hausdorff in 1914 and in his book Grundzüge der Mengenlehre.

Statement

Precisely, the paradox states that there exists a countable subset D of the sphere S² such that the remainder S² ∖ D can be split into three disjoint sets A, B, and C satisfying A = B = C = B ∪ C under rotations — that is, A, B, and C are pairwise congruent, and each is also congruent to the union of the other two. This is possible because the rotation group of the sphere contains a free subgroup on two generators, and the construction relies on the axiom of choice to select representatives from orbits.

Consequences

Because a finitely additive measure invariant under rotations would have to assign A, B, and C equal values while also making one equal to the sum of the other two, no such measure can exist on all subsets of the sphere. The Hausdorff paradox was a key step toward the more famous Banach–Tarski paradox (1924), which extends the idea from the sphere's surface to the solid ball and to arbitrary bounded three-dimensional objects. Unlike Banach–Tarski, the Hausdorff result concerns the two-dimensional sphere's surface and yields "half" a sphere from a third of it in a rotational sense.
