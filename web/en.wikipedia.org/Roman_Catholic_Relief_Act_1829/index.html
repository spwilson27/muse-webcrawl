Roman Catholic Relief Act 1829

Citation10 Geo. 4 c. 7

The Roman Catholic Relief Act 1829, commonly called the Catholic Emancipation Act, was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that removed most of the remaining legal disabilities on Roman Catholics, above all by allowing them to sit in Parliament for the first time since the Reformation.

Background

Since the 17th century the Test and Corporation Acts and the penal laws had excluded Catholics from Parliament, the judiciary, high office and the universities. A series of Relief Acts (1778, 1782, 1791, 1793) had restored property rights and freedom of worship, but emancipation stalled on royal opposition. In Ireland, Daniel O'Connell's Catholic Association mobilised mass support for repeal of the remaining bars, funded by the "Catholic rent" of a penny a month.

Crisis of 1828

In June 1828 O'Connell himself stood for Parliament in the County Clare by-election and won — but as a Catholic could not take his seat. The prospect of either excluding him and provoking civil disorder in Ireland, or confronting the crisis, convinced the Prime Minister, the Duke of Wellington, and his Home Secretary Robert Peel — both long-time opponents of emancipation — that concession was unavoidable.

Provisions

The Act substituted a new oath of allegiance that Catholics could swear without renouncing their faith, opening Parliament, the bar, the armed forces (above the lowest ranks), corporations and most offices of state. Some restrictions survived: Catholics remained barred from the throne and from the offices of Lord Chancellor and Lord Lieutenant of Ireland, and the Act was paired with the Parliamentary Elections (Ireland) Act 1829, which raised the Irish county franchise from 40 shillings to £10, disfranchising the very freeholders who had elected O'Connell — cutting the Irish electorate from about 216,000 to 37,000.

Significance

Emancipation removed the largest single barrier to Catholic participation in British public life and demonstrated that an extra-parliamentary mass movement could force constitutional change; O'Connell's campaign became the model for subsequent movements, including his own campaign to repeal the Union.
