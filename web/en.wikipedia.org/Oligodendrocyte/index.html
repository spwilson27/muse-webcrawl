Oligodendrocyte

SystemCentral nervous system
TypeGlial cell (macroglia)
FunctionMyelination of CNS axons; metabolic support
Counterpart in PNSSchwann cell

Oligodendrocytes are a type of glial cell of the central nervous system (CNS). Their main function is to produce the myelin sheath that insulates axons in the brain and spinal cord, enabling fast saltatory conduction of action potentials. A single oligodendrocyte extends dozens of processes and can myelinate segments of many different axons — up to about 50 — unlike the Schwann cells of the peripheral nervous system, each of which wraps a single axon segment.

Development

Oligodendrocytes arise from oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs, also called NG2 glia), which migrate through the developing CNS and persist in the adult brain as a resident progenitor population. Differentiation involves transcription factors such as Olig1, Olig2 and Sox10, and myelin proteins including myelin basic protein (MBP), proteolipid protein (PLP) and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG).

Function beyond insulation

Besides speeding conduction, oligodendrocytes provide metabolic support to axons, shuttling lactate and other substrates through monocarboxylate transporters, and help regulate the ionic environment at the nodes of Ranvier. Loss of this support can cause axonal degeneration even where myelin remains partly intact.

Clinical significance

Damage to oligodendrocytes and myelin underlies demyelinating diseases such as multiple sclerosis, in which autoimmune attack destroys CNS myelin, and the leukodystrophies, inherited disorders of myelin formation. Tumours derived from these cells are called oligodendrogliomas. Remyelination by surviving OPCs occurs in the adult CNS but often fails in chronic disease, a major target of regenerative research.
