DefinitionLocally length-minimising (stationary) curve on a surface or manifold

In geometry, a geodesic (from Greek geodaisia, "division of Earth") is a curve representing in some sense the shortest path (arc) between two points in a surface, or more generally in a Riemannian manifold. The term also has meaning in any differentiable manifold with a connection. It is a generalization of the notion of a "straight line".

On a sphere, geodesics are great-circle arcs — the routes flown by long-haul aircraft.

Geodesics are of particular importance in general relativity: free-falling bodies and light rays follow geodesics of spacetime (timelike and null geodesics respectively), and the geodesic equation describes how objects move under gravity alone.

Properties

A geodesic is characterised by having zero geodesic curvature: it is "straight" relative to the surface itself. On a plane, geodesics are ordinary straight lines; on a sphere they are great circles; on a cylinder they are helices (including circles and lines as special cases). Between two nearby points there is generally a unique shortest geodesic, but globally geodesics need not be length-minimising beyond the first conjugate point — for example, the long way around a great circle is still a geodesic but not the shortest path.

Geodesic equation

In local coordinates on a Riemannian manifold with Christoffel symbols Γ, geodesics satisfy the geodesic equation, a system of second-order ordinary differential equations stating that the covariant derivative of the tangent vector along the curve vanishes. Geodesics locally minimise the energy functional, and stationary points of length satisfy the same equation.

Applications

Great-circle geodesics determine the shortest air and sea routes; geodesics on the reference ellipsoid underlie geodesy and map-making; and geodesic domes (popularised by Buckminster Fuller) borrow the name for structures subdividing a sphere with great-circle-like arcs. In computer graphics and robotics, geodesics on meshes are used for path planning and surface parameterisation.
