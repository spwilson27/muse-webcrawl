Personal information management

Files, email, notes, and tasks are all part of a personal information collection.

Personal information management (PIM) is the study and practice of the activities a person performs to acquire or create, store, organize, maintain, retrieve, and use information items such as documents (paper and digital), web pages, and email messages for everyday tasks. One ideal of PIM is that people should always have the right information in the right place, in the right form, and of sufficient completeness and quality to meet their current need.

Activities

Research in PIM, notably the framework developed by William Jones and colleagues, frames the field around a set of recurring activities:

  • Keeping — deciding what to save and in what form
  • Finding — retrieving previously encountered information, by navigation, search, or sorting
  • Organizing — arranging items into folders, tags, and other structures
  • Maintaining — updating, backing up, and pruning collections over time
  • Managing privacy, security, and flow — controlling who can access one's information
  • Measuring and evaluating — assessing whether the collection still serves one's goals
  • Making sense of and using information — the purpose that motivates the other activities

Challenges

Information is fragmented across devices, applications, and formats — a phenomenon termed information fragmentation — forcing people to juggle separate organizational schemes for files, email, photos, bookmarks, and tasks. Classic findings include the prevalence of "filing" versus "piling" strategies, the difficulty of refinding one's own items, and the tendency to keep information "just in case." Email overload and the accumulation of digital clutter are widely studied problems.

Tools

PIM tools include personal information managers (software combining email, calendars, contacts, and tasks, such as Microsoft Outlook), note-taking applications, reference managers, desktop search, and personal knowledge management systems. Recent research explores unified search, tagging as an alternative to folders, and automated organization assisted by machine learning.
