Proper time

In relativity, proper time (from Latin proprius, meaning own) along a timelike world line is defined as the time as measured by a clock following that world line. The proper time interval between two events on a world line is the change in the proper time, and it is independent of coordinates — all observers who calculate it from their own coordinate systems obtain the same value. It depends, however, on the path taken between the events.

Symbolτ (tau)
UnitSecond
Defined inSpecial and general relativity
Related conceptCoordinate time, time dilation

Definition

In special relativity, for an object moving at velocity v relative to an inertial frame, the proper time interval dτ relates to the coordinate time interval dt by dτ = dt·√(1 − v²/c²). Thus a moving clock ticks more slowly in the frame than stationary clocks — the phenomenon of time dilation. In general relativity, proper time is computed from the spacetime metric along the object's trajectory, and clocks deeper in a gravitational field tick more slowly.

History

The concept was first introduced by Hermann Minkowski in 1908 in his geometric formulation of special relativity, where he called it Eigenzeit ("own time"). It is a central quantity in the mathematics of four-dimensional spacetime, and the proper time along a world line is maximised by the path of a freely falling (inertial) observer.

Examples

The twin paradox is resolved using proper time: the travelling twin's world line accumulates less proper time than the stay-at-home twin's, so the traveller returns younger. Particle physicists routinely use proper time (or proper lifetime) to describe the decay of unstable particles moving at relativistic speeds, and the proper time of GPS satellites must be corrected for both special and general relativistic effects.
