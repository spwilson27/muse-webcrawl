Néel temperature

The Néel temperature or magnetic ordering temperature, TN, is the temperature above which an antiferromagnetic material becomes paramagnetic — that is, the thermal energy becomes large enough to destroy the microscopic magnetic ordering within the material. It is named for Louis Néel, who first identified this type of magnetic ordering and received the Nobel Prize in Physics in 1970 for related work.

Description

Below the Néel temperature, neighboring atomic magnetic moments align antiparallel to one another, giving zero net magnetization in the absence of an applied field. Above TN, thermal fluctuations randomize the moments and the material follows the Curie–Weiss law of a paramagnet. The transition is marked by a characteristic cusp in the magnetic susceptibility.

Examples

Some well-known Néel temperatures include chromium at about 311 K, manganese oxide (MnO) at 122 K, nickel oxide (NiO) at 515 K, and hematite (Fe2O3) at about 950 K. Measuring TN is a standard way of characterizing antiferromagnetic materials, including the parent compounds of high-temperature superconductors.
