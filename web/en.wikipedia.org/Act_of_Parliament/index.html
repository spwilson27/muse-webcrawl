Act of Parliament

An Act of Parliament is a law enacted as primary legislation by a national or sub-national parliament. In Commonwealth countries and others influenced by the Westminster system, Acts are the principal source of statute law. Before a proposal becomes an Act, it is a bill, which must pass through several stages in the legislature and receive assent from the head of state or their representative before being enacted.

United Kingdom

In the United Kingdom, an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom is primary legislation passed by the House of Commons and the House of Lords and given royal assent by the monarch. Public general Acts apply across the whole country or specified parts of it, while private Acts affect only particular individuals or organisations, and local Acts only specific localities. Acts are cited by their short title, regnal year or calendar year and chapter number (e.g. the "Human Rights Act 1998"). Under the doctrine of parliamentary sovereignty, Acts of Parliament are the highest form of law in the UK and cannot be struck down by the courts, though courts may issue declarations of incompatibility under the Human Rights Act 1998. The Parliament Acts 1911 and 1949 limit the ability of the House of Lords to delay most public bills.

Other jurisdictions

Parliaments in Australia, Canada, India, Ireland (where the legislature is the Oireachtas and its laws are called Acts of the Oireachtas), New Zealand, and many other states also enact Acts of Parliament. In some jurisdictions the term "Act of Congress" or "statute" is used instead. Acts may amend or repeal earlier legislation, delegate power to make secondary (delegated) legislation such as statutory instruments, and may come into force immediately, on a specified date, or when brought into effect by a commencement order.
