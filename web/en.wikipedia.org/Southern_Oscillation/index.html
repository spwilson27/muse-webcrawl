The Southern Oscillation is a recurring pattern of sea-level air pressure changes between the eastern and western tropical Pacific Ocean. It is the atmospheric component of the coupled ocean–atmosphere phenomenon known as the El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO), the most significant source of year-to-year climate variability on Earth.

Discovery

The oscillation was identified in the 1920s by Sir Gilbert Walker, Director General of Observatories in India, who was searching for a way to predict the Indian monsoon. Walker noticed that pressure changes in the Pacific and Indian Oceans were correlated with droughts and floods on several continents — statistical links contemporaries called "teleconnections". In the 1960s, Jacob Bjerknes connected Walker's oscillation to the oceanic El Niño warming off Peru, uniting them as a single coupled phenomenon driven by the Walker circulation.

The Southern Oscillation Index

The strength of the oscillation is quantified by the Southern Oscillation Index (SOI), calculated from the difference in standardised sea-level pressure between Tahiti in the central Pacific and Darwin in northern Australia. Sustained negative SOI values accompany El Niño events; sustained positive values accompany La Niña.

Mechanism

In the neutral state, high pressure sits over the eastern Pacific and low pressure over Indonesia, driving easterly trade winds and the rising branch of the Walker circulation over the warm western Pacific. During El Niño, the pressure gradient slackens, trades weaken, warm water spreads east, and the pattern of tropical rainfall shifts. During La Niña the gradient steepens and the normal pattern intensifies. These swings alter rainfall and temperature patterns as far away as East Africa, North America and Southeast Asia, affecting droughts, floods, fisheries and tropical cyclone activity.
