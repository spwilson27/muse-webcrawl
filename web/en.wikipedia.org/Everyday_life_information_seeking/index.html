Everyday life information seeking

Everyday life information seeking (ELIS) is a concept in library and information science referring to the acquisition of various informational elements — both factual and orienting — that individuals employ to orient themselves in daily life or to solve problems not directly connected with their occupational tasks. The term was coined by Finnish information scientist Reijo Savolainen, who introduced it in a 1995 article in Library & Information Science Research.

Concept

ELIS emerged as a complement to research on information seeking in task- and work-related contexts. While earlier research concentrated on how professionals, scientists, and students seek information for occupational tasks, ELIS focuses on the seeking of information for everyday purposes: consumption and health decisions, hobbies, household management, travel, and other facets of daily living.

Savolainen situated ELIS within a broader framework of "way of life" and "mastery of life". Way of life refers to the relatively stable order of things in a person's everyday existence — the habits and preferences structuring daily activities. Mastery of life denotes the general preparedness to keep this order coherent and meaningful, and information seeking is one of the tools used in that process.

Orientating and practical information

Savolainen distinguished two major types of everyday life information. Orientating information helps individuals monitor everyday events and plan daily activities; examples include news media consumption and weather forecasts. Practical information is needed to solve specific problems, such as finding a recipe, repairing an appliance, or choosing a product to buy.

Sources and habits

Studies of ELIS emphasize that people's choice of information sources in everyday life is strongly shaped by habit and by the perceived accessibility and cost of sources. Interpersonal sources — family, friends, colleagues — as well as mass media and, increasingly, the internet play central roles. Information seeking may be active (seeking) or passive (encountering information incidentally), and researchers have developed related concepts such as information grounds and small worlds to describe the social contexts of everyday information behaviour.
