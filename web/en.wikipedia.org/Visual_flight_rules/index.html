Visual flight rules (VFR) are a set of regulations under which a pilot operates an aircraft in weather conditions clear enough to see where the aircraft is going. Under VFR, pilots navigate and maintain separation from terrain, obstacles, and other aircraft primarily by looking out of the cockpit — the "see and avoid" principle — rather than relying on instruments or air traffic control separation, as under instrument flight rules (IFR).

Requirements

VFR flight is permitted only in visual meteorological conditions (VMC), defined by minimum flight visibility and distances from cloud that vary with airspace class and altitude. Typical requirements include visibility of 3–5 statute miles and specified horizontal and vertical clearance from clouds (under U.S. rules, e.g., 500 feet below, 1,000 feet above, and 2,000 feet horizontal in many airspace classes). Pilots must also meet aircraft equipment rules — more stringent for night VFR — and, in controlled airspace, obtain ATC clearances where required. Special VFR clearances may allow operations below standard VMC minima in controlled airspace near airports.

Variants and risks

VFR flight plans may be filed for search-and-rescue purposes but separation services are limited. A notorious hazard is VFR flight into instrument meteorological conditions ("VFR into IMC"), a leading cause of fatal general-aviation accidents, often involving spatial disorientation when visual references are lost.
