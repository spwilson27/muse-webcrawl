Double negative

Double negative
Field: Linguistics, grammar
Example: "I don't know nothing"
Related term: Negative concord

A double negative is a construction in which two forms of grammatical negation occur in a single sentence. Depending on the language or dialect, the two negatives may reinforce each other — a phenomenon linguists call negative concord, as in "I don't know nothing" in many varieties of English — or cancel each other out to yield a positive meaning, as in standard-logic readings of "I can't not go."

Across languages and dialects

Negative concord is the grammatical standard in many languages, including Spanish ("No sé nada"), Italian, and Russian, and in many English dialects such as African American Vernacular English and Cockney. Old and Middle English also used multiple negation for emphasis; Chaucer stacked as many as four negatives in one sentence. In standard modern English, prescriptive grammar treats stacked negatives as errors, a rule popularized by 18th-century grammarians who argued by analogy with logic that two negatives must make a positive.

In logic

In classical logic, double negation elimination holds: ¬¬p is equivalent to p. Intuitionistic logic does not accept this equivalence in general.
