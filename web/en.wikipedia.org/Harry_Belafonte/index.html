Harry Belafonte (born Harold George Bellanfanti Jr.; March 1, 1927 – April 25, 2023) was an American singer, actor, and civil rights activist who popularized calypso music with international audiences in the 1950s. His breakthrough album Calypso (1956) was the first million-selling LP by a single artist, featuring the hit "Banana Boat (Day-O)". Belafonte was one of the few performers to have won an Emmy, a Grammy, an Oscar (the Jean Hersholt Humanitarian Award), and a Tony Award (EGOT, non-competitive).

Early life

Belafonte was born in Harlem, New York City, to Jamaican-born parents of Afro-Jamaican and Afro-Scottish descent. He spent much of his childhood in Jamaica with his grandmother. After returning to New York and dropping out of high school, he served in the U.S. Navy during the Second World War, then worked as a janitor's assistant. A pair of tickets to the American Negro Theatre changed his life; he began taking acting classes at the Dramatic Workshop of the New School alongside classmates including Marlon Brando, Tony Curtis and Sidney Poitier, supporting himself by singing in nightclubs.

Music and film

Belafonte recorded folk songs and Caribbean music; his 1956 album Calypso topped the charts for 31 weeks and included "Jamaica Farewell" and "Banana Boat (Day-O)". He earned the nickname the "King of Calypso", though he resisted the label. As an actor he starred in films such as Carmen Jones (1954), Island in the Sun (1957), and Odds Against Tomorrow (1959), and became the first African American to produce in television and to win an Emmy Award (for his 1959 variety special Tonight with Belafonte). He later collaborated on the charity single "We Are the World" (1985), which he helped initiate to raise money for famine relief in Africa.

Activism

A close confidant of Martin Luther King Jr., Belafonte bankrolled civil rights campaigns, raised money for the Freedom Riders and voter registration drives, and helped organize the 1963 March on Washington. Despite being blacklisted during the McCarthy era, he remained a prominent voice on civil rights, later advocating against apartheid in South Africa and serving as a UNICEF Goodwill Ambassador from 1987. He was a vocal critic of U.S. foreign policy through the end of his life. Belafonte died of congestive heart failure at his home in Manhattan on April 25, 2023, at age 96.
