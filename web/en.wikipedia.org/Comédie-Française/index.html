The Comédie-Française (French: [kɔmedi fʁɑ̃sɛːz]), officially the Théâtre-Français, is one of the few state theatres in France and the oldest still-active theatre company in the world. Founded in 1680 by decree of Louis XIV, it merged the troupe of the Hôtel Guénégaud with that of the Hôtel de Bourgogne.

History

The company was formed seven years after the death of Molière, whose troupe formed its core; it is therefore fondly called La Maison de Molière ("The House of Molière"). It received a royal monopoly on spoken drama in Paris. During the French Revolution the company split: the actor François-Joseph Talma led a faction to the Théâtre de la République, while the remaining actors were imprisoned in 1793 after a controversial production. Napoleon reorganised the company by decree in 1812 (the Décret de Moscou), setting statutes that in part still govern it.

Organisation

The Comédie-Française is administered by an administrateur général appointed by the state. Its actors are organised in a hierarchy: pensionnaires (salaried actors) may after a year become sociétaires (shareholder-members) who share in profits and elect a management committee; retired sociétaires with twenty years of service may be named sociétaires honoraires.

Venues and repertoire

Its principal home is the Salle Richelieu at the Palais-Royal in Paris, with the Théâtre du Vieux-Colombier and the Studio-Théâtre as additional stages. The repertoire centres on the French classical canon — Molière, Racine, Corneille, Marivaux, Musset — alongside modern and foreign works.
