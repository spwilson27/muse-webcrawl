Phosphor

Phosphor powders glowing under ultraviolet illumination.

A phosphor is a substance that exhibits luminescence: it absorbs energy — from electrons, ultraviolet light, or X-rays — and re-emits it as visible light. The name derives from phosphorus, the "light-bearer," though most phosphors contain no elemental phosphorus; typical hosts are sulfides, aluminates, silicates, and rare-earth-doped compounds.

How it works

Excitation lifts electrons in activator ions (such as Eu2+, Mn2+, or Ce3+) to higher energy states. Their return releases photons whose color depends on the activator and host lattice. Short-lived emission is fluorescence; materials with metastable trap states glow long after excitation ends — phosphorescence, as in glow-in-the-dark strontium aluminate.

Applications

Phosphors coat the inside of fluorescent lamps, converting mercury's ultraviolet output to white light. Cathode-ray tubes used red, green, and blue phosphors (notably europium-doped yttrium compounds) for colour television. Modern white LEDs combine a blue gallium-nitride diode with a yellow cerium-doped YAG phosphor. Other uses include X-ray intensifying screens, scintillation detectors, luminous paints, and security printing.
