Shang Yang

Shang Yang (c. 390–338 BC), Legalist reformer of Qin

Shang Yang (Chinese: 商鞅; c. 390 – 338 BC), also known as Wei Yang (衞鞅) and later by the honorary name Lord Shang (商君), was a Chinese jurist, philosopher, politician, and writer from the State of Wei. Born in the state of Wey (衛), he rose to prominence as the chancellor of the State of Qin during the Warring States period, under Duke Xiao of Qin. He is regarded as the founding figure of the Chinese school of Legalism and is credited with a sweeping program of reforms that transformed Qin from a backward frontier state into a powerful, centralized kingdom — laying the institutional foundations that Qin would later use to unify China under Qin Shi Huang.

His reforms abolished the hereditary privileges of the aristocracy, reorganized the population into mutually responsible groups, rewarded military merit with ranks of nobility, encouraged agriculture and immigration, and standardized weights, measures, and administration. After the death of his patron Duke Xiao in 338 BC, Shang Yang was accused of treason by the new ruler, King Huiwen, and was executed by being torn apart by chariots (chelie), according to tradition — although his laws remained in force in Qin.

Early life

Shang Yang was born in Wey, a small state, as a minor descendant of its ruling family. As a young man he studied law and served as a retainer (shuzhang) to Gongshu Cuo, the chancellor of Wei. Gongshu recognized his talent and, on his deathbed in 362 BC, recommended that King Hui of Wei either employ Wei Yang as chancellor or kill him, warning that he would otherwise serve a rival state. The king ignored the advice, and Wei Yang, having heard of Duke Xiao of Qin's edict seeking talented men, travelled west to Qin in 361 BC.

Reforms in Qin

After winning Duke Xiao's confidence — reportedly after three audiences in which he progressively tailored his proposals from the way of the sage kings to the way of the strong state — Shang Yang was appointed chancellor (zuo shuzhang) and began his reforms in 356 BC. A second, more radical wave followed in 350 BC.

First reforms (356 BC)

  • Households were organized into groups of five or ten families (shi wu) under a system of mutual surveillance and collective responsibility; failure to report a crime was punished like committing one.
  • Military merit was rewarded with twenty ranks of honour; private feuds were punished severely.
  • Agriculture and weaving were encouraged with tax exemptions for high producers, while "lazy" merchants and artisans faced enslavement.
  • The old aristocracy lost its privileges unless earned by military service; the royal family itself was barred from status without merit.

Second reforms (350 BC)

  • Parents and adult sons were forbidden to live in the same household unless they paid extra taxes, breaking up large clan households into nuclear families.
  • The capital was moved to Xianyang, built on a grand grid plan.
  • Counties (xian) were established under centrally appointed magistrates, replacing feudal fiefs.
  • Weights and measures were standardized, and field boundaries and tax registers were regularized; the traditional well-field land system was abolished and private land ownership recognized.

To demonstrate that his laws would be enforced impartially, Shang Yang famously had a wooden pole erected at the south gate of the capital, offering ten — then fifty — pieces of gold to whoever moved it to the north gate. When a man did so and was paid, the populace concluded that the government's decrees were sincere. Later he punished even the crown prince's tutors when the prince broke the law, tattooing or mutilating the tutors in his place.

War with Wei and enfeoffment

In 340 BC Shang Yang persuaded the Wei general Gongzi Ang, an old acquaintance, to attend a banquet to negotiate peace, then ambushed and captured him, routing the Wei army. Wei was forced to cede territory west of the Yellow River to Qin. For this victory Shang Yang was enfeoffed with the lands of Shang and Yu — fifteen towns — and took the name by which history knows him, Lord Shang.

Death

When Duke Xiao died in 338 BC, the new king, Huiwen — the former crown prince whose tutors Shang Yang had punished — accused him of treason. Shang Yang fled; according to the Shiji, when he sought lodging at an inn, the innkeeper refused him because, under Shang Yang's own law, harbouring a guest without proper identification papers was punishable. He was caught and executed at Huiwen's order, his body torn apart by chariots as a warning, and his entire family exterminated. Despite his fall, his laws remained the governing system of Qin.

The Book of Lord Shang

The Book of Lord Shang (Shang jun shu 商君書), a Legalist treatise attributed to him and his followers, sets out the doctrine that the state should be strengthened through agriculture and war (geng zhan), strict and impersonal laws (fa), administrative technique, and the concentration of authority in the ruler. It is one of the principal surviving texts of the Legalist school.

Legacy

Historians have long debated Shang Yang's legacy. Confucian scholars vilified him as cruel and his reforms as draconian, while acknowledging that they made Qin rich and strong. Modern assessments often see his reforms as a decisive step toward the bureaucratic empire: equality before the law, government by appointed officials rather than hereditary lords, and the subordination of all social groups to state goals.
