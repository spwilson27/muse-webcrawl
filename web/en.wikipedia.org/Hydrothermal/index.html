Hydrothermal

A black smoker, a hydrothermal vent on the mid-ocean ridge.

Hydrothermal is a geological adjective referring to processes, fluids, rocks, or deposits associated with hot water circulating in the Earth's crust. The term derives from the Greek hydor (water) and therme (heat). Hydrothermal circulation occurs where water is heated by magma, hot rock, or the geothermal gradient, and the resulting hot, chemically reactive fluids dissolve, transport, and deposit minerals.

Hydrothermal circulation

Hydrothermal circulation in the oceans is the passage of seawater down through fractures in oceanic crust near spreading ridges, where it is heated to temperatures above 400 °C, reacts with the crust, and rises back to the seafloor. This circulation is a major control on the chemistry of the oceans and of the oceanic crust itself. On land, hydrothermal systems drive geysers, hot springs, and fumaroles in volcanic regions such as Yellowstone and Iceland.

Hydrothermal vents

Hydrothermal vents are fissures on the seafloor from which geothermally heated water discharges. They were first discovered in 1977 near the Galápagos Rift. Vents known as "black smokers" emit particle-laden fluids rich in iron sulfide that appear black, while "white smokers" emit lighter-coloured fluids rich in barium, calcium, and silicon. Remarkably, vents support dense biological communities—giant tube worms, clams, shrimp, and microbial mats—based on chemosynthesis rather than photosynthesis.

Hydrothermal mineral deposits

As hydrothermal fluids cool or react with wall rock, they precipitate minerals, forming economically important ore deposits. Examples include porphyry copper deposits, epithermal gold–silver veins, volcanogenic massive sulfide (VMS) deposits, and skarns. Hydrothermal alteration around such systems changes the mineralogy of the surrounding rock and is an important guide in mineral exploration.

Hydrothermal synthesis

In materials science and chemistry, hydrothermal synthesis refers to crystallising substances from high-temperature aqueous solutions at high vapour pressures. The method is used to grow single crystals (such as quartz), produce zeolites, and synthesise nanomaterials.
