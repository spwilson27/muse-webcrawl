History of video game consoles


The Magnavox Odyssey (1972), first commercial home video game console

The history of video game consoles traces dedicated home gaming machines from the Magnavox Odyssey in 1972 to the networked systems of the present day. Histories divide the industry into generations, from simple hardwired machines through the cartridge era, the 3D revolution, online gaming and hybrid handheld-home designs.

First generation (1972–1980)

Ralph Baer's "Brown Box" prototype led to the Magnavox Odyssey (1972), which played simple games using plastic overlays. Atari's home Pong (1975) ignited the market; dozens of dedicated Pong clones followed, many built around General Instrument's AY-3-8500 chip. The market for dedicated consoles collapsed in 1977 as consumers turned to programmable systems.

Second generation and the crash (1976–1992)

The Fairchild Channel F (1976) introduced interchangeable ROM cartridges; the Atari VCS/2600 (1977) popularised them with hits such as Space Invaders (1980) and Pac-Man (1982). Competitors included the Intellivision (1979), ColecoVision (1982) and Vectrex. Poor quality control and market saturation contributed to the North American video game crash of 1983, which bankrupted many firms.

Third and fourth generations (1983–1998)

Nintendo revived the industry with the Famicom/NES (1983/1985), enforcing licensing and quality seals; Super Mario Bros. and The Legend of Zelda defined the era. Sega's Master System competed, and the 16-bit generation — TurboGrafx-16 (1987), Genesis/Mega Drive (1988) and Super NES (1990) — fuelled the "console wars". Nintendo's Game Boy (1989) dominated handhelds. Neo Geo (1990) brought arcade hardware home at high cost.

Fifth and sixth generations: 3D and online (1993–2013)

The 3DO and Atari Jaguar stumbled, but Sony's PlayStation (1994) — with CD-ROM media and strong third-party support — outsold the Nintendo 64 (1996, cartridge-based) and Sega Saturn, exceeding 100 million units. Sega's Dreamcast (1998) pioneered built-in online play but failed commercially, ending Sega's hardware business. The PlayStation 2 (2000) became the best-selling console ever (over 160 million), while Microsoft entered with the Xbox (2001) and Nintendo with the GameCube.

Seventh generation onward

The Xbox 360 (2005), PlayStation 3 (2006) and Nintendo Wii (2006) diverged: Microsoft and Sony pursued HD power and online services (Xbox Live, PlayStation Network), while the Wii's motion controls reached a mass casual audience (over 101 million sold). Handhelds flourished with the Nintendo DS (2004, best-selling handheld) and PSP. The eighth generation — Wii U (2012), PlayStation 4 and Xbox One (2013) — shifted toward digital distribution; Nintendo recovered with the hybrid Switch (2017, among the best-selling consoles ever). The ninth generation (PlayStation 5, Xbox Series X/S, 2020) emphasised SSD speed, ray tracing and subscription services such as Game Pass, while retro "mini" consoles and handheld PCs like the Steam Deck (2022) blurred category lines.
