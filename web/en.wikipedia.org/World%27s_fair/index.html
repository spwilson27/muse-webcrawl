World's fair

A world's fair, also called an international exhibition or expo, is a large public exhibition staged by a host nation to showcase the achievements, industry, technology, arts and culture of participating countries. Since the mid-19th century, world's fairs have served as platforms of national prestige, technological debut and cultural exchange, drawing tens of millions of visitors.

History

The first world's fair generally recognised as such was the Great Exhibition of 1851 in London, organised under Prince Albert and held in the purpose-built Crystal Palace in Hyde Park. It displayed more than 100,000 objects from around the world and attracted some six million visitors. Its success inspired a succession of exhibitions: Paris in 1855 and repeatedly thereafter (the 1889 Exposition Universelle gave Paris the Eiffel Tower), Vienna in 1873, Philadelphia's Centennial Exposition in 1876, Chicago's World's Columbian Exposition of 1893, and the great 20th-century expositions of Brussels (1958), Montreal (Expo 67), Osaka (Expo '70) and Seville (1992).

Regulation

Since 1928, international expositions have been regulated by the Bureau International des Expositions (BIE), headquartered in Paris. The BIE distinguishes between large, infrequent World Expos (held every five years, lasting up to six months) and smaller Specialised Expos on a defined theme. Host cities are selected by BIE member states years in advance.

Legacy and notable debuts

World's fairs have introduced the public to technologies including the telephone (Philadelphia 1876), early television (New York 1939) and the monorail, and have left lasting landmarks such as the Eiffel Tower, Brussels' Atomium, Seattle's Space Needle (1962) and the Biosphere in Montreal. Expo 2010 in Shanghai drew a record attendance of about 73 million. Expo 2020, hosted by Dubai, was postponed to 2021–2022 by the COVID-19 pandemic. Expo 2025 was held in Osaka, Japan, and Expo 2030 is planned for Riyadh, Saudi Arabia.
