Marchantiophyta

Marchantiophyta (liverworts)
Kingdom: Plantae
Clade: Embryophytes
Division: Marchantiophyta
Species: about 9,000 described

The Marchantiophyta are a division of non-vascular land plants commonly referred to as liverworts. Like mosses and hornworts, they have a life cycle dominated by the haploid gametophyte generation. Liverworts number roughly 9,000 species worldwide and are found on every continent, including Antarctica.

Characteristics

Liverworts are generally small plants, from a few millimetres to a few centimetres across. Their gametophytes may be thalloid — flat ribbons of tissue without distinct stems and leaves — or leafy, with two or three rows of small overlapping leaves. Cells often contain distinctive membrane-bound oil bodies found in no other land plants. The dependent sporophyte is short-lived and usually consists of a foot, a stalk (seta) and a capsule that releases spores, often assisted by coiled elaters.

Classification

The division is divided into three classes: the Haplomitriopsida, the complex thalloid Marchantiopsida (including Marchantia), and the largely leafy Jungermanniopsida, which contains the great majority of species.

Evolution and ecology

Fossil and molecular evidence places liverworts among the earliest lineages of land plants, diverging in the Ordovician or earlier. Ecologically they colonise damp soil, rock, bark and decaying wood, contribute to soil formation, and serve as indicators of undisturbed habitats. Some species accumulate heavy metals and are used in environmental monitoring.
