Wernicke's encephalopathy

CauseThiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency
Classic triadConfusion, ophthalmoplegia, ataxia
TreatmentIntravenous thiamine
Named afterCarl Wernicke

Wernicke's encephalopathy is an acute neurological disorder caused by deficiency of thiamine (vitamin B1). It is characterised classically by a triad of confusion, eye movement abnormalities (ophthalmoplegia, nystagmus) and gait ataxia, though the full triad is present in only a minority of cases. It is a medical emergency; untreated, it may progress to Korsakoff syndrome or death.

Causes

The condition is most often associated with chronic alcohol use disorder, which impairs thiamine intake, absorption and storage. Other causes include malnutrition, prolonged vomiting (including hyperemesis gravidarum), bariatric surgery, dialysis, malignancy and refeeding after starvation.

Pathophysiology and diagnosis

Thiamine is a cofactor for enzymes of carbohydrate metabolism; deficiency causes selective damage to periventricular brain regions including the mammillary bodies, thalamus and periaqueductal grey matter, visible on MRI. Diagnosis is usually clinical, guided by criteria such as the Caine criteria, since blood thiamine assays are slow and unreliable.

Treatment

Treatment is immediate high-dose parenteral thiamine, given before glucose, together with correction of magnesium and other electrolytes and treatment of the underlying cause. Early treatment usually reverses the eye signs and may prevent permanent memory damage.
