Anthropic principle

The anthropic principle, also known as the observation selection effect, is the hypothesis that the range of possible observations that we could make about the universe is limited by the fact that observations are only possible in a universe capable of developing observers in the first place. Proponents suggest that it explains why the universe has the age and the fundamental physical constants necessary to accommodate conscious life.

Formulations

The term was coined by physicist Brandon Carter in 1973. The weak anthropic principle states that observers' observations of the universe are restricted by the conditions necessary for their own existence. The strong anthropic principle, as formulated by John D. Barrow and Frank Tipler, states that the universe must have those properties which allow life to develop within it at some stage in its history.

Debate

The principle is invoked in discussions of the apparent fine-tuning of the universe, the cosmological constant problem and multiverse theories: if many universes with different constants exist, observers would necessarily find themselves in one compatible with life. Critics argue that the principle is tautological or unscientific, while others defend it as a legitimate correction for selection bias in cosmology.
