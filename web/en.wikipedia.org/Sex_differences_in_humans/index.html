Sex differences in humans

TopicHuman biology
Related fieldsSexual dimorphism, endocrinology, medicine, psychology
Key mechanismsSex chromosomes (XX/XY), sex hormones

Sex differences in humans are the physiological, anatomical, medical and (in some respects) psychological characteristics that differ, on average or categorically, between males and females of the human species. The most fundamental differences are the primary sexual characteristics (gonads and reproductive organs), determined by sex chromosomes and the SRY gene, while secondary sexual characteristics emerge at puberty under the influence of sex hormones such as testosterone and oestrogens. Humans are a moderately sexually dimorphic species.

Physical differences

On average, adult men are taller (by roughly 13 cm globally), have greater muscle mass and upper-body strength, denser bones, larger hearts and lungs, and more body hair, while women have a wider pelvis, a higher proportion of body fat, and breasts developed for lactation. Pelvic differences are marked enough that forensic anthropologists can sex skeletal remains with high accuracy. Some traits show wide overlap between the sexes, and conditions such as Klinefelter syndrome (XXY), Turner syndrome (X0) and androgen insensitivity show that sex development is not a strict binary at every level.

Medicine and disease

Sex differences matter in medicine: males have higher rates of conditions such as colour blindness (X-linked), autism diagnoses and cardiovascular disease at younger ages; females have higher rates of autoimmune diseases, osteoporosis and Alzheimer's disease at older ages. Drug metabolism and dosing can differ by sex, prompting regulatory moves (e.g. by the US NIH and FDA) to require sex as a biological variable in research. Women also live longer than men in almost every country, by about five years on global average.

Psychology and behaviour

Research on psychological sex differences finds many similarities (the "gender similarities hypothesis") and some average differences of small to moderate size — for example in physical aggression, some spatial tasks such as mental rotation, and occupational interests along a "people–things" dimension. The origins of these differences (biological, socialisation, or both) are actively debated, and average differences do not predict individuals' traits.
