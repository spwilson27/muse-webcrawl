Ultrasound

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ultrasound is sound with frequencies greater than 20 kilohertz, which is the approximate upper limit of human hearing in healthy young adults. The physical properties of ultrasound — wavelengths shorter than those of audible sound and the ability to reflect from boundaries between materials — make it useful in many fields. Ultrasonic devices are operated on a wide scale in industry, medicine, and science, including for cleaning, mixing, measuring distances and flow, non-destructive testing, and medical imaging (sonography).

History

The science of acoustics began with the study of vibration; in 1794 the Italian biologist Lazzaro Spallanzani demonstrated that bats navigate using reflected sound. The piezoelectric effect, discovered by Jacques and Pierre Curie in 1880, provided the means to generate and detect ultrasound in air and water. Paul Langevin developed practical ultrasonic submarine detection during the First World War, and by the 1940s and 1950s Ian Donald and others had adapted the technology to medical diagnosis, first for obstetrics.

Medical sonography

Diagnostic sonography is a widely used imaging modality that is inexpensive, portable, and involves no ionising radiation. A transducer emits pulses of high-frequency sound (typically 2–18 MHz) into the body; echoes returning from tissue interfaces are processed into images in real time. Applications include obstetric monitoring, echocardiography, abdominal and pelvic imaging, vascular studies, and guidance of biopsies. Doppler ultrasound measures blood flow and is used to detect blockages and valve disease.

Industrial and other applications

  • Non-destructive testing: flaw detection in metals, welds, and structures
  • Cleaning: ultrasonic baths dissolve contaminants from jewellery, instruments, and machine parts
  • Range finding: sonar, parking sensors, and level measurement
  • Manufacturing: ultrasonic welding of plastics and metals, emulsification, and atomisation
  • Animals: bats and toothed whales use natural ultrasound for echolocation; dogs and other animals can hear frequencies inaudible to humans

Safety

Diagnostic ultrasound has an excellent safety record, although high-intensity ultrasound produces heating and cavitation in tissue. Therapeutic and industrial uses exploit these effects deliberately, for example in lithotripsy to shatter kidney stones and in high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) for tissue ablation.
