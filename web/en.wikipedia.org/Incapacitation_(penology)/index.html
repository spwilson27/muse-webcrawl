Incapacitation (penology)

Incapacitation in penology is a theory of punishment and one of the five commonly recognised objectives of sentencing, alongside deterrence, retribution, rehabilitation and restoration. It holds that a convicted offender can be prevented from committing further crimes by physically or legally restricting their ability to do so, most obviously through imprisonment, which removes the offender from society for the duration of the sentence.

Forms

Incapacitation is usually divided into two forms. Collective incapacitation applies uniform sentences to whole categories of offenders, such as mandatory minimum terms for drug offences. Selective incapacitation attempts to identify individual offenders predicted to be at high risk of reoffending and to impose longer sentences on them specifically, an approach associated with the work of the Rand Corporation in the 1980s.

Methods

Besides imprisonment, incapacitative measures include execution, banishment, disqualification from driving, revocation of professional licences, electronic monitoring, curfews and, historically, mutilation. Modern systems overwhelmingly rely on custody.

Criticism

Critics argue that incapacitation is expensive, that predictions of future dangerousness are unreliable and prone to false positives, and that imprisoning low-risk offenders may increase recidivism by exposing them to criminal networks. Supporters respond that even modest reductions in crime during custody can justify the policy where offences are serious and frequent.
