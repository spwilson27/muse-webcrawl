Xhosa cattle-killing
Date1856–1857
LocationXhosa lands, Eastern Cape, present-day South Africa
Key figureNongqawuse
OutcomeFamine; tens of thousands of deaths; collapse of Xhosa resistance

The Xhosa cattle-killing was a millenarian movement among the Xhosa people in 1856–57, which led to the destruction of their own crops and cattle in the belief that the spirits of the ancestors would return, drive the British into the sea, and renew the world. The resulting famine devastated the population of British Kaffraria.

The movement began at the Gxarha River, where the prophetess Nongqawuse reported visions.

The prophecy

In April 1856 the young prophetess Nongqawuse and her companion Nombanda reported encountering strangers at the Gxarha River who said the dead would arise if the Xhosa destroyed their cattle and grain. Her uncle Mhlakaza, a diviner, carried the message to the paramount chief Sarhili, who endorsed the prophecy. Through 1856 the Xhosa slaughtered an estimated 400,000 cattle and destroyed their grain stores; deadlines for the resurrection were repeatedly postponed.

Consequences

When the final predicted date in February 1857 passed without fulfillment, famine followed. The population of British Kaffraria fell from about 105,000 to 37,000; some 40,000 people died and others migrated into the Cape Colony as laborers. Colonial governor Sir George Grey used the crisis to seize land and break Xhosa political power.

Historiography

Historians debate the roles of cattle disease (lungsickness had recently spread), chiefs' authority, and colonial pressure. Jeff Peires's The Dead Will Arise (1989) is the standard study.
