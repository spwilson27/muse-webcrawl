Hamdan v. Rumsfeld

Hamdan v. Rumsfeld, 548 U.S. 557 (2006), was a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States holding that the military commissions established by the administration of President George W. Bush to try detainees at Guantanamo Bay lacked the authority to proceed, because their structure and procedures violated both the Uniform Code of Military Justice and the Geneva Conventions.

Background

Salim Ahmed Hamdan, a Yemeni citizen who had been Osama bin Laden's driver and bodyguard, was captured in Afghanistan in 2001 and detained at Guantanamo Bay. He was charged with conspiracy and designated for trial before a special military commission created by presidential order. Hamdan petitioned for a writ of habeas corpus challenging the commission's legality.

Decision

In a 5–3 decision delivered on 29 June 2006, with the opinion written by Justice John Paul Stevens, the Court held that the President had exceeded his authority: the commissions departed from the court-martial procedures required by the UCMJ without congressional authorisation, and Common Article 3 of the Geneva Conventions applied to the conflict with al Qaeda, requiring trial by a "regularly constituted court affording all the judicial guarantees which are recognised as indispensable by civilised peoples."

Aftermath

Congress responded by enacting the Military Commissions Act of 2006, which authorised new commissions with procedures closer to — though still distinct from — courts-martial, and purported to bar courts from applying the Geneva Conventions as an enforceable source of rights. The decision is a major precedent on the limits of presidential war powers, the role of international law in U.S. courts, and the judicial review of wartime detention. Hamdan was later tried and convicted by a commission in 2008, credited with time served, and transferred to Yemen; his conviction was vacated on appeal in 2012 on ex post facto grounds.
