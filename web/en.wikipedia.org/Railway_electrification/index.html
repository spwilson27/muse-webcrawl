Railway electrification

Railway electrification is the use of an electrical power supply system to provide energy to electric locomotives and multiple units, replacing onboard fuel-burning engines. Power is delivered through overhead lines (catenary) or a conductor rail (third rail), and is supplied through substations from the public grid. Roughly a third of the world's railway route-kilometres are electrified, with much higher shares in countries such as Switzerland (nearly 100%), India, and Japan.

Systems

Several electrification systems coexist for historical reasons. Direct current systems use voltages such as 600–750 V (typically with third rail on metros and some suburban networks), 1.5 kV, and 3 kV overhead. Alternating current systems include 25 kV at 50/60 Hz, now the standard for new main-line and high-speed electrification, and the 15 kV, 16.7 Hz system used in Germany, Austria, Switzerland, Sweden, and Norway. Japan uses 20 kV AC on many conventional lines and 25 kV on the Shinkansen. Multi-system locomotives can operate across borders under different voltages.

Advantages and disadvantages

Electric traction offers higher power-to-weight ratios, faster acceleration, lower local air pollution, reduced noise, and the ability to use regenerative braking. Energy can be drawn from any source available to the grid, including renewables. Disadvantages include the high capital cost of installing and maintaining fixed infrastructure, vulnerability to power supply failures, and clearance requirements in tunnels and under bridges.

History

Early experiments date to Werner von Siemens's 1879 demonstration locomotive in Berlin. Main-line electrification spread in the early 20th century in Alpine tunnels, the United States, and Scandinavia. The development of high-voltage AC systems using industrial frequency after the Second World War made large-scale electrification economical and enabled modern high-speed rail.
