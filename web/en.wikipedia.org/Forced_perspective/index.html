Forced perspective

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Forced perspective is a technique that employs optical illusion to make an object appear farther away, closer, larger, or smaller than it actually is. It manipulates human visual perception through the use of scaled objects and the correlation between them and the vantage point of the spectator or camera. It has uses in photography, filmmaking, and architecture.

In photography

In photography, forced perspective is often used playfully by tourists, who position themselves so that they appear to hold up, push over, or stand upon famous landmarks such as the Leaning Tower of Pisa or the Eiffel Tower. The effect depends on aligning the camera, the subject, and the background object along a single line of sight while keeping all elements in focus.

In filmmaking

Filmmakers have used forced perspective since the earliest days of cinema to make sets and actors appear at impossible scales without elaborate effects. By placing an actor closer to the camera and another farther away on a specially built set, performers of the same height can appear to be giants and dwarfs. The technique was used extensively in the Lord of the Rings film trilogy to depict hobbits alongside much taller characters, with moving camera rigs preserving the illusion during tracking shots.

In architecture

Architects have long used forced perspective to make buildings appear larger or longer than they are. Baroque architects such as Francesco Borromini employed gradually shrinking columns and a rising floor in the colonnade of Palazzo Spada in Rome to create the illusion of a gallery far longer than its actual length. Theme parks also use the technique: the buildings along Main Street, U.S.A. in Disney parks are built at progressively reduced scales on their upper floors so that the castle at the end appears more distant and grand.
