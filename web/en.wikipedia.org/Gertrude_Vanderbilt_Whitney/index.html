Gertrude Vanderbilt Whitney (1875–1942) was an American sculptor, art patron, and collector, best known as the founder of the Whitney Museum of American Art in New York City. A great-granddaughter of Cornelius Vanderbilt, she used her considerable fortune to champion living American artists at a time when American museums largely ignored them.

Life and career

Born into the Vanderbilt family, she studied sculpture at the Art Students League of New York and in Paris, working under teachers including Auguste Rodin. She maintained studios in Greenwich Village and Paris and produced public monuments, among them the Washington Heights–Inwood War Memorial, the Victory Arch in Madison Square, and the Titanic Memorial in Washington, D.C., honouring the men who died on the RMS Titanic.

Patronage and the Whitney Museum

From 1907 Whitney bought and exhibited the work of contemporary American artists, many of them from the Ashcan School. In 1914 she founded the Whitney Studio, followed by the Whitney Studio Club and galleries that supported hundreds of artists. After the Metropolitan Museum of Art declined her offer of some 500 works of modern American art in 1929, she founded her own museum; the Whitney Museum of American Art opened in 1931 on West 8th Street, with her associate Juliana Force as its first director. Whitney was also a central figure in the celebrated 1930s custody case over her niece, Gloria Vanderbilt.
