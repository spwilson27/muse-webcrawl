Julia Robinson

Julia Hall Bowman Robinson
Born: December 8, 1919, St. Louis, Missouri, U.S.
Died: July 30, 1985, Oakland, California, U.S.
Fields: Mathematics (number theory, logic)
Known for: Contributions to Hilbert's tenth problem

Julia Hall Bowman Robinson (December 8, 1919 – July 30, 1985) was an American mathematician noted for her contributions to the fields of computability theory and computational complexity theory, most famously her work on Hilbert's tenth problem. She was the first woman elected president of the American Mathematical Society.

Early life and education

Robinson was born in St. Louis and grew up in Arizona and San Diego. A bout of scarlet fever and later rheumatic fever interrupted her schooling and left her with lasting health problems. She studied at San Diego State College and the University of California, Berkeley, where she earned her PhD in 1948 under Alfred Tarski with a dissertation on definability in arithmetic.

Mathematical work

Robinson devoted much of her career to Hilbert's tenth problem, which asked for an algorithm to determine whether a Diophantine equation has integer solutions. In collaboration with Martin Davis and Hilary Putnam, she established key results — including the result that exponential Diophantine relations are Diophantine — that laid the groundwork for Yuri Matiyasevich's 1970 proof that no such algorithm exists. The combined result is known as the MRDP theorem. She also worked on game theory at the RAND Corporation, proving a convergence theorem for fictitious play in zero-sum games.

Honors

Robinson was elected to the National Academy of Sciences in 1975, the first woman mathematician so honored, received a MacArthur Fellowship in 1983, and served as president of the American Mathematical Society in 1983–84. She died of leukemia in 1985.
