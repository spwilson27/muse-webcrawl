The Declaration of Sentiments is a document signed in 1848 by 68 women and 32 men — delegates to the first women's rights convention in the United States, organised by Elizabeth Cady Stanton and Lucretia Mott and held in Seneca Falls, New York. Authored primarily by Stanton, it is generally regarded as the founding text of the American women's suffrage movement.

Text and structure

The Declaration closely parallels the United States Declaration of Independence, even in its opening lines: "We hold these truths to be self-evident: that all men and women are created equal." Stanton adapted Jefferson's catalogue of grievances against King George III into a list of eighteen injuries and usurpations on the part of man toward woman, charging that he had never permitted her to exercise her right to vote, had compelled her to submit to laws in whose formation she had no voice, had withheld property and wage rights in marriage, had barred her from higher education and the professions, and had "endeavoured, in every way that he could, to destroy her confidence in her own powers."

The suffrage resolution

The document concluded with resolutions demanding legal equality and, most controversially, women's suffrage. The suffrage resolution nearly failed at the convention and passed only after an impassioned speech by Frederick Douglass, the abolitionist and former enslaved person, who argued that he could not accept the right to vote himself without it extending to women.

Reception and legacy

The convention and its Declaration attracted national press attention, much of it mocking, and several signers withdrew their names under pressure. Nevertheless, the document inaugurated a seventy-year campaign that culminated in the Nineteenth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution in 1920, which prohibited denying the vote on account of sex. Stanton's text remains a landmark of American feminist literature.
