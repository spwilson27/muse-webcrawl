Catharsis

FromGreek κάθαρσις (katharsis), "purification, cleansing"
Introduced byAristotle, Poetics
FieldsLiterary theory, psychology, medicine

Catharsis is the purification and purgation of emotions — particularly pity and fear — through art, or any extreme change in emotion that results in renewal and restoration. The term derives from the Greek katharsis, meaning "purification" or "cleansing", and was given its enduring critical sense by Aristotle in the Poetics.


Tragedy in the ancient theatre, the original setting of catharsis

Aristotle and tragedy

In the Poetics, Aristotle defines tragedy as an imitation of an action that, "through pity and fear, effects the catharsis of such emotions." Scholars have debated for centuries whether catharsis is primarily medical (a purging of excess emotion), religious (a purification), or intellectual (a clarification of the emotions' objects). On any reading, it names the peculiar emotional release and understanding produced by tragic drama.

Psychology

The concept was adopted by early psychoanalysis: Breuer and Freud's "cathartic method" in Studies on Hysteria (1895) treated hysterical symptoms by encouraging patients to relive and discharge repressed traumatic memories. In modern psychology, "catharsis" commonly denotes the expression and release of pent-up emotion, though experimental research on the catharsis hypothesis — for example, that venting anger reduces aggression — has produced mixed and often contrary results.

Other uses

In ancient medicine, catharsis referred to physical purgation of the body. In religious practice it denoted ritual purification. The word remains in general use for any experience of emotional release followed by a sense of renewal.
