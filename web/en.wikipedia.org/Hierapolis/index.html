Hierapolis (Ancient Greek: Ἱεράπολις, "Holy City") was an ancient Greek city located on hot springs in southwestern Anatolia, in present-day Pamukkale in Turkey. Its extensive ruins — including a great theatre, temples, baths and one of the largest ancient necropolises in Anatolia — lie above the gleaming white travertine terraces formed by its mineral springs.

History

The city was founded in the early 2nd century BC under the Attalid kings of Pergamon and passed to Rome in 133 BC. Thanks to its thermal waters and its textile and wool industries, it prospered as a healing spa; a Jewish community and, later, an early Christian community flourished there. Tradition holds that the apostle Philip was martyred at Hierapolis around 80 AD, and an octagonal martyrium was built over his tomb. The city was repeatedly damaged by earthquakes, most severely in the 7th century, and declined through the Byzantine period.

Archaeology

Principal remains include the large Roman theatre (seating some 12,000), the Temple of Apollo, the Plutonium — a shrine to Pluto at a cave emitting toxic carbon dioxide gas, whose lethal effect on animals puzzled ancient writers and was confirmed by modern measurement — the nymphaeum, the colonnaded main street (Frontinus Street), the great baths, and the North Necropolis with more than 1,200 tombs. Cleopatra's Pool, a thermal basin among fallen columns, is still open to bathers. Hierapolis and Pamukkale were jointly inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1988.
