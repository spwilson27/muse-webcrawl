Strait of Magellan

The Strait of Magellan (Spanish: Estrecho de Magallanes) is a navigable sea route in southern Chile separating mainland South America to the north and Tierra del Fuego to the south. It is the most important natural passage between the Atlantic and Pacific oceans, though its winding, weather-beaten course made it feared by sailors.

History

The strait is named for the Portuguese explorer Ferdinand Magellan, whose expedition first navigated it in 1520 during the first circumnavigation of the Earth. Magellan named it the Strait of All Saints. For centuries it was the principal route between the oceans — used by Francis Drake and by clipper ships — until the Panama Canal opened in 1914, after which traffic declined sharply.

Geography

About 570 km (350 mi) long and as narrow as 2 km in places, the strait lies entirely within Chilean waters (confirmed by the 1881 treaty with Argentina) except for its eastern entrance. The city of Punta Arenas, founded in 1848, is its principal port. Fierce winds (williwaws), strong currents, and unpredictable weather remain hazards, and large ships today often prefer the open-ocean Drake Passage or the Panama Canal.
