Accretion disk

An accretion disk is a structure (often a circumstellar disk) formed by diffuse material in orbital motion around a massive central body. The central body is most frequently a star, but disks also form around white dwarfs, neutron stars, and black holes. Friction, turbulent viscosity, and magnetic stresses cause material in the disk to lose angular momentum and spiral inward while heating up and emitting radiation.

Physics

Gas in the disk orbits at nearly Keplerian speeds, with inner material orbiting faster than outer material. This differential rotation generates shear, and if the disk is turbulent, the shear produces friction that heats the gas and transports angular momentum outward. The alpha-viscosity prescription, introduced by Shakura and Sunyaev in 1973, parametrises this turbulence with a dimensionless parameter α. The leading candidate mechanism for generating the turbulence is the magnetorotational instability, described by Balbus and Hawley in 1991.

Observations

Accretion disks are observed around young stars (where they are the birthplaces of planets), in cataclysmic variable stars, in X-ray binaries, and at the centres of active galaxies, where supermassive black holes accrete gas. In 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope produced an image of the glowing accretion flow around the black hole in the galaxy M87.

Jets

Many accreting systems launch narrow, collimated jets of plasma perpendicular to the disk, probably powered by magnetic fields anchored in the disk or the spinning central object.
