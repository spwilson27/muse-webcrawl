A public utility company (usually just utility) is an organisation that maintains the infrastructure for a public service, most often electricity, natural gas, water, sewage, and historically telephony. Utilities are frequently natural monopolies — their networks are expensive to duplicate — and are therefore subject to public regulation of prices and service obligations, whether they are publicly owned, investor-owned, or cooperatives.

Structure and regulation

In the United States, investor-owned utilities are regulated by state public utility commissions (a system whose constitutional basis was set in Munn v. Illinois, 1877), which traditionally allowed the utility a fair rate of return on its capital ("rate base"). Municipal utilities, such as the Los Angeles Department of Water and Power, and rural electric cooperatives formed under the New Deal's Rural Electrification Act of 1936, serve large parts of the country. In much of Europe utilities were state-owned monopolies until liberalisation from the 1980s onward introduced competition in generation and retail supply while keeping transmission and distribution as regulated monopolies.

Current issues

Utilities face pressure to decarbonise electricity systems, integrate variable renewable generation and storage, replace ageing water infrastructure, and adapt tariffs as customers generate their own power with rooftop solar. Regulation increasingly uses performance incentives and price-cap formulas rather than simple cost-of-service pricing.
