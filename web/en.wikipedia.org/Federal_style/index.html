Federal style

Federal architecture flourished in the United States from about 1780 to 1830.

Federal-style architecture is the name for the classicising architecture built in the newly founded United States between c. 1780 and 1830, and particularly from 1785 to 1815, which was heavily influenced by the interpretation of Ancient Roman architecture by the Scottish architect Robert Adam and his Adam style. The term is used especially for American Federal-style furniture and house design.

The style broadly corresponds to the middle-class Biedermeier style in the German-speaking lands, the Regency style in Britain, and the French Empire style. Early examples of the Federal style include the work of Charles Bulfinch in Boston and the buildings of early Washington, D.C.

Characteristics

Federal-style buildings are typically symmetrical, with smooth facades, elliptical or circular fanlights over doors, three-part or Palladian windows, decorative crown moulding, and low-pitched roofs hidden behind balustrades. Interiors feature delicate, attenuated ornament, oval rooms, and mantelpieces adorned with swags, urns, and classical motifs. The style gradually gave way to the Greek Revival in the 1820s and 1830s.
