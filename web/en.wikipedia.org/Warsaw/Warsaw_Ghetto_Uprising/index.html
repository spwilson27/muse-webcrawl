Warsaw Ghetto Uprising

The Warsaw Ghetto Uprising (19 April – 16 May 1943) was the largest act of Jewish armed resistance against Nazi Germany during the Holocaust. It erupted when German forces entered the Warsaw Ghetto to complete its liquidation and deport the remaining inhabitants to extermination camps.

Causes

Between July and September 1942 the Germans deported about 265,000 of the ghetto’s Jews to Treblinka, where they were murdered. When a further deportation action in January 1943 met armed resistance and was halted after four days, the roughly 55,000–60,000 Jews remaining resolved to resist the next — and expected final — liquidation.

The fighting

The uprising was fought by two organisations: the ŻOB, commanded by Mordechai Anielewicz, and the ŻZW. Together they fielded perhaps 750 fighters, poorly armed with pistols, a handful of rifles and machine guns, and homemade explosives, supported by thousands of civilians hiding in prepared underground bunkers. SS and police forces under Jürgen Stroop, numbering some 2,000 men with artillery, armoured vehicles and flamethrowers, advanced slowly against fierce resistance. After days of combat, Stroop ordered the ghetto burned building by building. The ŻOB command bunker at Miła 18 fell on 8 May, Anielewicz dying there with his comrades. Stroop symbolically ended the operation on 16 May 1943 by destroying the Great Synagogue of Warsaw.

Consequences

Some 13,000 Jews were killed in the fighting and fires; roughly 56,000 were captured according to the Stroop Report, of whom about 7,000 were shot on the spot and the rest deported to Treblinka, Majdanek and labour camps, where most perished. A handful of fighters, including Marek Edelman and Zivia Lubetkin, escaped through the sewers with the help of the Polish underground. The uprising demonstrated Jewish armed resistance on a scale unmatched elsewhere and became a foundational symbol of Jewish heroism; it helped inspire revolts at Treblinka and Sobibór later in 1943.

Commemoration

The Monument to the Ghetto Heroes, designed by Nathan Rapoport, was unveiled on the fifth anniversary of the uprising in 1948 amid the ruins of the ghetto; the POLIN Museum of the History of Polish Jews opened opposite it in 2013. The anniversary is marked annually in Warsaw and in Israel.
