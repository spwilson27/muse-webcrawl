The Warsaw Ghetto was the largest of the Nazi ghettos in German-occupied Europe during the Second World War. Established by the German authorities in the autumn of 1940 in the Muranów district of Warsaw, it imprisoned more than 400,000 Jews — about 30 percent of the city's population — in an area of some 3.4 square kilometres, walled off and sealed on 16 November 1940.

Life and death in the ghetto

Conditions were deliberately lethal: official food rations were set at starvation levels, disease — especially typhus — spread through the overcrowded streets, and some 100,000 residents died of hunger and illness before deportations began. Despite this, the ghetto sustained schools, soup kitchens, clandestine newspapers, and Emanuel Ringelblum's Oyneg Shabes archive, which documented ghetto life for posterity. The ghetto was administered internally by a Judenrat under Adam Czerniaków, who took his own life in July 1942 rather than sign deportation orders for children.

Deportation and uprising

Between 22 July and September 1942, during the Grossaktion Warschau, roughly 265,000–300,000 ghetto Jews were deported to the Treblinka extermination camp and murdered. The remaining population — some 55,000–60,000 — formed resistance organisations, the Jewish Combat Organization (ŻOB) and the Jewish Military Union (ŻZW). When the Germans moved to liquidate the ghetto on 19 April 1943, the Warsaw Ghetto Uprising began: poorly armed fighters held out for nearly a month, until SS commander Jürgen Stroop's forces burned the ghetto block by block and blew up the Great Synagogue on 16 May 1943. Survivors were deported to camps, most to be murdered.

Legacy

The ghetto site, razed to rubble, was rebuilt after the war as a residential district atop the ruins. The Monument to the Ghetto Heroes (1948), the POLIN Museum of the History of Polish Jews (opened 2013 on the ghetto site), and surviving fragments of the ghetto wall preserve its memory.
