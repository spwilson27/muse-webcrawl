Treblinka extermination camp

Treblinka was a Nazi extermination camp in occupied Poland during the Second World War, built by Nazi Germany near the village of Treblinka, about 80 kilometres northeast of Warsaw. Operating from 23 July 1942 to 19 October 1943 as part of Operation Reinhard, the deadliest phase of the Final Solution, the camp murdered an estimated 800,000 to 925,000 Jews, along with some 2,000 Romani people — more victims than any Nazi killing centre except Auschwitz-Birkenau.

Operation

The camp, staffed by a small SS detachment under the Operation Reinhard headquarters in Lublin and by Trawniki guards, was divided into a reception area disguised as a railway station — complete with a fake clock, timetables and signs — and an extermination area containing gas chambers. Transports arrived by train, chiefly from the Warsaw Ghetto during the Grossaktion of summer 1942, in which some 265,000 Warsaw Jews were sent to Treblinka. Victims were forced to undress and were driven along a fenced path, called the “Road to Heaven”, to gas chambers in which they were killed with carbon monoxide from engine exhaust. Jewish prisoner labourers, the Sonderkommando, were forced to remove and burn the bodies; hundreds of these workers were themselves murdered and replaced.

Command and revolt

The first commandant, Irmfried Eberl, was replaced by Franz Stangl after chaos and corpses piling up overwhelmed the camp; Kurt Franz served as deputy and later commandant. On 2 August 1943 the prisoners staged a revolt, seizing weapons, setting buildings ablaze and attempting a mass escape. Several hundred broke out, though most were hunted down; around 70 survived the war. The camp was then dismantled, the ground ploughed over and a farm established to conceal the site ahead of the Soviet advance.

Trials and memorial

Stangl, extradited from Brazil, was sentenced to life imprisonment at the Düsseldorf Treblinka trial’s successor proceedings and died in prison in 1971; Kurt Franz also received a life sentence. John Demjanjuk was convicted in Israel in 1988 as “Ivan the Terrible”, a Treblinka guard, but the conviction was overturned on appeal for reasonable doubt. A memorial of some 17,000 jagged stones, representing the destroyed Jewish communities, was erected at the site in 1964. Escapee Jankiel Wiernik’s memoir A Year in Treblinka and the accounts of survivors Chil Rajchman and Richard Glazar remain central testimony.
