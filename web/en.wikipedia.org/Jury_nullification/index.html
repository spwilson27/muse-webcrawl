Jury nullification

Jury nullification occurs when a jury in a criminal trial returns a verdict of not guilty even though the jurors believe the defendant committed the offence charged, because they disagree with the law itself, consider its application unjust, or disapprove of the prosecution. Because acquittals generally cannot be appealed or overturned, and jurors cannot be punished for their verdicts, nullification is considered an inherent — though contested — power of the jury.

History

The principle traces in English law to Bushel's Case (1670), which established that jurors could not be fined or imprisoned for a verdict against the judge's direction; the case arose from the trial of Quakers William Penn and William Mead. In colonial America, the 1735 acquittal of printer John Peter Zenger on a seditious libel charge is a famous early example. In the nineteenth century, northern juries sometimes acquitted defendants charged under the Fugitive Slave Acts.

Legal status

Courts generally acknowledge that juries can nullify but deny that they have a legal right to be told so. In Sparf v. United States (1895), the U.S. Supreme Court held that judges need not instruct jurors about nullification, and modern courts routinely remove jurors who state an intent to nullify. A few state constitutions, such as those of Indiana and Maryland, contain provisions historically read as giving juries a role in judging the law.

Debate

Supporters describe nullification as a vital check on unjust laws and government overreach; critics warn it substitutes jurors' prejudices for the rule of law, and note its use in acquitting racially motivated violence in the Jim Crow South. Advocacy organisations such as the Fully Informed Jury Association campaign to inform jurors of the power.
