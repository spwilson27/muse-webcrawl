Promoter (genetics)

In genetics, a promoter is a sequence of DNA to which proteins bind to initiate transcription of a single RNA transcript from the DNA downstream of it. This RNA may be messenger RNA or a functional RNA such as transfer RNA or ribosomal RNA. Promoters are located near the transcription start sites of genes, upstream on the same strand, and can be about 100–1,000 base pairs long. The promoter is the central control element of gene regulation: the molecular machinery of transcription assembles there, and its activity determines when, where, and how strongly a gene is expressed.

Prokaryotic promoters

In bacteria, the promoter is recognised directly by RNA polymerase together with a sigma factor. Conserved sequence elements — the −35 region and the −10 region (the Pribnow box, consensus TATAAT) — position the polymerase so that transcription begins at the start site. Different sigma factors direct the polymerase to different promoter sets, coordinating responses such as heat shock or sporulation. The classic example is the lac promoter of the E. coli lac operon, regulated by the lac repressor and the activator CAP.

Eukaryotic promoters

Eukaryotic promoters are bound by general transcription factors that recruit RNA polymerase II. Many contain a TATA box about 25–30 base pairs upstream of the start site, recognised by the TATA-binding protein of TFIID; others are TATA-less and use initiator (Inr) or downstream promoter elements instead. Promoters are integrated with distant enhancers and silencers, chromatin state, and DNA methylation to produce precise patterns of expression. CpG islands mark the promoters of most human housekeeping genes.

Study and use

Promoter strength and regulation are studied with reporter genes, footprinting, and genome-wide assays of transcription factor binding. Engineered promoters are essential tools in biotechnology — strong constitutive viral promoters such as CMV drive transgene expression, while inducible promoters (for example, tetracycline-controlled systems) allow genes to be switched on at will. Mutations in promoters can cause disease by altering expression, as in some forms of β-thalassemia and in cancers where the TERT promoter is reactivated.
