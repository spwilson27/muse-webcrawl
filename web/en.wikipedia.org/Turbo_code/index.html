Turbo code

Structure of a parallel concatenated turbo encoder

In information theory, turbo codes (originally in French turbocodes) are a class of high-performance forward error correction (FEC) codes developed around 1990–91, but first published in 1993. They were the first practical codes to closely approach the maximum channel capacity, or Shannon limit, at which reliable communication is still possible given a specific noise level.

History

Turbo codes were introduced by Claude Berrou, Alain Glavieux and Punya Thitimajshima of Télécom Bretagne in 1993. Their reported performance — reliable communication within about 0.5 dB of the Shannon limit — was initially met with scepticism but was soon confirmed, revolutionising channel coding and stimulating the rediscovery and development of related codes such as LDPC codes.

Description

A turbo encoder typically combines two (or more) recursive systematic convolutional encoders in parallel, separated by an interleaver that permutes the data bits. The decoder works iteratively: two soft-decision decoders exchange "extrinsic" information about the data bits over several iterations, converging on a reliable estimate — an analogy with turbo engines feeding back exhaust gases gave the codes their name.

Applications

Turbo codes have been used in 3G and 4G mobile telephony standards (UMTS and LTE), in deep-space communications, in satellite links, and in other applications where maximising data transfer over bandwidth-limited or noisy channels is critical. In 5G standards, turbo codes were largely superseded by LDPC and polar codes.
