Szlachta

The szlachta (Polish: [ˈʂlaxta]) were the noble estate of the realm in the Kingdom of Poland, the Grand Duchy of Lithuania and the Polish–Lithuanian Commonwealth. Unusually numerous by European standards — around 8–10% of the population — the szlachta enjoyed extensive legal and political privileges, including electing the king.

Origins and strata

Emerging from medieval knighthood, the estate ranged from great magnate families (Radziwiłł, Potocki, Czartoryski) owning latifundia, through middling landowners, to poor petty nobles (szlachta zagrodowa) barely distinguishable from peasants except in law. In principle all nobles were legally equal — the saying held that a petty noble on his plot was the equal of a voivode.

Political role

Nobles met in local sejmiks, elected deputies to the Sejm, and from 1573 elected kings viritim. Golden Liberty (złota wolność) exalted noble freedom but underpinned later dysfunction via the liberum veto. Sarmatism — the belief in descent from ancient Sarmatians — shaped noble culture, dress and portraiture.

After the partitions

Under the partitions, many petty nobles lost legal status; the estate's traditions nonetheless fed Polish national movements of the 19th century.
