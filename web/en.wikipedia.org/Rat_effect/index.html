Rat effect

The rat effect (German: Ratteneffekt) is a term used in economics, particularly in the study of labour markets and incentive systems, describing the counterproductive consequences that arise when workers are rewarded solely according to measured output. When a bonus or piece-rate system is introduced, employees may concentrate on the rewarded indicator at the expense of quality, cooperation, or other unrewarded tasks — much as laboratory rats learn to press the lever that delivers the reward, and nothing else.

Origin of the term

The metaphor draws on behaviourist experiments with rats in Skinner boxes, where animals optimised their behaviour strictly for the dispensed reward. German management literature popularised the phrase in discussions of target agreements and variable pay in the 1990s and 2000s.

Manifestations

Typical manifestations include quantity being maximised while quality declines, employees withholding help from colleagues whose output competes with their own, and the neglect of maintenance, documentation, and other tasks not captured by the metric. The effect is closely related to Goodhart's law and to the cobra effect, in which an incentive produces the opposite of its intended result.

Mitigation

Proposed remedies include balanced scorecards combining several indicators, qualitative assessments alongside quantitative ones, team-based rather than purely individual incentives, and periodic rotation or revision of targets so that gaming a single measure becomes less rewarding.
