Vinča culture

The Vinča culture (also known as the Turdaş culture or Turdaş–Vinča culture) was a Neolithic and early Copper Age archaeological culture of Southeast Europe, centred on the Danube valley, dating roughly to 5700–4500 BC. It is named for its type site, Vinča-Belo Brdo, near Belgrade in Serbia, excavated from 1908 by Miloje Vasić.

Society and settlements

Vinča settlements included some of the largest communities of prehistoric Europe, with tells such as Vinča-Belo Brdo and Pločnik housing perhaps thousands of inhabitants. Houses were rectangular, built of wattle-and-daub, with clay ovens and multiple rooms. The economy was based on farming wheat, barley, lentils, and peas, herding cattle and pigs, and extensive trade in obsidian, salt, and marine shells.

Material culture

The culture is noted for distinctive dark burnished pottery, anthropomorphic and zoomorphic figurines, and very early copper metallurgy — copper smelting at Belovode and Pločnik dates to around 5000 BC, among the earliest in the world. Most debated is the so-called Vinča symbols, marks found on pottery and figurines, which some researchers (notably Marija Gimbutas) interpreted as a form of proto-writing ("Old European script"), though most archaeologists regard them as potters' marks or decorations.
