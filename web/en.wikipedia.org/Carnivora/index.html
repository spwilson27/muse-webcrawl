Carnivora is an order of placental mammals specialised primarily in eating flesh, though diets among its members range from obligate carnivory (as in cats) through omnivory to near-total herbivory (the giant panda). The order includes about 300 living species — among them dogs, cats, bears, weasels, raccoons, hyenas, mongooses, seals, sea lions, and walruses — making it one of the most diverse and ecologically dominant orders of mammals.

Characteristics

Most carnivorans possess a specialised pair of shearing teeth, the carnassials (the upper fourth premolar and lower first molar), used to slice meat and tendon. They typically have strong, curved claws, keen senses, and — relative to other mammals — large brains and complex behaviour. Members range in size from the least weasel (about 25 g) to the southern elephant seal (over 3,000 kg).

Evolution and classification

Carnivora arose in North America around 60–55 million years ago, shortly after the end-Cretaceous extinction, and later spread worldwide. The order is divided into two suborders: Caniformia, the "dog-like" carnivorans (canids, bears, mustelids, pinnipeds, and others), and Feliformia, the "cat-like" carnivorans (cats, hyenas, mongooses, civets, and others). Pinnipeds — seals, sea lions, and the walrus — are caniforms that returned to the sea roughly 25–30 million years ago.
