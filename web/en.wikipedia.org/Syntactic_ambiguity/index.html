Syntactic ambiguity

Syntactic ambiguity, also called structural ambiguity or grammatical ambiguity, is a property of a sentence or phrase that can be parsed and interpreted in more than one way because of its grammatical structure, even when every word in it is unambiguous. It is a central topic in linguistics, psycholinguistics, and natural language processing.

Examples

The classic example “I saw the man with the telescope” is ambiguous because the prepositional phrase with the telescope can attach either to the verb phrase (the telescope was the instrument of seeing) or to the noun phrase (the man had the telescope). Other examples include “The chicken is ready to eat” and “Visiting relatives can be boring”. Newspaper headlines exploit or stumble into this ambiguity, producing “crash blossoms” such as “Squad helps dog bite victim”.

Theoretical significance

In formal language theory, a grammar that generates a string with two distinct parse trees is an ambiguous grammar; some context-free languages are inherently ambiguous. In human sentence processing, syntactic ambiguity gives rise to garden-path sentences, where readers typically commit to one analysis and must reanalyse on encountering disambiguating material — evidence for how the brain's parser works. Legal drafting and programming-language design both go to great lengths to avoid such ambiguity, since different readings can carry real-world consequences.
