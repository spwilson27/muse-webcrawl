Community of practice

A community of practice (CoP) is a group of people who "share a concern or a passion for something they do and learn how to do it better as they interact regularly." The concept was first proposed by cognitive anthropologist Jean Lave and educational theorist Étienne Wenger in their 1991 book Situated Learning: Legitimate Peripheral Participation.

Characteristics

Wenger identifies three structural characteristics of communities of practice: the domain — a shared area of interest that gives the community its identity; the community — members who interact, help each other, and build relationships; and the practice — a shared repertoire of resources, tools, stories, and ways of addressing recurring problems developed over time. Newcomers typically enter through "legitimate peripheral participation," taking on simpler tasks and gradually moving toward full participation as they master the practice.

Applications

The concept has been widely applied in business, education, government, and healthcare. Organizations cultivate communities of practice to support knowledge management, spread best practices, onboard new employees, and foster innovation. Examples range from networks of claims processors and engineers in large corporations to teachers sharing classroom techniques and open-source software developers.

Related concepts

Communities of practice differ from project teams (formed around a task and dissolved on completion), communities of interest (united by a topic rather than a practice), and formal work groups. Later work by Wenger and collaborators extended the idea to "landscapes of practice" made up of many interrelated communities.
