The Mafia, also known as Cosa Nostra, is a criminal society that originated in Sicily in the mid-19th century and later spread to other parts of Italy and to the United States. A "mafioso" is a member of the Mafia. The word derives from Sicilian mafiusu, possibly from Arabic roots meaning "aggressive boasting" or "swagger". The Mafia's core activities are protection racketeering, the arbitration of disputes between criminals, and the organising of illegal agreements and transactions.

Structure

The basic group is the "family" (cosca), which claims sovereignty over a territory, usually a town or village. Members swear an oath of loyalty and silence (omertà). Sicilian families are loosely coordinated by a provincial commission, and family bosses meet over disputes and strategy. In the United States, the Italian-American Mafia developed its own Five Families in New York and outfits in other cities, linked to Sicily but organisationally separate.

History

The Mafia emerged amid Sicily's transition from feudalism, exploiting weak state authority to sell protection to landowners and merchants. Mussolini's prefect Cesare Mori suppressed it in the 1920s, but it revived after the Allied invasion of 1943. Postwar Cosa Nostra grew rich from construction rackets and the international heroin trade. The Maxi Trial of 1986–87, based on testimony of pentiti such as Tommaso Buscetta, convicted hundreds; the Mafia answered with the 1992 murders of judges Giovanni Falcone and Paolo Borsellino, provoking a state crackdown and the arrest of boss Salvatore Riina in 1993 and his successor Bernardo Provenzano in 2006.

Other Italian mafias

Related but distinct organisations include the Camorra of Campania, the 'Ndrangheta of Calabria—now considered Italy's most powerful syndicate—and Sacra Corona Unita in Apulia.
