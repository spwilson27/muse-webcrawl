Pyrrhic War

Southern Italy at the time of the Pyrrhic War (280–275 BC)

The Pyrrhic War (280–275 BC) was fought by the Roman Republic against Pyrrhus, the king of Epirus, who had been asked by the Greek city of Tarentum in southern Italy to help them in their dispute with Rome. Pyrrhus's opening battles against the Romans at Heraclea and Asculum, although victories, cost him so many of his own troops that the expression "Pyrrhic victory" was coined to describe a victory won at such a cost that it amounts to a defeat.

Background

By the early 3rd century BC, Rome had become the dominant power in central Italy and was steadily extending its influence southward into Magna Graecia, the region of Greek colonies along the Italian coast. Tarentum, the most powerful of these cities, appealed for help to Pyrrhus of Epirus, one of the most celebrated generals of the Hellenistic age, who crossed the Adriatic in 280 BC with some 25,000 men and about 20 war elephants — the first elephants the Romans had ever faced in battle.

Course of the war

Pyrrhus defeated the Romans at the Battle of Heraclea in 280 BC and again at the Battle of Asculum in 279 BC, where his losses were so severe that, according to Plutarch, he remarked that one more such victory would ruin him. Unable to force a decision in Italy, he intervened in Sicily against Carthage in 278–276 BC, nearly expelling the Carthaginians from the island, but alienated his Greek allies and withdrew. Returning to Italy, he was defeated by the Romans at the Battle of Beneventum in 275 BC and withdrew to Epirus, leaving a garrison at Tarentum that surrendered in 272 BC.

Aftermath

The war established Rome as the master of the entire Italian peninsula south of the Po valley and brought it into direct confrontation with Carthage over Sicily, setting the stage for the Punic Wars. It was also the first major war between Rome and a Hellenistic kingdom, foreshadowing Rome's later expansion into the Greek east.
