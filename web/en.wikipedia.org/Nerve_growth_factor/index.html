Nerve growth factor

Nerve growth factor (NGF) is a neurotrophic factor and small secreted protein essential for the growth, maintenance, proliferation, and survival of certain neurons, particularly sympathetic and sensory neurons of the peripheral nervous system. It was the first growth factor to be discovered, in experiments by Rita Levi-Montalcini and Stanley Cohen in the 1950s, work that earned them the 1986 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Function

NGF is released by target tissues and taken up by the nerve terminals of innervating neurons, which depend on this supply for survival during development — neurons that fail to obtain enough NGF die by apoptosis, a mechanism that matches neuron numbers to their targets. In adults, NGF maintains the phenotype of nociceptive (pain-sensing) and sympathetic neurons and sensitises nociceptors, making it a mediator of inflammatory pain. NGF acts through two receptors: the high-affinity TrkA receptor, which mediates its trophic effects, and the low-affinity p75 neurotrophin receptor, which can promote survival or apoptosis depending on context.

Clinical significance

Because NGF drives pain signalling, monoclonal antibodies that block NGF (such as tanezumab) have been developed as analgesics for osteoarthritis and chronic back pain. Conversely, recombinant human NGF (cenegermin) is used as an eye-drop treatment for neurotrophic keratitis, a degenerative corneal disease. Mutations in the NGF gene or in TrkA cause congenital insensitivity to pain syndromes (HSAN types IV and V). Elevated or reduced NGF has also been studied in connection with allergic inflammation, Alzheimer's disease, and various cancers.
