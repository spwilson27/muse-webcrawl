Siege of La Rochelle

The siege of La Rochelle (French: Siège de La Rochelle) was a military operation by the forces of King Louis XIII of France against the Huguenot city of La Rochelle between 1627 and 1628. The siege, directed by Cardinal Richelieu, ended with the capitulation of the city after a 14-month blockade that reduced its population by starvation. It marked the end of the Huguenots as an independent political and military power in France.

Background

La Rochelle was the principal stronghold of the French Calvinist Huguenots, enjoying a measure of self-government and looking to England for support. Hostilities between the crown and the Huguenots resumed in the 1620s. After the Huguenot leader, the Duke of Rohan, allied with England, royal forces resolved to subdue La Rochelle.

The siege

Royal troops surrounded the city with a line of fortifications, and Richelieu ordered the construction of an enormous seawall (dike) across the harbour entrance to prevent English relief fleets from reaching the city. The dike, built under the engineer Clément Métezeau, stretched over 1,400 metres and was completed in 1628. Three English relief expeditions led by the Duke of Buckingham and later the Earl of Denbigh and the Earl of Lindsey failed to break through. Inside the city, led by mayor Jean Guitton, the inhabitants endured extreme famine; of a population of about 27,000, only around 5,000 survived.

Aftermath

La Rochelle surrendered on 28 October 1628. Under the Peace of Alès (1629), the Huguenots retained freedom of worship under the Edict of Nantes but lost their fortified towns, armies, and political assemblies. The siege established the supremacy of the French crown and is famously dramatized in Alexandre Dumas' novel The Three Musketeers.
