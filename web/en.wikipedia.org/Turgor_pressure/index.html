Turgor pressure

Turgor pressure is the force within a cell that pushes the plasma membrane against the cell wall. It is caused by the osmotic flow of water from an area of lower solute concentration outside the cell into the cell's vacuole, which has a higher solute concentration. Turgor pressure is essential in plants: it keeps non-woody plants upright, drives cell enlargement during growth, and powers the opening and closing of stomata through changes in guard-cell turgor.

Mechanism

When a plant cell is placed in a hypotonic solution, water enters by osmosis and the vacuole swells, pressing the cytoplasm against the rigid cell wall. The wall resists expansion, generating a hydrostatic pressure — the turgor pressure — that can reach several atmospheres. A cell in this state is described as turgid. If water is lost, the cell becomes flaccid and the plant wilts; in strongly hypertonic conditions the membrane pulls away from the wall in a process called plasmolysis.

Functions

Beyond structural support, turgor is the immediate driving force of plant cell growth: wall loosening allows turgor to expand the cell irreversibly. Rapid turgor changes also underlie plant movements, such as the folding of Mimosa pudica leaves and the snapping of the Venus flytrap. In fungi and bacteria, turgor pressure similarly presses against cell walls and can be harnessed for penetrating host tissues.
