Double-stack rail transport

Intermodal containers stacked two high on well cars.

Double-stack rail transport is a form of intermodal freight transport in which railway wagons carry two layers of shipping containers stacked one on top of the other. Using specialised low-floor "well cars", the practice nearly doubles the capacity of a train compared with single-stack container service and greatly reduces the cost per container, though it requires generous vertical clearance under bridges and tunnels.

History

The concept was developed in the United States in the late 1970s by Southern Pacific Railroad together with SeaLand and ACF Industries; the first all-double-stack train ran in 1984 for American President Lines. After US railroad deregulation, dedicated intermodal corridors were upgraded to the roughly 6.15 m (20 ft 2 in) clearances needed.

Use worldwide

Double-stack service dominates long-distance container traffic in the United States, Canada, and Mexico, and is used in Australia and on some routes in China and India. Most of Europe cannot accommodate it because of low historic clearances; India has built dedicated freight corridors with high clearances and electrification designed around double-stack trains, using raised pantographs.

Economics

A double-stack train can carry roughly twice the containers of a conventional train with only modest extra fuel and crew cost, and it avoids the aerodynamic and handling penalties of piggyback trailer service. These savings made rail competitive for long-haul container traffic against trucking.
