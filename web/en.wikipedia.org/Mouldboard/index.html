Mouldboard

A mouldboard (American spelling: moldboard) is the curved part of a plough that receives the slice of soil cut by the share and turns it over, burying surface material such as weeds, stubble and manure while bringing fresh soil to the top. Together with the share, landside and frog, it forms the body of a conventional mouldboard plough, the implement that defined mechanised tillage from the late eighteenth century onwards.

Function

As the plough moves forward, the share cuts a horizontal slice through the soil and the shin and share point lift it. The slice then travels up and along the mouldboard, whose helical or cylindrical curvature rolls it over and drops it, inverted, against the previous furrow. The geometry of the mouldboard controls how completely the furrow is inverted and how much the soil is pulverised: long, gently twisted boards invert without breaking the slice, while short, steeply pitched boards shatter it.

Types

Traditional classifications include general-purpose boards, semi-digger boards and digger boards, distinguished by the depth and width of work for which they were designed. Slatted mouldboards, made of spaced steel strips, were developed for sticky soils in which a solid board would not scour cleanly. Materials have ranged from wood faced with iron, through chilled cast iron and soft-centre steel, to modern plastic coatings that reduce friction in wet clay.

History

Curved iron mouldboards appeared in Europe as plough design moved away from the simple ard. Thomas Jefferson designed a mathematically derived "mouldboard of least resistance" in the 1790s, and the American steel plough associated with John Deere from 1837 used a polished steel board that scoured in prairie soils where cast iron failed. The mouldboard plough remained the primary tillage tool of temperate agriculture until the late twentieth century, when reduced-tillage and no-till methods began to replace it in many regions, partly to limit erosion and moisture loss caused by full inversion.
