Treaty of Benevento
Signed18 June 1156
LocationBenevento, southern Italy
PartiesPope Adrian IV; William I of Sicily
EffectPapal recognition of William as King of Sicily; Norman–papal peace

The Treaty of Benevento was concluded on 18 June 1156 between Pope Adrian IV and King William I of Sicily. It ended the state of war between the papacy and the Norman Kingdom of Sicily and formally recognised William—hitherto treated by the papacy as a usurper—as legitimate king.

Background and terms

After the death of Roger II in 1154, Pope Adrian IV refused to recognise his son William and supported baronial revolts and a Byzantine invasion of Apulia. When William crushed the rebellion and defeated the Byzantines at Brindisi, the pope, cut off in Benevento, chose negotiation. By the treaty William acknowledged papal overlordship and promised an annual tribute, military support and respect for church liberties; in return Adrian invested him as king of Sicily, the duchy of Apulia and the principality of Capua, conceding the king unusual authority over the Sicilian church (the so-called Monarchia Sicula privileges).

Significance

The treaty stabilised the Norman kingdom's legitimacy and defined papal–Sicilian relations for decades, though disputes over its ecclesiastical clauses recurred. It also marked the papacy's abandonment of hopes of a Byzantine alliance to expel the Normans from southern Italy.
