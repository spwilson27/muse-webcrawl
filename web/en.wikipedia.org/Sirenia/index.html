Sirenia

Sirenia Order: Sirenia
Families: Dugongidae; Trichechidae
Extant species: 4
Distribution: Tropical and subtropical coastal waters and rivers

The Sirenia, commonly referred to as sea cows or sirenians, are an order of fully aquatic, herbivorous mammals that inhabit swamps, rivers, estuaries, marine wetlands and coastal marine waters. The order comprises four living species: three manatees (family Trichechidae) and the dugong (family Dugongidae). A fifth recent species, Steller's sea cow, was hunted to extinction within decades of its discovery in 1741.

Evolution

Sirenians are members of the clade Afrotheria and are the closest living relatives of elephants, with which they share the group Tethytheria. The earliest sirenians appear in the Eocene fossil record, about 50 million years ago, and the group diversified considerably during the Oligocene and Miocene before declining to its present relict diversity.

Anatomy and behaviour

Sirenians have fusiform bodies, paddle-like forelimbs, and horizontal tail flukes — rounded in the dugong's relatives manatees bear paddle-shaped tails, while the dugong has a fluked, whale-like tail. They possess exceptionally dense bones that act as ballast, helping them remain submerged while feeding. Sirenians are slow-moving grazers that feed almost entirely on seagrasses and other aquatic vegetation, consuming up to ten per cent of their body weight daily.

Conservation

All four living species are classified as Vulnerable by the IUCN. Threats include habitat loss, collisions with watercraft, entanglement in fishing gear, pollution and hunting. Manatees are protected under legislation such as the United States Marine Mammal Protection Act, and dugongs are protected across much of their range in the Indo-Pacific.
