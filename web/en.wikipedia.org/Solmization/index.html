Solmization

Solmization is a system of attributing a distinct syllable to each note of a musical scale. Various forms of solmization are in use worldwide; the best known in the West assigns the syllables do, re, mi, fa, sol, la, ti (or si) to the degrees of the major scale — a system often called solfège.

History

The Western system derives from the eleventh-century Benedictine monk Guido d'Arezzo, who took the syllables ut, re, mi, fa, sol, la from the initial syllables of the half-lines of the Latin hymn Ut queant laxis. Guido's syllables named the notes of overlapping six-note hexachords, and singers located notes with the aid of the "Guidonian hand". In the seventeenth century ut was largely replaced by the more singable do, and si (later ti in English-speaking countries) was added for the seventh degree, completing the octave.

Systems

In "fixed do" systems, the syllables always denote the same pitches (do = C); in "movable do" systems, do denotes the tonic of whatever key is in use, making transposition easier — the approach used in the Kodály method. Other traditions have their own solmization: Indian classical music uses sa re ga ma pa dha ni (sargam), Korean music uses its own syllables, and Byzantine chant uses pa vou ga di ke zo ni.
