Seleucia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Ruins of Seleucia on the Tigris, founded by Seleucus I Nicator around 305 BCE.

Seleucia, also known as Seleucia on the Tigris, was a major Mesopotamian city founded by Seleucus I Nicator, one of Alexander the Great's successors, around 305 BCE. Located on the west bank of the Tigris River in what is now Iraq, south of modern Baghdad, it served as the eastern capital of the Seleucid Empire and became one of the largest cities of the ancient world, with a population estimated at up to 600,000 at its height.

History

Seleucus laid out the city on a Hippodamian grid near the junction of the Tigris and a royal canal connecting to the Euphrates, making it a hub of trade between the Mediterranean, Mesopotamia, Persia, and India. Its population of Greeks, Babylonians, Jews, and Syrians made it a cosmopolitan Hellenistic metropolis. The city remained the Seleucid royal capital in the east until the court moved more permanently to Antioch, and even then it retained immense commercial importance.

The Parthians under Mithridates I captured Seleucia in 141 BCE. It prospered under Parthian rule, though the Parthian kings built their capital at Ctesiphon on the opposite bank, which gradually overshadowed it. Roman forces sacked the city twice — under Trajan in 116 CE and under Avidius Cassius in 165 CE — and Septimius Severus found it largely deserted in 198 CE. Seleucia was abandoned as the Tigris shifted course.

Excavations

The site (Tell Umar) was excavated by the University of Michigan and partners between 1927 and 1937, revealing city blocks, a large ziggurat-like temple, archives with thousands of clay sealings (bullae), pottery, coins, and glass and metal ornaments that illuminate Hellenistic and Parthian daily life.
