Sacrificial anode

A sacrificial anode on a ship's hull corrodes preferentially, protecting the steel.

A sacrificial anode, or galvanic anode, is the main component of a galvanic cathodic protection system used to protect buried or submerged metal structures from corrosion. It is made from a metal alloy with a more "active" voltage — a more negative reduction potential — than the metal of the structure it protects. The difference in potential causes the sacrificial anode to corrode preferentially, so the protected metal is preserved while the anode is consumed and must periodically be replaced.

Materials

Sacrificial anodes are generally made of zinc, magnesium, or aluminum alloys. Zinc anodes are standard for ships and seawater applications; magnesium, with a higher driving voltage, suits freshwater and buried pipelines and water-heater tanks; aluminum anodes offer greater capacity per unit weight and perform well in seawater and brackish water. Selection depends on the electrolyte's resistivity and chemistry: magnesium can over-protect and damage coatings in seawater, while zinc can passivate in hot water service.

Applications

Sacrificial anodes protect ship hulls, propellers, and rudders; offshore oil platforms and pipelines; buried steel pipelines and storage tanks; and the steel heating elements and tanks of domestic water heaters, where a magnesium or aluminum "anode rod" extends tank life. The principle was first described by Humphry Davy in the 1820s, who recommended attaching zinc or iron blocks to protect the copper sheathing of Royal Navy warships.
