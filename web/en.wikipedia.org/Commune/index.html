Commune

This article is about the general concept of a commune. For the model of government, see Commune (model of government).

A commune is an intentional community of people living together, sharing common interests, often having common values and beliefs, as well as shared property, possessions, resources, and, in some communes, work, income or assets. In addition to the communal economy, consensual decision-making, non-hierarchical structures and ecological living have become important core principles for many communes.

A communal meal in an intentional community

Definition and characteristics

The term "commune" derives from the Old French comune, originally meaning a group of people sharing a common life. Communes vary widely in size, purpose and organisation. Common features identified by sociologists include common residence, shared economic life, collective decision-making, and a shared ideology or set of values. Many communes are formed around religious, political, ecological or artistic purposes.

History

Early examples

Religious communities such as monasteries, the Hutterites, and the Shakers are among the earliest communal societies. In the 19th century, utopian socialist thinkers such as Robert Owen and Charles Fourier inspired experimental communities, including New Harmony in Indiana and numerous Fourierist phalanxes in the United States.

Twentieth century

The kibbutz movement in Palestine and later Israel became one of the most durable communal experiments. The 1960s counterculture produced a wave of communes in North America and Europe, many of them short-lived. Communes continue to exist worldwide, ranging from ecovillages and cohousing projects to religious and political communities.

Classification

Communes are often classified by their organising principle: religious communes, political communes (such as those based on anarchist or socialist principles), economic communes, and alternative-lifestyle communities such as ecovillages.
