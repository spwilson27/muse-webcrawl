Cuisine of the Southern United States

Southern cuisine
Region: Southern United States
Influences: African, Native American, English, Scots-Irish, French, Spanish
Staples: cornmeal, pork, rice, greens, fried chicken, barbecue

The cuisine of the Southern United States is the regional cooking of the American South, shaped over centuries by Native American, West African, British, Scots-Irish, French and Spanish traditions. It is defined by dishes such as fried chicken, slow-smoked barbecue, cornbread, collard and turnip greens, black-eyed peas, grits, country ham, biscuits with gravy, and pecan and sweet-potato pies, washed down with sweet iced tea.

Origins

Indigenous peoples contributed corn, beans, squash, and techniques such as hominy-making. Enslaved Africans brought okra, black-eyed peas, sorghum, sesame and rice-growing expertise, and exerted a foundational influence on dishes from gumbo to Hoppin' John — the tradition now celebrated as soul food. European settlers added pork preservation, frying in cast iron, and wheat-flour baking.

Regional sub-cuisines

  • Lowcountry (coastal South Carolina and Georgia): rice dishes such as shrimp and grits and Frogmore stew
  • Cajun and Creole (Louisiana): gumbo, jambalaya, étouffée, po'boy sandwiches
  • Appalachian: soup beans, stack cakes, ramps and preserved foods
  • Barbecue belts: distinct pork and brisket traditions in the Carolinas, Memphis, Kansas City's southern sphere and Texas
  • Tex-Mex and Gulf influences along the western and coastal edges

Modern developments

Since the late 20th century, chefs and food writers have both revived heirloom Southern ingredients — Carolina Gold rice, heritage pork, stone-ground grits — and reinterpreted the cuisine in fine-dining settings, while debates continue over crediting the African-American cooks who created much of it.
