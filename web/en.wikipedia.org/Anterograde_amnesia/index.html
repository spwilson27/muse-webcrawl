Anterograde amnesia

Anterograde amnesia is a loss of the ability to create new memories after the event that caused amnesia, leading to a partial or complete inability to recall the recent past, while long-term memories from before the event remain intact. This contrasts with retrograde amnesia, in which memories created prior to the event are lost.

Causes

The disorder is usually caused by damage to the hippocampus and other structures of the medial temporal lobe, as in Korsakoff syndrome from thiamine deficiency, Alzheimer's disease, traumatic brain injury, herpes simplex encephalitis, stroke or surgical removal of brain tissue. Benzodiazepines and other sedatives can produce a temporary drug-induced anterograde amnesia.

Notable cases

The most famous case is Henry Molaison ("Patient H.M."), whose bilateral medial temporal lobectomy in 1953 left him unable to form new declarative memories while preserving procedural learning — a finding that transformed the science of memory. Clive Wearing, a British musician affected by encephalitis, suffers from both severe anterograde and retrograde amnesia.
