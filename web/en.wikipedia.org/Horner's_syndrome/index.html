Horner's syndrome

Horner's syndrome, also known as oculosympathetic paresis, is a combination of signs caused by disruption of the sympathetic nerve supply to the eye and face. It is named after the Swiss ophthalmologist Johann Friedrich Horner, who described it in 1869, though earlier descriptions exist.

Signs

The classic triad on the affected side is ptosis (drooping of the upper eyelid), miosis (a constricted pupil), and anhidrosis (reduced sweating of the face). The eye may also appear sunken (apparent enophthalmos), and the iris may be lighter in congenital cases (heterochromia).

Causes

The sympathetic pathway runs in three segments — from the hypothalamus down to the spinal cord, up the neck beside the carotid artery, and into the skull to the eye — and a lesion anywhere along it can produce the syndrome. Causes range from stroke and tumours (such as a Pancoast tumour at the lung apex) to carotid artery dissection, which is a medical emergency, and cluster headache. In children, congenital Horner's syndrome can be associated with neuroblastoma.

Diagnosis

Pharmacological eye-drop tests (historically cocaine, now often apraclonidine) confirm the diagnosis and can help localise the lesion; imaging of the pathway follows, guided by the suspected segment.
