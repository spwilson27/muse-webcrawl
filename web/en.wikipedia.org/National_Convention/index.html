The National Convention (French: Convention nationale) was the constitutional and legislative assembly that governed France during the most radical phase of the French Revolution, from September 1792 to October 1795. Elected by universal male suffrage after the insurrection of 10 August 1792 suspended King Louis XVI, its first act was to abolish the monarchy and proclaim the French Republic on 21–22 September 1792.

Course of the Convention

The Convention tried Louis XVI for treason and voted for his execution; he was guillotined on 21 January 1793. Dominated successively by the Girondins and then, after their purge on 2 June 1793, by the Montagnards led by figures such as Maximilien Robespierre, Georges Danton, and Jean-Paul Marat, the Convention governed through the Committee of Public Safety during the Reign of Terror (1793–94), in which tens of thousands were executed or died in custody. Facing foreign invasion and civil war in the Vendée, it decreed the levée en masse, price controls (the Law of the Maximum), and revolutionary tribunals.

The fall of Robespierre on 9 Thermidor (27 July 1794) began the Thermidorian Reaction; the Convention closed the Jacobin Club, relaxed controls, and drafted the Constitution of the Year III, dissolving itself on 26 October 1795 in favour of the Directory. Its legacy includes the abolition of slavery in the French colonies (1794), the metric system, and the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen of 1793.

Factions

Deputies sat informally as the Girondins, the Mountain (Montagnards), and the uncommitted centre known as the Plain (Marais). The Convention's shifting balance among these groups drove the Revolution's radicalisation and eventual conservative turn.
