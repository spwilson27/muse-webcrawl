Recital

A solo piano recital in a concert hall.

A recital is a concert of music performed by a single musician or a small number of performers, typically a solo instrumentalist or a singer with a piano accompanist. The term also covers performances by students — the dance recital or student music recital — and, in a separate usage, the spoken delivery of a memorised text. Recitals are a staple of concert life and of music education, where they serve both as performance training and as a form of examination.

History

The modern solo recital is largely a nineteenth-century creation. Franz Liszt is credited with inventing the solo piano recital in London in 1840: he performed alone, from memory, played a varied programme, and turned the piano sideways so the audience could see his profile — conventions that became standard. Before this, virtuosos normally appeared within mixed concerts alongside orchestras, singers and chamber groups. The word "recital" itself, borrowed from the literary sense of reciting, was initially mocked in the press but quickly adopted.

The song recital developed in parallel, centred on the German Lied repertoire of Schubert, Schumann, Brahms and Wolf. Singers such as Dietrich Fischer-Dieskau made the solo song recital a major concert genre in the twentieth century.

Formats

A typical recital programme lasts about two hours with an interval and balances major works — a sonata or song cycle — with shorter pieces. Organ recitals, often given in cathedrals and churches, form a distinct tradition with free or retiring-collection admission. In music schools and conservatoires, students give assessed degree recitals that may constitute a substantial part of their final examination; children learning instruments commonly perform in studio recitals for parents.

Dance and other recitals

In North America, "recital" also denotes the annual performance staged by a dance school, in which students of all ages perform choreographed pieces, usually in costume. Poetry recitals and dramatic recitations, once a popular form of parlour entertainment, survive in competitions and commemorative events.
