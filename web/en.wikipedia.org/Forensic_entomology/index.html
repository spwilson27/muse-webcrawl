Forensic entomology

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Forensic entomology is the application and study of insect and other arthropod biology to criminal matters. It involves the study of arthropods found on and near a corpse to estimate the post-mortem interval (PMI) — the time elapsed since death — and may also be used in cases of neglect, food contamination, and the movement of a body after death.

Methods

Blow flies (family Calliphoridae) are usually the first insects to arrive at a body, often within minutes to hours of death. Because insect development is strongly temperature-dependent, the age of the oldest larvae or pupae on the body can be calculated from accumulated degree-hours and used to estimate a minimum PMI. The predictable succession of insect communities — flies, then beetles and other taxa as decomposition proceeds — provides a second, longer-range clock. Commonly examined groups include flesh flies (Sarcophagidae), carrion beetles (Silphidae), and cheese skippers.

History

One of the earliest documented cases comes from 13th-century China, recorded in the legal text Collected Cases of Injustice Rectified (Xi Yuan Ji Lu, 1248), where a murderer's sickle was identified when blow flies gathered on it, attracted by traces of blood. In the modern era, the French physician Louis François Étienne Bergeret used insect evidence in an 1855 case, and the field was popularised in the late 19th century by Jean Pierre Mégnin's work on faunal succession on corpses.

Branches

Forensic entomology is usually divided into medico-legal (medico-criminal) entomology, urban entomology (pests affecting people and buildings in legal disputes), and stored-product entomology (insect infestation of food and commodities).
