Laplace–Runge–Lenz vector

In classical mechanics, the Laplace–Runge–Lenz (LRL) vector is a vector used chiefly to describe the shape and orientation of the orbit of one astronomical body around another, such as a planet revolving around a star. For the two-body problem with an inverse-square central force (the Kepler problem), the LRL vector is a constant of the motion: it is conserved throughout the orbit.

Definition

For a particle of mass m moving under a central force with potential −k/r, the LRL vector is defined as A = p × L − mkr̂, where p is the momentum and L the angular momentum. The vector A points from the center of force toward the periapsis of the orbit, and its magnitude equals mk times the orbital eccentricity.

Significance

Conservation of the LRL vector explains why Keplerian orbits are closed ellipses that do not precess: the fixed direction of A pins the orientation of the ellipse's major axis. Its existence reflects a "hidden" SO(4) symmetry of the bound Kepler problem, beyond the obvious rotational symmetry. In quantum mechanics, Wolfgang Pauli used the LRL vector in 1926 to derive the energy spectrum of the hydrogen atom algebraically, before the Schrödinger-equation solution appeared; the vector is named after Pierre-Simon Laplace, Carl Runge, and Wilhelm Lenz.
