Absorption (electromagnetic radiation)

Related quantities: Attenuation coefficient, absorbance, absorptance

Governing law: Beer–Lambert law

In physics, absorption of electromagnetic radiation is the process by which the energy of a photon is taken up by matter, typically the electrons of an atom, converting radiant energy into internal energy of the absorber — for example, heat. A material that absorbs all radiation falling on it across the visible spectrum appears black.

Mechanisms

Absorption occurs when photon energy matches a transition available in the material: electrons move to higher energy states, or molecules are excited into higher rotational and vibrational modes. Each material therefore absorbs selectively, producing characteristic absorption spectra used in spectroscopy to identify substances — from the Fraunhofer lines in the solar spectrum to the infrared bands by which greenhouse gases absorb outgoing radiation.

Quantitative measures

The decrease of intensity through a medium is described by the Beer–Lambert law, in which transmitted intensity falls exponentially with path length and absorber concentration. Absorptance is the fraction of incident radiation absorbed, while absorbance (optical density) is its logarithmic measure widely used in analytical chemistry. The imaginary part of a material's complex refractive index quantifies its absorption per unit distance.

Applications

Absorption underlies photography, solar cells, optical filters, laser physics, remote sensing and radiative transfer in the atmosphere and stars. Dark surfaces, pigments and sunscreens all function by controlled absorption of particular wavelengths.
