Shaped charge

A shaped charge is an explosive charge shaped to focus the effect of the explosion's energy. Different types are used to cut and form metal, initiate nuclear weapons, penetrate armour, and perforate oil and gas wells. A typical modern lined shaped charge can penetrate armour steel many times the charge's diameter.

Principle

The key mechanism is the Munroe or Neumann effect: a hollow cavity in the explosive, especially one lined with a thin metal cone (often copper), collapses on detonation and extrudes a hypervelocity jet of liner material. Jet tip speeds can exceed 7–9 km/s, generating pressures that cause armour to behave like a fluid and be displaced radially, allowing deep penetration.

History

The effect was noted by Charles E. Munroe in 1888 and developed by Egon Neumann in Germany around 1910. Shaped charges were used extensively in the Second World War in weapons such as the bazooka, Panzerfaust, and rifle grenades, and remain central to anti-tank guided missiles and RPG warheads using HEAT (high-explosive anti-tank) designs.

Civilian uses

In the petroleum industry, small shaped charges perforate well casings to allow oil and gas to flow. Linear shaped charges are used in demolition and in aircraft escape systems to cut canopies and sever cables.
