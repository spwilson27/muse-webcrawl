Laramide orogeny

Laramide orogeny
WhenLate Cretaceous – Paleogene, ~80–55 million years ago
WhereWestern North America (Rocky Mountains)
CauseLow-angle (flat-slab) subduction of the Farallon Plate
Named forLaramie Range, Wyoming

The Laramide orogeny was a period of mountain building in western North America that began in the Late Cretaceous, about 80 million years ago, and ended roughly 55–35 million years ago in the Paleogene. It produced the Rocky Mountains, from British Columbia and Montana south through the United States to northern Mexico, in a series of discrete basement-cored uplifts separated by subsiding basins.

Geology

Unlike the thin-skinned thrusting of the earlier Sevier orogeny to its west, Laramide deformation involved thick slabs of ancient Precambrian basement rock pushed up along steep reverse faults, creating ranges such as the Wind River, Bighorn, and Front Ranges and the Uinta Mountains. The leading explanation is "flat-slab" subduction: the Farallon Plate, possibly buoyed by an oceanic plateau, slid beneath North America at an unusually shallow angle, transmitting compressional stresses far inland and shutting off the volcanic arc in between. As the slab later rolled back and steepened, magmatism swept back westward during the mid-Tertiary ignimbrite flare-up.

Effects

The orogeny coincided with the final retreat of the Western Interior Seaway, which had split North America during the Cretaceous. The intermontane basins between the new ranges filled with sediments that preserved rich fossil records of the end-Cretaceous extinction and the early Cenozoic radiation of mammals.
