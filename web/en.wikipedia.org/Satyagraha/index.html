Satyagraha

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Satyagraha (Sanskrit: सत्याग्रह, from satya, "truth," and āgraha, "insistence" or "holding firmly to") is a philosophy and practice of nonviolent resistance developed by Mahatma Gandhi. Gandhi coined the term in South Africa in 1906–1908 to distinguish his method from "passive resistance," which he considered a weapon of the weak; satyagraha, by contrast, was "truth-force" or "soul-force," a method of the strong aimed at converting, not coercing, the opponent.

Principles

Gandhi held that satyagraha rests on three pillars: truth (satya), nonviolence (ahimsa), and self-suffering (tapasya). A satyagrahi seeks to win over the opponent through voluntary suffering and moral example rather than inflicting harm; the goal is reconciliation and the realization of truth by both sides, not victory over an enemy. Practitioners take vows of truthfulness, non-possession, fearlessness, and discipline. Campaigns typically follow a sequence of negotiation and arbitration, preparation of the group, agitation, issuing an ultimatum, economic boycott or strike, civil disobedience, and finally non-cooperation with the regime.

History

Gandhi first developed satyagraha during the Indian community's struggle against discriminatory registration laws in the Transvaal, South Africa (1906–1914). In India, he led the Champaran (1917) and Kheda (1918) campaigns, the nationwide Non-cooperation movement (1920–22), the Salt March and Civil Disobedience movement (1930–34), and the Quit India Movement (1942), which together built the mass basis of Indian independence in 1947.

Influence

Satyagraha deeply influenced later movements for justice, including Martin Luther King Jr. and the American civil rights movement, Nelson Mandela and the anti-apartheid struggle in South Africa, and numerous nonviolent movements worldwide. Political scientist Gene Sharp systematized many of its techniques in his writings on nonviolent action.
