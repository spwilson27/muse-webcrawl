Cult of personality

A cult of personality, or cult of the leader, is the result of an effort to create an idealized and heroic image of a leader by a government, often through unquestioning flattery and praise. Historically, it has developed through techniques of mass media, propaganda, spectacle, the arts, patriotism, and government-organized demonstrations and rallies. A cult of personality is similar to apotheosis, except that it is established by modern social engineering techniques, usually by the state or the party in one-party states and dominant-party states.

Terminology

The term "cult of personality" was popularized by Soviet leader Nikita Khrushchev in his "Secret Speech" of 25 February 1956 to the 20th Congress of the Communist Party of the Soviet Union, in which he condemned the glorification of his predecessor, Joseph Stalin. The concept has older precedents: the imperial cults of Rome, the divine right of kings, and the charismatic authority described by sociologist Max Weber all supplied elements later assembled by modern propaganda states.

Characteristics

Cults of personality typically feature the leader's image on posters, statues, buildings and currency; honorific titles and superlatives; attribution of the nation's achievements to the leader's genius; mandatory study of the leader's writings; and suppression or rewriting of history that contradicts the official image. The cult often extends to the leader's family and outlives the leader, as in North Korea, where Kim Il-sung remains "Eternal President" decades after his death.

Examples

Twentieth-century examples include Stalin in the Soviet Union, Adolf Hitler in Nazi Germany, Mao Zedong in China, Benito Mussolini in Italy, Francisco Franco in Spain, and the Kim dynasty in North Korea. Saparmurat Niyazov of Turkmenistan built one of the most elaborate modern cults, renaming months after himself and his family and making his book, the Ruhnama, compulsory reading. Saddam Hussein in Iraq and Nicolae Ceaușescu in Romania maintained extensive cults until their violent falls. Analysts debate whether similar dynamics appear in democracies, where celebrity, media concentration and populism can elevate leaders above institutions, though typically without state monopoly on information.

Purpose and effects

Cults of personality serve to legitimize authoritarian rule, personalize the state, deter dissent, and simplify political loyalty into devotion to one figure. They distort policymaking, because subordinates report what the leader wishes to hear, and they create succession crises, since the cult is rarely transferable to a successor.
