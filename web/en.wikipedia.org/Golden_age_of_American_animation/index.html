Golden age of American animation

The golden age of American animation is the period in United States animation history from the late 1920s, with the advent of synchronised sound cartoons, through the 1960s, when theatrical animated shorts began losing popularity to television animation. It is characterised by lavishly produced cel-animated shorts and features from major Hollywood studios.

Studios and milestones

Walt Disney's Steamboat Willie (1928) launched the sound era, and Snow White and the Seven Dwarfs (1937) proved the viability of the animated feature. Warner Bros.' Termite Terrace produced Bugs Bunny, Daffy Duck, and Porky Pig under directors such as Tex Avery, Chuck Jones, and Friz Freleng; MGM's unit gave Tom and Jerry and the work of Hanna and Barbera; other major contributors included the Fleischer Studios (Betty Boop, Popeye, Superman serials), Walter Lantz, and the stylised, modernist films of UPA.

Decline

Rising costs, the 1948 Paramount Decrees ending block booking, and the rise of television eroded the theatrical short. Disney's features continued, but by the early 1960s most studios had closed their shorts units, and limited television animation dominated — giving way eventually to the "renaissance age" beginning in the late 1980s.
