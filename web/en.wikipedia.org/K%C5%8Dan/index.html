Kōan

A kōan (Japanese: 公案; Chinese: 公案, gōng'àn) is a story, dialogue, question or statement used in Zen Buddhist practice to provoke the "great doubt" and to test a student's progress. Kōans often appear paradoxical or inaccessible to rational thought — a famous example asks, "What is the sound of one hand clapping?"

Origins

The term derives from Chinese legal language for a precedent-setting public case. In Song-dynasty China, recorded encounters between Chan masters and students were compiled into collections and used as objects of meditation and instruction. In Japan, systematic kōan study became the hallmark of the Rinzai school, especially after Hakuin Ekaku (1686–1769) reorganised the curriculum.

Practice

A teacher assigns a student a kōan, such as Zhaozhou's "Mu" in response to "Does a dog have Buddha-nature?", and the student meditates on it intensively, presenting their understanding in private interviews (dokusan). The aim is not a verbal solution but a breakthrough experience (kenshō or satori) that exhausts discursive reasoning. The Sōtō school, following Dōgen, emphasised shikantaza ("just sitting") and was historically more ambivalent about formal kōan introspection.
