Occupational exposure limit

An occupational exposure limit (OEL) is an upper limit on the acceptable concentration of a hazardous substance in workplace air for a particular material or class of materials. It is typically set by competent national authorities and enforced by legislation to protect occupational health. OELs are a tool of risk assessment and control of exposure to hazardous substances.

Types

Limits are usually expressed as an eight-hour time-weighted average (TWA), a short-term exposure limit (STEL) averaged over fifteen minutes, and a ceiling value that must never be exceeded. In the United States, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration sets legally enforceable permissible exposure limits (PELs), while the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health publishes recommended exposure limits (RELs) and ACGIH publishes threshold limit values (TLVs).

Setting limits

OELs are derived from toxicological and epidemiological evidence, with uncertainty factors applied for sensitive populations and gaps in data. For many older limits, feasibility of measurement and control technology also influenced the value, so limits for the same substance can differ between jurisdictions.

Application

Employers demonstrate compliance through workplace air monitoring and compare results with the applicable OEL. Where exposure approaches or exceeds the limit, controls following the hierarchy of controls — elimination, substitution, engineering controls, administrative controls and personal protective equipment — are required.
