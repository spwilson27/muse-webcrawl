Tell es-Sultan

Tell es-Sultan, also known as Tell Sultan, is an archaeological site and tell located in the city of Jericho in the Palestinian territories of the West Bank, about 2 kilometres (1.2 mi) north of the modern city's centre. The tell is the site of ancient Jericho and is one of the oldest continuously settled places on Earth, with evidence of occupation reaching back to the Epipaleolithic period, around 10,000 BCE. In 2010 it was inscribed on UNESCO's tentative list, and Jericho was inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 2023.

Location and description

The mound lies west of the modern town, near the spring of Ein es-Sultan (Elisha's Spring), whose abundant water made settlement possible in the dry Jordan Valley. The site sits roughly 258 metres (846 ft) below sea level, making Jericho's claim to be the lowest permanently inhabited city on Earth. The tell measures about 400 by 200 metres (1,300 by 660 ft) and preserves some 20 successive layers of settlement accumulated over thousands of years.

History of settlement

The earliest inhabitants were Natufian hunter-gatherers who camped by the spring around 10,000 BCE. During the Pre-Pottery Neolithic A period (c. 8500–7500 BCE) the settlement grew into one of the world's first towns, protected by a massive stone wall and the famous Tower of Jericho, a structure about 8.5 metres (28 ft) tall with an internal staircase of 22 stone steps. Farming of wheat and barley and the domestication of animals supported a population estimated in the thousands. Later phases include Bronze Age Canaanite cities, whose great ramparts and Middle Bronze Age tombs furnished rich finds, and a modest Iron Age and later occupation.

Excavations

The tell was first explored by Charles Warren in 1867–68 for the Palestine Exploration Fund. Major excavations were conducted by an Austro-German expedition under Ernst Sellin and Carl Watzinger (1907–1909), by John Garstang (1930–1936), and most famously by Kathleen Kenyon (1952–1958), whose careful stratigraphy transformed understanding of the Neolithic sequence and dated the great tower to the Neolithic. Kenyon's work also concluded that the Bronze Age city was destroyed centuries before the period traditionally associated with the biblical conquest. A joint Italian-Palestinian expedition from Sapienza University of Rome and the Palestinian Department of Antiquities has worked at the site since 1997.

Significance

Tell es-Sultan illustrates the transition from hunting and gathering to sedentary farming life and is frequently described as one of the oldest towns in the world. The tower, walls, plastered human skulls from the Pre-Pottery Neolithic B, and sequence of domestic architecture make it a key reference site for the archaeology of the Levant.
