Münster rebellion

Date1534–1535
LocationMünster, Westphalia
LeadersJan Matthys; John of Leiden
OutcomeRebellion crushed by the Prince-Bishop's forces

The Münster rebellion (German: Täuferreich von Münster, "Anabaptist dominion of Münster") was an attempt by radical Anabaptists to establish a communal, theocratic New Jerusalem in the German city of Münster during 1534–1535. It remains one of the most notorious episodes of the Radical Reformation.

Course of events

Under the preaching of Bernhard Rothmann, Münster moved toward radical reform, and in early 1534 Anabaptist emigrants flooded in. The prophet Jan Matthys proclaimed the city the New Jerusalem; after his death in a sortie against the besieging army of the Prince-Bishop Franz von Waldeck, leadership passed to John of Leiden (Jan van Leiden), who had himself crowned king, instituted community of goods, and — citing Old Testament precedent — legalised polygamy, taking sixteen wives. Money was abolished, books other than the Bible were burned, and non-believers were expelled.

Fall and aftermath

After a sixteen-month siege, the city was betrayed and stormed on the night of 24–25 June 1535. John of Leiden and two associates were tortured and executed in January 1536; their bodies were displayed in three iron cages hung from the tower of St. Lambert's Church, where the cages remain to this day. The catastrophe discredited revolutionary Anabaptism and pushed the surviving movement toward the pacifism associated with Menno Simons.
