Cable television in the United States

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Cable television in the United States developed from the late 1940s as community antenna systems bringing broadcast signals to areas with poor reception, and grew into one of the country's dominant media industries, at its peak serving the large majority of American television households and spawning hundreds of national networks.

Growth and deregulation

The industry expanded rapidly after satellite distribution of programming began in the 1970s and the Cable Communications Policy Act of 1984 deregulated rates. HBO pioneered pay cable and satellite delivery; ESPN, CNN, MTV and Nickelodeon defined the made-for-cable network era. The Telecommunications Act of 1996 reshaped competition between cable, telephone and satellite companies. Major operators consolidated into giants such as Comcast and Charter Communications (Spectrum).

Regulation

Cable systems are generally franchised by local authorities and regulated federally by the FCC. Rules have covered must-carry and retransmission consent for local broadcast stations, channel capacity, leased access, and, in recurring political debates, à la carte pricing and net neutrality for cable broadband.

Decline and transition

US cable television households peaked at roughly 100 million in the early 2000s and have since fallen sharply as subscribers cut the cord in favour of streaming; operators have refocused their businesses on broadband internet, where cable's hybrid fibre-coaxial networks carry most American fixed-line connections.
