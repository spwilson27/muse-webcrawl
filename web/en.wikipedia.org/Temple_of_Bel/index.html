Temple of Bel

Temple of Bel
Location: Palmyra, Syria
Dedicated: 32 CE
Deity: Bel (with Yarhibol and Aglibol)
Destroyed: August 2015

The Temple of Bel (Arabic: معبد بل) was an ancient religious building in Palmyra, Syria, dedicated in 32 CE to the Mesopotamian god Bel, worshipped at Palmyra together with the lunar god Aglibol and the sun god Yarhibol. A centre of religious life in the caravan city, it was regarded as one of the best-preserved and most important temples of the ancient Near East. The temple was destroyed by the Islamic State (ISIL) in August 2015 during the Syrian civil war.

History

The temple stood on a site occupied by earlier sanctuaries going back to the third millennium BCE. The main building was dedicated on 6 April 32 CE; construction continued through the 1st and 2nd centuries CE. The temple was built within a vast, roughly square temenos (sacred precinct) surrounded by colonnades, entered through a monumental propylaeum. During the Byzantine period the cella was converted into a church, and in the Islamic era the precinct became a fortified village, with a mosque within the cella.

Architecture

The temple combined Near Eastern and Greco-Roman elements. Externally it resembled a classical temple, with Corinthian columns, but the interior cella had two adyta (cult niches) at either end rather than one, and the roof terraces were reached by stair towers. The northern adyton's monolithic ceiling was carved with a relief showing the seven planetary gods surrounded by the signs of the zodiac, and the beam reliefs depicted processions and sacrifices, including a famous carved procession of camels and veiled women. The main altar stood in the courtyard to the northwest of the cella.

Destruction

After capturing Palmyra in May 2015, ISIL destroyed several monuments at the site. On 30 August 2015 it was reported that the Temple of Bel had been blown up; satellite imagery analysed by the United Nations confirmed the main building's destruction, though the entrance arch and the surrounding colonnade survived. UNESCO described the act as a war crime and a loss to humanity. The site's head of antiquities, Khaled al-Asaad, had been murdered by ISIL earlier that month.
