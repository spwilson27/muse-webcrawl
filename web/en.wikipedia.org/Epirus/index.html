Epirus

Epirus (Greek: Ήπειρος, Ípiros, "mainland") is a geographical and historical region in southeastern Europe, now shared between Greece and Albania. The modern Greek region of Epirus, with its capital at Ioannina, occupies the southwestern part; northern Epirus lies within southern Albania. Rugged and mountainous, dominated by the Pindus range, it borders the Ionian Sea to the west.

Ancient history

In antiquity Epirus was inhabited by Greek-speaking tribes — the Chaonians, Molossians and Thesprotians — and hosted the great oracle of Zeus at Dodona, among the oldest in Greece. United under the Aeacid dynasty of the Molossians, Epirus rose to prominence under King Pyrrhus (319/318–272 BC), whose costly victories over the Romans in Italy gave rise to the expression "Pyrrhic victory". Rome conquered the region in 167 BC, after which it became part of the province of Macedonia and later a province in its own right.

Medieval and modern history

After 1204, Epirus became the seat of the Despotate of Epirus, a Byzantine successor state. The region later fell under Serbian and then Ottoman rule, lasting into the Balkan Wars of 1912–13, when the south was incorporated into Greece; the north was assigned to Albania, and the status of the Greek minority there remained contentious through the twentieth century — notably during the era of Ali Pasha of Ioannina, the Ottoman Albanian ruler who dominated the region until 1822.

Geography

The Vikos Gorge in the Pindus is among the deepest gorges in the world relative to its width; Lake Pamvotida lies beside Ioannina. Epirus is one of the least densely populated regions of Greece, known for its stone-built villages of Zagori and its traditional polyphonic singing.
