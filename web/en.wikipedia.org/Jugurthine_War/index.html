Jugurthine War

The Jugurthine War (Latin: Bellum Iugurthinum; 112–106 BC) was an armed conflict between the Roman Republic and Jugurtha, king of Numidia in North Africa. Although Rome ultimately prevailed, the war exposed corruption in the Roman Senate — Jugurtha famously bribed senators and tribunes — and it brought prominence to the generals Gaius Marius and Lucius Cornelius Sulla, whose rivalry foreshadowed the civil wars that ended the Republic. The war is the subject of the monograph Bellum Iugurthinum by the historian Sallust.

Background

Jugurtha, the illegitimate grandson of Masinissa, was adopted by King Micipsa and designated joint heir with Micipsa's sons Hiempsal and Adherbal. Jugurtha murdered Hiempsal and, after a civil war, besieged Adherbal at Cirta, killing him along with Italian merchants in 112 BC — an outrage that forced Rome to declare war. Jugurtha's skill at bribery secured favourable terms from successive Roman commissions and commanders, prompting his reputed parting remark that Rome was "a city for sale, and doomed to perish if it finds a buyer."

Course of the war

Early Roman efforts were ineffectual, culminating in the humiliating surrender of an army under Aulus Postumius Albinus at Suthul in 110 BC. Quintus Caecilius Metellus restored discipline and won a hard-fought victory at the River Muthul (109 BC), but the war dragged on. His legate Gaius Marius, having been elected consul, took command in 107 BC, captured Capsa and other strongholds, and defeated the combined forces of Jugurtha and his father-in-law Bocchus I of Mauretania at the Second Battle of Cirta (106 BC). Marius's quaestor Sulla then negotiated Bocchus's betrayal of Jugurtha, who was delivered to the Romans in chains.

Aftermath

Jugurtha was paraded in Marius's triumph on 1 January 104 BC and died in the Tullianum prison. Numidia was reduced in size, with its western part granted to Bocchus and the remainder left to a compliant Numidian king. Marius's enrolment of propertyless volunteers during the war is traditionally associated with the "Marian reforms" of the Roman army, and the quarrel between Marius and Sulla over credit for Jugurtha's capture lit the fuse of their later civil wars.
