Ancient Egyptian funerary practices

The ancient Egyptians had an elaborate set of funerary practices that they believed were necessary to ensure immortality after death. These rituals included mummifying the body, casting magic spells, and burials with specific grave goods thought to be needed in the afterlife. The funerary process evolved over the course of Egyptian history, from the pit burials of the Predynastic period to the elaborate royal tombs of the New Kingdom.

Mummification

Mummification involved removing moisture from the body so that it dried out and retained a lifelike form. Embalmers removed the internal organs, which were preserved separately in canopic jars (with the heart, believed to be the seat of thought and feeling, left in place), packed the body with natron salt to desiccate it for about forty days, then anointed, wrapped it in linen bandages, and placed amulets within the wrappings. The process for the elite could take up to seventy days.

Funerary rituals

Key rituals included the Opening of the Mouth ceremony, performed by priests to restore the deceased's ability to speak, eat, and breathe in the afterlife. Funeral processions carried the mummy to the tomb, accompanied by mourners and offerings. Texts such as the Pyramid Texts, Coffin Texts, and the Book of the Dead provided spells to guide and protect the dead.

Judgment and the afterlife

Egyptians believed the soul's fate was decided by the Weighing of the Heart, in which the heart was balanced against the feather of Maat before Osiris; a heart heavier with sin would be devoured by the monster Ammit. Those who passed could dwell in the Field of Reeds, an idealized version of earthly life.

Tombs and grave goods

Burial monuments ranged from simple graves to mastabas, pyramids, and rock-cut tombs in the Valley of the Kings. Tombs were stocked with food, furniture, clothing, shabti figurines (to perform labour for the deceased), and other goods for the afterlife.
