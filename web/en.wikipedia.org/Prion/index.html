Prion

A prion is a misfolded protein that induces other properly folded proteins to adopt its abnormal conformation. The word derives from "proteinaceous infectious particle". Prions are unlike all other known pathogens in that they lack nucleic acid.

Discovery

In the 1960s, radiation biologist Tikvah Alper suggested that the agent responsible for the diseases scrapie and kuru might be a protein rather than a virus. Stanley B. Prusiner coined the term "prion" in 1982 and later received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1997 for his research.

Structure and propagation

The prion protein (PrP) is encoded by the PRNP gene and is normally found on cell surfaces, especially in the nervous system. The infectious form, PrPSc, is rich in beta sheets, resistant to proteases, and aggregates into amyloid fibrils. Contact between PrPSc and the normal cellular form (PrPC) catalyses conversion of the normal protein, leading to a chain reaction of misfolding.

Diseases

Prion diseases, or transmissible spongiform encephalopathies (TSEs), include Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease and kuru in humans, bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE, "mad cow disease") in cattle, scrapie in sheep, and chronic wasting disease in deer and elk. They are invariably fatal, cause spongiform degeneration of brain tissue, and have no known cure.
