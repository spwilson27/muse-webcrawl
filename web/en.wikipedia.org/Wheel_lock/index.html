Wheel lock (wheellock)

A wheel lock, or wheellock, is a friction-wheel mechanism used to ignite a firearm. It was the next major development in firearms technology after the matchlock and the first self-igniting firearm, appearing in Europe around 1500 and remaining in use, mainly for pistols and fine sporting arms, into the 17th century.

Mechanism

The lock contains a spring-loaded steel wheel with a serrated edge. Before firing, the wheel is wound with a spanner (key), storing energy in its mainspring. A piece of iron pyrite is held against the wheel in the jaws of a pivoting arm called the dog. When the trigger is pulled, the wheel spins rapidly and the pan cover slides open; the pyrite, pressed onto the spinning wheel, generates a shower of sparks that ignites the priming powder in the pan, which in turn fires the main charge through the touch hole.

History

Leonardo da Vinci sketched a wheel-lock mechanism in the Codex Atlanticus (c. 1500), and the earliest surviving wheellock firearms date from the first decade of the 16th century, produced in Germany and Italy. Because the mechanism was complex and expensive, wheellocks were chiefly weapons of cavalry and the wealthy — pistols that could be carried loaded and fired with one hand. Their reliability compared to the smouldering match made them suitable for concealed use, prompting early bans in some jurisdictions. The simpler and cheaper snaplock and then the true flintlock displaced the wheellock during the 17th century.
