Delirium tremens

Delirium tremens (DTs) is a severe form of alcohol withdrawal characterised by confusion, hallucinations, tremor and autonomic hyperactivity. It typically begins 48 to 96 hours after the last drink in a person with prolonged heavy alcohol use and is a medical emergency.

Signs and symptoms

Symptoms include agitation, disorientation, vivid visual hallucinations (classically of insects or small animals), fever, sweating, racing heart, high blood pressure and coarse tremor. The condition results from the brain's adaptation to chronic alcohol: removal of alcohol's inhibitory effect, combined with down-regulated GABA receptors and up-regulated excitatory glutamate systems, leaves the nervous system dangerously overexcited. Without treatment the mortality was historically as high as 15–40%; with modern care it falls to around 1–5%, deaths usually resulting from arrhythmia, hyperthermia or infection.

Treatment

Management centres on benzodiazepines such as diazepam or lorazepam, given in doses sufficient to control agitation, together with thiamine to prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy, correction of fluids and electrolytes, and monitoring, often in an intensive care setting. Only a minority — roughly 3–5% — of people undergoing withdrawal develop full delirium tremens.
