Grassroots


Grassroots organising begins with local volunteers rather than central leadership.

A grassroots movement is one that uses the people in a given district, region or community as the basis for a political or social movement. Grassroots movements and organizations use collective action from volunteers at the local level to implement change at the local, regional, national, or international levels. Grassroots movements are associated with bottom-up decision-making, rather than top-down, and are sometimes considered more natural or spontaneous than more traditional power structures.

Grassroots movements use a variety of strategies, from fundraising and registering voters, to simply encouraging political conversation among the citizenry and participation by ordinary people. Goals of specific movements vary and change, but the movements often take shape around advocating a particular cause, such as environmental protection, civil rights, or public health, and reflect on the whole the idea that ordinary citizens, rather than elites, drive social change.

Etymology and history

Political use of the term "grass roots" in the United States dates to the early 20th century; it was popularised around 1912, when it was said that Senator Albert Jeremiah Beveridge's party would "come from the grass roots". The metaphor draws on the roots of grass as the fundamental, self-sustaining base of a plant community — as opposed to the visible, funded "top" of an organisation. The term now applies worldwide: the concept has been credited in movements from the American civil rights movement, with its local church- and community-based organisation, to anti-colonial struggles and the environmental movement's slogan "think globally, act locally".

Techniques

Grassroots campaigns typically rely on door-to-door canvassing, community meetings, petitions, local media, volunteer phone banks, and small-donor fundraising, and increasingly on social media and online organising tools. Community organising — notably the model developed by Saul Alinsky and carried on by groups such as ACORN — systematised grassroots methods for building power in low-income communities. Barack Obama's 2008 presidential campaign is often cited as a modern large-scale application of grassroots organising techniques, as is the small-donor fundraising machinery of later campaigns.

Astroturfing

The perceived authenticity of grassroots movements makes the label valuable, giving rise to astroturfing: the practice of masking the sponsors of a message or organisation (such as a corporation or political campaign) to make it appear as though it originates from grassroots participants. Analysts and journalists attempt to distinguish genuine community-driven efforts from manufactured ones by following funding and organisational control.
