Azazel

In the Hebrew Bible, the name Azazel represents a desolate place where a scapegoat bearing the sins of the Jews was sent during Yom Kippur. During the late Second Temple period, Azazel came to be viewed as a fallen angel responsible for introducing humans to forbidden knowledge, as told in the Book of Enoch. His role as a fallen angel partly remains in Christian and Islamic traditions.

In the Hebrew Bible

In the Book of Leviticus (chapter 16), two goats are chosen on the Day of Atonement: one is sacrificed to the Lord, and the other, after the high priest lays the sins of Israel upon it, is sent into the wilderness "for Azazel". The meaning of the name is debated: it may mean "God strengthens", or be understood as "the goat that departs" — the origin of the word scapegoat.

In the Book of Enoch

In the apocryphal Book of Enoch, Azazel is one of the chief of the fallen angels, the Watchers, who descended to earth, taught men the making of swords, knives and cosmetics, and corrupted humanity. For this the archangel Raphael binds him hand and foot and casts him into a pit in the desert of Dudael, where he remains until the final judgement.

In later tradition

Rabbinic literature variously interprets Azazel as a rugged mountain or cliff from which the goat was pushed, a demon, or a name for the sacrificial rite itself. In Islam, Azazel (Azazil) is sometimes identified with the original name of Iblis before his fall. In modern literature and popular culture Azazel frequently appears as a demon or fallen angel character.
