Veto power in the United Nations Security Council

The veto power in the United Nations Security Council is the power of the five permanent members of the UN Security Council — China, France, Russia, the United Kingdom, and the United States (the "P5") — to block any substantive resolution by casting a negative vote. It derives from Article 27 of the United Nations Charter, which requires substantive decisions to be made by nine of the Council's fifteen members "including the concurring votes of the permanent members."

Origins

The veto was a condition insisted upon by the great powers at the Yalta Conference and the San Francisco Conference of 1945: the major victors of the Second World War would not join the new organisation — as they had not joined the League of Nations in force — without assurance that they could not be bound against their will. Abstention by a permanent member came to be accepted as not constituting a veto, a practice confirmed by the International Court of Justice.

Use

The Soviet Union (and later Russia) was the most frequent user in the early Cold War, with diplomat Andrei Gromyko nicknamed "Mr Nyet." The veto paralysed the Council during much of the Cold War; more recent controversial uses include vetoes concerning Syria, Ukraine, and the Israeli–Palestinian conflict. Since the Charter's adoption, the five powers have cast well over 250 vetoes in total. The United States first used its veto in 1970; the United Kingdom and France have not cast one since 1989.

Reform debate

The veto is the most contested feature of the UN system. Critics argue it renders the Council impotent in the face of atrocities and entrenches a 1945 power structure; proposals include voluntary restraint in mass-atrocity situations (the French–Mexican initiative and the ACT group's code of conduct) and various schemes of Security Council enlargement. The General Assembly's 2022 "veto initiative" requires it to meet whenever a veto is cast. Defenders reply that the veto keeps the great powers inside the system and prevents the UN from ordering enforcement it cannot sustain.
