Gay-Lussac's law

Pressure of a fixed mass of gas rises in proportion to absolute temperature at constant volume.

Gay-Lussac's law usually refers to Joseph-Louis Gay-Lussac's law of combining volumes of gases, discovered in 1808, but in modern usage it most often denotes the pressure law: for a given mass of an ideal gas kept at constant volume, the pressure is directly proportional to its absolute temperature. That is, pressure divided by temperature remains constant.

Statement

If P is the pressure of the gas and T its temperature in kelvins, then P/T = constant for fixed mass and volume. Hence if a gas at pressure P₁ and temperature T₁ is heated to T₂ at constant volume, its new pressure is P₂ = P₁ × (T₂/T₁).

Related laws

Gay-Lussac's name is also attached to the law of thermal expansion of gases, which states that all gases expand by the same fraction of their volume for a given rise in temperature, and to the law of combining volumes, which states that when gases react together they do so in volumes bearing simple whole-number ratios to one another. Together with Boyle's law and Charles's law, these empirical laws combine into the ideal gas law.
