Optimal tax

Optimal tax theory is the study of how a tax system should be designed to maximize a social welfare function subject to raising a required amount of revenue and to economic and administrative constraints. Because lump-sum taxes are generally infeasible, real taxes distort behavior; optimal tax analysis weighs the equity gains of redistribution against the efficiency losses (deadweight loss) that taxes cause.

Key results

  • Ramsey rule (1927): commodity taxes should be set in inverse proportion to the elasticity of demand, minimizing total distortion for a given revenue.
  • Mirrlees model (1971): the optimal income tax schedule balances redistribution against incentives to work, given that the government cannot observe ability directly; marginal rates need not rise monotonically with income.
  • Atkinson–Stiglitz theorem (1976): with an optimal nonlinear income tax and weak separability of preferences, differential commodity taxation is unnecessary.
  • Chamley–Judd result: in standard growth models the optimal long-run tax on capital income is zero, a finding later work has qualified considerably.

Influence and criticism

James Mirrlees and William Vickrey received the 1996 Nobel Memorial Prize in Economic Sciences for this body of work. Optimal tax results have influenced debates over progressivity, capital taxation, and tax-base design, though critics note their sensitivity to assumptions about preferences, elasticities, and the welfare weights assigned to different taxpayers.
