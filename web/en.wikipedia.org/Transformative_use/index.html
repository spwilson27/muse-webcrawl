Transformative use

Collage art is often analysed as transformative use

In United States copyright law, transformative use is a concept relevant to the fair use defence, describing a use of a copyrighted work that adds new expression, meaning or message to it, rather than merely superseding the original. The concept was introduced into fair use analysis by Judge Pierre N. Leval in his influential 1990 article "Toward a Fair Use Standard" and was adopted by the Supreme Court in Campbell v. Acuff-Rose Music, Inc. (1994).

Doctrine

Fair use under section 107 of the Copyright Act is assessed through four factors, the first being "the purpose and character of the use." In Campbell, which held that 2 Live Crew's parody of "Oh, Pretty Woman" could be fair use, the Court held that the more transformative a new work is, the less significance attaches to other factors such as commercialism. Later cases extended transformativeness to uses that copy entire works for new purposes, notably Authors Guild v. Google (2015), in which mass digitisation for search and snippet display was held highly transformative. Appropriation art cases such as Cariou v. Prince (2013) applied the doctrine broadly to altered photographs, while Andy Warhol Foundation v. Goldsmith (2023) narrowed the inquiry, holding that where a secondary use shares substantially the same purpose and commercial character as the original — there, licensing a photograph for a magazine cover — the first factor weighs against fair use despite new aesthetic expression.

Scope

Transformative use is not an independent defence but a consideration within the first fair use factor; a non-transformative use may still be fair, and a transformative one may still infringe. The doctrine has become central to disputes over parody, sampling, thumbnails and search indexing, fan works, text and data mining, and machine learning training, and it remains one of the most debated ideas in modern American copyright jurisprudence.
