Peasant

A peasant is a pre-industrial agricultural labourer or farmer with limited land ownership, especially one living in the Middle Ages under feudalism and paying rent, tax, fees, or services to a landlord. In modern usage, peasant farmers typically farm small plots of land, largely for subsistence, using family labour.

Etymology

The word derives from the French paysan, from pays ("country"), itself from the Latin pagus (a rural district) and paganus. In medieval English, the peasantry formed the great majority of the population and were divided into ranks including free tenants, villeins, and serfs, the latter bound to the lord's manor and land.

Medieval Europe

Under the manorial system, peasants owed labour services on the lord's demesne and paid various dues in cash or kind. Their obligations varied widely by region and period. Peasant discontent periodically erupted in revolts, including the French Jacquerie (1358), the English Peasants' Revolt (1381), and the German Peasants' War (1524–1525).

Peasant economies

The Russian economist Alexander Chayanov developed an influential theory of the peasant family farm, arguing that peasant households balance drudgery against the needs of family consumption rather than maximising profit. Anthropologists and historians such as James C. Scott have studied peasant subsistence ethics, everyday resistance, and rebellion.

Modern era

Peasants were central to the great revolutions of the twentieth century in Russia, China, Mexico, and Vietnam, and land reform movements across the world sought to break up large estates in their favour. Although industrialisation and urbanisation have greatly reduced peasant populations in developed countries, smallholder farmers still number in the hundreds of millions worldwide, and movements such as La Vía Campesina advocate for their rights under the banner of food sovereignty.
