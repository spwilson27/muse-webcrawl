Carried interest

A typical fund distribution waterfall, showing where carried interest is paid

Carried interest, or carry, in finance, is a share of the profits of an investment fund paid to the investment manager in excess of the proportion of the capital they contributed. It is a performance fee earned by the general partners of private equity, venture capital and hedge funds, designed to align the manager's interests with those of the fund's investors (the limited partners).

Structure

The standard arrangement in private equity is known as "two and twenty": an annual management fee of about 2 per cent of committed capital, plus carried interest of 20 per cent of profits. Carried interest is usually paid only after limited partners have received back their contributed capital plus a preferred return or "hurdle rate", commonly 8 per cent; a "catch-up" provision then accelerates payments to the general partner until the 80/20 split of total profits is reached, and excess payments may be subject to "clawback" if later investments lose money.

History

The term originates with the ship captains of early modern Europe, who were said to carry a share — often a fifth — of a cargo's profits. It became standard in the venture capital partnerships formed in Silicon Valley in the 1970s and 1980s and spread throughout alternative asset management as the industry grew. The typical structures are computed deal-by-deal (the "American waterfall") or on the fund as a whole (the "European waterfall").

Tax controversy

Carried interest has been politically controversial, chiefly in the United States, because it is generally taxed as a capital gain rather than as ordinary income, producing a lower effective tax rate for fund managers than for most wage earners. Critics, including prominent investors and politicians, describe this as an unjustified loophole; defenders argue the carry is a genuine entrepreneurial profit share on capital at risk. Successive US administrations have proposed closing the treatment, and the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 lengthened the required holding period to three years for the preferential rate. Similar debates occur in the United Kingdom, where carried interest is also taxed under capital gains rules following reforms of the 2020s.
