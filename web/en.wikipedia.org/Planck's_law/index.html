Planck's law (also Planck's radiation law) describes the spectral density of electromagnetic radiation emitted by a black body in thermal equilibrium at a given temperature T. Proposed by Max Planck in 1900, the law resolved the "ultraviolet catastrophe" of classical physics and, by introducing energy quantisation, marked the birth of quantum mechanics.

The law

The spectral radiance of a black body per unit wavelength is

Bλ(λ, T) = (2hc²/λ⁵) · 1/(ehc/(λkBT) − 1)

where h is the Planck constant, c the speed of light, and kB the Boltzmann constant. An equivalent form gives the radiance per unit frequency. The law predicts a spectrum that rises steeply at short wavelengths, reaches a single maximum, and falls off at long wavelengths.

Derived laws

Planck's law contains earlier empirical results as limiting cases and consequences. At long wavelengths or high temperatures it reduces to the Rayleigh–Jeans law; at short wavelengths it approximates Wien's approximation. The wavelength of peak emission obeys Wien's displacement law (λmaxT ≈ 2.898×10−3 m·K), and integrating over all wavelengths yields the Stefan–Boltzmann law, with total emission proportional to T⁴.

History

By 1900 precision measurements of cavity radiation, notably by Rubens and Kurlbaum, showed that Wien's law failed at long wavelengths. Planck interpolated between the Wien and Rayleigh–Jeans limits and presented his formula in October 1900, then in December 1900 gave a derivation assuming that oscillator energies come in discrete quanta E = hν — a step he initially regarded as a mathematical device. Einstein's 1905 explanation of the photoelectric effect made the quantum hypothesis physical reality.

Applications

Planck's law underlies pyrometry and remote temperature sensing, models stellar spectra and the cosmic microwave background (measured by COBE to match a 2.725 K black body almost perfectly), and is fundamental to climate models of radiative transfer.
