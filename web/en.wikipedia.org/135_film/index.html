135 film, more commonly referred to as 35 mm film, is a format of photographic film with a film gauge of 35 mm (1.4 in), loaded into a standardized type of magazine — also referred to as a cassette or cartridge — for use in 135 film cameras. The term 135 was introduced by Kodak in 1934 as a designation for 35 mm film specifically for still photography, perforated with a standard gauge.

The format quickly grew in popularity, surpassing 120 film to become the most common photographic film size by the 1960s. Despite competition from formats such as 828, 126, 110, and APS, it remained the dominant still-photography film format into the 2000s, when digital photography displaced it in the consumer market. 135 film continues to be manufactured by Kodak, Fujifilm, Ilford and others for a persistent community of film photographers.

Characteristics

Individual rolls of 135 film are em-packed in a single-spool, metal, felt-lipped cartridge which must be loaded into the camera in daylight. The film is fixed to the spool so it can be wound back into the cartridge after exposure. The standard image format is 36 × 24 mm, occupying a length of 38 mm of film per frame (eight perforations, or sprocket holes, per frame), giving a customary maximum of 36 exposures on a standard roll. Half-frame cameras such as the Olympus Pen expose 18 × 24 mm frames and double the exposure count.

Since the 1980s, cartridges carry DX encoding — patterns of conductive and insulating squares on the cartridge and a barcode — that communicate film speed, length and exposure tolerance to suitably equipped cameras and processing machinery.

History

35 mm film stock was created by William Dickson for Edison's motion picture cameras in the 1890s. Oskar Barnack's Leica camera of 1913–1914 adapted the movie stock for still photography. Kodak's 1934 introduction of pre-loaded daylight cartridges (the designation "135") and affordable cameras such as the Kodak Retina made the format practical for the mass market. After the Second World War, Japanese manufacturers including Nikon and Canon made 135 the standard for professional 35 mm rangefinder and SLR systems.
