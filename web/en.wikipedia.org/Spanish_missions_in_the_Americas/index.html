Spanish missions in the Americas

The Spanish missions in the Americas were Catholic missions established by the Spanish Empire across the Americas from the sixteenth to the nineteenth centuries. Run principally by the Franciscan, Jesuit, Dominican and Augustinian orders, they aimed to convert the indigenous peoples to Christianity, teach them European agriculture and crafts, and extend and secure the frontiers of Spanish colonial territory.

Organisation

Missions were usually founded in pairs of settlements — a mission church and community, often guarded by a nearby presidio (fort) and accompanied by a civil pueblo. Indigenous residents (neophytes) were gathered into mission settlements in a policy known as reducción, where they lived under the friars' supervision, received religious instruction and worked communal lands and workshops. The system was funded by the Spanish Crown, which regarded evangelisation as both a duty and a justification of empire deriving from the papal grants of 1493.

Regional systems

Early missions spread through the Caribbean and central Mexico; later chains extended to Florida, New Mexico, Texas, the Californias, Arizona and Sonora, to the Amazon and Paraguay — where the Jesuit reductions among the Guaraní became famous — and to Chiloé and other frontier zones of South America. In the present-day United States, the 21 missions of Alta California (1769–1833) are the best known.

Effects and criticism

Mission populations suffered devastating epidemics of European diseases and often harsh discipline; many missions were abandoned after revolts such as the Pueblo Revolt of 1680. Following independence, the Latin American republics secularised the missions and sold or granted their lands. Historians and indigenous communities continue to debate the missions' balance of cultural preservation and coercion; several mission complexes are now UNESCO World Heritage sites.
