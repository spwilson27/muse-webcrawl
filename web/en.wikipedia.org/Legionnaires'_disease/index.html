Legionnaires' disease

Legionnaires' disease is an atypical pneumonia caused by bacteria of the genus Legionella, most commonly Legionella pneumophila. Signs and symptoms include cough, shortness of breath, high fever, muscle pains, headaches, nausea and diarrhea. The disease usually begins two to ten days after exposure. A milder, self-limited flu-like form of legionellosis without pneumonia is called Pontiac fever. Legionnaires' disease acquired its name from the 1976 outbreak among attendees of an American Legion convention in Philadelphia, in which 182 people fell ill and 29 died, leading to the identification of the bacterium.

Cause and transmission

Legionella bacteria live naturally in fresh water and multiply inside amoebae and in biofilms of human-made water systems — cooling towers, hot tubs, showerheads, decorative fountains, hotel and hospital plumbing — especially at temperatures between 25 and 45 °C. People become infected by inhaling fine droplets (aerosols) containing the bacteria; the disease is essentially never transmitted from person to person.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually by urinary antigen test or culture of respiratory secretions on specialized media. Treatment is with antibiotics that penetrate cells, such as azithromycin or levofloxacin; beta-lactam antibiotics are ineffective because the bacterium lives intracellularly. Prompt treatment greatly reduces mortality.

History

In July 1976, during the American Bicentennial, members of the American Legion attending a convention at Philadelphia's Bellevue-Stratford Hotel developed a mysterious pneumonia; six months later CDC investigators Joseph McDade and Charles Shepard identified the causative bacterium, traced to the hotel's cooling tower. Retrospective investigation linked the same organism to earlier unexplained outbreaks, including a 1968 illness at a health department in Pontiac, Michigan (Pontiac fever), and a large 1999 outbreak in the Netherlands traced to a whirlpool spa at a flower show.

Prevention

Prevention centers on water-system management: maintaining hot water above 60 °C and cold water below 20 °C where feasible, disinfecting cooling towers, flushing little-used outlets, and implementing formal water management programs in hospitals, hotels and other large buildings. Reported cases have been rising in the United States and Europe — about 10,000 cases were reported in the U.S. in 2018 — partly due to better detection as well as aging infrastructure and populations.
