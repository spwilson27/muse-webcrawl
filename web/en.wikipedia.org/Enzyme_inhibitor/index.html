Enzyme inhibitor

An enzyme inhibitor is a molecule that binds to an enzyme and decreases its activity. Inhibition is a fundamental mechanism of biological regulation — cells use inhibitors (often the end products of metabolic pathways, in feedback inhibition) to control enzyme activity — and many drugs and poisons act by inhibiting specific enzymes.

Types of inhibition

  • Competitive inhibition — the inhibitor resembles the substrate and competes for the active site; it can be overcome by raising substrate concentration. It raises apparent Km but does not change Vmax.
  • Non-competitive inhibition — the inhibitor binds a separate (allosteric) site and reduces activity regardless of substrate binding; it lowers Vmax.
  • Uncompetitive inhibition — the inhibitor binds only the enzyme–substrate complex.
  • Irreversible inhibition — the inhibitor forms a covalent bond with the enzyme, permanently disabling it; examples include penicillin (bacterial transpeptidases), aspirin (cyclooxygenase) and many nerve-agent and pesticide compounds acting on acetylcholinesterase.

Applications

Enzyme inhibitors are a major class of pharmaceuticals: statins inhibit HMG-CoA reductase to lower cholesterol; ACE inhibitors treat hypertension; HIV protease inhibitors are central to antiretroviral therapy; and methotrexate inhibits dihydrofolate reductase in chemotherapy. Inhibitors are also widely used as research tools to dissect metabolic pathways, and as pesticides and herbicides.
