Bugle

ClassificationBrass aerophone
Related instrumentsTrumpet, cornet, natural trumpet, flugelhorn
Primary useMilitary signalling, ceremonies

The bugle is one of the simplest brass instruments, having no valves or other pitch-altering devices. All pitch control is achieved by varying the player's embouchure to select notes from the natural harmonic series. The bugle developed from early musical and signalling instruments made from animal horns, and in its modern form is a coiled brass tube with a cup-shaped mouthpiece and a flared bell, usually pitched in B♭ or G.

Military use

Because commands could not be shouted across a noisy battlefield, armies adopted the bugle to signal daily routines and battlefield orders. A standard repertoire of bugle calls regulates camp life: "Reveille" wakes the troops, "Assembly" summons them to gather, "Mess Call" announces meals, and "Tattoo" and "Taps" mark the end of the day. "Taps", composed during the American Civil War in 1862, is also played at military funerals and memorial ceremonies. Cavalry and light infantry regiments traditionally used bugles where heavier infantry used drums, and the instrument remains the emblem of many rifle regiments.

Other uses

Bugles are used in drum and bugle corps, in scouting organisations, and at ceremonies such as Remembrance Day services, where "Last Post" is played. A keyed bugle, invented in the early 19th century, added keys to fill the gaps in the harmonic series and briefly flourished before being displaced by the valved cornet.
