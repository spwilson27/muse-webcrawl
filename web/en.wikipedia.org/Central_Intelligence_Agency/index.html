Central Intelligence Agency

The Central Intelligence Agency (CIA; /siː.aɪˈeɪ/) is a civilian foreign intelligence service of the federal government of the United States tasked with advancing national security through collecting and analyzing intelligence from around the world and conducting covert operations. The agency is headquartered in Langley, Virginia, a few miles west of Washington, D.C.

The CIA was established on September 18, 1947, when President Harry S. Truman signed the National Security Act of 1947. It succeeded the wartime Office of Strategic Services. Unlike the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), which is a domestic security service, the CIA has no law enforcement function and is mainly focused on intelligence collection abroad.

Organization

The agency is headed by a Director, confirmed by the Senate, who reports to the Director of National Intelligence. Its main directorates cover analysis, operations (clandestine service), science and technology, and support, plus mission centers for regions and topics such as counterterrorism and counterintelligence.

History

Major episodes in CIA history include the 1953 Iranian coup, the 1954 Guatemalan coup, the failed Bay of Pigs invasion (1961), the U-2 and SR-71 reconnaissance programmes, MKUltra experiments, the Church Committee investigations of the 1970s, Iran–Contra, intelligence failures concerning Iraqi weapons of mass destruction in 2003, the post-9/11 detention and interrogation programme, and the decade-long hunt for Osama bin Laden, located and killed in 2011.

Oversight

The CIA is overseen by the House and Senate intelligence committees, the Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Court for certain activities, and its own Inspector General. Its budget and personnel numbers are classified, though the aggregate US intelligence budget is published.
