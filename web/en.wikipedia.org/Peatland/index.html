A peatland or mire is a type of wetland whose soils consist largely of accumulated partially decayed plant material (peat). Peat forms under waterlogged, oxygen-poor conditions that slow decomposition, allowing organic matter to build up over thousands of years. Peatlands are found on every continent, from boreal and temperate zones to the tropics.

Formation

Peat accumulates where waterlogging prevents complete decay of plant remains. In northern peatlands, sphagnum mosses dominate: their chemistry acidifies the water and further inhibits decomposition. Peat deposits may be several metres thick and can preserve pollen, archaeological material and even human bodies (bog bodies) in remarkable detail.

Climate significance

Although peatlands cover only about 3% of the Earth's land surface, they store roughly 600 billion tonnes of carbon — more than all the world's vegetation combined and around a third of the world's soil carbon. Drainage for agriculture, forestry or peat extraction releases this carbon as carbon dioxide, and drained peatlands account for a disproportionate share of land-use greenhouse gas emissions. Rewetting and restoration are widely promoted as climate mitigation measures.

Types

Bogs are rain-fed (ombrotrophic), acidic and nutrient-poor. Fens receive groundwater or surface water (minerotrophic) and are richer in nutrients. Tropical peatlands, such as the peat swamp forests of Indonesia and the Congo Basin, store vast carbon stocks beneath forest cover.
