Aria

An aria is a self-contained piece for one voice, with or without instrumental accompaniment, normally part of a larger work such as an opera, oratorio, or cantata. In the context of opera, an aria usually involves a character expressing their emotions, in contrast to recitative, which advances the plot through sung dialogue. The term, from the Italian for "air", came into use in the 17th and 18th centuries.

History and forms

Early operas of the Baroque period developed the aria as a formal, often highly ornamented set piece. The da capo aria — with an A section, a contrasting B section, and a return to A with improvised ornamentation — became the dominant form in opera seria. In the 19th century, composers such as Verdi and Wagner increasingly integrated arias into continuous musical drama, though celebrated standalone arias, such as "Nessun dorma" from Puccini's Turandot and the "Queen of the Night aria" from Mozart's The Magic Flute, remain central to the repertoire.

Arioso and arietta

Related forms include the arioso, a shorter, more lyrical passage between recitative and full aria, and the arietta, a brief, simple aria. Concert arias are standalone works written for performance in concert rather than within an opera.
