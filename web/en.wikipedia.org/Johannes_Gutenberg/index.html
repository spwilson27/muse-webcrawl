Johannes Gensfleisch zur Laden zum Gutenberg (c. 1393–1406 – 3 February 1468) was a German inventor and craftsman who originated the system of printing with movable type in Europe. His major work, the Gutenberg Bible, was printed around 1454–55 and was the first major book produced using mass-produced movable metal type in Europe.

Life

Gutenberg was born in Mainz, the son of a wealthy merchant and goldsmith. He was trained as a goldsmith and spent part of his life in Strasbourg, where he experimented with metalworking, gem cutting, and — according to a 1439 lawsuit record — a secretive venture involving mirrors and printing techniques. By 1448 he had returned to Mainz, where he borrowed money from the merchant Johann Fust to set up his workshop.

The printing system

Gutenberg's achievement was to combine several elements into a practical system: durable metal movable type cast in matrices with a hand mould, a lead-tin-antimony alloy, oil-based ink suited to metal type, and a screw press adapted from paper and wine presses. Around 1450 his workshop was operational, printing smaller items such as indulgences and school grammars before the great Latin Bible, of which about 180 copies were printed.

Later years and legacy

In 1455 Fust sued Gutenberg and took over the workshop and much of the Bible stock. Gutenberg may later have set up a smaller press in partnership with Konrad Humery. In 1465 the Archbishop of Mainz granted him a pension and the title of courtier. He died in Mainz in 1468.

Within decades, printing shops had spread to hundreds of European towns, dramatically lowering the cost of books and transforming the diffusion of knowledge, religion, and science. Gutenberg is routinely ranked among the most influential people of the second millennium.
