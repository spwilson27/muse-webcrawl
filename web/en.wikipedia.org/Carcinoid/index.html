Carcinoid

A carcinoid is a slow-growing type of neuroendocrine tumour originating in the neuroendocrine system. The term was coined in 1907 by the German pathologist Siegfried Oberndorfer, who considered the tumours "carcinoma-like" but more benign. In modern classification such tumours are called neuroendocrine tumours (NETs), graded by their rate of proliferation; the word carcinoid is still commonly used for well-differentiated NETs.

Sites and symptoms

Carcinoids arise most often in the gastrointestinal tract — especially the small intestine, appendix, and rectum — and in the lungs. Many are found incidentally. Larger tumours may cause obstruction, bleeding, or abdominal pain. When a gastrointestinal NET metastasises to the liver, hormones it secretes (notably serotonin) escape first-pass metabolism and can produce carcinoid syndrome: flushing, diarrhoea, bronchospasm, and eventually right-sided heart valve damage (carcinoid heart disease).

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis uses imaging, including somatostatin-receptor PET/CT, and biomarkers such as urinary 5-HIAA, a serotonin metabolite. Treatment centres on surgical removal where possible. Somatostatin analogues such as octreotide control symptoms and slow growth; peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) with lutetium-177 dotatate is used for advanced disease, along with targeted drugs such as everolimus.
