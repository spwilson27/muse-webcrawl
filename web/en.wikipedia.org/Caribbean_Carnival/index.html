Caribbean Carnival

Caribbean Carnival is the name for the pre-Lenten festival traditions of the Caribbean and their diaspora, most famously the Trinidad and Tobago Carnival, regarded as the largest and archetypal celebration. Blending French Catholic masquerade traditions with African festival culture of enslaved people, Carnival developed into a festival of music, elaborate costume bands, street parades and competitions.

[Image: Masqueraders in feathered costumes parading on Carnival Tuesday in Port of Spain, Trinidad]

History

French planters in Trinidad held masquerade balls before Lent in the 18th century; enslaved Africans, excluded from those balls, held their own celebrations, known as Canboulay, incorporating drumming, stick-fighting (kalinda) and African-derived masquerade characters. After emancipation in 1834, Carnival became a festival of the freed population, and despite periodic colonial suppression it absorbed calypso, steelpan — invented in Trinidad in the 20th century and the only acoustic musical instrument created in that century — and later soca music.

Elements

Modern Carnival runs from J'ouvert, the pre-dawn street party of mud- and paint-covered revellers on Carnival Monday, through the parade of costumed "mas bands" on Monday and Tuesday. Competitions include Calypso Monarch, Panorama for steel orchestras, and the King and Queen of Carnival costume titles. Fetes (parties) fill the preceding weeks.

Diaspora carnivals

Caribbean emigrants transplanted Carnival worldwide: London's Notting Hill Carnival (founded 1964, Europe's largest street festival), Toronto Caribbean Carnival, Brooklyn's Labor Day Parade, and carnivals in New York, Miami, Rotterdam and many other cities, typically held in summer rather than before Lent.
