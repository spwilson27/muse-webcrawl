Old-growth forest

Old-growth forest
Also calledPrimary forest, virgin forest, primeval forest
Key traitsLarge old trees, snags, coarse woody debris, multi-layer canopy
ExamplesPacific Northwest temperate rainforest, Białowieża Forest, Amazon rainforest

An old-growth forest is a forest that has developed over a long period without significant disturbance and thereby exhibits unique ecological features. Such forests typically contain large, old trees, standing dead trees (snags), fallen logs in various stages of decay, and a multi-layered canopy with gaps — characteristics that may take centuries to develop after a stand-replacing disturbance.

Ecology

Old-growth forests are important reservoirs of biodiversity, providing habitat for species found nowhere else — famously the northern spotted owl of the Pacific Northwest and the marbled murrelet. Contrary to an older assumption that mature forests are carbon-neutral, research has shown that old-growth forests continue to sequester and store large quantities of carbon in biomass and soil.

Distribution and loss

Extensive old-growth remains in the Amazon and Congo basins, boreal Canada and Russia, and temperate rainforests of the Pacific Northwest, Chile, Tasmania, and New Zealand. In Europe, old-growth is rare; Poland's Białowieża Forest is the best-known remnant. Logging, conversion to plantation forestry, and fragmentation have reduced old-growth worldwide, prompting long-running conservation conflicts, such as the "timber wars" of the U.S. Pacific Northwest and the 1994 Northwest Forest Plan.

Definition debates

Because developmental pathways differ among forest types, no single age threshold defines old growth; definitions rely on structural criteria that vary by ecosystem, from roughly 120–150 years for some eastern North American hardwoods to 500 years or more for conifer forests of the Pacific coast.
