Implicit stereotype

An implicit stereotype is an unconscious or automatic association between a social group and a trait or attribute — for example, associating women with family or men with careers — that can operate without a person's conscious awareness or endorsement. The concept is central to research on implicit social cognition that began in earnest in the 1990s.

Measurement

Implicit stereotypes are typically measured indirectly, most famously with the implicit-association test (IAT), which infers association strength from differences in sorting speed, and with priming tasks in which exposure to a group cue speeds or slows responses to stereotype-related words.

Formation and effects

Researchers argue that implicit stereotypes are absorbed from cultural exposure — media, language, and observed social patterns — and can diverge from a person's explicitly stated beliefs. Studies have examined their possible influence on hiring decisions, medical treatment, policing, and courtroom outcomes, though the strength of the link between measured implicit stereotypes and individual behaviour remains debated.

Related concepts

Implicit stereotypes are related to, but distinct from, implicit attitudes (automatic evaluations of groups as good or bad) and explicit stereotypes (consciously endorsed beliefs). Interventions such as counter-stereotypical exposure and individuation have been studied as means of reducing their activation.
