Christian Lorenz Ernst Engel (26 March 1821 – 8 December 1896) was a German statistician and economist, remembered for Engel's law: the observation that as a household's income rises, the proportion of income spent on food falls, even though absolute spending on food may increase. The relationship, expressed graphically as the Engel curve, remains a foundational empirical finding in economics and is still used to measure living standards and poverty.

Life and career

Engel studied mining engineering at Freiberg and in Paris, and took his doctorate at Leipzig in 1846. After working in Saxon government service he became head of the Saxon Statistical Bureau in Dresden in 1850, organising the kingdom's censuses and founding a respected school of official statistics. In 1882 he became director of the Royal Prussian Statistical Bureau in Berlin, a post he held until 1890. He also sat in the Prussian parliament and served as an adviser on statistics, insurance, and census organisation to several governments.

Engel's law

Engel published his finding in 1857, drawing on budget data for Belgian working families collected by Édouard Ducpétiaux and Frédéric Le Play. Comparing households at different income levels, he showed that poorer families devoted a much larger share of expenditure to food than richer ones. The "Engel coefficient" — the food share of total household expenditure — is still used, including in official Chinese statistics, as an index of prosperity: a coefficient above about 59% indicates poverty, while below 30% indicates affluence. Engel's broader work on the cost of living and on consumption patterns helped found the empirical study of household budgets.
