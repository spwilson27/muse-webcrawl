DiGeorge syndrome, also called 22q11.2 deletion syndrome, is a genetic disorder caused by the deletion of a small segment of chromosome 22 at the q11.2 location. It is the most common chromosomal microdeletion syndrome, occurring in roughly 1 in 4,000 live births, and produces a highly variable combination of congenital heart defects, immune deficiency, palate abnormalities, low blood calcium, and developmental and psychiatric problems.

Signs and features

The syndrome disrupts the embryonic development of the third and fourth pharyngeal pouches, which give rise to the thymus and parathyroid glands. Affected infants may have an underdeveloped thymus, causing T-cell immune deficiency; hypoparathyroidism, causing hypocalcaemia; conotruncal heart defects such as tetralogy of Fallot and interrupted aortic arch; cleft palate or velopharyngeal insufficiency; and characteristic facial features. Later in life there are increased rates of learning difficulties and psychiatric illness, including schizophrenia. The classic features are often remembered by the acronym CATCH-22.

Genetics and diagnosis

Most cases result from a new (de novo) deletion of about 1.5–3 million base pairs containing around 40 genes, including TBX1, thought to drive many of the developmental features; about 10% are inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern. Diagnosis is confirmed by genetic testing such as FISH, chromosomal microarray, or MLPA. The syndrome encompasses presentations once described separately as DiGeorge syndrome, velocardiofacial syndrome, and conotruncal anomaly face syndrome.

Management

There is no cure; care is multidisciplinary and targeted at individual problems: cardiac surgery, calcium and vitamin D supplementation, immune monitoring and, in severe cases, thymus transplantation, speech therapy, and developmental and mental-health support.
