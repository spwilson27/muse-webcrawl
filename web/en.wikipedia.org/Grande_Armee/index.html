Grande Armée

Active1804–1815
CountryFirst French Empire
TypeField army
Size~600,000 at its peak (1812)
CommanderNapoleon Bonaparte

The Grande Armée (French for "Great Army") was the principal field army commanded by Napoleon Bonaparte during the Napoleonic Wars. Originally named the Armée des côtes de l'Océan (Army of the Ocean Coasts), assembled for a planned invasion of Britain, it was renamed by Napoleon in 1805 when it marched east to fight Austria and Russia.

Organization

The Grande Armée was organized into largely self-contained army corps (corps d'armée), each commanded by a Marshal of the Empire and containing infantry, cavalry, artillery and support services. This corps system allowed the army to march dispersed along parallel routes — living off the land and moving quickly — and concentrate rapidly for battle. Alongside the line infantry (fusiliers, voltigeurs, grenadiers and chasseurs), the army included heavy and light cavalry, the Imperial Guard, and large numbers of allied and foreign troops.

Campaigns

At its height the Grande Armée won a string of celebrated victories, including Ulm and Austerlitz (1805), Jena-Auerstedt (1806), Friedland (1807) and Wagram (1809). In June 1812 Napoleon invaded Russia with the largest army yet assembled in European history, roughly 600,000 men of many nationalities. The campaign ended in catastrophe: disease, starvation, desertion, battle losses and the Russian winter reduced the army to a fraction of its size during the retreat from Moscow.

Rebuilt in 1813, the army fought the campaigns of Germany and France but could not match the numbers of the Sixth Coalition, and was dissolved after Napoleon's abdication in 1814. A reconstituted Grande Armée fought the Waterloo campaign of 1815.

Legacy

The military historian Richard Brooks wrote: "Feeding, paying, clothing and equipping an army of this size was a logistical triumph." The Grande Armée's corps system, staff organization and operational methods influenced European armies for the rest of the nineteenth century.
