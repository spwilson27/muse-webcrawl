White Revolution (India)

The White Revolution, known in India as Operation Flood, was a series of dairy development programmes launched in 1970 by the National Dairy Development Board (NDDB) that transformed India from a milk-deficient nation into the world's largest milk producer. Spearheaded by Dr. Verghese Kurien, often called the "Father of the White Revolution," the programme linked milk producers in rural villages to urban consumers through a nationwide network of cooperatives modelled on the Anand pattern pioneered by the Kaira District Co-operative Milk Producers' Union (Amul).

Operation Flood proceeded in three phases (1970–1980, 1981–1985, and 1985–1996), financed initially by the sale of skimmed milk powder and butter oil donated by the European Economic Community through the World Food Programme. It created a National Milk Grid connecting producers and consumers in more than 700 towns and cities, and it built self-sustaining village-level dairy cooperatives in which farmers owned the assets, hired professionals to manage them, and received the bulk of the consumer rupee.

Background

Before the programme, Indian milk production was stagnant, per-capita availability was falling, and the country relied on imports and food aid. The success of the Amul cooperative in Gujarat, founded in 1946 under the guidance of Tribhuvandas Patel and Verghese Kurien, demonstrated that smallholder farmers could collectively process and market their milk. The NDDB, established in 1965, was charged with replicating this "Anand pattern" across the country.

Phases

Phase I (1970–1980) linked 18 of India's premier milk sheds with consumers in the four metropolitan cities of Delhi, Mumbai, Kolkata, and Chennai, establishing mother dairies in each. Phase II (1981–1985) expanded coverage to some 136 milk sheds and over 290 urban markets, growing membership to about 4.25 million milk producers. Phase III (1985–1996) consolidated the gains, adding tens of thousands of new village cooperatives and strengthening infrastructure for animal health, feed, and breeding services.

Impact

India's milk production rose from roughly 22 million tonnes in 1970 to over 200 million tonnes by the early 2020s, and per-capita milk availability more than doubled. The programme raised rural incomes, particularly for women and landless households, for whom dairying provided a regular cash income, and it made India self-sufficient in milk and milk products.
