Nernst equation

In electrochemistry, the Nernst equation is a chemical thermodynamical relationship that permits the calculation of the reduction potential of a reaction (half-cell or full cell reaction) from the standard electrode potential, absolute temperature, the number of electrons involved in the redox reaction, and activities (often approximated by concentrations) of the chemical species undergoing reduction and oxidation respectively. It was named after the German physical chemist Walther Nernst, who formulated it in 1889.

The Nernst equation relates cell potential to the concentrations of reacting species.

The equation

For a general reduction reaction, the reduction potential E is given by:

E = E° − (RT / nF) ln Q

where E° is the standard reduction potential, R is the gas constant, T is the absolute temperature, n is the number of moles of electrons transferred, F is the Faraday constant, and Q is the reaction quotient. At 25 °C and using base-10 logarithms, the factor RT/F ln(10) is approximately 0.05916 volts, giving the common laboratory form:

E = E° − (0.05916 V / n) log10 Q

Applications

The Nernst equation underlies potentiometric measurements such as the pH meter, in which the potential of a glass electrode varies with hydrogen ion activity. It is used to determine equilibrium constants of redox reactions, to construct Pourbaix diagrams, and in physiology, where in the form of the Goldman equation it describes the membrane potential of cells based on the permeabilities and concentration gradients of ions such as sodium, potassium, and chloride.

Limitations

The equation applies strictly to equilibrium and uses activities rather than concentrations; in concentrated solutions, activity coefficients depart substantially from unity and corrections are required. At very low ionic concentrations, electrode measurements become slow and unreliable.
