Gibbs free energy

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In thermodynamics, the Gibbs free energy (or Gibbs energy; symbol G) is a thermodynamic potential that can be used to calculate the maximum amount of work, other than pressure–volume work, that may be performed by a thermodynamically closed system at constant temperature and pressure. It also provides a necessary condition for processes such as chemical reactions that may occur under these conditions. The concept was developed by the American scientist Josiah Willard Gibbs in the 1870s.

Definition

The Gibbs free energy is defined as

G = H − TS

where H is the enthalpy, T the absolute temperature, and S the entropy of the system. Equivalently, G = U + pV − TS, where U is the internal energy, p the pressure, and V the volume. Because H, T, and S are state functions, G is itself a state function, and its SI unit is the joule.

Change and spontaneity

For a process at constant temperature and pressure, the change in Gibbs free energy is

ΔG = ΔH − TΔS

The sign of ΔG determines spontaneity: if ΔG < 0, the process is exergonic and can proceed spontaneously; if ΔG > 0, it is endergonic and requires an input of work; if ΔG = 0, the system is at equilibrium. ΔG is also directly related to the equilibrium constant of a chemical reaction through ΔG° = −RT ln K, and in electrochemistry to cell potential through ΔG° = −nFE°, linking thermodynamics to practical quantities measurable in the laboratory.

Applications

Gibbs free energy governs the direction of chemical reactions, phase transitions, and the maximum electrical work obtainable from batteries and fuel cells. In biochemistry, the hydrolysis of ATP to ADP releases about 30.5 kJ/mol under standard conditions, and cells couple this exergonic reaction to drive endergonic processes such as biosynthesis and active transport. The related Helmholtz free energy, A = U − TS, plays the corresponding role for processes at constant temperature and volume.
