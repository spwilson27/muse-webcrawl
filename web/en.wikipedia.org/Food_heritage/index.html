Food heritage

Traditional foods and foodways are increasingly treated as a form of cultural heritage.

Food heritage — also referred to as culinary heritage or gastronomic heritage — is the body of foods, dishes, ingredients, techniques, and eating practices that a community regards as part of its cultural inheritance. It encompasses traditional recipes and cooking methods, heritage crop varieties and animal breeds, festive and ritual meals, and the knowledge and skills used to produce and prepare food. Since the late twentieth century, food heritage has been the subject of growing institutional recognition, local promotion, and academic study.

Recognition by UNESCO

A major milestone was the inscription of food-related elements on UNESCO's Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity. In 2010 UNESCO inscribed the "gastronomic meal of the French", the traditional Mexican cuisine of Michoacán, and the Mediterranean diet (jointly with several countries) — the first food traditions to be recognized under the 2003 Convention for the Safeguarding of the Intangible Cultural Heritage. Many further elements have followed, including washoku (traditional dietary culture of Japan, 2013), kimjang (the making and sharing of kimchi in Korea, 2013), Turkish coffee culture (2013), and the art of the Neapolitan pizzaiuolo (pizza maker, 2017).

Protection of products and origins

Food heritage is also protected through geographical indication systems that tie a product's name to its place and method of production. The European Union operates the Protected Designation of Origin (PDO), Protected Geographical Indication (PGI), and Traditional Speciality Guaranteed (TSG) schemes, covering products such as Parmigiano Reggiano, Champagne, and feta. Similar systems exist in many other countries. Slow Food's Ark of Taste catalogues endangered heritage foods, and its presidia projects support small-scale producers of traditional products.

Significance and debates

Advocates argue that safeguarding food heritage supports biodiversity by preserving heritage varieties and breeds, sustains rural livelihoods, and strengthens cultural identity and tourism. Critics note risks that official recognition can freeze living traditions, favour commercial interests, or turn everyday foodways into tourist commodities. Scholars of food studies examine how traditions are selected, codified, and sometimes newly "invented" in the heritage-making process, and how migration and globalization continually reshape what counts as a community's food heritage.
