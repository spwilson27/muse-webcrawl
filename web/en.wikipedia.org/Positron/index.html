Positron

The positron or antielectron is the particle with an electric charge of +1 e, a spin of 1/2, and the same mass as an electron. It is the antiparticle of the electron. When a positron collides with an electron, annihilation occurs, producing two or more gamma-ray photons.

Prediction and discovery

Paul Dirac predicted the positron in 1928 from his relativistic equation of the electron, whose negative-energy solutions he reinterpreted as a positively charged counterpart. Carl David Anderson discovered the particle in 1932 while studying cosmic rays in a cloud chamber, observing tracks curving opposite to electrons; the discovery won him the 1936 Nobel Prize in Physics. Patrick Blackett and Giuseppe Occhialini confirmed it the following year.

Production and uses

Positrons are produced in beta-plus radioactive decay (e.g. fluorine-18), by pair production from energetic photons, and in some particle accelerators. Their most widespread application is positron emission tomography (PET), a medical imaging technique in which a positron-emitting tracer is administered to a patient and the annihilation photons are detected to construct images of metabolic activity. Positrons from natural sources also arrive in cosmic rays and are emitted by potassium-40 in the environment.
