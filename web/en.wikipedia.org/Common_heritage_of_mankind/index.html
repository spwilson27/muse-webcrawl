Common heritage of mankind

Common heritage of mankind (also termed the common heritage of humanity, common heritage of humankind or common heritage principle) is a principle of international law which holds that defined territorial areas and elements of humanity's common heritage (cultural and natural) should be held in trust for future generations and be protected from exploitation by individual nation states or corporations.

Origins and application

The principle was prominently proposed by Malta's ambassador Arvid Pardo in a 1967 address to the United Nations, concerning the resources of the deep seabed beyond national jurisdiction. It was incorporated into the 1982 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea, which declares the seabed Area and its mineral resources to be the common heritage of mankind, administered by the International Seabed Authority, and into the 1979 Moon Treaty for celestial bodies.

Core elements generally identified with the concept include the prohibition of national appropriation, shared international management, the equitable sharing of benefits, peaceful use, and preservation for future generations. Related but distinct ideas apply to the "common heritage" framing of UNESCO World Heritage sites and to debates over genetic resources and outer space.
