Via Dolorosa

The Via Dolorosa winds through the Muslim and Christian Quarters of the Old City.

The Via Dolorosa (Latin for 'Sorrowful Way', often translated 'Way of Suffering'; Arabic: طريق الآلام; Hebrew: ויה דולורוזה) is a processional route in the Old City of Jerusalem. It represents the path that Jesus took, forced by the Roman soldiers, on the way to his crucifixion. The winding route from the former Antonia Fortress to the Church of the Holy Sepulchre — a distance of about 600 metres — is a celebrated place of Christian pilgrimage. The route was established in the 18th century. It is today marked by fourteen Stations of the Cross, nine of them outdoors on the street itself and the last five inside the Church of the Holy Sepulchre.

History

Byzantine pilgrims followed various routes to Golgotha. In the Crusader period, the route began to be fixed around the sites of the Antonia Fortress, the traditional location of Pilate's praetorium, and the Holy Sepulchre. The Franciscans, custodians of the Holy Land from the 14th century, promoted the devotion of the Stations of the Cross, and by the 18th century the present route — running from the Lions' Gate area westward through the Old City — had become standard. Every Friday afternoon, Franciscan friars lead a procession along the Via Dolorosa, joined by pilgrims from around the world.

The fourteen stations

  1. Jesus is condemned to death.
  2. Jesus carries his cross.
  3. Jesus falls for the first time.
  4. Jesus meets his mother Mary.
  5. Simon of Cyrene helps Jesus carry the cross.
  6. Veronica wipes the face of Jesus.
  7. Jesus falls a second time.
  8. Jesus meets the women of Jerusalem.
  9. Jesus falls a third time.
  10. Jesus is stripped of his garments.
  11. Jesus is nailed to the cross.
  12. Jesus dies on the cross.
  13. Jesus is taken down from the cross.
  14. Jesus is laid in the tomb.

Historical assessment

Archaeologists and historians note that the route is largely traditional rather than demonstrably historical; the exact location of Pilate's judgement seat and the city walls of the period remain debated. Nonetheless, the Via Dolorosa remains one of the most powerful devotional landscapes in Christianity, and its stations are marked by small chapels, plaques, and reliefs along the stone streets.
