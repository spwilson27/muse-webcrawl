Alcohol proof

Alcohol proof (usually termed simply proof) is a measure of the content of ethanol (alcohol) in an alcoholic beverage. The term was originally used in England and was equal to about 1.821 times the percentage of alcohol by volume (ABV). The United Kingdom now uses ABV standard instead of proof when referring to the measurement of alcohol content. In the United States, alcoholic proof is defined as twice the percentage of ABV: an 80-proof spirit contains 40% alcohol by volume. In Canada, the term is not officially used, with ABV preferred on labels, though proof values inherited from the US system are common in marketing.

History

The term "proof" dates to 16th-century England, when spirits were taxed at different rates depending on their alcohol content. A rough test involved soaking gunpowder in the spirit and igniting it: if the powder still burned, the spirit was deemed "proved" to be of sufficient strength — 100 proof under the old British system. Later, measurement by specific gravity with a hydrometer replaced the gunpowder test, and the legal definition was tied to precise density comparisons at specified temperatures.

Definitions by country

The British system defined 100 proof spirit as one with a specific gravity of 12/13 that of water at 51 °F, equivalent to 57.15% ABV — hence the conversion factor of roughly 7/4 between the British proof number and ABV. The United States adopted the simpler convention of doubling ABV in 1848. France uses the Gay-Lussac scale, numerically identical to ABV. Since 1980 the United Kingdom and the European Union have required labelling by ABV, and US regulations require ABV statements while permitting proof as supplementary information.
