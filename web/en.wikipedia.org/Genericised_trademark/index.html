Generic trademark

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A generic trademark, also known as a genericised trademark or proprietary eponym, is a trademark or brand name that, because of its popularity or significance, has become the generic term for, or synonymous with, a general class of products or services, usually against the intentions of the trademark's owner. The process is known as genericide. When it occurs, trademark protection may be lost: in the United States, a mark that becomes the generic name for the goods it identifies can be cancelled under the Lanham Act.

Examples

Well-known former trademarks that became generic in at least some jurisdictions include aspirin (originally Bayer's brand of acetylsalicylic acid), heroin, escalator (Otis), thermos, trampoline, linoleum, zipper, cellophane and kerosene. Other marks remain legally protected but are widely used generically in everyday speech in some countries, including Kleenex (tissues), Band-Aid (adhesive bandages), Hoover (vacuum cleaners, in British English), Google (as a verb for web searching), and Xerox (photocopying). Courts distinguish between such colloquial generic use and true legal genericide, which depends on the primary significance of the term to the relevant public.

Avoiding genericide

Trademark owners police usage to preserve their rights: they use the mark as an adjective with a generic noun ("Kleenex tissues"), avoid using it as a verb or plural, run advertising campaigns correcting generic usage (such as Xerox's and Velcro's "Don't Say Velcro" campaign), and challenge dictionary definitions. The legal risk is illustrated by cases such as E. I. du Pont de Nemours & Co. v. Celanese and the loss of "yo-yo" and "aspirin" rights in various countries.
