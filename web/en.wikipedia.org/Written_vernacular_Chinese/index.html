Written vernacular Chinese

Written vernacular Chinese (simplified Chinese: 白话文; traditional Chinese: 白話文; pinyin: báihuàwén, "plain speech writing") is the standard written form of Chinese based on spoken Mandarin, as opposed to Classical Chinese (wényán), the literary language used for formal writing for over two thousand years. It became the standard for books, newspapers, education and official documents in the early twentieth century.

History

Although vernacular literature flourished during the Ming and Qing dynasties — the great novels Water Margin, Journey to the West and Dream of the Red Chamber were written largely in baihua — formal writing remained classical. In 1917 the scholar Hu Shih published a manifesto calling for a literary revolution, arguing that literature should be written in the living language; Chen Duxiu's journal New Youth championed the cause, and Lu Xun's "Diary of a Madman" (1918) became the first celebrated modern short story in the vernacular.

During the May Fourth era the Ministry of Education adopted baihua for primary textbooks (1920) and it soon displaced Classical Chinese in most contexts. Missionaries and reformers had earlier used vernacular forms associated with the term guanhua ("official speech"), the ancestor of modern Standard Mandarin.

Features

Written vernacular Chinese mirrors Mandarin grammar and vocabulary: it uses multi-character words, aspect particles such as 了 (le), 把 (bǎ) constructions, and pronouns such as 他/她/它. It is written both in simplified characters (mainland China, Singapore) and traditional characters (Taiwan, Hong Kong, Macau). Written Cantonese, a separate vernacular written form used in Hong Kong, differs in vocabulary and some characters.
