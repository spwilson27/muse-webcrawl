Motivation crowding theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Motivation crowding theory, in labor economics and psychology, proposes that extrinsic incentives such as monetary rewards can undermine ("crowd out") intrinsic motivation for a task, so that paying people for an activity they would otherwise perform from interest, duty or civic virtue can reduce total performance or willingness. Conversely, under some conditions external interventions can "crowd in" intrinsic motivation.

Origins and evidence

The idea was anticipated by Richard Titmuss's The Gift Relationship (1970), which argued that paying blood donors could undermine voluntary donation. Psychologist Edward Deci's experiments in the 1970s, and the related "overjustification effect" studied by Lepper and colleagues, showed that expected tangible rewards reduced later free-choice engagement with initially enjoyable tasks. Economist Bruno Frey developed the theory systematically for economics, applying it to tax morale, siting of noxious facilities, and public policy. A celebrated field study in Swiss cantons found that offering compensation for hosting a nuclear waste repository halved acceptance, because the payment crowded out civic duty.

Mechanisms and debate

Self-determination theory explains crowding out through the undermining of perceived autonomy and competence: a controlling reward shifts the perceived reason for acting from the self to the external incentive. Crowding out is most likely when rewards are tangible, expected and contingent, and when the task was initially interesting. The size and generality of the effect remain debated — meta-analyses disagree, rewards can crowd in motivation for dull tasks, and verbal praise often enhances intrinsic motivation.
