The Constitution of the Italian Republic (Italian: Costituzione della Repubblica Italiana) was ratified on 22 December 1947 by the Constituent Assembly, promulgated by the provisional head of state on 27 December, and entered into force on 1 January 1948. It replaced the Statuto Albertino of 1848 and established Italy as a democratic republic founded on labour, following the 1946 institutional referendum that abolished the monarchy.

Composition

The constitution consists of 139 articles, preceded by twelve fundamental principles, and concludes with eighteen transitory and final provisions. The first part (articles 13–54) concerns the rights and duties of citizens; the second part (articles 55–139) describes the organization of the Republic: the Parliament, the President of the Republic, the Government, the judiciary, the regions, and the Constitutional Court.

Fundamental principles

Article 1 declares that Italy is a democratic Republic founded on labour and that sovereignty belongs to the people. Article 2 recognizes inviolable human rights; Article 3 proclaims equality before the law and commits the Republic to removing economic and social obstacles to full equality. Article 11 repudiates war as an instrument of aggression.

Amendment

The constitution is rigid: amendments require two successive deliberations by each house of Parliament, and certain amendments may be submitted to a confirmatory referendum. Article 139 places the republican form of government beyond amendment. Several reform proposals — including broad revisions rejected in referendums in 2006 and 2016 — have failed, while narrower amendments, such as the 2020 reduction in the number of parliamentarians, have passed.

Constitutional Court

The Constitutional Court, which began functioning in 1956, reviews the constitutionality of laws, resolves conflicts between state powers and regions, and judges charges against the President of the Republic.
