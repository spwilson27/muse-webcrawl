Bernabò Visconti

Bernabò Visconti (also spelled Barnabò; 1323 – 19 December 1385) was an Italian soldier and statesman who was Lord of Milan, along with his brothers Matteo II and Galeazzo II, and later ruled jointly with his brother Galeazzo and contested power with his nephew Gian Galeazzo. He is remembered as a formidable and feared ruler, a patron of the hunt and of building, and as a persistent enemy of the Papacy.

Life

Bernabò was the son of Stefano Visconti and Valentina Doria, and nephew of Luchino and Archbishop Giovanni Visconti. On Giovanni's death in 1354 the Visconti dominions were divided among Bernabò and his brothers; Bernabò received the eastern territories including Bergamo, Brescia, Cremona, and Parma. After Matteo II died in 1355 (in circumstances that aroused suspicion), Bernabò and Galeazzo II ruled together. Bernabò waged repeated wars against the Papal States, Bologna, the Scaligeri of Verona, the Gonzaga of Mantua, and the Este of Ferrara, and was excommunicated by Pope Urban V. He narrowly survived a plague year and an assassination attempt, and in 1369 forced a favourable settlement upon imperial and papal opponents at various times.

Rule and character

Contemporary and later accounts depict Bernabò as energetic, intelligent, and ruthless, ruling through terror as well as law: he was said to keep thousands of hunting dogs whose upkeep was imposed on his subjects, and to punish officials who let the animals starve. He married Regina della Scala, daughter of Mastino II of Verona, by whom — along with many illegitimate children — he had numerous offspring whom he married into ruling houses across Europe, including the Wittelsbachs of Bavaria and the royal house of Cyprus. He built and embellished castles and churches, notably at Trezzo sull'Adda.

Downfall and death

In May 1385 his nephew and son-in-law Gian Galeazzo Visconti, feigning a pilgrimage, seized Bernabò near Milan and imprisoned him in the castle of Trezzo, where he died in December of the same year, possibly by poison. His monumental equestrian tomb, originally made for San Giovanni in Conca, is now in the Castello Sforzesco in Milan.
