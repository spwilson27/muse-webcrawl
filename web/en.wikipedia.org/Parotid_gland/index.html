Parotid gland

The parotid gland is a major salivary gland in many animals, including humans. In humans the two parotid glands are present on either side of the mouth and in front of each ear; they are the largest of the salivary glands. They secrete saliva through the parotid (Stensen's) duct into the mouth, opposite the upper second molar, facilitating mastication and swallowing and initiating the digestion of starches with the enzyme amylase.

Structure

Each gland weighs about 15–30 grams and is wrapped in a tough fascial capsule. It lies in front of the ear, over the masseter muscle and the ramus of the mandible, and extends down toward the angle of the jaw. Several important structures pass through or immediately deep to the gland: the facial nerve, which divides within it into its terminal branches and makes parotid surgery delicate; the retromandibular vein; and the external carotid artery. The gland's secretions are purely serous, unlike the mixed secretions of the submandibular gland.

Clinical significance

The best-known disease of the parotid is mumps, a viral infection that causes painful swelling of one or both glands. Salivary stones (sialoliths) and bacterial infection can obstruct or inflame the duct, and Sjögren's syndrome causes chronic autoimmune inflammation and dry mouth. Most parotid tumours are benign — the commonest is the pleomorphic adenoma, followed by Warthin tumour — but because the facial nerve traverses the gland, removal (parotidectomy) carries a risk of facial weakness, and a recognised complication is Frey's syndrome, in which regenerating secretomotor fibres cause sweating of the cheek when eating.
