Heritage railway

A heritage railway (also called a preserved railway or, in the United States, a tourist railroad or scenic railroad) is a railway operated as a living museum: a line of either local or historical interest that is maintained and run, usually by volunteers and enthusiasts, to preserve historic locomotives, rolling stock, stations and operating practices, while carrying fare-paying passengers as a visitor attraction.

History

The movement began in Britain with the Talyllyn Railway in Wales, whose preservation society took over the line in 1951 — often cited as the world's first volunteer-run preserved railway — and the Ffestiniog Railway soon followed. The closure of branch lines under the Beeching cuts of the 1960s and the end of mainline steam in 1968 prompted the rescue of many lines, including the Bluebell Railway, the Severn Valley Railway, the North Yorkshire Moors Railway and the West Somerset Railway. Parallel movements developed worldwide: in the United States, lines such as the Strasburg Rail Road and the Durango and Silverton Narrow Gauge Railroad carry tourists; Australia, New Zealand, India, Japan and continental Europe all have substantial heritage sectors. Some, like the Welsh Highland Railway, have been rebuilt over many miles from near-total dereliction.

Operation

Heritage railways typically rely on volunteers for everything from driving and firing steam locomotives to track maintenance, signalling and catering, funded by fares, shops, dining trains and special events such as steam galas and wartime weekends. They are subject to railway safety regulation in much the same way as the national network, though often with concessions reflecting their low speeds. Many also serve a practical transport function for their communities and connect with national rail stations.

Significance

Beyond nostalgia, heritage railways preserve industrial skills — boiler-making, machining, carriage restoration — that might otherwise be lost, conserve historic structures, and contribute significantly to local tourism economies. In the United Kingdom alone the sector comprises well over 200 lines and attracts millions of visitors annually.
