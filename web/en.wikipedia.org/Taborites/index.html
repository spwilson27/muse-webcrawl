Taborites

The Taborites (Czech: Táborité) were the radical faction of the Hussite movement in 15th-century Bohemia. Named after the town of Tábor, founded by Hussite refugees in 1420 and named for the biblical Mount Tabor, they advocated thorough religious and social reform, rejected much Catholic doctrine and practice, and became famous for their military successes during the Hussite Wars.

Beliefs and social programme

The Taborites demanded communion "in both kinds" (bread and wine) for laity, free preaching of the word of God, and the punishment of mortal sins — three of the Four Articles of Prague shared with moderate Hussites (Utraquists). They went further: rejecting transubstantiation, purgatory, prayers for the dead, auricular confession, and the veneration of saints and images. Early Tábor briefly experimented with communal property, members depositing goods in common chests, and its preaching carried millenarian expectations of an imminent end of the age.

Military history

Led first by the one-eyed general Jan Žižka, Taborite armies of peasants and townsmen pioneered the war-wagon fort and the combined use of field artillery, flails, and handguns, defeating five crusades proclaimed against Bohemia. After Žižka's death in 1424, leadership passed to Prokop the Great; Hussite forces carried the war into neighbouring lands in the "beautiful rides".

Decline

Divisions between radical Taborites and moderate Utraquists widened. On 30 May 1434 a combined Utraquist–Catholic army crushed the Taborites at the Battle of Lipany, where Prokop was killed. Tábor held out as a fortified community until 1452, when it submitted to George of Poděbrady. Taborite ideas survived in part through the Unity of the Brethren.
