Swallowing

Swallowing, also called deglutition, is the process in the human or animal body that allows a substance to pass from the mouth, through the pharynx, into the oesophagus, and ultimately into the stomach. It is a complex, partly voluntary and partly reflexive action involving the coordinated activity of numerous muscles and cranial nerves, and it occurs many hundreds of times a day, including during sleep.

Stages

Swallowing is usually described in stages. In the oral phase, which is voluntary, the tongue gathers chewed food, mixed with saliva, into a bolus and propels it to the back of the mouth. In the pharyngeal phase, which is involuntary, the soft palate seals the nasal passages, the larynx rises, the epiglottis and vocal folds close the airway to prevent aspiration, breathing momentarily pauses, and the bolus is driven into the oesophagus. In the oesophageal phase, peristaltic waves carry the bolus to the stomach.

Neural control

The pharyngeal and oesophageal phases are coordinated by a swallowing centre in the medulla of the brainstem, receiving sensory input via the trigeminal, glossopharyngeal and vagus nerves. Disorders of swallowing, known as dysphagia, can arise from stroke, neurological disease, or structural abnormalities, and may lead to aspiration pneumonia and malnutrition.

Clinical significance

Assessment of swallowing may involve bedside examination, videofluoroscopy or endoscopic evaluation. Management ranges from dietary modification and swallowing therapy, provided by speech and language therapists, to tube feeding in severe cases.
