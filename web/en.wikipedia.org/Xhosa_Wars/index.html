Xhosa Wars

The Xhosa Wars (also known as the Cape Frontier Wars or, in older Afrikaans historiography, the Kaffir Wars) were a series of nine conflicts between 1779 and 1879 between the Xhosa people and European settlers, first Dutch and then British, on the eastern frontier of the Cape Colony in present-day South Africa. They formed one of the longest-running colonial wars in African history and ended with the incorporation of Xhosa lands into the Cape Colony.

Background

By the late eighteenth century Trekboer pastoralists expanding eastward collided with Xhosa cattle farmers along the Great Fish River. Competition for grazing land, cattle raiding and reprisals escalated into open war in 1779. Each subsequent conflict pushed the frontier of colonial control eastward, to the Keiskamma and eventually the Great Kei.

The wars

The fourth war (1811–12) saw Colonel John Graham expel the Xhosa from the Zuurveld and found Grahamstown. In the fifth war (1818–19) the prophet-warrior Makhanda (Nxele) led a mass assault on Grahamstown that was repelled. The sixth war (1834–36), sparked by the killing of a chief, saw Xhosa forces sweep into the colony before Governor Harry Smith annexed territory as the "Province of Queen Adelaide", later abandoned. The seventh, the "War of the Axe" (1846–47), and the eighth (1850–53), in which the prophet Mlanjeni rallied resistance and some Khoi soldiers of the Cape Mounted Rifles rebelled, were long, gruelling guerrilla conflicts.

Between the eighth and ninth wars came the catastrophe of the cattle-killing movement of 1856–57: following the prophecies of Nongqawuse, the Xhosa slaughtered their herds and destroyed crops in expectation of a miraculous deliverance, producing a famine in which tens of thousands died and Xhosa society was shattered — Governor George Grey used the disaster to seize land and labour. The ninth and final war (1877–79) completed the annexation of the remaining independent chiefdoms.

Legacy

The wars dispossessed the Xhosa of most of their land and drew them into the colonial economy as labourers — a pattern of land loss that shaped South African history long afterward. Figures such as Makhanda and Nongqawuse remain potent symbols in South African memory and literature.
