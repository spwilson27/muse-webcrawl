Leibniz–Clarke correspondence

The Leibniz–Clarke correspondence was a celebrated exchange of letters in 1715–1716 between the philosopher Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz and Samuel Clarke, an English clergyman speaking substantially for Isaac Newton. It is a landmark in the philosophy of space, time and God.

Issues

Leibniz attacked the Newtonian ideas of absolute space and time, arguing space is merely the order of coexisting things and time the order of successive things — a relational view. He deployed his principles of sufficient reason and the identity of indiscernibles: God could have no reason to place the universe one metre to the left, so absolute position is meaningless.

Other topics

The letters range over gravity, vacuums, atoms, miracles and whether God must intervene to maintain the world — Leibniz mocked the Newtonian God as a watchmaker obliged to rewind his watch. The correspondence ended with Leibniz death in 1716 and was published the next year.
