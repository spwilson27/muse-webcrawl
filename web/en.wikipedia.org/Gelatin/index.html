Gelatin

Gelatin (or gelatine) is a translucent, colourless, flavourless food ingredient and gelling agent derived from collagen, the main structural protein of animal connective tissue. It is produced by the partial hydrolysis of collagen extracted from the skin, bones and connective tissues of animals such as cattle and pigs, and to a lesser extent from fish.

Production and properties

To make gelatin, collagen-rich raw materials are treated with acid (type A) or alkali (type B), then extracted with hot water, purified, concentrated and dried, yielding sheets, granules or powder. Gelatin dissolves in hot water and forms a thermoreversible gel on cooling, melting around body temperature — the "melt in the mouth" quality prized in foods. Its gelling strength is graded in Bloom units. Unlike most proteins it is low in the essential amino acid tryptophan and is not a complete protein.

Uses

  • Food: gummy sweets, marshmallows, jellies, mousses, aspics, yoghurts and desserts; as a stabiliser, thickener and clarifying agent (fining) in wine and juice.
  • Pharmaceuticals: hard and soft capsules, tablet coatings, and some plasma expanders and wound dressings.
  • Photography and industry: historically the binder in photographic film and paper emulsions; also in cosmetics and in the manufacture of glues.

Dietary considerations

Because it is animal-derived, gelatin is avoided by vegetarians and vegans and may raise issues under halal and kosher rules depending on source. Common plant-based alternatives include agar-agar, carrageenan and pectin. Fish gelatin is sometimes used to meet religious requirements.
