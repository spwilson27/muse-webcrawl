Cavalry (from French cavalerie, from Italian cavalleria, from Latin caballus, "horse") originally describes soldiers who fight on horseback. Historically, cavalry was the most mobile and often the most prestigious arm of armies, using speed for shock charges, reconnaissance, screening, pursuit of broken enemies, and raiding. In modern usage, many units designated "cavalry" fight with tanks, armoured vehicles, or helicopters rather than horses.

History

Ancient world

Mounted warfare emerged on the Eurasian steppes with peoples such as the Scythians and later the horse archers of Central Asia. Heavy cavalry developed with Alexander the Great's Companion cavalry, Parthian and Sasanian cataphracts (fully armoured rider and horse), and Roman and Byzantine mounted forces.

Medieval period

In medieval Europe, the armoured knight — supported by the stirrup, high saddle, and couched lance — dominated battlefields for centuries, while Mongol horse archers conquered much of Eurasia in the 13th century with disciplined, highly mobile armies.

Early modern and Napoleonic eras

Gunpowder shifted cavalry toward pistols and sabres (reiters, dragoons mounted infantry), but the Napoleonic Wars restored the massed shock charge by cuirassiers and lancers, exemplified at battles such as Austerlitz, Eylau, and Waterloo.

Decline and transformation

Rifled firearms, barbed wire, machine guns, and artillery made frontal charges suicidal by the American Civil War and especially the First World War, though cavalry remained useful on the Eastern Front and in colonial campaigns. The last major horse-cavalry actions occurred in the Second World War (e.g., Polish, Soviet, and Mongolian cavalry). Thereafter, cavalry roles passed to armour and aviation, while ceremonial mounted units survive in many countries.

Types

  • Heavy cavalry — cuirassiers and lancers, used for decisive shock action.
  • Light cavalry — hussars, chasseurs, cossacks, used for scouting, screening, and pursuit.
  • Dragoons — originally mounted infantry who rode to battle and fought on foot.
  • Armoured/air cavalry — modern successors using tanks, reconnaissance vehicles, and attack helicopters.
