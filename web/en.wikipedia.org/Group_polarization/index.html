Group polarization

FieldSocial psychology
Original name"Risky shift" (Stoner, 1961)
Term coinedSerge Moscovici and Marisa Zavalloni (1969)
Key mechanismsPersuasive arguments; social comparison

Group polarization is the tendency for a group to make decisions that are more extreme than the initial inclinations of its members. After discussion, a group's average position typically shifts toward whichever pole — risk or caution, praise or blame, hawkishness or dovishness — it already leaned toward, and individual members' views become more extreme as well.

Discovery

The phenomenon was first reported as the "risky shift" by James Stoner in 1961, who found that groups recommended riskier courses of action than individuals deciding alone. When later studies found shifts toward caution on other items, researchers recognised the general pattern: discussion polarises toward the pre-existing tendency. Serge Moscovici and Marisa Zavalloni supplied the modern term in 1969.

Explanations

Two mechanisms are best supported. Persuasive arguments theory holds that discussion pools arguments favouring the majority lean (since more members generate more such arguments), exposing each person to novel supporting reasons. Social comparison theory holds that people shift to match, and slightly outdo, the perceived group norm in order to be viewed favourably as committed group members. Self-categorisation theory adds that polarisation intensifies when the group norm is contrasted with an out-group.

Applications and debate

Group polarisation has been invoked to explain jury decisions and punitive damages, escalating political partisanship, corporate delusion ("boardroom polarisation"), radicalisation in extremist milieus, and online echo chambers and filter bubbles — Cass Sunstein's #Republic popularised the claim that like-minded online discussion drives political extremes, although empirical work shows the effect online is often weaker or more complicated than expected. Related phenomena include groupthink, the Abilene paradox and choice shift.
