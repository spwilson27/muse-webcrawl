Swahili people

The Swahili people (Swahili: Waswahili) are a Bantu ethnic group native to the Swahili coast of Southeast Africa, principally in present-day Kenya, Tanzania, northern Mozambique and the Zanzibar archipelago. Their name derives from the Arabic sawāḥil, meaning "coasts". They speak Swahili as their native language and have historically been a maritime, mercantile and overwhelmingly Muslim society.

Origins

The Swahili emerged from Bantu-speaking farming and fishing communities of the East African coast who, over many centuries, intermarried with and absorbed Arab, Persian and other traders from across the Indian Ocean. Their society developed around independent city-states such as Lamu, Mombasa, Kilwa and Zanzibar, each ruled by its own sultan or merchant elite.

Culture

Swahili culture blends African, Arab and Persian influences. Distinctive features include stone-built towns, carved wooden doors, dhow sailing vessels, taarab music, and a rich literary tradition of poetry in the Swahili language written in both Arabic and Latin scripts. Cuisine features coconut, spices, rice dishes such as pilau and biryani, and seafood. Dress includes the kanga and kitenge cloths and, for men, the kanzu robe and kofia cap.

Language

Swahili (Kiswahili) is a Bantu language whose vocabulary includes a large proportion of Arabic loanwords, with later borrowings from Persian, Portuguese, English and German. Though native speakers number in the millions, the language is used by well over a hundred million people as a lingua franca across East and Central Africa.
