Prehistoric Britain

Prehistoric Britain covers the period of human occupation of what is now Great Britain from the earliest evidence, around 900,000 years ago, until the Roman conquest in AD 43, after which written records about Britain become substantial. Several species of humans settled in Britain during the long alternation of glacial and interglacial periods.

Palaeolithic and Mesolithic

The earliest evidence of human occupation comes from Happisburgh in Norfolk, dating to about 900,000 years ago, and from Boxgrove in Sussex, where Homo heidelbergensis lived about 500,000 years ago. Neanderthals occupied Britain intermittently, and anatomically modern humans were present by about 40,000 years ago (the "Red Lady" of Paviland). After the last glacial maximum, Britain was resettled from about 12,000 years ago; rising seas submerged Doggerland and separated Britain from continental Europe around 8,200–6,500 years ago.

Neolithic and Bronze Age

Farming arrived around 4000 BC, bringing domesticated crops and animals, pottery, causewayed enclosures, long barrows and eventually great monuments: Stonehenge and Avebury, the passage tombs of Orkney (including Skara Brae, the best-preserved Neolithic village in northern Europe), and thousands of stone circles. Around 2500 BC the Beaker culture introduced metalworking; genetic studies show a large-scale population replacement associated with its spread. The Bronze Age saw round barrows, hoards, hillforts' predecessors and trade in tin from Cornwall across Europe.

Iron Age

From about 800 BC, iron-using Celtic-speaking peoples built hillforts such as Maiden Castle, produced fine metalwork and coinage, and traded with the Roman world before the conquest. Julius Caesar raided Britain in 55 and 54 BC; Emperor Claudius's invasion of AD 43 ended British prehistory as written sources take over.
