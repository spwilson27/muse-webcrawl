Flight and expulsion of Germans (1944–1950)

During the later stages of World War II and the post-war period, Germans and Volksdeutsche fled and were expelled from various Eastern and Central European countries, including Czechoslovakia and the former German provinces of Silesia, Pomerania, and East Prussia, which were annexed by Poland and the Soviet Union. The idea to expel the Germans from the annexed territories had been proposed by Winston Churchill, in conjunction with the Polish and Czechoslovak exile governments in London at least since 1942.

Between 1944 and 1950, an estimated 12 to 14.6 million Germans fled or were expelled, making it the largest movement of a single ethnic population in modern history. The events have been described variously as a population transfer, ethnic cleansing, and — by some historians — a demicide. Estimates of deaths attributable to the expulsions range from about 500,000 to 2 million.

History

Flight and evacuation, 1944–1945

As the Red Army advanced westward in 1944 and 1945, millions of German civilians in the eastern provinces fled before it, fearing reprisals for Nazi crimes and encouraged by reports of Soviet atrocities such as the killings at Nemmersdorf. The Nazi authorities organized partial evacuations, but many civilians were overtaken by the fighting. The sinking of the refugee ship Wilhelm Gustloff in January 1945, with over 9,000 deaths, remains the deadliest single maritime disaster in history.

"Wild" expulsions, 1945

In the months immediately after the war, before the Potsdam Conference, local authorities in Poland and Czechoslovakia carried out so-called "wild" expulsions, driving Germans across the Oder–Neisse line and out of the Sudetenland, often under brutal conditions.

Potsdam Agreement and organized transfers

Article XIII of the Potsdam Agreement of August 1945 stated that the transfer of German populations from Poland, Czechoslovakia, and Hungary "will have to be undertaken" and should be carried out "in an orderly and humane manner." Organized transports followed in 1946–1947, though conditions in transit and in internment camps remained frequently lethal.

Aftermath

The expellees (Vertriebene) were absorbed into the populations of West and East Germany, where they formed a politically influential constituency. The Federal Expellee Law of 1953 granted them citizenship rights and integration assistance. The events remain a contested subject in German, Polish, and Czech historical memory.
