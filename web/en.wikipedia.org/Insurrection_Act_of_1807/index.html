Insurrection Act of 1807

Enacted10 March 1807
Signed byPresident Thomas Jefferson
Codification10 U.S.C. §§ 251–255
PurposeAuthorises the President to deploy the military domestically in specified emergencies

The Insurrection Act of 1807 is a United States federal law that empowers the President of the United States to deploy US military and federalised National Guard troops within the United States in particular circumstances, such as to suppress civil disorder, insurrection or rebellion. It is the principal exception to the Posse Comitatus Act of 1878, which otherwise limits the use of federal troops for domestic law enforcement.

Provisions

As codified at 10 U.S.C. §§ 251–255, the Act permits federal military intervention: at the request of a state's legislature or governor to suppress an insurrection against the state (§251); where unlawful obstructions make it impracticable to enforce federal law by ordinary judicial proceedings (§252); and where domestic violence deprives citizens of constitutional rights and the state is unable or unwilling to protect them (§253). Section 254 requires the President to issue a proclamation ordering the insurgents to disperse before deploying forces.

History

The original 1807 act, signed by Thomas Jefferson amid fears of Aaron Burr's conspiracy, extended to regular federal forces the militia-calling authority Congress had granted in the Militia Acts of 1792 and 1795. Amendments after the Civil War — notably the Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871, which added the rights-protection provision — broadened its use against the Ku Klux Klan during Reconstruction.

Invocations

Presidents have invoked the Act on a number of occasions, including to enforce desegregation: Dwight D. Eisenhower sent the 101st Airborne to Little Rock, Arkansas in 1957, and John F. Kennedy federalised the Mississippi and Alabama National Guards in 1962–1963. It was invoked during the Detroit riot of 1967 and, at the request of California's governor, during the 1992 Los Angeles riots. In 2006 an amendment briefly expanded presidential authority over the National Guard for natural disasters and emergencies, but it was repealed in 2008 after opposition from state governors. Debates over reforming the Act — to add congressional notification, time limits or consultation requirements — recur whenever its possible invocation is publicly discussed.
