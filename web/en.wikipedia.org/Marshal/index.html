Marshal

Marshal is a term used in several official titles in various branches of society and government in many countries, originally designating a head groom or stable master. Derived from Old High German marahscalh, "horse-servant", it rose through the medieval royal household to become one of the highest military ranks and, in other contexts, a senior law-enforcement or ceremonial office.

Military rank

In France, the Marshal of France (Maréchal de France) was the kingdom's foremost military dignity, symbolised by a baton; Napoleon revived the title for his Marshals of the Empire. Equivalent senior ranks include the British field marshal, the German Generalfeldmarschall, the Marshal of the Soviet Union, and the Marshal of Poland. In many armies a marshal outranks all generals and the rank is often reserved for wartime or honorary appointments.

Law enforcement and other uses

In the United States, marshals of the United States Marshals Service (created 1789) enforce federal courts, protect judges, transport prisoners and pursue fugitives; "marshal" is also used for some state, county and town officers, fire marshals, and air marshals. In England, the Earl Marshal is a hereditary royal office held by the Duke of Norfolk, responsible for state ceremonies. The title also denotes officials who organise processions and races (a race marshal or parade marshal, including the honorary Grand Marshal).
