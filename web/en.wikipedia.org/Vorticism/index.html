Vorticism


Years active: 1914–1915 (effectively)
Location: London, England
Key figures: Wyndham Lewis, Ezra Pound
Journal: BLAST

Vorticism was a London-based modernist art movement formed in 1914 by the artist and writer Wyndham Lewis. It is considered the only significant British avant-garde movement of the early twentieth century, and it drew energy from Cubism and Italian Futurism while insisting on its own identity.

Name and manifesto

The poet Ezra Pound coined the name. The "vortex" was, in Pound's phrase, "the point of maximum energy" — a still centre of concentrated force around which modern life whirled. The movement announced itself in the first issue of its journal BLAST, published in June 1914, whose aggressive typography and manifestos "blasted" Victorian culture and "blessed" the machine age.

Style

Vorticist painting and sculpture favoured hard-edged, angular, semi-abstract forms that suggested machines, industry and modern urban life, composed with a sense of dynamic structure held around a calm centre. Leading practitioners included Lewis, William Roberts, Edward Wadsworth, Frederick Etchells, Jessica Dismorr, Dorothy Shakespear, Helen Saunders, and the sculptors Jacob Epstein and Henri Gaudier-Brzeska.

History

The group exhibited together once, at the Doré Gallery in London in 1915; a second issue of BLAST, the "War Number", appeared in July 1915. The First World War destroyed the movement: Gaudier-Brzeska was killed at the front in 1915, others were scattered into war service, and postwar efforts to revive the style under the name "Group X" in 1920 came to little. Lewis's later memoirs, including Blasting and Bombardiering (1937), remain key accounts of the movement.
