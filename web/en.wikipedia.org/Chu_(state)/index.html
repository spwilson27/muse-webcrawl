Chu (state)

Chu (Chinese: 楚) was a powerful state in ancient China during the Zhou dynasty, existing from roughly the eleventh century BC until 223 BC. Centred on the middle Yangtze River valley in what is now Hubei and Hunan, Chu grew from a small viscounty on the Zhou periphery into one of the great powers of the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods, and was among the last states conquered in Qin's unification of China.

History

According to tradition, the Chu ruling house descended from the mythical Zhuanxu and was enfeoffed under King Cheng of Zhou. Long regarded as semi-foreign by the Zhou heartland states, Chu expanded steadily, absorbing dozens of smaller states along the Yangtze and Han rivers. Under kings such as King Zhuang (r. 613–591 BC), reckoned one of the Five Hegemons, Chu contested supremacy with Jin. Its kings early on assumed the royal title in defiance of Zhou protocol.

Culture

Chu developed a distinctive culture, famous for its shamanistic religious practices, elaborate lacquerware and bronze work, silk textiles, and literature. The Chu Ci ("Songs of Chu"), associated with the poet Qu Yuan, is one of the two great anthologies of early Chinese poetry. Chu tombs, such as the fourth-century BC tomb at Baoshan, have yielded rich manuscripts, including early versions of Taoist texts.

Fall

Weakened by court corruption, the failed reforms of Wu Qi, and the loss of its capital Ying to Qin in 278 BC, Chu declined through the third century BC. Its vast territory made it the last major obstacle to Qin; despite a final massive mobilisation under the general Xiang Yan, Chu was conquered by the Qin general Wang Jian in 223 BC. The Chu legacy survived: the leaders who overthrew the Qin dynasty after 209 BC, including Liu Bang and Xiang Yu, came from the Chu region.
