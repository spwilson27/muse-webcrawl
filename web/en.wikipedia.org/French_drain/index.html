French drain
Also calledWeeping tile, trench drain, rubble drain
Named forHenry Flagg French (1813–1885)
ComponentsTrench, gravel, perforated pipe, geotextile
UsesGroundwater drainage, foundation protection

A French drain (also called a weeping tile, trench drain, or rubble drain) is a trench filled with gravel or rock containing a perforated pipe that collects groundwater and redirects it away from an area. French drains are used to prevent ground and surface water from penetrating or damaging building foundations, to relieve water pressure behind retaining walls, and to distribute water from septic systems.

Etymology

Despite the name, the drain is not French in origin. It is named after Henry Flagg French, a Massachusetts farmer and drainage advocate whose 1859 book Farm Drainage popularized the technique in the United States. Similar rubble-filled drains had been used in agriculture for centuries.

Construction

A typical installation involves digging a sloped trench (commonly at least 1% gradient), lining it with water-permeable landscape fabric (geotextile) to keep out silt and roots, laying a perforated pipe with its holes oriented downward or upward (practice varies), and backfilling with washed gravel. The pipe discharges at a lower outlet, a dry well, or a sump with a pump. Interior basement systems run a similar channel below the floor slab, feeding a sump basin.

Maintenance and considerations

French drains can clog over time with sediment and roots, reducing effectiveness; filter fabric and proper gravel selection extend service life. Some jurisdictions regulate where collected water may be discharged, and drains must not route water onto neighbouring property.
