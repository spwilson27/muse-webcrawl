Kin selection

Kin selection
FieldEvolutionary biology, sociobiology
Key formulationW. D. Hamilton (1963–1964)

ExplainsAltruism toward relatives, sterile castes in eusocial insects
Related conceptInclusive fitness

Kin selection is an evolutionary strategy in which organisms favour the reproductive success of their relatives, even at a cost to their own survival and reproduction. It is a central component of the theory of inclusive fitness, proposed by W. D. Hamilton in 1963–64, which resolved the longstanding puzzle of how seemingly altruistic behaviour — paradigmatically, the sterile worker castes of ants, bees, and wasps — could evolve by natural selection. The term "kin selection" was coined by John Maynard Smith.

Hamilton's rule

Hamilton showed that a gene for altruistic behaviour will spread when rB > C, where C is the reproductive cost to the actor, B is the reproductive benefit to the recipient, and r is the coefficient of relatedness — the probability that actor and recipient share the gene by common descent (0.5 for full siblings and offspring, 0.25 for half-siblings and grandchildren, 0.125 for cousins). J. B. S. Haldane had anticipated the idea with the quip that he would lay down his life for two brothers or eight cousins.

Evidence

Classic cases include worker sterility in the Hymenoptera — whose haplodiploid sex determination makes sisters related by 0.75, unusually high — alarm calling in Belding's ground squirrels, cooperative breeding in birds such as Florida scrub-jays, and helping in social mammals like meerkats and naked mole-rats, one of only two eusocial mammal groups. Kin recognition mechanisms — familiarity, spatial association, and phenotype matching — allow many species to direct help preferentially to relatives.

Extensions and debate

The inclusive-fitness framework also predicts conflict within families: parent–offspring conflict (Robert Trivers, 1974), sibling rivalry, and sex-ratio manipulation in social insects, all broadly borne out empirically. A 2010 paper by Martin Nowak, Corina Tarnita, and E. O. Wilson challenged the necessity of inclusive-fitness theory, provoking a vigorous defence signed by over a hundred researchers; kin selection remains a standard explanatory framework, generally regarded as mathematically compatible with multilevel-selection approaches. The "green-beard effect" — a gene that both signals itself and directs aid to bearers — illustrates a rare alternative route to altruism.

Human behaviour

Kin selection is invoked in evolutionary psychology to explain patterns of inheritance, nepotism, grandparental investment, and the greater altruism shown to closer relatives across cultures, though the extent to which human sociality depends on reciprocity, culture, and institutions beyond kinship remains debated.
