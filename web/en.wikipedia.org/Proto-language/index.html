Proto-language

A proto-language is the reconstructed common ancestor of a language family.

In the tree model of historical linguistics, a proto-language is a language that was the common ancestor of a related set of languages that form a language family. Proto-languages are usually unattested — no direct records survive — and are reconstructed by comparing their descendants using the comparative method. In linguistic notation, reconstructed forms are marked with an asterisk (for example, Proto-Indo-European *ph₂tḗr, "father").

Well-known proto-languages include Proto-Indo-European, Proto-Uralic, Proto-Bantu, Proto-Austronesian and Proto-Semitic. Some proto-languages are attested in part: Proto-Romance, for instance, is largely approximated by Vulgar Latin, though the reconstruction and the attested language do not coincide exactly.

Reconstruction

The comparative method identifies systematic sound correspondences between cognate words in related languages and infers the ancestral sounds that explain them. Internal reconstruction, which exploits alternations within a single language, can supplement the picture. Reconstructions are hypotheses: they approximate the prehistoric language but omit variation, and features that left no trace in any daughter language are in principle irrecoverable.

Nested proto-languages

Families typically contain intermediate proto-languages at successive depths — for example Proto-Indo-European, then Proto-Balto-Slavic, then Proto-Slavic, then the individual Slavic languages. Each level is the most recent common ancestor of a subgroup, and the deeper the node, the greater the uncertainty of the reconstruction.

Terminology

When a proto-language is attested rather than reconstructed, linguists may simply call it the ancestral language. The term is also applied metaphorically outside linguistics — for instance to hypothetical ancestors of writing systems or creole languages — but its technical home is historical linguistics.
