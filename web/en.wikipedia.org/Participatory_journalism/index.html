Participatory journalism

Participatory journalism
SynonymsCitizen journalism, user-generated content (UGC), grassroots journalism
Key concept"The people formerly known as the audience" (Jay Rosen)
Early exemplarsIndymedia (1999); OhmyNews (2000); blogs and forums
Landmark bookWe the Media (Dan Gillmor, 2004)

Participatory journalism is the act of citizens "playing an active role in the process of collecting, reporting, analyzing and disseminating news and information", in the definition given by Shayne Bowman and Chris Willis in their 2003 report We Media. Enabled by inexpensive digital technology, the internet and social media, it blurs the traditional boundary between professional news producers and their audiences, and overlaps with the related concepts of citizen journalism, user-generated content and collaborative (wiki-based) news production.

History

Although members of the public have always contributed letters, photographs and eyewitness accounts to newspapers, participatory journalism as a mass phenomenon emerged with the web. The Independent Media Center (Indymedia), founded during the 1999 Seattle WTO protests with the slogan "Don't hate the media, become the media", pioneered open publishing. South Korea's OhmyNews (2000) built a news site largely on contributions from tens of thousands of registered "citizen reporters", and Dan Gillmor's We the Media (2004) and Jay Rosen's writings theorised the shift: the audience, equipped with publishing tools, was no longer passive. Mainstream outlets followed by adding comment threads, reader blogs, "have your say" features and dedicated UGC desks; events such as the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami and the 7 July 2005 London bombings were documented first and most vividly by bystanders with camera phones.

Forms

  • Commentary and discussion — comments, forums and social-media reactions attached to professional news.
  • Original reporting — eyewitness text, photos and video from the scene of events.
  • Crowdsourcing and open-source investigation — distributed fact-checking, geolocation and document analysis, as practised by groups such as Bellingcat.
  • Collaborative platforms — wikis and community-moderated sites such as Wikinews and Reddit's news communities.

Criticism and challenges

Critics, including journalists such as Andrew Keen, raise concerns about accuracy, the absence of professional verification and ethics, susceptibility to manipulation and "fake news", and the unpaid exploitation of contributors' labour. Verification of user content — confirming time, place and authenticity of images — became a professional specialism in response. Supporters counter that participation democratises the news agenda, surfaces stories and viewpoints ignored by legacy media, and provides crucial documentation where professional journalists are absent or barred.
