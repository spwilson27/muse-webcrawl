Share repurchase

Share buybacks by S&P 500 companies grew sharply from the 1980s onward.

Share repurchase, also known as a share buyback or stock buyback, is the re-acquisition by a company of its own shares. It represents an alternative to dividends as a way of returning money to shareholders; when a company repurchases shares, the number of shares outstanding falls, increasing earnings per share and typically the value of each remaining share.

Repurchases may be executed through open-market purchases, tender offers at a fixed or auction-determined price, or privately negotiated transactions. In the United States, large-scale buybacks became common after the Securities and Exchange Commission adopted Rule 10b-18 in 1982, which created a safe harbour protecting open-market repurchases from market-manipulation liability.

Motives and criticism

Firms cite motives including returning surplus cash, offsetting dilution from employee stock options, signalling that management considers the shares undervalued, and increasing financial flexibility (buybacks, unlike dividends, create no expectation of continuation). Critics, including Senator Elizabeth Warren and others, argue that buybacks enrich executives and shareholders at the expense of investment, wages, and research; the US Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 imposed a 1% excise tax on net repurchases by large corporations, effective 2023. Total annual buybacks by S&P 500 companies have approached or exceeded one trillion US dollars in peak years.
