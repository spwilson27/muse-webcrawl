Non-cognitivism

Non-cognitivism is the meta-ethical view that ethical sentences do not express propositions and therefore cannot be true or false. Whereas cognitivists hold that moral statements are beliefs that purport to describe reality (and can be mistaken), non-cognitivists hold that moral utterances instead express non-cognitive states such as emotions, attitudes, preferences or commands — states that are not the kind of thing that can be true or false.

Varieties

Emotivism, associated with A. J. Ayer and C. L. Stevenson, holds that a statement like "stealing is wrong" functions like saying "Boo to stealing!" — it expresses disapproval and seeks to influence others rather than stating a fact. Prescriptivism, developed by R. M. Hare, treats moral judgements as universal prescriptions — a kind of imperative that the speaker applies consistently to all relevantly similar cases. Expressivism and Allan Gibbard's norm-expressivism are more recent developments; Gibbard argues that moral judgements express acceptance of a system of norms. Simon Blackburn's quasi-realism attempts to explain how moral discourse can "earn the right" to talk of truth and facts while starting from an expressivist base.

Motivations

Non-cognitivism is often motivated by David Hume's observation that moral judgements seem inherently motivating, whereas beliefs alone do not move us to act (the Humean theory of motivation), and by scepticism about the existence of the objective moral facts that cognitivism appears to require — the "queerness" argument later pressed by J. L. Mackie in favour of the related error theory.

Objections

The best-known difficulty is the Frege–Geach problem: moral sentences occur unembedded in conditionals and other logical contexts ("If stealing is wrong, then encouraging stealing is wrong"), where they do not obviously express any attitude, yet must keep a constant meaning for the inference to be valid. Non-cognitivists have proposed sophisticated semantic theories to answer this. Critics also argue that non-cognitivism struggles to account for moral disagreement and moral reasoning.
