North American Free Trade Agreement


Labelled image: NAFTA member states

The North American Free Trade Agreement (NAFTA) was an agreement signed by Canada, Mexico, and the United States that created a trilateral trade bloc in North America. The agreement came into force on January 1, 1994, and superseded the 1988 Canada–United States Free Trade Agreement. It eliminated most tariffs on products traded among the three countries over a fifteen-year transition period.

History

Negotiations began in 1991 under Presidents George H. W. Bush and Carlos Salinas de Gortari and Prime Minister Brian Mulroney. In the United States the agreement was fiercely debated: presidential candidate Ross Perot warned of a "giant sucking sound" of jobs moving to Mexico, while the Clinton administration negotiated labor and environmental side agreements (NAALC and NAAEC) to secure congressional passage in 1993. NAFTA established mechanisms including Chapter 11 investor-state dispute settlement and Chapter 19 panels reviewing antidumping and countervailing duty determinations.

Effects and criticism

Trade among the three countries roughly quadrupled in nominal terms, supply chains — especially in automobiles — became deeply integrated across the continent, and Mexican exports and foreign investment grew substantially. Critics attributed declines in some US manufacturing regions and pressure on Mexican small farmers (notably corn producers competing with subsidized US imports) to the agreement; economists generally found modest aggregate effects on the US economy but significant sectoral and local disruption.

Replacement

During his presidency, Donald Trump sought to renegotiate or withdraw from NAFTA. The resulting United States–Mexico–Canada Agreement (USMCA) was signed in 2018 and entered into force on July 1, 2020, replacing NAFTA with updated rules on automobiles, dairy access, digital trade, labor, and a sixteen-year sunset clause with periodic review.
