Nucleotide excision repair

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nucleotide excision repair (NER) is a particularly important DNA repair mechanism that removes bulky, helix-distorting lesions from DNA, including the cyclobutane pyrimidine dimers and (6-4) photoproducts caused by ultraviolet light, as well as adducts produced by some chemical carcinogens. Unlike base excision repair, which handles individual damaged bases, NER excises a short single-stranded segment — about 24–32 nucleotides in eukaryotes — containing the lesion, then fills the gap using the undamaged strand as a template.

Mechanism

The pathway proceeds in ordered steps: (1) recognition of the lesion, either anywhere in the genome (global genome NER, initiated by the XPC protein complex sensing helix distortion) or when RNA polymerase stalls at damage in transcribed genes (transcription-coupled NER, involving the CSB and CSA proteins); (2) unwinding of the DNA around the lesion by the TFIIH helicase; (3) verification and assembly of the repair complex; (4) dual incision of the damaged strand by the XPG and ERCC1–XPF endonucleases; (5) excision of the fragment; and (6) repair synthesis by DNA polymerase and sealing by ligase.

Clinical significance

Inherited defects in NER cause serious human diseases. Xeroderma pigmentosum (XP) results from mutations in the XPA–XPG genes and produces extreme sun sensitivity and a hugely elevated incidence of skin cancer, because UV damage cannot be repaired. Cockayne syndrome, caused by defects in transcription-coupled repair (CSA/CSB), produces growth failure, neurodegeneration, and premature ageing without the same cancer predisposition. Trichothiodystrophy, linked to mutations in TFIIH subunits, combines brittle hair with developmental abnormalities. The pathway was elucidated in bacteria in the 1960s by Richard Setlow and others, and its abc excinuclease (UvrABC) system remains a classic model of damage recognition.
