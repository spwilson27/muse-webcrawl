Digestion is the biological process by which organisms break down food into smaller components that can be absorbed into the body and used for energy, growth and repair. It combines mechanical digestion — chewing, churning and segmentation — with chemical digestion, in which enzymes hydrolyse macromolecules: carbohydrates into sugars, proteins into amino acids, and fats into fatty acids and glycerol. In humans and other vertebrates, digestion takes place in the gastrointestinal tract, a specialised tube supplemented by accessory glands (salivary glands, liver, gallbladder and pancreas).

Phases in humans

Oral and gastric phases

Digestion begins in the mouth, where teeth masticate food and salivary amylase begins starch breakdown. The bolus passes down the oesophagus by peristalsis to the stomach, where hydrochloric acid and pepsin denature and cleave proteins, and churning converts food into chyme. Sight, smell and thought of food trigger the cephalic phase of gastric secretion via the vagus nerve.

Intestinal phase

In the duodenum, chyme is mixed with bile (which emulsifies fats), pancreatic juice (rich in amylase, lipase, proteases and bicarbonate) and intestinal secretions. Most chemical digestion and nearly all nutrient absorption occur in the small intestine, whose surface area is multiplied by folds, villi and microvilli. Monosaccharides and amino acids enter the blood capillaries; fatty acids and fat-soluble vitamins are packaged into chylomicrons and enter the lymph. In the large intestine, gut bacteria ferment residual fibre — producing short-chain fatty acids, vitamin K and some B vitamins — and water and electrolytes are absorbed, leaving faeces to be stored and expelled.

Hormonal and neural control

The enteric nervous system and gut hormones coordinate digestion: gastrin stimulates gastric acid; secretin prompts bicarbonate release; cholecystokinin triggers gallbladder contraction and pancreatic enzyme secretion; ghrelin and leptin modulate appetite. The gut microbiome, concentrated in the colon, is now recognised as a major contributor to metabolism and immunity.

Other animals

Digestive systems vary widely: ruminants ferment cellulose in a multi-chambered stomach; birds grind food in a gizzard; many invertebrates digest intracellularly or in a simple gastrovascular cavity; and carnivorous plants secrete enzymes externally to absorb nutrients from prey.

Disorders

Common digestive disorders include gastro-oesophageal reflux disease, peptic ulcers (often caused by Helicobacter pylori), coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, gallstones, pancreatitis and malabsorption syndromes.
