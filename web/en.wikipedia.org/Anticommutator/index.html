Anticommutator

The anticommutator {A, B} = AB + BA, symmetric counterpart of the commutator

In mathematics and mathematical physics, the anticommutator of two operators (or elements of an algebra) A and B is defined by

{A, B} = AB + BA

It is the symmetric counterpart of the commutator [A, B] = AB − BA. Two elements are said to anticommute when their anticommutator vanishes, i.e. AB = −BA. Note that AB + BA can be split into the combinations AB = ½([A,B] + {A,B}) that decompose a product into antisymmetric and symmetric parts.

Properties

The anticommutator is commutative in its arguments: {A, B} = {B, A}. It satisfies identities such as associating with products: {A, BC} = {A, B}C − B[A, C]. Repeated anticommutation relations underlie several important algebraic structures:

  • Jordan algebras arise from the symmetrised product ½{A, B} of associative algebras.
  • Clifford algebras are defined by the anticommutation relation {γμ, γν} = 2ημν of the gamma matrices.
  • Pauli matrices satisfy {σi, σj} = 2δij.

Role in quantum theory

Anticommutation relations are fundamental in quantum field theory: fermionic creation and annihilation operators obey canonical anticommutation relations {a, a†} = 1, which implement the Pauli exclusion principle and distinguish fermions from bosons (whose operators obey commutation relations instead). The anticommutator also appears in supersymmetry, where supercharges obey anticommutation relations with each other that close on the Hamiltonian.
