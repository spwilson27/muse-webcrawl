Matroid

Abstract structure capturing linear independence

In mathematics, a matroid is a structure that abstracts and generalises the notion of linear independence in vector spaces and of acyclicity in graphs. A matroid consists of a finite ground set E together with a collection of subsets of E called independent sets, required to satisfy three axioms: the empty set is independent; every subset of an independent set is independent; and if one independent set is larger than another, some element of the larger can be added to the smaller while preserving independence (the exchange axiom).

Examples and concepts

The maximal independent sets of a matroid, called bases, all have the same size, the rank of the matroid. Minimal dependent sets are called circuits. Key examples include vector configurations (the columns of a matrix), forests of a graph (graphic matroids), matchings (transversal matroids) and uniform matroids. The Fano matroid, from the seven-point Fano plane, cannot be represented by vectors over the real numbers.

History and applications

Matroids were introduced by Hassler Whitney in 1935 to unify properties of graphs and matrices. They underpin the theory of greedy algorithms: a classic theorem (Rado, Edmonds) shows that the greedy algorithm optimises a linear weight function over the independent sets of a combinatorial structure exactly when that structure is a matroid. Matroid theory also connects to coding theory, algebraic geometry and optimisation.
