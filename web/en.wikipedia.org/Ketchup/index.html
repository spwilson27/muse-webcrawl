Ketchup

Ketchup or catsup is a sweet and tangy table condiment, in modern times made almost exclusively from tomatoes with vinegar, sugar, salt and spices. It is one of the most popular condiments in the world, widely served with foods such as french fries, hamburgers and hot dogs.

History

The word "ketchup" derives from a Chinese fermented fish sauce — kê-tsiap in Hokkien — which reached Britain via Southeast Asia in the 17th and 18th centuries. Early British "ketchups" were thin sauces made from mushrooms, walnuts, oysters or anchovies, without tomatoes. Tomato ketchup recipes appeared in the United States in the early 19th century, and the condiment was industrialised later that century; Henry J. Heinz began selling his tomato ketchup in 1876, and the Heinz company remains emblematic of the product. In the early 1980s the U.S. Department of Agriculture's proposal to classify ketchup as a vegetable in school lunches provoked public controversy and was withdrawn.

Properties

Ketchup is a non-Newtonian, shear-thinning fluid: it flows more easily when shaken or squeezed, famously slowly from a glass bottle — the Heinz slogan "good things come to those who wait" alluded to a pour speed of about 0.028 miles per hour. Modern recipes are typically cooked from tomato concentrate and contain roughly 20–25% sugar in some commercial versions.
