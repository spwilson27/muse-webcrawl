Found footage (film technique)

Not to be confused with found footage (appropriation), the reuse of pre-existing film material.

Found footage
[Image: a handheld camcorder viewfinder frame]
Type: Film technique / genre device
Era: 1980–present
Key example: The Blair Witch Project (1999)
Common in: Horror, science fiction, thriller

Found footage is a cinematic technique and film genre in which all or a substantial part of the work is presented as if it were film or video recordings discovered after the events depicted — "found" footage. The events on screen are typically seen through the camera of one or more of the characters, often accompanied by their real-time, off-camera commentary. The technique creates a sense of realism and immediacy, and is frequently used in horror films, where the shaky, imperfect camerawork heightens tension and obscures the threat.

History

The device has early antecedents in epistolary fiction and in films that imitate documentary evidence, but its modern form is usually traced to Cannibal Holocaust (1980), which presented itself as recovered footage of a missing documentary crew. The technique achieved mainstream breakthrough with The Blair Witch Project (1999), an enormous commercial success made on a tiny budget, whose marketing famously blurred the line between fiction and reality. The success of Paranormal Activity (2007) launched a wave of low-budget found-footage horror in the 2010s, alongside entries in other genres such as the science-fiction film Cloverfield (2008) and the superhero film Chronicle (2012).

Characteristics

  • The camera exists within the story world, and characters acknowledge or operate it.
  • Footage is presented as recovered, edited, or released after the events.
  • Handheld camerawork, night vision, CCTV, webcams, and dashcams are common recording devices.
  • The narrative is often framed by titles or epilogues explaining the footage's discovery.

Criticism

Critics have noted the technique's overuse in low-budget horror, the implausibility of characters continuing to film during danger, and motion sickness caused by unstable camerawork. Nevertheless, the format remains popular because it is inexpensive to produce and lends immediacy to fantastic events.
