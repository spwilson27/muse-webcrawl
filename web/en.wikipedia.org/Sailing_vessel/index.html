Sailing vessel

A sailing vessel is a boat or ship that uses sails to harness the power of the wind for propulsion. Sailing craft were the principal means of maritime transport, trade, warfare, and exploration from antiquity until the nineteenth century, when steam power gradually displaced them in commercial service. Today sailing vessels survive mainly in recreational sailing, sail training, racing, and a small number of specialised roles, though interest in wind-assisted propulsion for cargo ships has revived in response to fuel costs and emissions concerns.

History

The earliest sailing vessels appear in Egyptian art of the fourth millennium BCE, with a single square sail on a river craft. Mediterranean peoples developed the lateen sail, allowing better progress to windward, while Chinese shipwrights perfected the battened lug or "junk" rig. By the fifteenth century, European carracks and caravels combined square and lateen sails on multiple masts, enabling the voyages of exploration that linked the world's oceans. The following centuries produced the galleon, the ship of the line, the clipper, and finally the great steel-hulled windjammers of the late nineteenth century, some carrying five masts and thousands of tons of cargo under sail alone.

Rig types

Sailing vessels are classified by their rig — the arrangement of masts and sails. Square-rigged vessels carry sails set across the hull on yards and excel before the wind; they include brigs, barques, and full-rigged ships. Fore-and-aft rigged vessels — sloops, cutters, ketches, yawls, and schooners — carry sails aligned with the keel and can sail closer to the wind, making them handier and requiring smaller crews. The lateen rig, with its triangular sail on a long angled yard, is characteristic of the Mediterranean and of dhows in the Indian Ocean.

How sailing works

A sail acts as a wing: wind flowing across its curved surface generates lift, whose forward component drives the vessel, while the keel or centreboard resists sideways drift (leeway). By tacking — zig-zagging at an angle to the wind — a vessel can make progress almost directly upwind. Skilled handling of sail trim, rudder, and ballast keeps a vessel upright and efficient in conditions from calm to storm.

Decline and legacy

Steamships offered schedules independent of weather and steadily took over passenger, mail, and high-value cargo trades in the nineteenth century. Sailing ships lingered in bulk trades such as grain, nitrate, and wool into the early twentieth century — the last commercial square-riggers, the Flying P-Liners and the grain racers from Australia, sailed into the 1930s and 1940s. Their legacy endures in sail-training fleets, classic yacht racing, and modern rotor-sail and kite-assisted cargo experiments.
