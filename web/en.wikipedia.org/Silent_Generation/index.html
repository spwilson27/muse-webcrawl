Silent Generation

The Silent Generation, also known as the Traditionalist Generation, is the Western demographic cohort following the Greatest Generation and preceding the baby boomers. The generation is generally defined as people born from 1928 to 1945. The term originated in a 1951 Time magazine article describing the younger generation of the day as quiet, cautious and conformist compared with their parents.

Characteristics

Members of the Silent Generation were children of the Great Depression and the Second World War. Too young to have fought in that war — though some served in the Korean War — they came of age during the prosperity of the 1950s and the era of McCarthyism, experiences often credited with shaping a generation said to value security, discipline, and working within the system rather than protesting against it.

Despite the stereotype, the generation produced many of the leaders of the civil rights movement, 1960s counterculture figures, and leading artists and musicians, including Martin Luther King Jr., Malcolm X, and much of the rock-and-roll generation. Demographically it was a relatively small cohort, owing to the low birth rates of the Depression and war years, and it became known as the "silent" generation partly because of its comparatively low numbers in public life; notably, no U.S. president was born in its core range until Joe Biden (born 1942), often counted among its youngest members.
