T helper cell

The T helper cells (Th cells), also known as CD4+ T cells, are a type of T cell that play an important role in the adaptive immune system. They aid the activity of other immune cells by releasing cytokines. These cells help suppress or regulate immune responses, and they are essential in B cell antibody class switching, breaking immunological tolerance, in the activation and growth of cytotoxic T cells, and in maximising the bactericidal activity of phagocytes such as macrophages and neutrophils.

Activation

T helper cells become activated when presented with peptide antigens by MHC class II molecules on the surface of antigen-presenting cells such as dendritic cells. Once activated, they divide rapidly and secrete cytokines that regulate or assist the immune response.

Subtypes

Activated helper cells differentiate into functional subsets with characteristic cytokine profiles, including Th1 cells, which promote cell-mediated immunity against intracellular pathogens; Th2 cells, involved in responses to parasites and in allergy; Th17 cells, important at mucosal surfaces; and T follicular helper (Tfh) cells, which support antibody production in germinal centres. Regulatory T cells form a related suppressive lineage.

Clinical significance

The human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infects and destroys CD4+ T cells; the CD4 count is a principal marker of progression to AIDS. Abnormalities of helper T cell function also contribute to autoimmune and allergic diseases.
