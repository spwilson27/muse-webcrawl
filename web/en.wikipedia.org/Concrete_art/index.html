Concrete art

Concrete art is an abstractionist movement founded in Paris in 1930 by the Dutch artist Theo van Doesburg, who published its manifesto in the first and only issue of the journal Art Concret. Concrete art emphasises pure abstraction: a work should have no symbolic meaning and refer to nothing outside itself, being composed of planes and colours constructed according to simple, rational systems rather than derived from nature or emotion.

The manifesto

The 1930 manifesto, signed by van Doesburg, Otto G. Carlsund, Jean Hélion, Léon Tutundjian and Marcel Wantz, declared that the work of art must be entirely conceived and formed by the mind before its execution, that it should receive nothing from nature's formal properties or from sensuality, and that pictorial elements — line, plane and colour — have no meaning beyond themselves. Van Doesburg chose the word "concrete" rather than "abstract" because the resulting work was, for him, a real thing in its own right rather than an abstraction from something else.

Development

After van Doesburg's death in 1931, the idea was carried forward by groups such as Abstraction-Création in Paris. Its most influential later exponent was the Swiss artist Max Bill, who organised the exhibition Konkrete Kunst in Zurich in 1944 and promoted concrete art as a universal, mathematically ordered art. The movement had strong offshoots in Italy, in Argentina — where the Asociación Arte Concreto-Invención was founded in 1945 — and in Brazil, where the Grupo Ruptura in São Paulo (1952) championed concrete art before some members broke away to form the more expressive Neo-Concrete movement in 1959.

Principles and influence

Concrete artists favoured clear geometric structure, exact execution and, often, mathematical or serial procedures for generating compositions. The movement influenced later developments including op art, kinetic art, minimal art and systematic art, and its design philosophy fed directly into the Bauhaus-descended Ulm School of Design, which Max Bill co-founded.
