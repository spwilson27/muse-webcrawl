Dependency grammar (DG) is a class of modern grammatical theories that analyse the structure of a sentence in terms of binary relations — dependencies — between individual words, rather than in terms of nested phrases (constituents). Each word, except the root (usually the main verb), depends on a head word, producing a tree structure.

History

Ideas resembling dependency analysis appear in the Sanskrit grammarian Pāṇini and in medieval grammar, but the modern theory is credited to the French linguist Lucien Tesnière, whose posthumously published Éléments de syntaxe structurale (1959) founded structural syntax around the verb as the root of the clause. Dependency approaches were developed further by Igor Mel'čuk (Meaning–Text Theory), Richard Hudson (Word Grammar) and in the Prague school tradition.

Principles

A dependency tree connects words with labelled arcs (subject, object, modifier, and so on). Because structure is defined directly on words, dependency grammar handles free word order and morphologically rich languages naturally. Most versions assume a single root and projectivity or non-projectivity conditions on whether dependency arcs may cross.

Computational use

Dependency representations underpin modern natural language processing: treebanks such as the Prague Dependency Treebank and the Universal Dependencies project annotate corpora in dependency form, and dependency parsers are standard tools for information extraction, machine translation and question answering.
