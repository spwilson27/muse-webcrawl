Rule of lenity

Rule of lenity

FieldCriminal law, statutory interpretation
Also known asStrict construction of penal statutes
EffectAmbiguity in a criminal statute is resolved in favour of the defendant

The rule of lenity, also called the rule of strict construction, is a principle of statutory interpretation in United States criminal law under which a court, when faced with an ambiguous criminal statute, applies the interpretation more favourable to the defendant. It rests on two foundations: fair notice — people should not be punished for conduct they could not reasonably know was criminal — and the separation of powers, which reserves the definition of crimes to the legislature.

Application

The rule is generally a canon of last resort: it applies only when, after a court has used all other tools of statutory construction, a "grievous ambiguity" remains in the statute's meaning. The Supreme Court articulated the doctrine in cases such as McBoyle v. United States (1931) and reaffirmed it in Bostock-era and modern interpretive debates; it has been invoked in decisions narrowing federal offences relating to firearms, fraud and computer misuse.

Analogues

Closely related principles exist in other systems: the common-law tradition historically required penal statutes to be construed strictly, and civil-law systems apply the maxim in dubio pro reo ("in doubt, for the accused") and the constitutional prohibition on extending criminal law by analogy.

Criticism and debate

Modern textualist judges debate the rule's scope, some arguing it should apply earlier in the interpretive process, while others treat it as a tiebreaker of minimal force. Several states have abolished strict construction by statute, directing instead that penal provisions be construed "according to the fair import of their terms".
