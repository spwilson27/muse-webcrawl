Philosophy of science is the branch of philosophy concerned with the foundations, methods, and implications of science. It studies what science is, how it works, and what its claims amount to: among its central questions are what qualifies as science, the reliability of scientific theories, the nature of scientific explanation and confirmation, and the ultimate purpose of science.

The demarcation problem

A longstanding problem is to demarcate science from non-science and pseudoscience. Karl Popper proposed falsifiability: a theory is scientific only if it forbids some observable states of affairs and can in principle be refuted by evidence. The logical positivists of the Vienna Circle had earlier proposed verifiability as the criterion of meaningfulness. Thomas Kuhn argued that science progresses through paradigms punctuated by revolutionary shifts rather than steady accumulation, making demarcation a matter of puzzle-solving within a paradigm. Imre Lakatos proposed evaluating research programmes as progressive or degenerating, while Paul Feyerabend argued against any universal method ("anything goes").

Induction and confirmation

The problem of induction, raised by David Hume, asks how universal generalizations can be justified by finite observations. Philosophers have responded with probabilistic and Bayesian accounts of confirmation, hypothetico-deductivism, and inference to the best explanation. Related puzzles include the ravens paradox (Hempel) and Goodman's "grue" paradox.

Realism and explanation

Scientific realists hold that successful theories are approximately true and that their unobservable entities (electrons, genes) really exist; anti-realists, such as constructive empiricists, hold that science need only be empirically adequate. Accounts of explanation include Carl Hempel's deductive-nomological model, causal-mechanical accounts, and unificationism. Other major topics include reductionism, the structure of scientific theories, values in science, underdetermination of theory by data (the Duhem–Quine thesis), and the social dimensions of scientific knowledge.
