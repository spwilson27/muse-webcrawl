Fischer–Tropsch process

The Fischer–Tropsch process is a collection of chemical reactions that converts a mixture of carbon monoxide and hydrogen, known as synthesis gas or syngas, into liquid hydrocarbons. Developed by Franz Fischer and Hans Tropsch in Germany in 1925, it is the key step in coal liquefaction and gas-to-liquids technology, allowing liquid fuels to be produced from coal, natural gas or biomass instead of crude oil.

Chemistry

The overall reaction for producing alkanes can be written as (2n + 1) H2 + n CO → CnH2n+2 + n H2O. The process operates at temperatures of roughly 150–300 °C and pressures from one to several tens of atmospheres over transition-metal catalysts. Cobalt catalysts are favoured for natural-gas feedstocks, while cheaper iron catalysts are often used with coal-derived syngas. The product follows an Anderson–Schulz–Flory distribution of chain lengths, so a mixture of hydrocarbons is always obtained and further upgrading, such as hydrocracking of the heavy wax fraction, is required to maximise diesel or jet fuel output.

History

Fischer and Tropsch developed the process at the Kaiser-Wilhelm-Institut für Kohlenforschung in the 1920s. During the Second World War, Germany operated Fischer–Tropsch plants to supply a significant fraction of its liquid fuel from coal. After the war, interest waned in most countries, but South Africa's Sasol company commercialised the process at scale to circumvent oil embargoes during the apartheid era. Large gas-to-liquids plants, such as Shell's Pearl GTL in Qatar, were built in the 2000s and 2010s.

Applications and outlook

Fischer–Tropsch fuels are essentially free of sulfur and aromatics and burn cleanly. The process is also a route to sustainable aviation fuel when fed with biomass or with syngas made from captured CO2 and green hydrogen, although economics remain challenging compared with conventional refining.
