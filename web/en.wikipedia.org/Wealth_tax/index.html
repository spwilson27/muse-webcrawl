Wealth tax

A wealth tax (also called a net wealth tax or capital tax) is a tax on an entity's holdings of assets. It is typically levied annually on net worth — the total value of assets such as cash, property, investments and business interests, minus debts — above an exemption threshold. Unlike income tax, which falls on flows of earnings, a wealth tax falls on the stock of wealth itself.

Typical ratesAbout 0.1%–3% per year above threshold
Countries with broad wealth taxes (2020s)Norway, Spain, Switzerland, among others

Current and historical examples

Switzerland levies cantonal and municipal net wealth taxes on worldwide assets; Norway taxes net wealth above a threshold at combined state and municipal rates around 1%; Spain has a national wealth tax revived with a "solidarity tax" on large fortunes. France's impôt de solidarité sur la fortune applied from 1989 until 2018, when it was replaced by a tax on real estate wealth only. Several other European countries, including Germany, Austria, Denmark and Sweden, abolished their wealth taxes in the 1990s and 2000s; in the 1990s about a dozen OECD countries had them, and the number later shrank to a handful.

Arguments

Supporters, including economists such as Thomas Piketty, argue that wealth taxes counteract rising wealth concentration, tax wealthy people whose incomes are lightly taxed, and — because registries of assets are required — improve tax enforcement generally. Critics point to valuation difficulties for private businesses and illiquid assets, administrative cost, capital flight and emigration of the wealthy, double taxation alongside income and property taxes, and historically modest revenues. Debates intensified after 2019 proposals in the United States by Senators Elizabeth Warren and Bernie Sanders for annual taxes of 2–6% on the largest fortunes, and after Gabriel Zucman's proposal of a coordinated 2% minimum tax on billionaires, discussed by the G20 in 2024.
