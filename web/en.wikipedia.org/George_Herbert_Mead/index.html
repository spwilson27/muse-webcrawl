George Herbert Mead (February 27, 1863 – April 26, 1931) was an American philosopher, sociologist, and psychologist, primarily affiliated with the University of Chicago. He is regarded as a founder of symbolic interactionism and of the American sociological tradition of social psychology.

Life

Mead was born in South Hadley, Massachusetts, and educated at Oberlin College and Harvard University. He studied in Germany before joining the University of Michigan and then, in 1894, the University of Chicago, where he remained until his death. He was closely associated with the pragmatist John Dewey.

Social philosophy

Mead argued that the self emerges through social interaction, arising when an individual takes the attitudes of others toward themselves. His distinction between the "I" (the spontaneous self) and the "me" (the socialized self) became foundational in sociology. Communication through significant symbols, he held, makes mind and society possible.

Works

Mead published little in book form during his lifetime. His lectures were compiled posthumously by students, most famously in Mind, Self, and Society (1934), which established his reputation as a central figure in social theory.
