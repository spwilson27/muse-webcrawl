Semisimple module

In mathematics, given a ring R, a (left) R-module M is called semisimple (or completely reducible) if it is the direct sum of simple submodules, where a simple module is a nonzero module with no nontrivial submodules. Equivalently, a module is semisimple if every submodule has a complement — a submodule such that the module is their direct sum. Semisimple modules are the building blocks of much of representation theory because they decompose entirely into irreducible pieces.

Examples and characterizations

Every vector space is semisimple (any basis gives a decomposition into one-dimensional simple subspaces). The ring Z of integers acting on Z/6Z gives a semisimple module (it is Z/2 ⊕ Z/3), whereas Z/4Z is not semisimple. A module is semisimple if and only if its Jacobson radical-type obstructions vanish and every short exact sequence ending at it splits.

Semisimple rings

A ring is called semisimple if it is semisimple as a module over itself. The Wedderburn–Artin theorem classifies semisimple (Artinian) rings as finite products of matrix rings over division rings. By Maschke's theorem, the group algebra of a finite group over a field whose characteristic does not divide the group order is semisimple, which makes ordinary representation theory of finite groups tractable: every representation decomposes into irreducibles.

Properties

Submodules, quotient modules, and direct sums of semisimple modules are semisimple. Not every module is semisimple — the integers as a Z-module are a standard counterexample, since the subgroup 2Z has no complement.
