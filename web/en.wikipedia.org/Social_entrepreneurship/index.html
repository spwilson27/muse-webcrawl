Social entrepreneurship

Social entrepreneurship is an approach by individuals, groups, start-up companies, or entrepreneurs, in which they develop, fund, and implement solutions to social, cultural, or environmental issues. This concept may be applied to a wide range of organizations, which vary in size, aims, and beliefs. Whereas business entrepreneurs typically measure performance in profit, social entrepreneurs also take into account a positive "return to society".

History

The terms "social entrepreneur" and "social entrepreneurship" were used in the literature as early as the 1960s and 1970s, but were popularized in the 1980s by Bill Drayton, founder of Ashoka, which identifies and supports leading social entrepreneurs as Ashoka Fellows. Muhammad Yunus, founder of the Grameen Bank and pioneer of microcredit, is often cited as a prototypical example; he received the Nobel Peace Prize in 2006.

Definitions

J. Gregory Dees described the social entrepreneur as a change agent who adopts a mission to create and sustain social value, pursues new opportunities relentlessly, engages in continuous innovation, and acts boldly without being limited by resources in hand. The Schwab Foundation and the Skoll Foundation similarly support networks of social entrepreneurs addressing systemic problems.

Differences from related concepts

Social entrepreneurship differs from social enterprise (the organizational form) in emphasizing the individual innovator, and from corporate social responsibility in that the social mission is central rather than ancillary. It is sometimes criticized for celebrating individual heroes over collective or state action, and for metrics of "social return on investment" that are hard to verify.
