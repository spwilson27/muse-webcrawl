The Kingdom of York, known in Old Norse as Jórvík, was a Scandinavian kingdom in northern England centred on the city of York. From 867, when the Great Heathen Army captured York, Danish and later Norwegian kings ruled much of the former Northumbria — the heart of the Danelaw — until the last king, Eric Bloodaxe, was expelled and killed in 954 and the kingdom was absorbed into England.

History

York, the former Roman Eboracum and Anglian Eoforwic, fell to the sons of Ragnar Lodbrok in November 866 (by the traditional calendar, 867). Halfdan Ragnarsson ruled as king and from 876 distributed land among his men, beginning Scandinavian settlement of Yorkshire. After periods of Hiberno-Norse rule from Dublin — kings such as Sigtrygg and Olaf Guthfrithson — and fluctuating English overlordship under Athelstan and his successors, the Norwegian Eric Bloodaxe held York twice (947–948 and 952–954). His death at Stainmore in 954 ended the independent kingdom; Eadred of Wessex took control of Northumbria.

Jórvík as a trading city

Under Scandinavian rule, York became one of northern Europe's great trading ports, with a population possibly around 10,000 — second in England only to London. Excavations at Coppergate (the street of the cup-makers) revealed workshops producing goods in metal, wood, leather, glass, and amber, and trading links reaching from Ireland and Scandinavia to the Byzantine world and the Islamic caliphate, evidenced by finds such as a silk cap and Arabic coins. The York Archaeological Trust's Jorvik Viking Centre stands on the Coppergate excavation site.

Legacy

The kingdom left Yorkshire a dense Scandinavian place-name heritage (names ending in -by, -thorpe, -toft), distinctive sculptural traditions such as hogback tombstones, and York's enduring importance as the north's ecclesiastical and commercial capital.
