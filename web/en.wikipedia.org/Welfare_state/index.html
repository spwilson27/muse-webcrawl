Welfare state

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A welfare state is a form of government in which the state (and, in some cases, social partners) protects and promotes the economic and social well-being of its citizens, based upon the principles of equal opportunity, equitable distribution of wealth, and public responsibility for citizens unable to avail themselves of the minimal provisions for a good life.

Welfare states typically provide some combination of public education, universal or subsidised health care, unemployment and sickness insurance, old-age pensions, child benefits and public housing, financed through taxation and social insurance contributions.

History

Elements of social provision date to ancient and early modern poor relief, but the modern welfare state is usually traced to the social insurance schemes introduced in Germany under Chancellor Otto von Bismarck in the 1880s — health insurance (1883), accident insurance (1884) and old-age pensions (1889). In Britain, Liberal reforms before the First World War were followed after 1942 by the Beveridge Report, which proposed a comprehensive system of social security "from cradle to grave"; the post-war Labour government created the National Health Service in 1948. In the United States, the New Deal of the 1930s introduced Social Security and unemployment insurance.

Models

The sociologist Gøsta Esping-Andersen famously distinguished three worlds of welfare capitalism: the liberal model (e.g. the United States, United Kingdom), with modest, means-tested benefits; the conservative-corporatist model (e.g. Germany, France), organised around occupational insurance and the family; and the social-democratic model of the Nordic countries, with universal, generous benefits and high public employment.

Effects and criticism

Welfare states are associated with lower poverty rates and greater income equality, but critics argue they can create dependency, disincentives to work, and unsustainable fiscal burdens, particularly as populations age. Since the 1980s many states have pursued reforms aimed at activation, targeting and cost control, though overall social spending in OECD countries has remained substantial.
