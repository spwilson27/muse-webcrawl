Holi

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Revellers throwing coloured powder during Holi.

Holi (Hindi: होली) is a major Hindu festival celebrated as the Festival of Colours, Love and Spring. It celebrates the eternal and divine love of the deities Radha and Krishna, and also signifies the triumph of good over evil, commemorating the legend of Vishnu as Narasimha defeating Hiranyakashipu. It also heralds the arrival of spring and the end of winter.

Holi originates in the Indian subcontinent and is predominantly celebrated there, but has spread worldwide through the South Asian diaspora. It is celebrated on the last full moon day of the Hindu lunisolar month of Phalguna (typically in March), beginning with Holika Dahan — bonfires lit on the eve of the festival — followed by the main day of play with coloured powders (gulal) and water.

Legends and rituals

Several legends are associated with Holi. The demon king Hiranyakashipu sought to kill his son Prahlada for his devotion to Vishnu; Prahlada was saved while the king's sister Holika, who tried to burn him, perished in the fire — remembered in the Holika bonfire. The love of Krishna and Radha is central in the Braj region around Vrindavan and Mathura, where festivities last for days and include the playful Lathmar Holi of Barsana and Nandgaon. The festival also marks the spring harvest and a time for renewing relationships and settling conflicts; traditional drinks such as bhang thandai and sweets such as gujiya are shared.

Celebrations

On the main day, people gather in streets and open spaces to throw coloured powder and water at one another, accompanied by music, dancing and festive foods. Social distinctions are temporarily set aside, and strangers as well as friends are greeted with colour. Holi is a national holiday in India and Nepal and is publicly celebrated in cities across the world, from London and New York to Singapore and Sydney.
