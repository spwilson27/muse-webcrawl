Demesne

In the feudal system, the demesne (pronounced dih-MAYN, from Old French demeine, ultimately from Latin dominus, "lord") was all the land retained and managed by a lord of the manor under the system of manorialism for his own use, occupation, or support. This distinguished it from land sub-enfeoffed by the lord to other freehold tenants.

A manor's demesne lands were worked directly for the lord

Manorial structure

A typical manor was divided between the lord's demesne and the holdings of the peasant tenants. The demesne was worked by the labour services owed by villeins and other unfree tenants, supplemented by hired labour. Produce from the demesne went to the lord's household or was sold for his profit, while tenants supported themselves from their own strips in the open fields.

Royal demesne

The lands held directly by the Crown were called the royal demesne or ancient demesne. In Domesday Book (1086), the estates held by King William I are recorded in detail; tenants of ancient demesne manors enjoyed certain legal privileges, including special procedures in the royal courts.

Decline

As the money economy grew in the later Middle Ages, many lords leased out their demesnes to tenant farmers for money rents rather than managing them directly — a shift known as leasing the demesne or "farming" it. The practice spread after the Black Death, when labour became scarce and costly.

Later usage

The word survives in Ireland, where demesne denotes the landscaped parkland surrounding a great house, and in legal phrases such as "held in demesne as of fee".
