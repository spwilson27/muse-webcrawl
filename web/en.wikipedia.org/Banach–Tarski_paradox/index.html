Banach–Tarski paradox

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia
FieldSet theory, geometric measure theory
Proved byStefan Banach and Alfred Tarski (1924)
Key axiomAxiom of choice

The Banach–Tarski paradox is a theorem in set-theoretic geometry which states that a solid ball in three-dimensional space can be split into a finite number of disjoint subsets, which can then be reassembled in a different way to yield two identical copies of the original ball.

Statement and proof idea

Formally, a subset of Euclidean space is called paradoxical if it can be partitioned into finitely many pieces that can be moved by rigid motions (rotations and translations) and reassembled into two copies of the original. The proof exploits a paradoxical decomposition of the free group on two generators, embedded in the rotation group SO(3), together with the axiom of choice to select points from orbits of that group acting on the sphere.

Interpretation

The pieces in the decomposition are non-measurable sets: they cannot be assigned a consistent volume, so no physical ball could actually be duplicated this way. The result, published by Stefan Banach and Alfred Tarski in 1924 and building on work by Hausdorff and Vitali, is sometimes cited as a reason to question the axiom of choice, though most mathematicians accept it because of its indispensability elsewhere.
