Dielectric strength

In physics, the term dielectric strength has two related meanings: the maximum electric field that a pure material can withstand under ideal conditions without undergoing electrical breakdown and becoming electrically conductive, and, in engineering practice, the breakdown voltage divided by the thickness of a specific configuration of dielectric material and electrodes.

Breakdown

When the applied field exceeds the dielectric strength, electrons become mobile and current flows, often permanently damaging the material. For dry air the value is about 3 MV/m, which is why high-voltage equipment uses oil, SF6 gas, or solid insulation with higher strengths; common insulators such as glass and porcelain reach 10–30 MV/m, and mica and certain polymers higher still.

Factors affecting measurement

Measured dielectric strength depends on sample thickness, temperature, humidity, electrode geometry, and the rate of voltage rise, so published values are comparative rather than absolute constants. It is usually expressed in volts per unit thickness, such as kV/mm.
