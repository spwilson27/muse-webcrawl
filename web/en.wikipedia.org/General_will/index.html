General will

The general will (French: volonté générale) is a central concept in the political philosophy of Jean-Jacques Rousseau, most fully developed in The Social Contract (1762). It denotes the will of the body politic as a whole, directed toward the common good, and serves in Rousseau's theory as the source of legitimate law and sovereignty.

The concept

Rousseau distinguishes the general will from the mere aggregate of individual desires, which he calls the will of all (volonté de tous). The will of all is a sum of particular wills and takes private interest into account; the general will, by contrast, considers only the common interest. Rousseau argues that if citizens deliberate adequately, informed and without partial associations distorting their judgment, their particular errors tend to cancel out and what remains expresses the general will.

Because sovereignty consists essentially in the general will, it is inalienable and indivisible: it cannot be transferred to a ruler or divided among branches of government. Laws, properly speaking, are acts of the general will, and government is merely its executive agent. A citizen who refuses to obey the general will, Rousseau controversially writes, may be "forced to be free" — compelled to follow the law that he, as a member of the sovereign people, has prescribed for himself.

Antecedents and influence

The term was used before Rousseau by theologians and by Denis Diderot, whose article "Droit naturel" in the Encyclopédie treated the general will of the human race as a standard of right. Rousseau transformed it into a distinctively political doctrine. The idea strongly influenced the French Revolution — the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789) states that law is the expression of the general will — and later democratic and republican theory.

Criticism

Critics, including Jacob Talmon, have associated the general will with "totalitarian democracy", arguing that a unitary collective will can be invoked to justify coercion of minorities. Defenders respond that Rousseau's doctrine binds law to conditions of generality and equality, and presupposes a small, virtuous republic rather than a mass state.
