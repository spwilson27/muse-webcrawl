Danish resistance movement

The Danish resistance movements were an underground insurgency movement to resist the German occupation of Denmark during World War II. The German invasion on 9 April 1940, code-named Operation Weserübung, met only token resistance, and the Danish government initially pursued a policy of cooperation. Resistance grew markedly after 1943, when Germany imposed harsher measures following strikes and unrest.

Early resistance

Early resistance centred on intelligence gathering, illegal newspapers such as Frit Danmark and De frie Danske, and small acts of sabotage. The British Special Operations Executive (SOE) maintained contact with Danish intelligence officers. The Danish Freedom Council (Danmarks Frihedsråd) was formed in September 1943 to coordinate the growing movement.

Escalation after 1943

When the Germans moved to arrest Danish Jews in October 1943, the resistance and ordinary Danes helped ferry more than 7,000 Jews across the Øresund to neutral Sweden—the rescue of the Danish Jews. Sabotage of railways and factories intensified, carried out by groups such as BOPA and Holger Danske. In June 1944 a general strike in Copenhagen, the People's Strike, forced concessions from the occupiers.

Liberation

Denmark was liberated in May 1945, except for the island of Bornholm, which was occupied by Soviet forces until 1946. Resistance fighters emerged to secure key installations and arrest collaborators. Roughly 850 members of the resistance were killed during the occupation.
