Los Glaciares National Park

Los Glaciares National Park
Location: Santa Cruz Province, Argentina
Area: approximately 7,269 km²
Established: 1937
Status: UNESCO World Heritage Site (since 1981)

Los Glaciares National Park (Parque Nacional Los Glaciares) is a federal protected area in Santa Cruz Province, Argentina. Established in 1937, it is the largest national park in the country, covering some 7,269 square kilometres in the southwest of Santa Cruz, in the heart of Patagonia, along the border with Chile.

The park owes its name to the enormous ice cap in the Andes, the largest outside Antarctica and Greenland, which feeds 47 large glaciers, 13 of them flowing down towards the Atlantic basin. Among its most famous features are the Perito Moreno Glacier, one of the few advancing glaciers in the world, and the Fitz Roy and Cerro Torre massifs in the northern sector near the village of El Chaltén.

Geography

The park's landscape combines massive glaciers, milky turquoise glacial lakes, rugged mountains and sub-Antarctic Magellanic forests. Its two great lakes are Lago Argentino, the largest lake in Argentina, and Lago Viedma. Glaciers such as Upsala, Onelli and Perito Moreno calve icebergs into these waters. The Southern Patagonian Ice Field, shared with Chile, dominates the western part of the park.

Flora and fauna

Vegetation ranges from Patagonian steppe in the east to dense southern beech forests of lenga and ñire in the west. Fauna includes the guanaco, the Andean condor, the South Andean deer (huemul), the puma and the grey fox. Birdlife is rich, with species such as the black-chested buzzard-eagle and the torrent duck.

Tourism

The town of El Calafate, on the shore of Lago Argentino, serves as the main gateway for visitors to the southern sector and the Perito Moreno Glacier, where walkways face the glacier's calving front. El Chaltén is the base for treks to Laguna de los Tres beneath Mount Fitz Roy and to Laguna Torre beneath Cerro Torre. In 1981 the park was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List.
