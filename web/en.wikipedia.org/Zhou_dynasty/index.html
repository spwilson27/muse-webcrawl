The Zhou dynasty (c. 1046–256 BC) was the longest-lasting dynasty in Chinese history, ruling for nearly eight centuries. It followed the Shang dynasty and preceded the Qin dynasty, which unified China in 221 BC. The Zhou kings ruled from the Wei River valley in the west, first from Fenghao near modern Xi'an, and, after 771 BC, from the eastern capital at Luoyang. Zhou rule is traditionally divided into the Western Zhou (c. 1046–771 BC) and the Eastern Zhou (770–256 BC), the latter further subdivided into the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods.

Founding and the Mandate of Heaven

According to tradition, the Zhou, a people of the western frontier, overthrew the Shang at the Battle of Muye around 1046 BC under King Wu, whose father King Wen had laid the foundations of the state. To justify the conquest, Zhou rulers developed the doctrine of the Mandate of Heaven (天命): Heaven grants the right to rule to a virtuous sovereign and withdraws it from a corrupt one, legitimising dynastic change. The Duke of Zhou, King Wu's brother, served as regent for the young King Cheng and is credited with consolidating Zhou institutions; Confucius later revered him as a model statesman.

Western Zhou

During the Western Zhou, the kings governed through a network of hereditary regional lords bound by kinship and ritual obligations, a system often described as feudal (fengjian). Bronze ritual vessels bearing inscriptions are a hallmark of the period and an important source for its history. Zhou power gradually weakened as regional states grew stronger. In 771 BC, the Quanrong nomads, allied with discontented lords, sacked the western capital and killed King You. The court fled east to Luoyang, beginning the Eastern Zhou.

Eastern Zhou: Spring and Autumn and Warring States

As royal authority waned, power passed to the great regional states. The Spring and Autumn period (770–476 BC) saw hegemons such as Duke Huan of Qi dominate in the Zhou king's name, while interstate warfare and diplomacy filled the chronicle Spring and Autumn Annals. The Warring States period (475–221 BC) was marked by the consolidation of seven major states and total warfare, alongside extraordinary intellectual flourishing: Confucianism, Daoism, Legalism, Mohism and other schools of the Hundred Schools of Thought arose in this era. Iron tools, crossbows, cavalry, and administrative reforms transformed society. In 256 BC, the state of Qin extinguished the Zhou royal line; Qin completed the unification of China in 221 BC.

Society, religion and legacy

Zhou society was stratified and ritualised, centred on ancestor worship, bronze ritual culture, and rites (li) that Confucius later idealised as a model of harmonious order. Texts composed or edited in the Zhou era — including the Book of Documents, the Book of Poetry, and parts of the I Ching — became the core of the Chinese classical canon. The Zhou created enduring concepts, above all the Mandate of Heaven, that shaped Chinese political thought for the next three millennia.
