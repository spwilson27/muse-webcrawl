Econometrics

Econometrics is the application of statistical methods to economic data in order to test economic theories, estimate relationships and evaluate and implement government and business policy. A basic tool of econometrics is the multiple linear regression model, used to quantify how changes in explanatory variables are associated with changes in an outcome of interest.

History

The term was coined by Polish economist Paweł Ciompa in 1910 and developed by Ragnar Frisch in the 1930s. The Econometric Society was founded in 1930, and its journal Econometrica began publication in 1933. The Cowles Commission advanced simultaneous-equations modelling, while the rise of computing after the Second World War enabled estimation of large macroeconomic models.

Methodology

Econometric analysis begins with an economic theory that suggests a relationship between variables. The theorist specifies a mathematical model, adds a disturbance term capturing unobserved influences, collects data — cross-sectional, time-series or panel — and estimates the unknown parameters, commonly by ordinary least squares or maximum likelihood. Hypotheses suggested by theory are then tested statistically.

Key problems

Because economics rarely allows controlled experiments, econometricians confront endogeneity: omitted variables, simultaneity and measurement error can bias estimates. Methods such as instrumental variables, fixed effects, regression discontinuity and difference-in-differences designs are used to identify causal effects from observational data. Time-series econometrics addresses non-stationarity through cointegration and autoregressive models such as ARIMA and VAR.

Applications

Econometrics is used in forecasting output and inflation, evaluating labour-market programmes, estimating demand systems, asset pricing in financial econometrics, and policy evaluation across public economics and development economics.
