The emu (Dromaius novaehollandiae) is a species of flightless bird endemic to Australia, where it is the tallest native bird. It is the only extant member of the genus Dromaius and the third-tallest living bird after its African ratite relatives the common ostrich and Somali ostrich. The emu's native ranges cover most of the Australian mainland, but subspecies that lived in Tasmania and several small offshore islands became extinct after the arrival of Europeans in the 18th and 19th centuries; the marooned subspecies on Kangaroo Island has been preserved.

Emus have soft, brown, shaggy plumage, a long thin neck and long legs. They can grow up to 1.9 metres (6.2 ft) in height and travel great distances, occasionally at speeds of up to 48 km/h (30 mph). They forage on a variety of plants and insects, but have been known to go for weeks without eating. They drink infrequently, but take in copious amounts of water when the opportunity arises.

Behaviour and ecology

Breeding takes place in May and June, and fighting among females for a mate is common. Females can mate several times and lay several clutches of eggs in one season. The male does the incubation; during this process he hardly eats or drinks and loses a significant amount of weight. The eggs hatch after around eight weeks, and the young are nurtured by their fathers. They reach full size after around six months, but can remain as a family unit until the next breeding season.

Relationship with humans

The emu is an important cultural icon of Australia, appearing on the coat of arms and various coinage. It features prominently in Indigenous Australian mythology. Historically, emus were hunted by settlers, most famously during the "Emu War" of 1932, when military personnel were deployed in Western Australia in an unsuccessful attempt to curb emu numbers. Emus are farmed commercially for meat, leather, feathers and oil in several countries.
