Conditional convergence

In mathematics, a series or integral is said to be conditionally convergent if it converges, but it does not converge absolutely. A classic example is the alternating harmonic series 1 − 1/2 + 1/3 − 1/4 + ⋯, which sums to ln 2, while the corresponding series of absolute values — the harmonic series — diverges.

Riemann series theorem

The Riemann series theorem (or Riemann rearrangement theorem) states that if an infinite series of real numbers is conditionally convergent, then its terms can be rearranged into a permutation that converges to any prescribed real number, or diverges. This striking result shows that conditional convergence is a fragile property depending on the order of summation.

In economics

The term is also used in growth economics: conditional convergence is the hypothesis that poorer economies grow faster than richer ones once factors such as savings rates, population growth, and institutional quality are held constant, so that countries converge to their own steady states rather than to a common income level.
