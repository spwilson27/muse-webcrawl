Eternalism (philosophy of time)

Eternalism is a philosophical approach to the ontological nature of time which takes all points in time to be equally "real", as opposed to presentism, which holds that only the present exists, and the growing block theory, which holds that past and present exist but the future does not. Eternalism is often associated with the "block universe" or "block time" conception of spacetime: time is another dimension like space, and the universe is a four-dimensional block in which all events — past, present, and future — are fixed and equally existent; temporal becoming is an illusion of conscious experience.

Arguments

The most influential arguments for eternalism arise from the special theory of relativity. Simultaneity is relative: observers in relative motion disagree about which distant events are happening "now", slicing spacetime into different sets of simultaneous events. Arguments in this spirit, developed by C. W. Rietdijk (1966) and Hilary Putnam (1967), conclude that if what is real for one observer must be real simpliciter, then events in another observer's future must already exist — sometimes stated as the slogan "there is no privileged present". Philosophers also cite the need for truthmakers for statements about the past, and the tenseless B-theory of time associated with J. M. E. McTaggart's distinction between the A-series (past–present–future) and B-series (earlier–later).

Criticism

Critics object that eternalism conflicts with the lived experience of temporal passage and with the apparent openness of the future and of free will; presentists such as those following Arthur Prior's tense logic hold that only present things exist, and that relativity's simultaneity concerns measurement conventions rather than ontology. Defenders respond that physics is the best guide to the structure of time and that the psychological "flow" of time can be explained within a block universe. Eternalism also features in discussions of time travel, fatalism, and persistence (perdurantism, in which objects are four-dimensional "worms" extended through time).
