Land reclamation

Also called: Land fill, poldering
Notable examples: Netherlands polders; Kansai International Airport; Palm Islands, Dubai; Hong Kong and Singapore expansion

Land reclamation, usually known as reclamation, and also known as land fill (not to be confused with landfill), is the process of creating new land from oceans, lakes, seas, and riverbeds, or from previously waterlogged or low-lying terrain. The land thus reclaimed is known as reclamation ground or land fill.

Methods

Methods include draining enclosed water using dikes and pumps — the Dutch polder technique refined over centuries; dry dumping of earth and rock to raise ground above water level; hydraulic filling, in which dredged sand is pumped or rainbowed into place; and deep cement mixing and other ground-improvement techniques used for airport islands in Japan. About one-fifth of the Netherlands' land area consists of polders reclaimed from the sea and lakes, protected by its celebrated Delta Works.

Uses and examples

Reclaimed land supports ports, airports (Kansai and Chubu Centrair in Japan, Hong Kong International Airport), urban expansion (much of Singapore, which has grown its land area by roughly a quarter; Monaco; the Palm Islands of Dubai), agriculture, and beach nourishment to combat coastal erosion. Large-scale reclamation has also been used in the South China Sea, drawing geopolitical and environmental controversy.

Environmental impact

Reclamation destroys marine and wetland habitats, alters currents and sediment transport, and can worsen flooding elsewhere. Newly reclaimed ground is prone to subsidence and liquefaction in earthquakes, as seen in parts of Tokyo Bay and Kobe in 1995. Modern projects increasingly require environmental impact assessments and habitat compensation.
