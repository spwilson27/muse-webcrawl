Royal prerogative in the United Kingdom

The royal prerogative in the United Kingdom is a body of customary authority, privilege and immunity, recognised in law, as belonging to the sovereign alone. It comprises the remaining residue of powers once exercised personally by the monarch that have not been removed by Parliament. In modern practice, prerogative powers are exercised almost entirely by ministers in the sovereign's name, subject to constitutional convention and, increasingly, to judicial review.

Scope

Important prerogative powers include the appointment and dismissal of the prime minister; summoning, proroguing and dissolving Parliament (modified by the Dissolution and Calling of Parliament Act 2022, which revived the prerogative after the Fixed-term Parliaments Act 2011 was repealed); the granting of royal assent; conducting foreign affairs and concluding treaties; deploying the armed forces and declaring war; issuing passports; granting honours and pardons; and appointing judges and bishops. Some powers — such as refusing assent — have not been exercised in ways contrary to ministerial advice for centuries.

Limits and case law

Statute overrides the prerogative where the two conflict (Attorney General v De Keyser's Royal Hotel, 1920), and the prerogative cannot be used to change the common law or frustrate legislation. Courts have asserted reviewability in cases such as Council of Civil Service Unions v Minister for the Civil Service (the GCHQ case, 1985). The limits of the prerogative featured centrally in the Brexit litigation: R (Miller) v Secretary of State for Exiting the European Union (2017) held that ministers could not use the prerogative to withdraw from the EU treaties, and R (Miller) v The Prime Minister (2019) held the 2019 prorogation of Parliament unlawful.
