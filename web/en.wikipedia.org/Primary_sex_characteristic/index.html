Primary sex characteristic

Primary sex characteristics are the sexual organs that are directly involved in reproduction and are present at birth. In humans, these include the gonads — the testes in males and the ovaries in females — and the internal and external genitalia: in males, the penis, scrotum, vas deferens, prostate, and seminal vesicles; in females, the vagina, uterus, fallopian tubes, cervix, and clitoris. They are distinguished from secondary sex characteristics, such as breasts, facial hair, and voice changes, which develop at puberty and are not directly involved in reproduction.

The reproductive organs are the primary sex characteristics.

Development

Primary sex characteristics are determined during embryonic development. In mammals, the sex-determining region of the Y chromosome (SRY gene) triggers development of the testes, which then secrete testosterone and anti-Müllerian hormone, directing development of the male internal and external organs. In the absence of that signal, the ovaries develop and the female pattern emerges. Differences of sex development (intersex conditions) can result in primary sex characteristics that are atypical or ambiguous at birth.

Contrast with secondary characteristics

While primary sex characteristics are present from birth and directly reproductive, secondary sex characteristics appear at puberty under the influence of sex hormones and serve roles in sexual signalling and dimorphism rather than in the mechanics of reproduction itself.
