Packet-switched network

A packet-switched network is a digital communications network in which data is broken into small blocks called packets, each of which is routed independently through the network — possibly by different paths — and reassembled at the destination. It contrasts with circuit switching, in which a dedicated path is reserved for the duration of a connection, as in the traditional telephone network.

History

Packet switching was developed independently in the early 1960s by Paul Baran at the RAND Corporation, who sought a communications system that could survive network damage, and Donald Davies at the UK's National Physical Laboratory, who coined the term "packet". Leonard Kleinrock developed the underlying queueing theory. The first packet-switched network, the ARPANET, began operation in 1969 and became the foundation of the modern Internet.

How it works

Each packet contains a header with source and destination addresses and control information, plus a payload of user data. Routers and switches forward packets toward their destination, usually by the best available route at that moment. Because links are shared statistically rather than reserved, packet switching uses network capacity far more efficiently when traffic is bursty, as with web browsing and email.

Modes

Networks may be connectionless (datagram) service, like the Internet Protocol, where packets are routed independently with no setup, or virtual circuit service, like X.25, Frame Relay, and MPLS, where a logical path is established first. TCP adds reliable, ordered delivery on top of IP's datagram service.
