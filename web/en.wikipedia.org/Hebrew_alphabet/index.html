The Hebrew alphabet (Hebrew: אָלֶף־בֵּית עִבְרִי, alefbet ivri), known variously by scholars as the Ktav Ashuri, Jewish script, square script and block script, is an abjad used in the writing of the Hebrew language and other Jewish languages, most notably Yiddish, Ladino, Judeo-Arabic and Judeo-Persian. It consists of 22 letters, all consonants, written from right to left.

History

The modern "square" script was adopted from the Imperial Aramaic alphabet during the Second Temple period, gradually replacing the older Paleo-Hebrew alphabet (itself derived from Phoenician) by about the second century BCE. The Paleo-Hebrew script survives in limited use among Samaritans. The square script's letterforms were standardised in the scribal tradition used for Torah scrolls.

Letters and features

The alphabet runs from alef (א) to tav (ת). Five letters — kaf, mem, nun, pe and tsadi — have distinct "final" (sofit) forms used at the ends of words. Because the script is an abjad, vowels are normally not written; they may be indicated by the diacritic system called niqqud (points), developed by the Masoretes in the early medieval period, and by certain consonants used as matres lectionis. Cantillation marks (te'amim) in biblical texts guide chanting and syntax.

Numerals and other uses

Hebrew letters also serve as numerals in a traditional system (gematria), in which letters have numerical values — a practice that gave rise to interpretive traditions assigning significance to the numerical values of words. The alphabet is also used for writing Yiddish, where some letters are adapted to represent vowels systematically.

Cursive and print

Alongside the square print script, a cursive handwriting form (Ktav) is used in everyday writing in Israel, differing substantially in the shapes of many letters.
