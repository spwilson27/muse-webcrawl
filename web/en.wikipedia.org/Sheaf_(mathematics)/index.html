Sheaf (mathematics)

In mathematics, a sheaf is a tool for systematically tracking data attached to the open sets of a topological space — such as functions defined on those sets — in a way that is consistent under restriction to smaller sets and gluing from local data.

Definition

A presheaf on a space X assigns to each open set U a set (or abelian group, ring, etc.) F(U) of sections, together with restriction maps for inclusions of open sets, satisfying identity and composition laws. A presheaf is a sheaf if it satisfies two further axioms: locality (sections agreeing on an open cover are equal) and gluing (compatible sections on an open cover combine into a unique global section).

Examples and applications

Standard examples include sheaves of continuous, differentiable, or holomorphic functions; the sheaf of germs of functions; and constant and locally constant sheaves. Introduced by Jean Leray in the 1940s and developed by Henri Cartan and Alexander Grothendieck, sheaf theory underlies modern algebraic geometry, complex analysis, and sheaf cohomology, which measures obstructions to gluing local solutions into global ones.
