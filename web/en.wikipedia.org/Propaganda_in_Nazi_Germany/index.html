Propaganda in Nazi Germany

The propaganda of Nazi Germany was a central instrument of the regime of Adolf Hitler, used to acquire and maintain power and to implement its policies, including the persecution of Jews and other minorities. After the Nazi seizure of power in 1933, Joseph Goebbels became head of the Reich Ministry of Public Enlightenment and Propaganda, which controlled the press, radio, film, and much of cultural life through the Reich Chamber of Culture. Propaganda promoted the cult of the Führer, German nationalism and militarism, racial ideology, and the vision of a unified Volksgemeinschaft (people's community), while demonizing Jews, communists, and other designated enemies of the state.

Before the seizure of power

Hitler devoted two chapters of Mein Kampf (1925) to propaganda, arguing that it should address the emotions of the broad masses with simple, repeated messages. During the Nazi Party's rise, its propaganda combined posters, newspapers such as the Völkischer Beobachter and Julius Streicher's virulently antisemitic Der Stürmer, mass rallies, and modern techniques of advertising and spectacle. The party's message targeted economic distress after the Great Depression, resentment over the Treaty of Versailles, and fear of communism.

Media

Radio was a priority: the regime subsidized the cheap Volksempfänger ("people's receiver") so that broadcasts could reach most households, and listening to foreign stations was eventually criminalized. Cinema produced entertainment features alongside overtly political works such as Leni Riefenstahl's Triumph of the Will (1935), documenting the 1934 Nuremberg Rally, and the antisemitic films Jud Süß and Der ewige Jude (both 1940). The press was brought into line through editor directives and the consolidation of publishing, and schools and the Hitler Youth saturated education with regime ideology.

Wartime propaganda and legacy

During the Second World War, propaganda glorified the Wehrmacht, justified conquest as defensive struggle, and — though it rarely described the extermination of the Jews explicitly — saturated the public with antisemitic themes that historians regard as essential context for the Holocaust. After 1945, Nazi propaganda became a central case study in the study of mass persuasion, censorship, and totalitarian control of media, and its symbols and publications are restricted in modern Germany.
