The Revolutions of 1848, known in some countries as the Springtime of the Peoples or the Spring of Nations, were a series of political upheavals throughout Europe in 1848 — the most widespread revolutionary wave in European history. Beginning in France, the revolutions spread to the German states, the Austrian Empire, Italy, Denmark, Poland, Hungary, and beyond.

Causes

The revolutions combined long-term pressures — the spread of liberal, democratic, and nationalist ideas after 1815; the grievances of an emerging working class in industrialising cities; and the artificial state system maintained by the conservative Concert of Europe — with an acute economic crisis: the harvest failures of 1845–47, including the potato blight and grain shortages, and the financial crash of 1847 threw large populations into hardship just as the February 1848 revolution in Paris toppled King Louis-Philippe and proclaimed the Second Republic.

Course

Within weeks, revolts erupted across the continent. In Vienna, Chancellor Metternich, architect of the post-1815 order, resigned and fled; in Berlin, street fighting forced concessions from the Prussian king; the Frankfurt Parliament convened to draft a constitution for a united Germany. Hungarians under Lajos Kossuth declared autonomy from Austria, Italians rose against Austrian rule (the Five Days of Milan, the Roman Republic), and Danes, Poles, Czechs, Croats, Romanians, and others pressed national claims, often against one another as much as against empires.

Failure and legacy

By mid-1849 nearly all the revolutions had been defeated. Divisions between liberal bourgeoisie, workers, and peasants, the continued loyalty of professional armies, intervention by Russia (which crushed the Hungarian revolution), and conflicts among national movements doomed them. The Frankfurt Parliament's offer of an imperial crown was refused by Frederick William IV of Prussia, and its constitution never took effect. Yet 1848 left durable marks: serfdom was abolished in the Austrian Empire, Denmark and Piedmont retained constitutions, universal male suffrage survived in France (electing Louis-Napoléon Bonaparte), and the failures of 1848 set the agenda — national unification and social reform — pursued by other means over the following decades.
