Charles Sanders Peirce (pronounced "purse"; 10 September 1839 – 19 April 1914) was an American philosopher, logician, mathematician, and scientist who is regarded as the founder of pragmatism and a father of semiotics. Trained as a chemist and employed as a scientist for three decades by the United States Coast Survey, he made fundamental contributions to logic, statistics, geodesy, and philosophy, most of which were recognised only after his death.

Life

Peirce was born in Cambridge, Massachusetts, the son of Benjamin Peirce, Harvard's leading mathematician. He graduated from Harvard in 1859 and took a degree in chemistry summa cum laude in 1863. From 1861 to 1891 he worked for the US Coast and Geodetic Survey on pendulum measurements of gravity, while lecturing in logic at Johns Hopkins University (1879–1884). His academic career ended amid scandal, and he spent his final decades in poverty near Milford, Pennsylvania, writing prolifically with support from friends including William James.

Philosophy

In papers such as "The Fixation of Belief" (1877) and "How to Make Our Ideas Clear" (1878), Peirce articulated the pragmatic maxim: the meaning of a conception lies in its conceivable practical consequences. He developed a fallibilist theory of inquiry in which truth is the opinion inquiry would settle on in the long run, and distinguished deduction, induction, and a third mode of inference he called abduction (retroduction) — the generation of explanatory hypotheses.

Logic and semiotics

Peirce created much of modern symbolic logic: an algebra of logic and logic of relations, quantification theory (in parallel with Frege), truth-functional analysis, and existential graphs. His semeiotic analysed all thought as sign activity involving a sign, its object, and an interpretant, and classified signs as icons, indices, and symbols. Philosophically he defended scholastic realism, an evolutionary cosmology, and the categories of Firstness, Secondness, and Thirdness.

Legacy

Peirce published little and left some 80,000 pages of manuscripts; his Collected Papers (1931–1958) and the ongoing chronological edition have progressively revealed the scale of his achievement. Bertrand Russell judged him "certainly the greatest American thinker ever", and his ideas underpin fields from analytic philosophy and semiotics to computer science and statistics.
