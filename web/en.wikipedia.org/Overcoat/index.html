Overcoat

Overcoat

Type: Outer garment

Materials: Wool, cashmere, tweed, gabardine

Worn over: Suits and other clothing

An overcoat is a type of long coat intended to be worn as the outermost garment, over indoor clothing such as a suit. Overcoats are traditionally made of heavy materials such as wool, tweed or cashmere to provide warmth in cold weather, and usually extend below the knee. They are distinguished from topcoats, which are made of lighter-weight fabrics such as gabardine and are intended for cooler, but not severely cold, weather.

History

The modern overcoat evolved in the 18th and 19th centuries from earlier greatcoats and military coats. The frock overcoat of the Victorian era was a formal, closely fitted garment; the looser Chesterfield coat, named after an Earl of Chesterfield, became the classic dress overcoat, typically single-breasted with a velvet collar. Military needs produced enduring styles: the raglan coat, the British Warm, and the trench coat developed during the First World War by makers such as Burberry and Aquascutum. The duffel coat, with its wooden toggles, came from naval service in the Second World War.

Styles

  • Chesterfield — a formal knee-length or longer coat, single- or double-breasted, often with a velvet collar.
  • Trench coat — a belted, double-breasted raincoat in cotton gabardine with epaulettes and a storm flap.
  • Pea coat — a short, double-breasted naval coat of heavy wool.
  • Duffel coat — a hooded coat fastened with toggles, originating in Belgium and adopted by the Royal Navy.
  • Greatcoat — a very heavy, long military overcoat, often with a cape-like collar.
  • Ulster — a Victorian working coat with a cape and belt.
  • Covert coat — a lightweight hunting-derived coat in covert cloth.

In culture

The overcoat is a recurring motif in literature and film, from Nikolai Gogol's short story "The Overcoat" (1842) — about the poor clerk Akaky Akakievich and his treasured new coat — to the trench-coated figures of film noir. Gogol's story is often said to have founded the tradition of Russian realist fiction, prompting the remark attributed to Dostoevsky: "We all came out from under Gogol's 'Overcoat'."
