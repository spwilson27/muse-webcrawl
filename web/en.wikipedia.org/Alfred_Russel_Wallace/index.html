Alfred Russel Wallace

Born: 8 January 1823, Usk, Wales
Died: 7 November 1913, Broadstone, Dorset
Known for: Natural selection; biogeography; the Wallace Line

Alfred Russel Wallace (8 January 1823 – 7 November 1913) was a British naturalist, explorer, geographer, anthropologist, biologist, and illustrator. He independently conceived the theory of evolution through natural selection; his 1858 paper on the subject, sent from Ternate to Charles Darwin, was published jointly with extracts from Darwin's work, prompting Darwin to publish On the Origin of Species the following year.

Exploration

Largely self-educated, Wallace financed his research by collecting specimens. With Henry Walter Bates he explored the Amazon Basin from 1848; most of his collection was lost when his ship, the Helen, caught fire in 1852. From 1854 to 1862 he travelled the Malay Archipelago, collecting over 125,000 specimens and documenting the sharp faunal divide — the Wallace Line — between Asian and Australasian species. His book The Malay Archipelago (1869) remains a classic of travel and science.

Contributions

Wallace is regarded as the father of biogeography, notably through The Geographical Distribution of Animals (1876) and Island Life (1880). He wrote prolifically on natural and sexual selection, warning coloration, and speciation. Unlike Darwin, he later argued that natural selection alone could not explain the human mind, embracing spiritualism — a position that distanced him from some contemporaries.

Social thought and legacy

Wallace was also a social critic: a supporter of land nationalization, women's suffrage, and socialism, and an opponent of eugenics and compulsory vaccination. Though long overshadowed by Darwin, his reputation as co-discoverer of natural selection and founder of biogeography has been restored; he is commemorated by a statue at the Natural History Museum, London, and numerous species bear his name.
