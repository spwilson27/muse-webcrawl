Allies of World War II

The Allies of World War II were the coalition of nations that opposed the Axis powers during the Second World War (1939–1945). The principal members — the "Big Three" — were the United Kingdom, the Soviet Union, and the United States, joined by China, France, Poland, the British dominions, and eventually more than fifty states that signed the Declaration by United Nations in 1942.

The Allied nations (in blue/green) at the height of the coalition.

Origins

The alliance formed in response to aggression by Nazi Germany, Fascist Italy, and Imperial Japan. Poland, France, and Britain were at war with Germany from September 1939. The German invasion of the Soviet Union in June 1941 brought the USSR into the coalition, and the Japanese attack on Pearl Harbor in December 1941 brought in the United States. On 1 January 1942 the Allied governments signed the Declaration by United Nations, pledging to employ their full resources against the Axis and to make no separate peace — the first official use of the name "United Nations."

Cooperation and strategy

The Allies coordinated through summit conferences — including Tehran (1943), Yalta (February 1945), and Potsdam (1945) — and massive material cooperation such as the American Lend-Lease programme, which supplied the Soviet Union, Britain, and China with aircraft, vehicles, food, and raw materials. Strategy centred on defeating Germany first ("Europe first"), while holding and then rolling back Japanese advances in the Pacific.

Outcome

The coalition won complete victory in 1945: Germany was occupied and divided, and Japan surrendered in September after the atomic bombings and Soviet entry into the Pacific war. The wartime alliance's structures seeded the post-war United Nations, though cooperation between the Western Allies and the Soviet Union soon gave way to the Cold War.
