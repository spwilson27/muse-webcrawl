The United Nations Interpretation Service is the unit responsible for providing simultaneous interpretation at meetings of the United Nations. It is part of the Department for General Assembly and Conference Management (DGACM), the largest department of the UN Secretariat. Interpreters work into and out of the six official languages of the organization: Arabic, Chinese, English, French, Russian, and Spanish.

History

Simultaneous interpretation was used on a large scale for the first time at the Nuremberg trials of 1945–1946, and the United Nations adopted the technique from its earliest sessions. The League of Nations had relied mainly on consecutive interpretation, in which speakers pause while an interpreter renders long passages, but the UN's growing membership made the simultaneous system — pioneered with IBM equipment at Nuremberg — essential.

English and French were the original working languages of the Secretariat. Russian, Spanish, and Chinese acquired interpretation services as the organization developed, and Arabic was added as an official language of the General Assembly in 1973, with interpretation booths expanded accordingly.

How interpretation works

Interpreters sit in soundproof booths overlooking the meeting room, listening through headphones and speaking into microphones with only a fraction of a second's delay. Delegates listen on receivers set to one of six channels. Each booth is staffed by two or three interpreters who rotate roughly every 20 to 30 minutes because of the intense concentration the work requires.

Most interpretation is done through a relay system: an interpreter working from, for example, Chinese renders the speech into English or French, and the other booths work from that rendition. Staff interpreters must pass competitive examinations administered by the UN and typically work from at least two official languages into their main language.

Recruitment and training

Interpreters are recruited through language competitive examinations held by the United Nations. Candidates must hold a university degree, have a perfect command of their main language, and an excellent knowledge of at least two other official languages. Many interpreters are graduates of specialized schools of translation and interpretation and are members of professional bodies such as AIIC, the International Association of Conference Interpreters.

Other duty stations

Besides Headquarters in New York, interpretation services are maintained at the UN Offices at Geneva, Vienna, and Nairobi, and at the regional commissions. Meetings of the Security Council, the General Assembly, and other principal organs are always serviced in all six languages.
