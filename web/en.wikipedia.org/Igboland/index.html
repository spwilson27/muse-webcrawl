Igboland

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Igboland (Igbo: Àlà Ị̀gbò) is the homeland of the Igbo people of southeastern Nigeria. It lies mostly in the low-lying forest belt between the Niger River to the west and the Cross River to the east, encompassing most or parts of the modern states of Abia, Anambra, Ebonyi, Enugu, and Imo, together with northern Delta and Rivers states. Major cities include Onitsha, Enugu, Aba, Owerri, and Awka.

Geography and settlement

The region is one of the most densely populated rural areas of Africa, historically organised into hundreds of autonomous village-groups rather than centralised kingdoms—the exceptions being the kingdom of Nri, with its ritual influence, and the trading city-states of the Niger delta fringe, including Onitsha and the Aro Confederacy, whose oracle and trading networks dominated the region in the 18th and 19th centuries.

History

Archaeological finds such as the 9th–10th-century bronzes of Igbo-Ukwu testify to early metallurgical sophistication. From the 16th century the region was drawn into the Atlantic slave trade, through which many Igbo people were carried to the Americas. British colonial rule, consolidated after the Anglo-Aro War of 1901–1902, administered Igboland as part of Southern Nigeria; indirect rule through "warrant chiefs" provoked resistance, notably the Women's War of 1929.

In 1967 the Igbo-majority Eastern Region seceded as the Republic of Biafra, precipitating the Nigerian Civil War (1967–1970), in which blockade and famine killed an estimated one to three million people. After the war Igboland was reintegrated into Nigeria, though movements advocating Biafran independence have persisted.

Culture

Igboland is known for its market culture and commercial networks, the kola nut rituals of hospitality, masquerade (Mmanwu) traditions, and predominantly Christian population, alongside surviving practice of the traditional religion, Odinani.
