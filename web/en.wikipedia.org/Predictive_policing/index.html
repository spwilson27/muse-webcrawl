Predictive policing

Predictive policing is the usage of analytics and predictive methods in law enforcement to identify potential criminal activity. Predictive policing methods fall into four general categories: methods for predicting crimes, methods for predicting offenders, methods for predicting perpetrators' identities, and methods for predicting victims of crime.

Methods

Place-based predictive policing uses historical crime data — the type, location and time of past incidents — to forecast where and when crimes are more likely to occur, so that patrols can be directed to those areas. Well-known software products include PredPol (later Geolitica), developed from research at the University of California, Los Angeles and Santa Clara University, and systems marketed by companies such as IBM.

Person-based approaches attempt to identify individuals thought to be at elevated risk of committing or becoming victims of crime, such as the "heat list" once maintained by the Chicago Police Department as part of its Strategic Subject List.

History

Forecasting in policing has older roots in crime mapping and hot spot policing, but the term "predictive policing" gained prominence in the late 2000s and 2010s as departments experimented with algorithmic tools. The Los Angeles Police Department was among the early adopters of place-based prediction software.

Criticism

Critics argue that predictive policing can reinforce existing patterns of bias in police data, creating feedback loops in which historically over-policed neighbourhoods receive still more police attention. Civil liberties groups have raised concerns about transparency, due process for people placed on risk lists, and the reliability of the underlying algorithms. Some jurisdictions have restricted or discontinued predictive policing programmes in response to these concerns and to audits finding limited evidence of crime-reduction benefits.
