Nucleobase

Nucleobases (also known as nitrogenous bases or simply bases) are nitrogen-containing biological compounds that form nucleosides, which in turn are components of nucleotides, the monomers that make up nucleic acids such as DNA and RNA.

The primary bases

The five primary nucleobases are adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G), thymine (T) and uracil (U). Adenine and guanine are purines, derived from a double-ringed parent compound; cytosine, thymine and uracil are pyrimidines, with a single ring. In DNA, adenine pairs with thymine and guanine with cytosine through hydrogen bonding; in RNA, uracil replaces thymine.

Structure and bonding

Base pairing follows the Watson–Crick rules: A–T (or A–U) pairs have two hydrogen bonds, G–C pairs have three. Attached to a pentose sugar (ribose or deoxyribose) a base forms a nucleoside; adding phosphate groups yields nucleotides.

Modified bases

Many modified nucleobases occur in nature, especially in transfer RNA — for example inosine, pseudouridine and various methylated bases. Synthetic analogues such as 5-fluorouracil are used as drugs.
