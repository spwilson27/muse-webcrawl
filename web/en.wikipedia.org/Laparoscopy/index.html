Laparoscopy

Laparoscopy
TypeMinimally invasive surgical technique
InstrumentLaparoscope (thin telescope with camera)
Also calledKeyhole surgery; minimally invasive surgery
Common usesCholecystectomy, appendectomy, hernia repair, gynaecological surgery

Laparoscopy is an operation performed in the abdomen or pelvis using small incisions (usually 0.5–1.5 cm) with the aid of a camera. The laparoscope — a long, thin telescope with a video camera at its tip — transmits images of the internal organs to a monitor, allowing the surgeon to operate with long instruments passed through additional ports while avoiding a large open incision (laparotomy).

Technique

The abdomen is first inflated with carbon dioxide gas through a Veress needle or a small incision, creating a working space (pneumoperitoneum). Trocars are inserted to provide access ports, the laparoscope is introduced, and the surgery is performed under video guidance. Compared with open surgery, laparoscopy generally results in less postoperative pain, smaller scars, shorter hospital stays, and faster recovery, though it may take longer to perform and demands specialised training.

History and developments

Diagnostic laparoscopy was pioneered in the early 20th century (Georg Kelling and Hans Christian Jacobaeus), but operative laparoscopy took off only in the 1980s, when video cameras were miniaturised; the first laparoscopic cholecystectomy was performed by Erich Mühe in 1985 and popularised by Philippe Mouret in 1987. The technique has since expanded to most abdominal and pelvic operations and evolved into robot-assisted surgery and single-incision and natural-orifice approaches.
