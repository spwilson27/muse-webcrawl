Dry stone

Dry stone construction is a building method by which structures are made from stones without any mortar to bind them together. Stability comes from careful selection and placement: stones are fitted so that gravity, friction, and interlocking hold the structure together. Dry stone work includes field walls, terraces, bridges, houses, and ceremonial monuments, and the craft is among the oldest forms of building.

History

Prehistoric dry stone structures include Neolithic settlements such as Skara Brae in Orkney, brochs in Scotland, nuraghi in Sardinia, and clochán beehive huts in Ireland. Dry stone field walls are a signature of landscapes in Britain and Ireland, where thousands of miles were built especially during the enclosure era, and of Mediterranean terraced agriculture. In 2018, UNESCO inscribed the art of dry stone walling — knowledge and techniques shared by several European countries — on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Technique

A well-built wall has two faces of laid stones leaning slightly inward, a core of small filling stones ("hearting"), and through-stones tying the faces together, capped by coping stones. Good practice follows rules such as "one over two, two over one" to avoid vertical joints, and each stone is set to shed water and bear on the stones below. Because there is no rigid mortar, dry stone structures flex slightly and drain freely, which helps them survive frost and ground movement for centuries.

Modern use

Dry stone is still used for garden and landscape walls, restoration of historic structures, and erosion control. Organisations such as the Dry Stone Walling Association in Britain and the Stone Trust in the United States train and certify wallers.
