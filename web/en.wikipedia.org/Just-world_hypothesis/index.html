Just-world hypothesis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The just-world hypothesis or just-world fallacy is the cognitive bias that assumes that "people get what they deserve" — that actions have morally appropriate and fitting consequences for the actor. Good deeds are presumed to be rewarded and bad deeds punished, so that the world is seen as fundamentally fair and orderly.

Origins

The concept was developed by the social psychologist Melvin J. Lerner, whose experiments in the 1960s showed that observers, unable to intervene, would derogate innocent victims in order to preserve their belief that the victim's fate was deserved. Lerner's work grew out of clinical observations and earlier ideas about immanent justice, the notion — studied by Jean Piaget in children's moral development — that wrongdoing is automatically followed by misfortune.

Effects

Belief in a just world helps people cope with uncertainty and plan long-term, and it correlates with religiosity, authoritarianism, and support for existing social arrangements. Its darker consequences include victim blaming: attributing poverty, illness, or assault to the character or behaviour of those who suffer them. Researchers such as Zick Rubin and Letitia Anne Peplau developed scales measuring individual differences in just-world belief, and later work distinguishes belief in a just world for oneself from belief in a just world in general.

Criticism and related ideas

The hypothesis is related to the fundamental attribution error and to system justification theory. Critics note that just-world beliefs vary across cultures and that the experimental evidence, while robust for victim derogation, depends on context: empathy, similarity to the victim, and opportunities to help can all reduce blaming.
