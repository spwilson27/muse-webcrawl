Acquittal

In criminal law, an acquittal is a judgment that a person is not guilty of the crime with which they have been charged. An acquittal may be returned by a jury (or a judge in a bench trial) as a verdict of "not guilty", or it may result from a directed ruling by the court that the prosecution's evidence is legally insufficient. It is the opposite of a conviction.

Effect

Once acquitted, a defendant generally cannot be tried again for the same offence, under the protection against double jeopardy found in many legal systems — for example, the Fifth Amendment to the U.S. Constitution. In the United States an acquittal is final and cannot be appealed by the prosecution, however erroneous it may appear. An acquittal does not necessarily declare the defendant factually innocent; it means the prosecution failed to prove guilt beyond a reasonable doubt.

Variations by jurisdiction

Some jurisdictions permit partial acquittals (acquittal on some counts, conviction on others), and Scotland historically offered three verdicts — guilty, not guilty, and "not proven" — the last having the same effect as an acquittal while expressing residual doubt. A hung jury, by contrast, is neither an acquittal nor a conviction, and the case may be retried.

An acquitted defendant may still face civil liability for the same conduct, because civil cases use a lower burden of proof (preponderance of the evidence). A famous example is the O. J. Simpson case: acquitted of murder in 1995, he was later found liable for wrongful death in a civil suit.

Directed acquittal

A judge may direct an acquittal — sometimes called a judgment of acquittal or directed verdict — when no reasonable jury could convict on the evidence presented, or where a submission of "no case to answer" succeeds at the close of the prosecution's case.
