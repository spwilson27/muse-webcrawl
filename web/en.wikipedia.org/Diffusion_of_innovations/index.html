Diffusion of innovations

Diffusion of innovations is a theory that explains how, why, and at what rate new ideas and technology spread through cultures. It was popularised by sociologist Everett Rogers in his 1962 book Diffusion of Innovations, synthesising research that began with studies of hybrid seed corn adoption among Iowa farmers.

The S-shaped adoption curve and adopter categories.

Adopter categories

Rogers divided adopters into five categories along a bell curve: innovators (2.5%), early adopters (13.5%), early majority (34%), late majority (34%), and laggards (16%). Cumulative adoption over time typically follows an S-curve, slow at first, accelerating as the innovation becomes established, then levelling off at saturation.

Attributes affecting adoption

Rogers identified five characteristics that influence the speed of adoption: relative advantage, compatibility with existing values and practices, complexity, trialability, and observability of results.

Applications

The theory is widely used in marketing, public health, agriculture, education, and technology management. Geoffrey Moore's Crossing the Chasm adapted it to high-tech marketing, emphasising the gap between early adopters and the early majority.
