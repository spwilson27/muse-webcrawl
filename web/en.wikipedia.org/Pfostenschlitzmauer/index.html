Pfostenschlitzmauer

TypeFortification technique
PeriodBronze Age and Iron Age
RegionCentral and Western Europe

A Pfostenschlitzmauer (German, literally "post-slot wall") is a method of constructing defensive ramparts used at hillforts and oppida in Bronze Age and Iron Age Europe. In this technique, vertical wooden posts were set into slots cut in the front face of a stone wall, with the whole structure tied together by horizontal beams and a rubble or earth core.

Reconstructed Pfostenschlitzmauer: stone face slotted with upright timbers

The term belongs to a typology of Celtic rampart construction alongside the murus gallicus — a timber lattice nailed together and faced with stone, described by Julius Caesar — and the murus dacicus of Dacia. In a Pfostenschlitzmauer, the outer stone face is interrupted at regular intervals by vertical slots, each holding a stout post; horizontal timbers behind the face anchored the posts into the rampart body. The construction gave some flexibility and resistance to battering, though the exposed timber made the wall vulnerable to fire.

Examples

Walls of this type have been identified at major late Iron Age sites in Germany and France, including the oppidum of Manching and the Heidenmauer at the Odilienberg (Mont Sainte-Odile) in Alsace. Archaeologists distinguish them from related Pfostenschlitz variants by the arrangement and spacing of the post slots in surviving masonry.
