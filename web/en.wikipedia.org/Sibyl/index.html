Sibyl

The sibyls (Ancient Greek: Σίβυλλα, romanized: Síbylla) were prophetesses or oracles in Ancient Greece. The sibyls prophesied at holy sites under the divine influence of a deity, originally at Delphi and Pessinos. Their prophecies were influenced by divine inspiration from a deity, originally associated with Apollo.

The term "sibyl" comes through Latin from the ancient Greek word sibylla, meaning prophetess or fortune-teller. The first known Greek writer to mention a sibyl is Heraclitus (c. 500 BC). In later antiquity, various writers attested to the existence of several sibyls, often attached to particular places.

The Sibyls in tradition

The Roman author Varro, quoted by Lactantius, listed ten sibyls: the Persian, Libyan, Delphic, Cimmerian, Erythraean, Samian, Cumaean, Hellespontine, Phrygian, and Tiburtine sibyls. The most famous was the Cumaean Sibyl, who guided Aeneas through the underworld in Virgil's Aeneid and was said to have sold the Sibylline Books to Tarquin, the last king of Rome.

Sibylline Books and oracles

The Sibylline Books were a collection of oracular utterances in Greek hexameters, purchased from the Cumaean Sibyl by Tarquinius Superbus. Kept in the Temple of Jupiter on the Capitoline Hill, they were consulted by the Roman Senate in times of crisis until the temple's destruction by fire in 83 BC. Later collections of "Sibylline Oracles," composed in Jewish and Christian circles, blended biblical themes with pagan forms.

In art

Five sibyls were famously depicted by Michelangelo on the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel: the Delphic, Erythraean, Cumaean, Persian, and Libyan sibyls, shown as monumental figures alongside the Hebrew prophets.
