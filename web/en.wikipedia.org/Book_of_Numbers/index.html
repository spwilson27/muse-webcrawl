The Book of Numbers (from Greek Arithmoi; Hebrew: Bemidbar, "In the wilderness") is the fourth book of the Hebrew Bible and the Torah. It recounts the Israelites' journey from Mount Sinai through the wilderness to the plains of Moab on the border of the Promised Land, and takes its English name from the two censuses of the Israelites with which it begins and which it later repeats.

Summary

The book opens at Sinai with a census of the tribes and instructions for the arrangement of the camp around the Tabernacle. The Israelites then set out for Canaan, but a pattern of rebellion sets in: complaints about food, Miriam and Aaron's challenge to Moses, and — decisively — the people's refusal to enter the land after the ten faithless spies report giants in Canaan. That generation is condemned to wander forty years in the wilderness until it dies out.

Later chapters describe further rebellions (Korah's revolt, Moses striking the rock at Meribah), the bronze serpent, the prophet Balaam and his talking donkey, and a second census of the new generation. The book ends with Israel encamped on the plains of Moab, opposite Jericho, with laws concerning inheritance, festivals, vows, and the boundaries and cities of the land they are about to enter.

Composition

Scholars generally regard Numbers as a composite of Priestly material and older non-Priestly narrative, redacted into its final form in the Persian period. Some episodes, such as the Balaam oracles (chapters 22–24), are thought to preserve genuinely ancient poetry; the figure of Balaam is also attested in the Deir Alla inscription from Jordan.
