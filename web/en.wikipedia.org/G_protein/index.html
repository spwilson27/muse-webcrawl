G protein

G proteins
Type: Guanine nucleotide-binding proteins
Classes: Heterotrimeric (α, β, γ subunits); small monomeric GTPases
Discovered via: Work of Alfred G. Gilman and Martin Rodbell (Nobel Prize 1994)

G proteins, also known as guanine nucleotide-binding proteins, are a family of proteins that act as molecular switches inside cells, and are involved in transmitting signals from a variety of stimuli outside a cell to its interior. Their activity is regulated by their ability to bind to and hydrolyze guanosine triphosphate (GTP) to guanosine diphosphate (GDP). When they are bound to GTP, they are "on", and, when they are bound to GDP, they are "off".

G proteins belong to the larger group of enzymes called GTPases. There are two classes of G proteins. The first function as monomeric small GTPases, while the second form function as heterotrimeric G protein complexes. The latter class of complexes is made up of alpha (α), beta (β) and gamma (γ) subunits.

Heterotrimeric G proteins and receptors

Heterotrimeric G proteins are activated by G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), the largest family of cell-surface receptors, which respond to hormones, neurotransmitters, light, and odours. The activated receptor acts as a guanine nucleotide exchange factor, prompting the α subunit to release GDP and bind GTP; the α subunit and the βγ dimer then separately regulate effectors such as adenylyl cyclase, phospholipase C, and ion channels until GTP is hydrolyzed. Based on the α subunit, the main families are Gs (stimulatory), Gi/o (inhibitory), Gq/11, and G12/13.

History

Alfred G. Gilman and Martin Rodbell discovered G proteins when researching stimulation of cells by adrenaline; they received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1994 for the discovery. The second messenger cyclic AMP, produced by Gs-activated adenylyl cyclase, links G proteins to earlier work by Earl Sutherland.

Medical importance

GPCRs and their G proteins are the targets of a large fraction of marketed drugs. Bacterial toxins exploit G proteins: cholera toxin locks Gs in its active state, causing profuse diarrhoea, while pertussis toxin inactivates Gi. Mutations in G protein genes cause endocrine and developmental disorders, and the oncogene GNAS (encoding Gsα) is mutated in some pituitary tumours and in McCune–Albright syndrome.
