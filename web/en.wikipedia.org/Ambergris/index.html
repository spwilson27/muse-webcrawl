Ambergris

Ambergris (from Old French ambre gris, "grey amber") is a solid, waxy, flammable substance produced in the digestive system of sperm whales. Fresh ambergris has a faecal odour, but after floating for years in the ocean and oxidising it develops a sweet, earthy, marine scent prized in perfumery as a fixative that prolongs fragrance. Aged pieces wash ashore and have historically fetched very high prices.

Formation

Ambergris forms in the intestines of a small proportion of sperm whales, possibly around indigestible squid beaks, and is expelled (or released after death) into the sea. Its principal component is ambrein, an odourless triterpene alcohol that oxidises in sunlight and seawater into fragrant compounds including ambroxan. Modern perfumery largely uses synthetic ambroxan, but natural ambergris remains legal to trade in some countries and prohibited in others, including the United States, because of whale-protection laws.

History

Ambergris has been valued since antiquity across the Middle East, China and Europe — burned as incense, used in medicine, and even eaten as a delicacy and aphrodisiac by kings. Its origin was long mysterious: it was variously attributed to sea birds, bees, springs on the sea floor, or a fungus, until its connection to the sperm whale was established in the 18th century. It features in literature from One Thousand and One Nights to Moby-Dick.
