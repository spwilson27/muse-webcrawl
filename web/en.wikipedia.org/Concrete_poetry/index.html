Concrete poetry

Concrete poetry is an arrangement of linguistic elements in which the typographical effect is more important in conveying meaning than verbal significance alone. It is sometimes called visual poetry or shape poetry, though the concrete movement distinguished itself from purely shaped verse by treating language as a material whose spatial arrangement on the page is integral to the poem.

History

Patterned poems have a long lineage, from the shaped verses of Simmias of Rhodes in the Hellenistic era and George Herbert's "Easter Wings" (1633) to Lewis Carroll's mouse's tale and the calligrammes of Guillaume Apollinaire. The concrete poetry movement proper emerged in the 1950s as an international avant-garde tendency. In Brazil, the Noigandres group — Augusto de Campos, Haroldo de Campos, and Décio Pignatari — issued a "Pilot Plan for Concrete Poetry" in 1958; in Europe, Eugen Gomringer's "constellations" in Switzerland and Öyvind Fahlström's manifesto in Sweden developed parallel ideas. The term "concrete" was borrowed from concrete art.

Characteristics and influence

Concrete poems minimise syntax and narrative in favour of the word as object: repetition, spacing, typeface, and layout carry the meaning. The movement overlapped with lettrism, visual art, and later artists' books and digital poetry. Critics debated whether stripping language of discursive content liberated or impoverished it, but the movement's emphasis on the visuality of text influenced graphic design, advertising, and later forms of electronic literature.
