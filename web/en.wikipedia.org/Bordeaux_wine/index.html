Bordeaux wine

Bordeaux wine is produced in the wine region of Bordeaux, around the city of Bordeaux on the Garonne in southwest France, extending along the Gironde estuary and the Garonne and Dordogne rivers. With around 110,000 hectares of vineyards and some 6,000 producers, it is the largest fine-wine region in the world, producing roughly 700 million bottles a year, from everyday table wine to some of the most expensive and celebrated wines on earth.

Styles and grapes

Nearly 90% of Bordeaux wine is red ("claret" in Britain). Red Bordeaux is almost always a blend, principally of Merlot, Cabernet Sauvignon and Cabernet Franc, with smaller amounts of Petit Verdot, Malbec and Carménère. The gravelly "Left Bank" (Médoc and Graves) favours Cabernet Sauvignon, producing structured, long-lived wines, while the clay-limestone "Right Bank" (Saint-Émilion and Pomerol) favours Merlot, producing softer, rounder wines. Dry whites are made from Sauvignon Blanc and Sémillon, and the sweet botrytised wines of Sauternes and Barsac are world-famous.

Classifications

The Bordeaux Wine Official Classification of 1855, drawn up for Napoleon III's Exposition Universelle in Paris, ranked the red wines of the Médoc (plus Château Haut-Brion in Graves) into five classes of crus classés; it remains essentially unchanged, with only one promotion (Château Mouton Rothschild to first growth in 1973). The five "first growths" — Lafite Rothschild, Latour, Margaux, Haut-Brion and Mouton Rothschild — command the region's highest prices. Saint-Émilion maintains its own regularly revised classification, while Pomerol, home to the legendary Pétrus, has none.

History and trade

Winemaking in Bordeaux dates to Roman times, but the region's fortune was made in the 12th century, when the marriage of Eleanor of Aquitaine to Henry II of England opened the English market. The Dutch drained the marshy Médoc in the 17th century, creating the great gravel estates. Wines are sold through the place de Bordeaux, a network of négociants, largely via the spring en primeur futures campaign. Bordeaux must also protect its vines from mildew — the problem that led to the invention of Bordeaux mixture.
