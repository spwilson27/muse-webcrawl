Comet Shoemaker–Levy 9 (formally designated D/1993 F2) was a comet that broke apart in July 1992 after passing close to Jupiter and collided with the planet in July 1994, providing the first direct observation of an extraterrestrial collision of Solar System objects. It was discovered on 24 March 1993 by astronomers Carolyn and Eugene M. Shoemaker and David H. Levy in a photograph taken with the Schmidt telescope at Palomar Observatory.

Breakup

Shoemaker–Levy 9 was unusual at discovery: it was orbiting Jupiter rather than the Sun, having been captured by the planet decades earlier. In July 1992 it passed within Jupiter's Roche limit and was torn apart by tidal forces into a chain of at least 21 fragments — likened to a "string of pearls" — up to about 2 km across, which continued on a two-year orbit that would end inside the planet.

Impacts

Between 16 and 22 July 1994 the fragments struck Jupiter's southern hemisphere at about 60 km/s, just beyond the visible limb. The impacts raised fireballs thousands of kilometres high and left dark scars in the Jovian atmosphere, some larger than Earth, that persisted for months. Fragment G, the largest, released energy estimated at millions of megatons of TNT — far exceeding the world's nuclear arsenals. The event was observed worldwide, by the Hubble Space Telescope and by the Galileo spacecraft en route to Jupiter.

Significance

The collision dramatically demonstrated the role of impacts in the Solar System and spurred planetary-defence efforts to catalogue near-Earth objects; the comet's "D/" designation prefix denotes a defunct or destroyed periodic comet.
