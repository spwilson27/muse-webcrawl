Smart growth

Smart growth favours compact, transit-oriented, walkable development

Smart growth is an urban planning and transportation theory that concentrates growth in compact, walkable urban centres to avoid sprawl. It advocates mixed land uses, a range of housing choices, walkable neighbourhoods, preservation of open space and farmland, development directed toward existing communities, and varied transportation options including public transit, cycling and walking.

Principles

The Smart Growth Network, convened in the United States in the 1990s with the Environmental Protection Agency, articulated ten principles, including mixing land uses, taking advantage of compact building design, creating walkable neighbourhoods, fostering distinctive communities with a strong sense of place, preserving critical environmental areas, and making development decisions predictable and fair. Related concepts include New Urbanism and transit-oriented development.

Debate

Supporters argue smart growth reduces infrastructure costs, traffic, air pollution and loss of farmland. Critics contend that restrictive growth boundaries raise housing prices and that consumer preferences favour low-density living; economists and planners continue to debate the magnitude of these effects. Smart growth policies have been implemented most notably in Oregon, with its urban growth boundaries, and in Maryland's Smart Growth Areas Act of 1997.
