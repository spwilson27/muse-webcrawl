Tax basis

Under U.S. federal income tax law, the tax basis of an asset is the amount at which the taxpayer holds the asset for tax purposes — generally its cost, adjusted over time — and is used to compute gain or loss when the asset is sold or otherwise disposed of. The difference between the amount realized on a disposition and the asset's adjusted basis is the taxable gain (or deductible loss).

Initial basis

For purchased property, basis is usually its cost, including purchase price and certain acquisition expenses. Property received by gift generally takes a carryover basis from the donor for computing gain, while inherited property traditionally receives a "stepped-up" basis equal to its fair market value at the decedent's death. Property acquired in a tax-free exchange takes the basis of the property surrendered.

Adjusted basis

Basis is not static. It is increased by capital improvements and certain income items, and decreased by depreciation deductions, casualty losses, and returns of capital. For shares of stock, basis is adjusted for splits and reinvested distributions. Partnerships and S corporations use inside and outside basis concepts extensively, with partners' outside bases adjusted for income, losses, and distributions.

Importance

Because gain is measured from basis, record-keeping of basis is essential to correct tax reporting. Brokerage firms in the United States are required to report the basis of covered securities to the Internal Revenue Service on Form 1099-B.
