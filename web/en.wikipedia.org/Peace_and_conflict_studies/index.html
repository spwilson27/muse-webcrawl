Peace and conflict studies is a social science field that identifies and analyses violent and nonviolent behaviours as well as the structural mechanisms attending conflicts (including social conflicts), with a view towards understanding those processes which lead to a more desirable human condition. A variation, peace studies (irenology), is an interdisciplinary effort aiming at the prevention, de-escalation and solution of conflicts by peaceful means, thereby seeking "peace", defined by Johan Galtung as the absence not only of direct violence but also of structural violence — positive peace.

History

Peace research emerged after the Second World War, spurred by the threat of nuclear war. Early centres included the Peace Research Institute Oslo (PRIO), founded in 1959 under Johan Galtung, who also founded the Journal of Peace Research (1964) and is widely considered the discipline's founder. University programs developed from the 1960s — among them Bradford University's Department of Peace Studies (1973) — and the field expanded after the Cold War into conflict resolution, peacebuilding, transitional justice and human security.

Key concepts

  • Negative vs positive peace — the absence of war versus the presence of justice, equity and harmonious social relations.
  • Structural and cultural violence — harm built into social structures and the cultural beliefs that legitimize violence.
  • Conflict transformation — an approach, associated with John Paul Lederach, that views conflict as an opportunity for constructive change rather than merely something to be settled or resolved.
  • Peacebuilding and peacemaking — practical interventions before, during and after violent conflict, from mediation to post-conflict reconstruction.

Methods and scope

The field draws on political science, sociology, psychology, anthropology, economics and international relations, combining quantitative conflict datasets (such as the Uppsala Conflict Data Program) with case studies and action research. Scholars study interstate war, civil wars, terrorism, genocide and everyday violence, as well as nonviolent movements, diplomacy and international institutions.

Criticism

Critics have charged peace studies with normative bias — conflating scholarship with advocacy — while defenders reply that medicine similarly combines research with a commitment to health. Others debate the coherence of the field and the effectiveness of peacebuilding interventions in practice.
