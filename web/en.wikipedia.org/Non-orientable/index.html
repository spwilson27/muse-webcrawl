Non-orientable (Orientability)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, orientability is a property of surfaces and, more generally, of manifolds. A surface is non-orientable if it does not admit a consistent choice of "side" or orientation over the whole surface: an object transported around certain closed paths on the surface returns as its own mirror image.

Examples

The best-known non-orientable surface is the Möbius strip, formed by giving a strip of paper a half-twist before joining its ends; an ant walking along it returns to its starting point flipped upside down. The Klein bottle and the real projective plane are closed non-orientable surfaces: the Klein bottle cannot be embedded in three-dimensional space without self-intersection, while the Möbius strip can.

Formal definition

A manifold is orientable if its tangent bundle admits a consistent orientation, equivalently if its first Stiefel–Whitney class vanishes, or if it possesses a nowhere-zero volume form. An orientation double cover exists for every manifold; the manifold is orientable exactly when this cover is disconnected.

Applications

Orientability matters in integration on manifolds, in the classification of surfaces, and in physics, where non-orientable geometries appear in some cosmological models.
