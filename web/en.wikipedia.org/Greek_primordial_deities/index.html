Primordial deities

The first generation of gods in Greek mythology, born of Chaos and preceding the Titans and Olympians.

In Greek mythology, the primordial deities are the first generation of gods and goddesses, personifications of fundamental elements of the universe from whom all later beings descend. The standard account is that of Hesiod's Theogony (c. 700 BC), in which first comes Chaos, the yawning void, and then Gaia (Earth), Tartarus (the abyss below the world), and Eros (Desire). From Chaos spring Erebus (Darkness) and Nyx (Night); from Nyx come Aether (Brightness) and Hemera (Day), as well as a host of darker abstractions including Momus (Blame), Moros (Doom) and the Fates. Gaia alone produces Uranus (Sky), the Ourea (Mountains) and Pontus (Sea), and with Uranus she becomes the mother of the Titans, the Cyclopes and the Hecatoncheires.

Principal figures

  • Chaos — the void from which everything arose; in later cosmogonies interpreted as formless matter.
  • Gaia — the Earth, mother of sky, sea and mountains, and ancestress of nearly all gods.
  • Tartarus — a place and a being: the deepest region of the underworld, later a prison for the Titans.
  • Eros — desire, the force that brings beings together; not to be confused with the later son of Aphrodite.
  • Nyx — Night, so powerful that even Zeus feared her, according to the Iliad.
  • Erebus — Darkness, consort of Nyx and father of Aether and Hemera.
  • Aether and Hemera — the bright upper air and the Day.
  • Uranus and Pontus — Sky and Sea, born of Gaia without a partner.
  • Chronos (in Orphic cosmogonies) — Time, who produces Aether and Chaos; distinct from the Titan Cronus.
  • Ananke and Phanes — Necessity and the first-born creator in Orphic traditions.

Variations

Other sources give differing schemes. Homer knows Oceanus and Tethys as the origin of the gods; the Orphic theogonies begin with Chronos or with the world egg from which Phanes hatches; and Aristophanes' parody in The Birds has Chaos, Night, Erebus and Tartarus precede the egg of Eros. Unlike the Olympians, the primordials received little cult worship and function mainly within cosmogony, as personified forces rather than characters in myth.
