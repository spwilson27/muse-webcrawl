Ferrite (iron)

Ferrite, also known as α-iron or alpha iron, is a solid solution of carbon dissolved in iron with a body-centred cubic (BCC) crystal structure. It is the principal constituent of most steels at room temperature and gives them their magnetic properties. The name derives from the Latin ferrum, meaning iron.

Properties

Pure ferrite is soft, ductile, and strongly ferromagnetic below its Curie temperature of about 770 °C. Because the BCC lattice has only small interstitial sites, ferrite can dissolve very little carbon — a maximum of about 0.02 weight percent at 727 °C, and far less at room temperature. Silicon, manganese, and phosphorus dissolve readily in ferrite, with phosphorus markedly increasing its hardness and reducing ductility.

Role in steel microstructure

On the iron–carbon phase diagram, ferrite is stable below 912 °C in pure iron; above that temperature it transforms into face-centred cubic austenite (γ-iron). In slowly cooled hypoeutectoid steels, proeutectoid ferrite forms along with pearlite, a lamellar mixture of ferrite and cementite. The proportion and grain size of ferrite largely determine the strength and toughness of mild steel: finer ferrite grains raise yield strength, as described by the Hall–Petch relationship.

Delta ferrite

At temperatures above about 1,394 °C, iron reverts to a BCC form called δ-ferrite (delta ferrite), which persists until melting. A small retained fraction of delta ferrite is deliberately maintained in many stainless steel welds to prevent hot cracking.
