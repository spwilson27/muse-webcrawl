Poor Law Amendment Act 1834

The Poor Law Amendment Act 1834 (4 & 5 Will. 4 c. 76), commonly called the New Poor Law, was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that reformed the relief of poverty in England and Wales. It replaced the parish-based system descending from the Elizabethan Poor Law of 1601, which had provided "outdoor relief" to the able-bodied poor in their homes, with a centralized, deterrent system centered on the workhouse.

The Royal Commission

The Act followed the report of the Royal Commission on the Poor Laws of 1832–34, influenced by commissioners and assistant commissioners such as Nassau Senior and Edwin Chadwick. The commissioners argued that allowances in aid of wages — exemplified by the Speenhamland system — depressed wages and encouraged dependency, and recommended the principle of "less eligibility": the condition of a pauper maintained in a workhouse should be less desirable than that of the poorest independent laborer.

Provisions

The Act grouped parishes into Poor Law unions administered by elected boards of guardians, supervised by a central Poor Law Commission in London. Able-bodied claimants were, in principle, to receive relief only inside workhouses with deliberately harsh regimes — families separated, monotonous labor, and austere diet. Outdoor relief to the able-bodied was to be abolished, though in practice it continued on a large scale, especially in the industrial north, where the new law provoked fierce resistance (the Anti-Poor Law movement).

Legacy

The New Poor Law reshaped Victorian attitudes toward poverty and welfare administration, and its workhouses became notorious symbols of official harshness — later immortalized in Charles Dickens's Oliver Twist. The system was dismantled piecemeal in the early 20th century, and the last vestiges of the Poor Law were abolished by the National Assistance Act 1948.
