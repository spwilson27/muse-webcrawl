Surface mining

Surface mining, also called opencast or opencut mining, is a method of extracting minerals near the earth's surface by removing the soil and rock (overburden) that lies above the deposit. It contrasts with underground mining, which leaves the overburden in place and extracts minerals through tunnels or shafts. Surface mining is used when deposits are shallow, and accounts for the majority of the world's mineral production by tonnage.

Methods

The main techniques are strip mining (including mountaintop removal), open-pit mining, quarrying, and dredging or placer mining. In strip mining, long strips of overburden are removed to expose seams such as coal; in open-pit mines, the excavation proceeds downward in benches. Enormous machines — draglines, bucket-wheel excavators, and haul trucks among the largest vehicles ever built — move overburden and ore.

Environmental impact and reclamation

Surface mining profoundly alters landscapes, habitats, and drainage, and can generate acid mine drainage from exposed sulfides. In the United States, the Surface Mining Control and Reclamation Act of 1977 requires operators to restore mined land approximately to its original contour and use, funded in part by fees on coal production. Reclamation practices elsewhere vary widely; abandoned sites remain a significant environmental liability worldwide.
