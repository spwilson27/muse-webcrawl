Hydrogen bond

Hydrogen bonds (dashed) between water molecules

A hydrogen bond is an electrostatic attraction between a hydrogen atom covalently bound to a highly electronegative atom — typically nitrogen, oxygen, or fluorine (the donor) — and another electronegative atom bearing a lone pair of electrons (the acceptor). Hydrogen bonds are stronger than ordinary van der Waals interactions but far weaker than covalent or ionic bonds, with typical energies of about 5–30 kJ/mol. They may occur between molecules (intermolecular) or within different parts of a single molecule (intramolecular).

Properties and consequences

Hydrogen bonding explains many anomalous properties of water: its unusually high boiling point, melting point, and surface tension for so light a molecule, and the fact that ice is less dense than liquid water — each water molecule in ice forms four hydrogen bonds in an open tetrahedral network. Substances that can form hydrogen bonds with water are generally soluble in it, and the bonds are responsible for much of the "stickiness" and cohesion of water.

Importance in biology

Hydrogen bonds are fundamental to the structure of the most important biological molecules. The two strands of the DNA double helix are held together by hydrogen bonds between base pairs — two between adenine and thymine, three between guanine and cytosine. In proteins, hydrogen bonds between backbone amide and carbonyl groups stabilise secondary structures such as the alpha helix and beta sheet, and they contribute throughout to protein folding, enzyme–substrate recognition, and molecular recognition generally.

Nature of the bond

The hydrogen bond is primarily electrostatic in origin, involving the attraction between the partially positive hydrogen (made electron-poor by its bond to an electronegative atom) and the partially negative acceptor. Modern analyses also recognise contributions from charge transfer and a degree of covalency. The concept was developed in the 1920s and 1930s, notably by Linus Pauling, who popularised it in his book The Nature of the Chemical Bond (1939).
