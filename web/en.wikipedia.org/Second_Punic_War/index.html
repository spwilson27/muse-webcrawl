Second Punic War

Date218–201 BC
LocationItaly, Iberia, Sicily, North Africa, Cisalpine Gaul
BelligerentsRoman Republic vs. Carthage
ResultRoman victory; Carthage loses Iberia and its fleet

The Second Punic War (218–201 BC) was the second of three wars fought between Carthage and the Roman Republic, and the defining struggle for mastery of the western Mediterranean. It is famous above all for the campaigns of the Carthaginian general Hannibal, who crossed the Alps with war elephants and won a series of devastating victories in Italy, yet ultimately lost the war.

Background and outbreak

After losing Sicily in the First Punic War, Carthage compensated by building an empire in Iberia (Spain) under the Barcid family. A treaty set the river Ebro as the limit of Carthaginian expansion, but when Hannibal in 219 BC besieged and captured Saguntum, a city under Roman protection south of the Ebro, Rome demanded his surrender and, on refusal, declared war.

Hannibal in Italy

Rather than await attack, Hannibal marched overland through Iberia and Gaul, crossed the Alps in the autumn of 218 BC losing much of his army, and descended into Italy. He defeated Roman armies at the Trebia (218) and at Lake Trasimene (217), where he destroyed an army by ambush. In 216 BC at Cannae he achieved one of the most celebrated victories in military history, encircling and annihilating a Roman army of some 70,000 men. Many of Rome's southern allies defected, including Capua, but Rome itself refused to negotiate. Under the delaying strategy associated with Fabius Maximus ("the Delayer"), the Romans avoided pitched battle, harried Hannibal's foragers and cut down his reinforcements; his brother Hasdrubal's relief army was destroyed at the Metaurus in 207 BC. Unable to take Rome or replace his losses, Hannibal's campaign gradually stalled in the south of Italy.

The wider war and Zama

Meanwhile Rome carried the war to Iberia, where the young Scipio (later Africanus) destroyed Carthaginian power by 206 BC, and to Sicily and Macedonia. In 204 BC Scipio invaded North Africa. Hannibal was recalled to defend Carthage and was decisively defeated at the Battle of Zama in 202 BC.

Peace and consequences

The peace of 201 BC stripped Carthage of Iberia and its overseas possessions, limited its navy to ten warships, forbade it to make war without Roman permission, and imposed an indemnity of 10,000 talents payable over fifty years. Rome emerged as the dominant power of the western Mediterranean, though the war's cost — in lives, land devastated and social change, including the growth of large slave-worked estates — reshaped Roman society profoundly. The main ancient sources are the histories of Polybius and Livy.
