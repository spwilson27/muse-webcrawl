Holy Roman Emperor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Holy Roman Emperor, originally and officially the Emperor of the Romans (Latin: Imperator Romanorum, German: Kaiser der Römer) during the Middle Ages, and also known as the German-Roman Emperor, was the ruler and head of state of the Holy Roman Empire. The title was held in conjunction with the title of King of Germany. In theory and diplomacy, the emperors considered themselves primus inter pares, first among equals, among other Catholic monarchs across Europe.

The role was traditionally elective by the German prince-electors, the highest-ranking noblemen of the realm. The elected emperor-elect (Römisch-deutscher König, King of the Romans) would then be crowned by the Pope or his deputy. The practice of imperial coronation by the Pope lasted until the sixteenth century; thereafter the title was assumed on election, the practice being formalised by Maximilian I taking the style "Emperor-elect of the Romans" in 1508.

History

The imperial title in the West was revived when Charlemagne was crowned emperor by Pope Leo III on Christmas Day 800 in Rome. After the division of the Carolingian Empire, the title passed intermittently among Carolingian rulers and Italian claimants before lapsing in 924. It was restored by the East Frankish (German) king Otto I, crowned emperor in 962; modern historians usually date the beginning of the Holy Roman Empire from this event.

Through the central Middle Ages the emperors of the Ottonian, Salian, and Hohenstaufen dynasties struggled with the papacy over authority in the Church — most famously in the Investiture Controversy between Henry IV and Pope Gregory VII — and campaigned repeatedly in Italy. The Golden Bull of 1356, issued by Charles IV, fixed the electoral procedure: seven prince-electors (the archbishops of Mainz, Cologne, and Trier; the King of Bohemia; the Count Palatine of the Rhine; the Duke of Saxony; and the Margrave of Brandenburg) chose the emperor by majority vote at Frankfurt.

From 1438 the title was held almost continuously by the House of Habsburg, interrupted only by the Wittelsbach Charles VII (1742–1745). Voltaire famously quipped that the Empire was "neither holy, nor Roman, nor an empire." The last emperor, Francis II, abdicated on 6 August 1806, during the Napoleonic Wars, after Napoleon's victory at Austerlitz and the formation of the Confederation of the Rhine had dissolved the Empire's effective structure; Francis continued to rule as Emperor of Austria.

Titles and authority

Emperors styled themselves with an accumulating list of titles, commonly including King of Germany, King of Italy, and (from the early modern period) King of Bohemia and of Hungary in personal union. In practice, imperial authority rested on the emperor's own dynastic lands, on negotiated consent from the Imperial Diet, and on institutions such as the Reichskammergericht (Imperial Chamber Court) and the Imperial Circles established by the reforms of Maximilian I.
