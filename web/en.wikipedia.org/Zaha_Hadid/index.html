Dame Zaha Mohammad Hadid (31 October 1950 – 31 March 2016) was an Iraqi-British architect, artist and designer, recognised as a major figure in architecture of the late 20th and early 21st centuries. Born in Baghdad, Iraq, Hadid studied mathematics as an undergraduate and then enrolled at the Architectural Association School of Architecture in 1972. In search of an alternative system to traditional architectural drawing, and influenced by Suprematism and the Russian avant-garde, Hadid adopted painting as a design tool and abstraction as an investigative principle to "reinvestigate the experiments of Modernism [...] to unveil new fields of building."

She was described by UNESCO as a practitioner of deconstructivism, and The Guardian called her the "Queen of the curve". She liberated architectural geometry, giving it a whole new expressive identity. Her major works include the London Aquatics Centre for the 2012 Olympics, the Broad Art Museum, Rome's MAXXI Museum, and the Guangzhou Opera House. Some of her awards and recognitions include the Pritzker Architecture Prize — the first woman to win it — and appointments to the Order of the British Empire for services to architecture.

Early life and education

Hadid was born on 31 October 1950 in Baghdad, Iraq, to an upper class Iraqi family. Her father, Muhammad al-Hajj Husayn Hadid, was a wealthy industrialist from Mosul. He co-founded the left-liberal al-Ahali group in 1932, a significant political organisation in the 1930s and 1940s, and co-founded the National Democratic Party in Iraq. Her mother, Wajiha al-Sabunji, was an artist from Mosul. Hadid was educated in Baghdad at a convent school run by nuns, and later in England and Switzerland. She studied mathematics at the American University of Beirut before moving to London in 1972 to study at the Architectural Association School of Architecture, where her teachers included Rem Koolhaas, Elia Zenghelis and Bernard Tschumi.

Career

After graduating in 1977, Hadid joined the Office for Metropolitan Architecture in Rotterdam, working with Koolhaas and Zenghelis. She became a partner in 1980 and founded her own London-based practice, Zaha Hadid Architects, the same year. Her early built works included the Vitra Fire Station in Weil am Rhein, Germany (1993), and the Bergisel Ski Jump in Innsbruck, Austria (2002). For years her reputation rested largely on visionary, unbuilt projects and paintings, but after 2000 major commissions followed worldwide, including the Rosenthal Center for Contemporary Art in Cincinnati (2003), the BMW Central Building in Leipzig (2005), the Phaeno Science Center in Wolfsburg (2005), and the Heydar Aliyev Center in Baku (2012), noted for its flowing, curvilinear form.

Death and legacy

Hadid died of a heart attack on 31 March 2016 in a Miami hospital, where she was being treated for bronchitis. At her death, her practice employed some 400 people and had projects under way around the world. In 2004 she became the first woman — and the first Muslim — to receive the Pritzker Architecture Prize. She received the UK's most prestigious architectural award, the Stirling Prize, in 2010 and 2011, and in 2015 became the first and only woman to be awarded the Royal Gold Medal from the Royal Institute of British Architects in her own right.
