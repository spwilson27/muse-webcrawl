Cook Strait (Māori: Te Moana-o-Raukawa, 'the sea of Raukawa') separates the North and South Islands of New Zealand. The strait connects the Tasman Sea on the northwest with the South Pacific Ocean on the southeast. It is 22 kilometres (14 mi) wide at its narrowest point, and is considered one of the most dangerous and unpredictable waters in the world; nevertheless a regular ferry service runs across it between Wellington (Picton-bound) ports.

Geography and conditions

The strait runs roughly northwest–southeast between Cape Terawhiti on the North Island and Arapawa Island and Cape Campbell in the Marlborough Sounds. Strong tidal currents funnelled through the narrow channel, combined with the "roaring forties" winds channelled between the islands' mountain ranges, produce heavy seas; several ships have been lost, most famously the inter-island ferry Wahine in 1968 with 53 deaths.

Transport and infrastructure

Interislander and Bluebridge ferries carry passengers, vehicles, and rail freight between Wellington and Picton in about three and a half hours. Three submarine HVDC cables of the Inter-Island link cross the strait, transferring electricity between Benmore in the South Island and Haywards near Wellington. The strait's tidal currents have been studied for possible tidal power generation.

History

The strait is named for Captain James Cook, who navigated it in 1770 and established that New Zealand comprised two main islands. The first person recorded to swim it was Barrie Devenport in 1962; it is one of the "Oceans Seven" marathon swimming challenges.
