The Pont-Aven School (École de Pont-Aven) was a loose association of artists who worked in and around the village of Pont-Aven in Brittany, France, in the last decades of the 19th century. The artists' colony at Pont-Aven had existed since the 1860s, attracting American and European painters to Brittany's picturesque scenery and cheap living, but the name now chiefly denotes the avant-garde circle that formed around Paul Gauguin during his stays between 1886 and 1894.

Synthetism

Working at the Pension Gloanec and the Buvette de la Plage, Gauguin and the young Émile Bernard developed Synthetism: a style that rejected naturalistic imitation in favour of flat areas of bold, unnatural colour enclosed by dark outlines (Cloisonnism), simplified forms, and the expression of ideas and emotions rather than appearances. Gauguin's Vision of the Sermon (Jacob Wrestling with the Angel) (1888), painted at Pont-Aven, is its manifesto. Later that year at nearby Le Pouldu, Paul Sérusier painted The Talisman under Gauguin's direction — a small landscape of near-abstract colour patches that Sérusier carried back to Paris and used to found the Nabis group.

Members and influence

Other associates included Charles Laval, Meyer de Haan, Jan Verkade, Armand Séguin, Roderic O'Conor and Cuno Amiet. The Pont-Aven synthesis of symbolism, primitivism and expressive colour profoundly influenced the Nabis, Fauvism and the development of modern art, even as Gauguin's search for an "untainted" primitive culture soon drove him onward to Tahiti.
