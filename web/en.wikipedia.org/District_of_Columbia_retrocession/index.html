D.C. retrocession

Event: Return of land to Virginia

Date: 1846–1847

Area returned: About 31 square miles (Alexandria County)

Effect: District reduced to Maryland-ceded land only

The District of Columbia retrocession was the process, completed in 1847, by which the portion of the District of Columbia originally ceded by Virginia — present-day Arlington County and part of Alexandria, Virginia — was returned to the Commonwealth of Virginia. The original district was a square ten miles on a side; after retrocession it consisted only of the territory ceded by Maryland on the northeast bank of the Potomac River.

Background

The Residence Act of 1790 authorized a federal district carved from land ceded by Maryland and Virginia. Alexandria, a prosperous port town, was included in the Virginia portion. Over time, Alexandrians complained that Congress neglected the town, that its economy suffered because federal improvements concentrated on the Maryland side, and that residents had lost their representation in Congress and the Virginia legislature. The possibility that Congress might abolish slavery in the District — Alexandria hosted a major slave market — also motivated the retrocession movement.

The retrocession

In 1846, after petitions from Alexandria residents, Congress passed legislation authorizing retrocession subject to a referendum of the area's voters, and Virginia enacted a law accepting the territory. The referendum passed, and President James K. Polk issued a proclamation transferring the land on September 7, 1846. Virginia formally took possession in 1847.

Legacy and modern proposals

Questions were raised about the constitutionality of retrocession, though the courts never invalidated it. Modern proposals occasionally suggest retroceding most of the remaining District to Maryland as an alternative to D.C. statehood, an idea raised in debates over the District's lack of voting representation and discussed in connection with home rule.
