Metabolic syndrome is a clustering of at least three of five medical conditions: abdominal obesity, high blood pressure, high blood glucose, high serum triglycerides and low serum high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol. It is associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes and other conditions.

Definition

Several definitions exist (WHO, IDF, and the harmonised ATP III criteria). Under the commonly used harmonised criteria, a person has metabolic syndrome with any three of: waist circumference above population-specific cut-offs; triglycerides ≥ 150 mg/dL; HDL cholesterol < 40 mg/dL in men or < 50 mg/dL in women; blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg; and fasting glucose ≥ 100 mg/dL — or treatment for any of these.

Causes and pathophysiology

The syndrome arises from a combination of genetic susceptibility and lifestyle factors: excess calories, physical inactivity and central (visceral) fat accumulation. A central mechanism is insulin resistance — tissues respond poorly to insulin, driving compensatory hyperinsulinaemia, altered fat storage and inflammation. Associated conditions include non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, polycystic ovary syndrome, obstructive sleep apnoea and gout.

Prevalence and management

Around a quarter to a third of adults in many developed countries meet the criteria, with prevalence rising with age. The mainstay of treatment is lifestyle change — weight loss, a diet low in refined carbohydrate and saturated fat, and regular aerobic exercise — together with treatment of the individual components: antihypertensives, statins, metformin or other agents, and smoking cessation.
