Wild man

The wild man (German: Wilder Mann) is a figure of medieval European folklore and art: a hairy, naked or leaf-clad humanoid being who lives in the wilderness beyond the bounds of civilisation. Wild men appear in romances, heraldry, carnival customs, and church carvings across Europe from the 12th century onward.

Characteristics

Wild men were depicted covered in hair or fur, carrying a club or uprooted tree, and unable to speak. They embodied both the dangers and the imagined freedom of nature: medieval audiences saw them as brutish and lustful, yet also as strong, uncorrupted, and free of social constraint. Literary wild men include Enkidu in the Epic of Gilgamesh, Nebuchadnezzar in his madness in the Book of Daniel, and Myrddin Wyllt in Welsh tradition.

In art and heraldry

Wild men were popular supporters in coats of arms, especially in German and Swiss heraldry, and appear in prints by artists such as Martin Schongauer and Albrecht Dürer. The figure survives in Alpine and Swabian-Alemannic carnival traditions, where costumed "Wilde Männer" parade during Fastnacht, and in place names and pub signs.

Related figures

The motif has been linked to later legends of woodwoses, yetis, and Bigfoot, and to real encounters between Europeans and great apes. Scholars have also compared the wild man to feral children and to the "noble savage" of later centuries.
