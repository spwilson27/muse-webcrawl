The Chōshū Domain (長州藩, Chōshū-han), also known as the Hagi Domain, was a territorial domain (han) of the Tokugawa shogunate of Japan during the Edo period from 1600 to 1871. It was ruled by the Mōri clan and comprised the provinces of Nagato and Suō in the far west of Honshu, corresponding to present-day Yamaguchi Prefecture.

History

After the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600, the defeated Mōri Terumoto was stripped of most of his lands and confined to the two provinces of Nagato and Suō, with an assessed income of about 369,000 koku. The domain's castle town was established at Hagi on the Sea of Japan coast. Resentment toward the Tokugawa — whose founder Ieyasu the Mōri blamed for their reduction — persisted for over two and a half centuries and shaped the domain's later politics.

Role in the Meiji Restoration

In the 1860s, Chōshū became a center of the sonnō jōi ("revere the Emperor, expel the barbarians") movement. Its samurai attacked foreign shipping in the Shimonoseki Straits in 1863, provoking Western bombardment of Shimonoseki, and the domain was twice targeted by shogunal punitive expeditions. The second expedition of 1866 ended in a shogunate defeat that fatally weakened Tokugawa authority.

Through the secret Satsuma–Chōshū Alliance of 1866, brokered by Sakamoto Ryōma, Chōshū joined with its former rival Satsuma Domain to overthrow the shogunate in the Boshin War of 1868–1869. Chōshū men — including Itō Hirobumi, Yamagata Aritomo, and Inoue Kaoru — went on to dominate the government of Meiji Japan; the domain produced more prime ministers than any other. Its irregular militia, the Kiheitai founded by Takasugi Shinsaku, was a model of military modernization.

Abolition

The domain was abolished in 1871 in the nationwide abolition of the han system and became Yamaguchi Prefecture.
