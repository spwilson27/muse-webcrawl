Mudéjar (Spanish: [muˈðexaɾ], from Arabic مدجن, mudaǧǧan, "tamed" or "domesticated") were Muslims who remained in the former territories of Al-Andalus after the Christian Reconquista but were not converted to Christianity, in contrast to the Moriscos, who were nominally converted. The term also denotes the distinctive artistic style — Mudéjar art — that these communities' craftsmen, and later Christian imitators, produced under Christian rule between the 12th and 17th centuries.

Mudéjars lived under special charters (fueros) in communities called aljamas, keeping their religion, law and customs in exchange for taxes. Their legal position deteriorated over time: after the fall of Granada in 1492, pragmatic decrees forced conversion or exile in Castile (1502) and the Crown of Aragon (1520s), turning the remaining Mudéjars into Moriscos, who were finally expelled in 1609–1614.

Mudéjar art

Mudéjar architecture blends Islamic techniques — brick construction, horseshoe and polylobed arches, glazed tilework (azulejos), carved stucco and intricate wooden artesonado ceilings — with Romanesque, Gothic and Renaissance forms. Outstanding examples include the Alcázar of Seville, the synagogues of Toledo, and the towers and churches of Aragon; the Mudéjar architecture of Aragon is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. A revival style, Neo-Mudéjar, appeared in 19th- and 20th-century Spain.
