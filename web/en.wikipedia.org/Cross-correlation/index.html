Cross-correlation

In signal processing, cross-correlation is a measure of similarity of two series as a function of the displacement of one relative to the other. This is also known as a sliding dot product or sliding inner-product. It is commonly used for searching a long signal for a shorter, known feature. It has applications in pattern recognition, single particle analysis, electron tomography, averaging, cryptanalysis, and neurophysiology.

Definition

For two discrete functions f and g, the cross-correlation is defined as (f ★ g)[n] = Σm f*[m] g[m + n], where f* denotes the complex conjugate of f and n is the displacement (lag). For continuous functions the analogous definition uses an integral. If f = g, the result is the autocorrelation.

Properties

Cross-correlation is similar in nature to the convolution of two functions, differing in that correlation does not involve a time-reversal (folding) of one of the functions. The cross-correlation of functions and their Fourier transforms are related by the cross-correlation theorem, which allows fast computation of correlations using the fast Fourier transform (FFT).

Applications

Cross-correlation is used to estimate time delay between signals—for example in radar and sonar ranging, GPS signal acquisition, and passive seismic imaging. In time-series analysis it measures the relationship between two variables at different lags; in image processing, normalised cross-correlation is the standard technique for template matching and image registration.
