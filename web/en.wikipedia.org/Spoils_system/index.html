Spoils system

In politics and government, a spoils system (also known as a patronage system) is a practice in which a political party, after winning an election, gives government civil service jobs to its supporters, friends (cronyism), and relatives (nepotism) as a reward for working toward victory, and as an incentive to keep working for the party—as opposed to a merit system, where offices are awarded or retained on the basis of some measure of merit independent of political activity.

United States

The term was coined in 1832 by Senator William L. Marcy, defending the appointments of President Andrew Jackson: "to the victor belong the spoils". Patronage flourished under Jackson and his successors but was increasingly criticised for corruption and incompetence. The assassination of President James A. Garfield in 1881 by a disappointed office seeker spurred reform, leading to the Pendleton Civil Service Reform Act of 1883, which began the shift to merit-based appointments.

Modern practice

Although most US federal jobs are now under the merit system, presidents still appoint several thousand officials, including ambassadors and agency heads. Analogous patronage practices exist in many other countries.
