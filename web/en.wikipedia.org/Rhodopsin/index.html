Rhodopsin

Rhodopsin, also known as visual purple, is a protein that in humans is encoded by the RHO gene. It is the pigment found in the rod cells of the retina and is a G protein-coupled receptor (GPCR). Rhodopsin is extremely sensitive to light, and thus enables vision in low-light conditions. When rhodopsin is exposed to light, it immediately photobleaches; in humans it is regenerated on a timescale of about 30 minutes, the period of dark adaptation.

Structure and function

Rhodopsin consists of the protein opsin linked to the chromophore 11-cis-retinal, derived from vitamin A. Absorption of a photon isomerizes the retinal to all-trans, converting rhodopsin to its active form, metarhodopsin II, which activates the G protein transducin. This triggers a cascade in which cyclic GMP is hydrolysed, ion channels close, and the rod cell hyperpolarizes, reducing its release of the neurotransmitter glutamate — the signal that propagates through the retina to the brain. Rhodopsin was the first GPCR whose three-dimensional structure was determined, in 2000.

Discovery

The pigment was observed in frog retinas by Franz Boll in 1876 and named by Willy Kühne, who studied its bleaching and regeneration. George Wald's work on the visual cycle earned him a share of the 1967 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Clinical significance

Mutations in the RHO gene cause forms of retinitis pigmentosa and congenital stationary night blindness. Vitamin A deficiency impairs rhodopsin regeneration and produces night blindness. Related microbial rhodopsins, such as bacteriorhodopsin, function as light-driven ion pumps in archaea and are exploited in optogenetics.
