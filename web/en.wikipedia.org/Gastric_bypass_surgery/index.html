Gastric bypass surgery refers to a group of bariatric operations in which the stomach is divided into a small upper pouch and a much larger remnant, and the small intestine is rearranged to connect to both. The most common variant is the Roux-en-Y gastric bypass. The procedure both restricts food intake and reduces absorption, and it also alters gut hormones in ways that suppress hunger and improve blood-sugar control.

Procedure

In a Roux-en-Y bypass, the surgeon staples off a pouch of roughly 30 millilitres at the top of the stomach and connects it directly to a limb of the jejunum (the Roux limb). Food therefore bypasses most of the stomach, the duodenum and the upper jejunum, while bile and pancreatic juices travel down the bypassed limb and join the food stream further along. The operation is usually performed laparoscopically and takes one to three hours.

Outcomes

Patients typically lose 60–80% of their excess weight within the first two years. Type 2 diabetes frequently goes into remission, sometimes within days, and hypertension, sleep apnoea and joint pain often improve. Long-term success requires lifelong dietary change, vitamin and mineral supplementation (notably B12, iron, calcium and vitamin D) and follow-up.

Risks and complications

Complications include anastomotic leaks, strictures, internal hernias, marginal ulcers and dumping syndrome — nausea, cramps and diarrhoea after eating sugary foods. Operative mortality in accredited centres is low, around 0.1–0.3%, but the surgery carries greater risk than restrictive alternatives such as sleeve gastrectomy, which has overtaken it in popularity in many countries.
