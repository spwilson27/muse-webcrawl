Madhhab

A madhhab (Arabic: مَذْهَب, plural madhāhib) is a school of thought within Islamic jurisprudence (fiqh). The major Sunni madhhabs — the Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali schools — arose during the first centuries of Islam and remain the principal frameworks through which most Sunni Muslims understand and practise religious law. Shia Islam has its own schools, principally the Ja'fari (Twelver) school and the Zaydi school, while the Ibadi school survives in Oman and parts of North Africa.

Development

Early Islam saw regional schools of jurists in centres such as Medina, Kufa, Basra and Mecca. Over the 8th and 9th centuries these crystallised around the teachings of individual scholar-jurists, whose students systematised their methods and collected their opinions. Each school developed a characteristic methodology (uṣūl al-fiqh) for deriving rulings from the Qur'an, the Sunnah of the Prophet Muhammad, consensus (ijmāʿ) and analogical reasoning (qiyās), differing in the weight they gave to each source and to supplementary principles such as juristic preference (istiḥsān) and public interest (maṣlaḥa).

Role today

Following a madhhab is the norm for traditional Sunni scholarship, and the schools are generally regarded as mutually valid despite differing on innumerable details of ritual and law. Some modern movements, most notably Salafism, criticise strict adherence (taqlīd) to a single school and urge direct recourse to the Qur'an and hadith. The 2004 Amman Message by an international body of Muslim scholars affirmed the validity of the four Sunni schools together with the Ja'fari, Zaydi and Ibadi schools, forbidding declarations of apostasy among their followers.
