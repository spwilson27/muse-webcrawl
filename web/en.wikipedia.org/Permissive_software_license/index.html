Permissive software license

Permissive licenses impose minimal restrictions on reuse and redistribution.

A permissive software license, sometimes called a BSD-like license, is a free software license with minimal requirements about how the software can be used, modified, and redistributed. Such licenses typically require only that the original copyright notice and license text be retained, and they permit the software to be incorporated into proprietary products. This contrasts with copyleft licenses, such as the GNU General Public License, which require derivative works distributed to others to be licensed under the same terms.

Characteristics

Permissive licenses generally allow licensees to use, copy, modify, merge, publish, distribute, sublicense, and sell copies of the software, including as part of closed-source commercial products. Their conditions are usually limited to preserving copyright notices and disclaimers of warranty. Some, such as the Apache License 2.0, additionally include an express patent grant and a clause terminating rights for parties who bring patent litigation over the software.

Reception and debate

Permissive licenses are popular for libraries and infrastructure software because they maximize adoption, including by commercial users. Critics from the copyleft tradition argue that permissiveness allows companies to take community work private without giving improvements back, while advocates counter that the freedom to reuse software for any purpose is itself the goal and that permissive ecosystems attract broader participation.
