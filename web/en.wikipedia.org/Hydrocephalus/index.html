Hydrocephalus is a condition in which cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) builds up within the brain, causing increased pressure inside the skull. Symptoms may include headaches, vomiting, sleepiness, vision problems, and, in infants, rapid enlargement of the head. Older adults with normal pressure hydrocephalus typically present with a triad of gait disturbance, urinary incontinence, and cognitive impairment. Hydrocephalus can be life-threatening if intracranial pressure rises too high.

Causes and classification

CSF is produced mainly by the choroid plexus in the cerebral ventricles, circulates through the ventricular system and subarachnoid space, and is absorbed into the venous circulation. Hydrocephalus results from an imbalance between production and absorption. In communicating hydrocephalus, CSF can still flow between the ventricles but absorption is impaired, as may occur after meningitis or subarachnoid haemorrhage. In non-communicating (obstructive) hydrocephalus, flow is blocked within the ventricular system, for example by congenital aqueductal stenosis, tumours, or haemorrhage. Rarely, tumours of the choroid plexus cause CSF overproduction.

Congenital causes include aqueductal stenosis, neural tube defects such as spina bifida with Chiari malformation, and Dandy–Walker malformation. Acquired causes include infection, bleeding (especially intraventricular haemorrhage of prematurity), trauma, and tumours.

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is based on symptoms, neurological examination, and neuroimaging. Ultrasound through the fontanelle can assess infants; CT and MRI demonstrate enlargement of the ventricles (ventriculomegaly) and can reveal the site of obstruction.

Treatment

The standard treatment is surgical diversion of CSF using a shunt — most commonly a ventriculoperitoneal shunt, in which a catheter drains fluid from a cerebral ventricle to the peritoneal cavity, regulated by a valve. Shunts can malfunction or become infected and often require revision. For obstructive hydrocephalus, endoscopic third ventriculostomy (ETV), creating an opening in the floor of the third ventricle, is an alternative that avoids implanted hardware.

Normal pressure hydrocephalus

Normal pressure hydrocephalus (NPH), described by Salomón Hakim in 1965, is a communicating hydrocephalus of older adults in which opening pressure on lumbar puncture is normal. Its classic triad is gait disturbance (often described as "magnetic"), dementia, and urinary incontinence. NPH is important because it is a potentially treatable cause of dementia; many patients improve after shunt surgery.
