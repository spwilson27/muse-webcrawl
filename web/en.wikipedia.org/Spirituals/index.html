Spirituals are religious folk songs created by enslaved African Americans in the United States, principally during the 18th and 19th centuries. Rooted in African musical practices — call-and-response, improvisation, and rhythmic complexity — and in the Protestant hymnody the enslaved encountered, they form one of the largest and most significant bodies of American folk song.

Characteristics and themes

Spirituals draw their imagery overwhelmingly from the Bible, especially the Exodus story of liberation from slavery, the crossing of the River Jordan, and figures such as Moses, Daniel and Joshua. Well-known examples include "Go Down Moses", "Swing Low, Sweet Chariot", "Deep River", "Steal Away", "Wade in the Water" and "Sometimes I Feel Like a Motherless Child". Many commentators have noted a double meaning in some songs: alongside their religious hope, references to escape, rivers and "home" could also speak to the desire for freedom in this life — though the extent of literal "code" for escape via the Underground Railroad is debated by historians.

History

The songs arose in the "invisible church" of enslaved worshippers, in work gangs, and in camp meetings. After emancipation, the Fisk Jubilee Singers of Fisk University toured from 1871 to raise funds for their school, presenting arranged spirituals to audiences in America and Europe and earning the admiration of Queen Victoria and Mark Twain. The first major published collection, Slave Songs of the United States, appeared in 1867.

Legacy

Composers such as Harry Burleigh and Samuel Coleridge-Taylor arranged spirituals as art songs, and Antonín Dvořák drew on them in his "New World" Symphony. In the 20th century, singers including Marian Anderson and Paul Robeson made spirituals staples of the concert hall; "We Shall Overcome", adapted from the tradition, became the anthem of the civil rights movement. Spirituals are foundational to gospel music, the blues, and much subsequent American music.
