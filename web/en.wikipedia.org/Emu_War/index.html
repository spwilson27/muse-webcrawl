The Emu War, also known as the Great Emu War, was a nuisance wildlife management military operation undertaken in Australia over the later part of 1932 to address public concern over the number of emus said to be running amok in the Campion district of Western Australia. The unsuccessful attempts to curb the population of emus by military personnel armed with Lewis guns led the operation to be mocked in the press, and it is remembered as an emblematic episode of human–wildlife conflict.

Background

After the First World War, ex-soldiers were settled as farmers on marginal land around Campion, near the edge of the wheat belt. By 1932 the settlers were already struggling with low wheat prices during the Great Depression when some 20,000 emus, migrating after the breeding season, descended on the district, trampling crops, eating grain, and breaking holes in the vermin-proof fence that kept rabbits out. The farmers demanded government assistance, and — many being veterans — pointedly requested machine guns. The Minister of Defence, Sir George Pearce, agreed to send a military party, partly in the expectation of good publicity.

The operation

In November 1932 Major G. P. W. Meredith of the Royal Australian Artillery arrived with two Lewis guns and a small detachment. The emus proved unexpectedly difficult targets: they scattered in small groups, ran at speeds up to 50 km/h in zig-zag patterns, and could absorb remarkable punishment. A plan to chase the birds with a gun mounted on a truck ended when the vehicle crashed into a fence. Ambushes at dams and waterholes achieved modest results, and Meredith estimated that at least 500 emus had been killed, though contemporary reports put the total at between 200 and 500 — at a cost of some 2,500 rounds of ammunition for the largest single engagements. By early December the military withdrew, and Meredith's rueful remark that the emus could withstand machine guns like tanks was widely quoted.

Aftermath and legacy

Farmers again requested military assistance in 1934, 1943, and 1948, and were refused; the state instead extended the barrier fence and relied on bounties, which proved far more effective — over 57,000 bounty claims were recorded in a six-month period in 1934. The "war" became a durable comic legend, inspiring books, films (including a 2023 action-comedy), and countless retellings as the archetype of an operation defeated by its feathered opponents.
