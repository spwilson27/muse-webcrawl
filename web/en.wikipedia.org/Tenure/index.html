Tenure

Tenure is a category of academic appointment existing in some countries. A tenured post is an indefinite academic appointment that can be terminated only for cause or under extraordinary circumstances, such as financial exigency or program discontinuation. Tenure is a means of defending the principle of academic freedom, which holds that it benefits society in the long run if scholars are free to hold and examine a variety of views.

History

Under the tenure systems adopted by many universities in the United States and Canada, faculty are usually on a "tenure track" for a probationary period — most often around seven years — during which they must demonstrate a record of scholarship, teaching and service sufficient to earn promotion to tenure. The modern system was strongly influenced by the 1940 Statement of Principles on Academic Freedom and Tenure issued by the American Association of University Professors (AAUP) and the Association of American Colleges. Earlier controversies, such as the 1894 dismissal proceedings against economist Richard Ely at the University of Wisconsin, helped crystallize the doctrine; the Wisconsin Board of Regents' defense of "sifting and winnowing" became a landmark statement of academic freedom.

Rationale

The primary justification for tenure is the protection of academic freedom: scholars researching controversial topics, or criticizing their own institutions or governments, should not fear dismissal for the content of their work. A secondary argument is economic: tenure offers job security in exchange for salaries lower than comparably trained professionals might earn elsewhere, and it encourages senior faculty to mentor junior colleagues rather than treating them as competitors.

Criticism

Critics argue that tenure can protect unproductive or negligent faculty, reduce institutional flexibility, and shift teaching onto insecure contingent staff. The proportion of tenure-track positions in American higher education has declined markedly since the 1970s, with much teaching now done by adjuncts and other non-tenure-track instructors.

Outside academia

The word "tenure" is also used more generally for the conditions under which land or property is held (as in land tenure), and, in some jurisdictions, for job protections held by school teachers and civil servants judged by a competent authority.
