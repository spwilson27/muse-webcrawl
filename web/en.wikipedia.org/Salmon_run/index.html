The salmon run is the annual migration of salmon from the ocean up rivers to the freshwater gravel beds where they were hatched, in order to spawn. It is one of the most spectacular migrations in the animal kingdom, renowned for the fish's endurance as they leap waterfalls and fight currents, and for their near-perfect homing to their natal streams.

Life cycle

Salmon begin life in freshwater. After hatching from eggs buried in gravel nests (redds), juveniles spend months to years in streams and lakes before migrating to sea as smolts, undergoing physiological changes for saltwater life. After one to seven years feeding in the ocean, adults return to freshwater. Pacific salmon species spawn once and die (semelparity); Atlantic salmon may survive to spawn again.

Navigation

Salmon appear to navigate the open ocean using the Earth's magnetic field as a map, then home in on the unique chemical signature — the smell — of their natal stream, learned before they left for the sea. On the journey they stop feeding and live off stored fat, their bodies changing colour and shape.

Ecological importance

Runs are a keystone food pulse: bears, eagles, wolves and dozens of other species feed on migrating salmon, and carcasses fertilise riparian forests with marine-derived nitrogen, measurably boosting tree growth. Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest have depended on and celebrated the runs for millennia.

Threats

Dams, habitat loss, warming waters, overfishing and disease from fish farms have collapsed many runs. Famous examples include the declines of Columbia River and Fraser River stocks; the Adams River sockeye run in British Columbia remains one of the largest remaining spectacles.
