Walking (also known as ambulation) is one of the main gaits of terrestrial locomotion among legged animals. Walking is typically slower than running and other gaits. Walking is defined by an "inverted pendulum" gait in which the body vaults over the stiff limb or limbs with each step. This applies regardless of the usable number of limbs — even arthropods, with six, eight, or more limbs, walk.

In humans and other bipeds, walking is distinguished from running by the fact that at least one foot is always in contact with the ground and there is a period of double support. In contrast, running begins when both feet leave the ground with each step. This distinction has legal force in the sport of racewalking.

Health benefits

Regular brisk walking is associated with reduced risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, obesity and some cancers, improved mental health and cognition, and increased life expectancy. Public health guidelines in many countries recommend at least 150 minutes per week of moderate aerobic activity such as brisk walking for adults. Walking is the most commonly reported physical activity worldwide.

Walking for transport and recreation

Walking is the oldest and most basic form of transport, and remains essential in cities, where planners promote "walkability" through pedestrian infrastructure. Recreational forms include hiking, trekking, hillwalking, Nordic walking and long-distance trails such as pilgrimages (e.g., the Camino de Santiago). Historically, walking tours were a defining feature of Romantic-era travel and literature.

Biomechanics

The gait cycle alternates single support (one foot on the ground) and double support (both feet down). The body's centre of mass rises and falls in a smooth arc, and the exchange between potential and kinetic energy makes walking the most energy-efficient gait at low speeds. Infants typically take their first independent steps at around 12 months of age.
