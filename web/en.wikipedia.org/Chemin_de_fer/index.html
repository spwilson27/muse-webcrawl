Chemin de fer is a card game that was a popular version of baccarat in casinos, particularly in France and other parts of Europe. The name is French for "railway", a reference to the speed with which the game was played compared with earlier forms. In chemin de fer, the players themselves take turns holding the bank, and the punter playing against the bank makes the decisions on drawing cards.

Play

The game is played with six standard 52-card decks shuffled together. One player acts as banker and deals two cards each to themselves and to the opposing player (the punter). As in baccarat, hands total the units digit of the sum of card values, with face cards and tens counting zero. If either hand totals eight or nine — a "natural" — it is shown and wins unless both tie.

Drawing rules

Unlike punto banco, where drawing is fixed by a tableau, in chemin de fer the punter freely chooses whether to draw a third card on a total of five, the most strategically interesting decision in the game. The banker then decides whether to draw based on their own total, the punter's third card if any, and knowledge of the punter's likely holding.

Bank rotation and history

The bank passes to the next player when the banker loses or voluntarily retires; a winning banker may continue. Chemin de fer was the dominant casino baccarat variant in Europe for much of the twentieth century and features prominently in Ian Fleming's James Bond novels as Bond's game of choice.
