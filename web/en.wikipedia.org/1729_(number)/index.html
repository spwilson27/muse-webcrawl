1729 is the natural number following 1728 and preceding 1730. It is known as the Hardy–Ramanujan number after a famous anecdote involving the British mathematician G. H. Hardy and the Indian mathematician Srinivasa Ramanujan, and it is the smallest number expressible as the sum of two positive cubes in two different ways.

The Hardy–Ramanujan anecdote

Hardy recounted that he visited Ramanujan in hospital in Putney and remarked that the number of his taxicab, 1729, seemed rather a dull number. Ramanujan replied: "No, it is a very interesting number; it is the smallest number expressible as the sum of two cubes in two different ways." Indeed:

1729 = 1³ + 12³ = 9³ + 10³

Numbers with this property generalise to the concept of taxicab numbers, Ta(n), the smallest number expressible as the sum of two positive cubes in n distinct ways.

Mathematical properties

1729 is a composite number with prime factorisation 7 × 13 × 19 — a sphenic number and the product of the first three "lucky" pattern of primes in arithmetic progression. It is a Carmichael number, the second such number after 561 to be the product of three primes in this configuration (1729 = 7 × 13 × 19, with each of 6, 12 and 18 dividing 1728). It is also a Zeisel number and a centred cube number. Because 1729 = 19 × 91 and reversing digits relates it to 1297 and 9721 products, it appears in various digit-manipulation curiosities; in base 12 it reads exactly 1001.

In popular culture

The number is a frequent in-joke in the animated series Futurama, whose writers included mathematics graduates: a starship bears the registry number BP-1729, and a taxicab numbered 1729 appears in one episode. It also appears as a licence-plate number in the series Bender's Big Score contexts and in xkcd-style mathematical humour.
