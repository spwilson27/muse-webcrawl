Human skeleton

The human skeleton is the internal framework of the human body. It is composed of around 270 bones at birth — this total decreases to around 206 bones by adulthood after some bones fuse together. The bone mass in the skeleton makes up about 14% of the total body weight of an average adult. The skeleton supports the body, protects internal organs, enables movement in conjunction with muscles, produces blood cells in its marrow, and stores minerals such as calcium and phosphorus.

Divisions

Axial skeleton

The axial skeleton (80 bones) forms the central axis of the body. It comprises the skull (22 bones, including the cranium and facial bones), the ossicles of the middle ear (6), the hyoid bone, the vertebral column (26 bones: 24 vertebrae plus sacrum and coccyx), and the thoracic cage (25: the sternum and 24 ribs).

Appendicular skeleton

The appendicular skeleton (126 bones) consists of the pectoral girdles (4), the bones of the upper limbs (60), the pelvic girdle (2), and the bones of the lower limbs (60). It is specialized for movement and manipulation.

Functions

  • Support and shape — the skeleton maintains the body's form and posture.
  • Protection — the skull guards the brain, the rib cage the heart and lungs, and the vertebrae the spinal cord.
  • Movement — bones act as levers for skeletal muscles attached via tendons.
  • Blood cell production — red bone marrow performs hematopoiesis, producing red and white blood cells and platelets (including cells of the immune system such as granulocytes).
  • Mineral and fat storage — bone stores calcium and phosphate; yellow marrow stores fat.
  • Endocrine regulation — bone secretes osteocalcin, involved in metabolic regulation.

Development

Most bones form by endochondral ossification, in which a cartilage model is progressively replaced by bone; the flat bones of the skull form directly by intramembranous ossification. Growth in length occurs at epiphyseal (growth) plates until late adolescence. Babies are born with more cartilage and more separate bony elements, many of which fuse during childhood — for example, the sacrum and the bones of the skull.

Sexual dimorphism

Male and female skeletons differ most clearly in the pelvis, which in females is wider and has a broader sciatic notch and subpubic angle, adaptations for childbirth. Female skulls and long bones also tend to be less robust. These differences allow forensic anthropologists to estimate sex from skeletal remains with high accuracy.

Disorders

Diseases of the skeleton include osteoporosis (loss of bone density with age), osteoarthritis, fractures, scoliosis, rickets and osteomalacia (from vitamin D deficiency), Paget's disease, and bone cancers such as osteosarcoma.
