Imagined Communities

Imagined Communities: Reflections on the Origin and Spread of Nationalism is a book by Benedict Anderson, first published in 1983 and reissued in revised editions in 1991 and 2006. It is one of the most influential works in the study of nationalism. Anderson proposes that nations are socially constructed "imagined political communities" — imagined because their members will never know most of their fellow members, yet carry in their minds an image of their communion.

Argument

Anderson defines the nation as "an imagined political community — and imagined as both inherently limited and sovereign." He argues that nationalism emerged when three older cultural conceptions declined: the belief that particular languages granted privileged access to truth, the idea that society was naturally organized under divinely ordained monarchs, and an apprehension of time in which past and future were fused. In their place, "print capitalism" — the mass production of books and newspapers in vernacular languages — created unified fields of communication and a new sense of simultaneity, allowing strangers to imagine themselves as part of one community.

Origins and spread

Anderson locates the first nationalisms among creole communities in the Americas, whose members' careers and print markets were bounded by colonial administrative units. European "official nationalisms" and later anti-colonial nationalisms adapted this "modular" form, often through census, map, and museum — the instruments by which colonial states imagined their dominions.

Influence

The book transformed nationalism studies, shifting debate from whether nations are ancient or invented toward the cultural processes by which they are imagined. It has been translated into many languages and is a standard text in history, anthropology, and political science, though critics have questioned its emphasis on print and its treatment of the Americas' priority.
