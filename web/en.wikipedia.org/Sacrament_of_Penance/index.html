Sacrament of Penance

Also calledReconciliation; Confession
TraditionsCatholic Church; Eastern Orthodox Church; Oriental Orthodoxy; some Anglican and Lutheran practice
ElementsContrition; Confession; Satisfaction; Absolution
MinisterPriest or bishop

The Sacrament of Penance, also called the Sacrament of Reconciliation or Confession, is one of the seven sacraments of the Catholic Church, by which the faithful obtain absolution for sins committed after baptism. In the Eastern Orthodox Church the corresponding practice is the Mystery of Repentance. The sacrament is grounded in the words of the risen Christ in the Gospel of John (20:22–23), understood as granting the apostles authority to forgive or retain sins.

Elements

Catholic teaching distinguishes the acts of the penitent — contrition (sorrow for sin with resolve not to repeat it), confession of sins to a priest, and satisfaction (performing the penance assigned) — from absolution, the words by which the priest, acting in persona Christi, declares the sins forgiven. The Council of Trent (1551) defined the sacrament's doctrine against Reformation critiques, and the 1983 Code of Canon Law regulates its celebration, including the inviolable seal of confession binding priests never to reveal what is confessed.

History

In the early Church, grave sins were reconciled through a public order of penitents, with lengthy penances and readmission by the bishop. From the sixth century, Irish monastic practice spread private, repeatable confession accompanied by penitential handbooks assigning tariffed penances. The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) required every Christian to confess at least once a year. During the sixteenth century the confessional box became standard, and the post-Vatican II rite (1973) permits communal celebrations with individual confession and absolution.

In other traditions

Orthodox practice centres on confession before a priest, who acts as witness and spiritual physician rather than judge, and typically requires confession before receiving Communion at various seasons. Lutheran and Anglican traditions retained private confession optionally; Luther valued it highly, though it is not counted among the sacraments by most Protestants.
