Fault (geology)

In geology, a fault is a planar fracture or discontinuity in a volume of rock across which there has been significant displacement as a result of rock-mass movements. Large faults within Earth's crust result from the action of plate tectonic forces, with the largest forming the boundaries between the plates, such as the megathrust faults of subduction zones and conservative transform faults. Energy release associated with rapid movement on active faults is the cause of most earthquakes.

Terminology

A fault plane is the plane that represents the fracture surface of a fault. For a dipping (non-vertical) fault, the block of rock above the fault plane is the hanging wall and the block below is the footwall. Faults are classified mainly by the direction of slip: in a normal fault the hanging wall moves down relative to the footwall, as happens under extension; in a reverse or thrust fault the hanging wall moves up, as under compression; and in a strike-slip fault the blocks move horizontally past each other, either right-laterally (dextral) or left-laterally (sinistral). Faults combining vertical and horizontal slip are called oblique-slip faults.

Fault rocks and zones

Movement along faults deforms the adjacent rock, producing fault rocks such as brittle fault breccia and gouge near the surface, and ductile mylonite at depth. Faults rarely consist of a single clean fracture; instead they form fault zones of numerous interconnected fractures. Fluids moving along faults can deposit economically important mineral veins, and impermeable fault rocks can trap petroleum and groundwater.

Effects

Active faults generate earthquakes when accumulated stress is suddenly released, and movement can produce fault scarps, offset streams and other distinctive landforms. Famous examples include the San Andreas Fault in California, a right-lateral strike-slip fault; the Alpine Fault of New Zealand; and the normal faults bounding horst and graben structures such as the Rhine Graben.
