Plate tectonics

Plate tectonics is the widely accepted scientific theory that considers the Earth's lithosphere to comprise a number of large tectonic plates that have been slowly moving since about 3.4 billion years ago. The theory builds on the concept of continental drift, developed in the early 20th century, and the concept of seafloor spreading, developed in the late 1950s and early 1960s. Tectonic plates generally move at rates of a few centimetres per year, comparable to the growth rate of fingernails.

Theory

The lithosphere — the rigid outer shell comprising the crust and uppermost mantle — is broken into plates that float on the ductile asthenosphere. At divergent boundaries (such as mid-ocean ridges) new crust is created; at convergent boundaries crust is destroyed by subduction, forming trenches, mountain ranges, and volcanic arcs; at transform boundaries, such as the San Andreas Fault, plates slide past each other. Most earthquakes and volcanoes occur along plate boundaries.

Historical development

Alfred Wegener proposed continental drift in 1912, citing the fit of continents and fossil evidence, but lacked a mechanism. In the 1960s, discoveries of magnetic striping on the seafloor, the age progression of ocean crust, and Wadati–Benioff earthquake zones led to the synthesis of plate tectonics, one of the great unifying theories of geology.

Effects

Plate movements shape mountain building (orogeny), ocean basins, continental positions, long-term climate through the carbon cycle, and the distribution of natural resources, and they govern the hazards of earthquakes and volcanism.
