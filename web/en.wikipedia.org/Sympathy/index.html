Sympathy

Sympathy is the perception, understanding, and reaction to the distress or need of another person. It involves feelings of concern and sorrow for someone else's suffering, without necessarily sharing that suffering oneself. The word derives from the Greek sympatheia — syn "with" and pathos "feeling" — and in its older philosophical sense it described a fellow-feeling by which people are bound together.

History of the concept

David Hume treated sympathy as the psychological mechanism by which the feelings of others are communicated to us and converted into our own sentiments, making it central to his moral philosophy. Adam Smith opened The Theory of Moral Sentiments (1759) with the concept, arguing that we imagine ourselves in another's situation and judge actions by whether an impartial spectator could sympathise with the agent's motives. In the eighteenth century "sympathy" covered much of the ground now occupied by the newer word empathy — a term coined only in 1909 by Edward Titchener as a translation of the German Einfühlung.

Sympathy versus empathy

Modern psychology generally distinguishes sympathy from empathy: empathy involves sharing or mirroring another's emotional state and understanding their perspective, while sympathy involves feeling for the other — concern, pity, or sorrow — from a position of distance. Sympathy can motivate helping behaviour, though psychologists note it may also shade into pity and a sense of superiority, whereas empathy is associated with perspective-taking. Charles Darwin discussed sympathy in The Descent of Man (1871) as a virtue that evolved in social animals, and contemporary research links sympathy to prosocial development in children.
