Double consciousness

W. E. B. Du Bois introduced the concept in 1903.

Double consciousness is a concept introduced by the sociologist and historian W. E. B. Du Bois in his 1903 book The Souls of Black Folk. It describes the internal conflict experienced by subordinated or colonised groups in an oppressive society: the sense of "always looking at one's self through the eyes of others", of measuring one's soul "by the tape of a world that looks on in amused contempt and pity".

The concept

Du Bois described the African American as possessing "two souls, two thoughts, two unreconciled strivings; two warring ideals in one dark body" — being both Black and American in a society that denied the full compatibility of these identities. He also used the image of the Veil, the colour line separating Black Americans from the white world, which simultaneously shuts them out and gives a kind of second sight into the society that excludes them.

Origins

Du Bois first used the term in his 1897 Atlantic Monthly essay "Strivings of the Negro People". The phrase had earlier uses in psychology and in the writings of Emerson and Goethe, but Du Bois gave it its enduring sociological meaning.

Influence

Double consciousness became a foundational idea in African American studies, critical race theory, and postcolonial studies; Frantz Fanon developed related analyses in Black Skin, White Masks (1952). Scholars have extended the concept to gender, immigration, and other doubled identities, and debated whether it names purely a burden or also a resource of insight and critique.
