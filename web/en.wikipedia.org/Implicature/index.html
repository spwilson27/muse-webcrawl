Implicature

An implicature is what a speaker means beyond what their words literally say.
FieldPragmatics, philosophy of language
Introduced byH. P. Grice (1967 William James lectures)
FrameworkCooperative principle and conversational maxims
Main typesConversational, conventional
Key propertyCancellability (defeasibility)

Implicature is a technical term in the linguistic branch of pragmatics, coined by the philosopher H. P. Grice, for what a speaker means or implies by an utterance beyond what is literally said. If asked how a student's work is going, a tutor who replies "He has beautiful handwriting" implicates — without saying — that the work itself is poor.

Grice's theory

Grice proposed that conversation is governed by a cooperative principle: participants make their contributions as required by the accepted purpose of the exchange. Under it sit maxims of quantity (give the right amount of information), quality (be truthful), relation (be relevant) and manner (be clear and orderly). When a speaker conspicuously flouts a maxim, hearers do not assume irrationality; they infer an additional meaning that restores cooperation. That inferred meaning is a conversational implicature.

Types

Conversational implicature

Conversational implicatures arise from the maxims together with the context. They include generalised implicatures, which arise by default (saying "I ate some of the cake" implicates "not all"), and particularised ones, which depend on specific circumstances. The classic test is cancellability: a speaker can add "...but I don't mean to imply that" without contradicting themselves — something impossible for what is literally entailed.

Conventional implicature

Conventional implicatures are attached to the conventional meaning of particular words rather than to the maxims. Grice's example was "but", which contrasts its clauses beyond their truth-conditional content: "She is poor but honest" implicates a tension between poverty and honesty. The scope and even existence of this category remain debated.

Properties

Conversational implicatures are standardly held to be calculable (workable-out from the maxims and context), cancellable, non-detachable (rephrasing with synonyms keeps the implicature) and non-conventional. Later theorists, notably in relevance theory and neo-Gricean pragmatics, have revised how the maxims generate these inferences, but implicature remains a foundational concept in the study of meaning in communication.
