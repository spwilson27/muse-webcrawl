Von Willebrand disease

Von Willebrand disease (VWD) is the most common inherited bleeding disorder in humans, caused by a deficiency or defect of von Willebrand factor (VWF), a blood glycoprotein required for platelet adhesion to sites of vascular injury and for carrying and stabilising clotting factor VIII. It was first described in 1926 by the Finnish physician Erik von Willebrand, who studied a family from the Åland Islands with a severe bleeding tendency.

Von Willebrand factor mediates platelet adhesion and carries factor VIII.

Signs and symptoms

Most people with the common mild form (type 1) experience mucocutaneous bleeding: frequent nosebleeds, easy bruising, bleeding gums, prolonged bleeding from cuts, heavy menstrual bleeding (menorrhagia), and excessive bleeding after dental extraction or surgery. The rare severe form, type 3, involves virtually absent VWF and very low factor VIII, causing spontaneous joint and muscle bleeds similar to haemophilia. Type 2 comprises qualitative defects, including type 2B, in which abnormally adhesive VWF can cause low platelet counts.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is based on measurement of VWF antigen, VWF activity (such as ristocetin cofactor activity), and factor VIII levels, often repeated because VWF levels rise with stress, pregnancy, and oestrogen. Treatment depends on type and severity: desmopressin (DDAVP) releases stored VWF and is effective in most type 1 patients; plasma-derived or recombinant VWF concentrates are used for severe disease or major surgery; antifibrinolytics such as tranexamic acid help with mucosal bleeding. Blood group O individuals tend to have lower VWF levels, which complicates diagnosis at the margins.
