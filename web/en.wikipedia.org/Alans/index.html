Alans

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Alans were an ancient Iranian nomadic pastoral people of the Eurasian steppe, closely related to the Sarmatians and speaking an Eastern Iranian language. From the 1st century AD they were prominent between the Don River and the Caucasus, and during the Migration Period groups of Alans ranged across Europe as far as Iberia and North Africa.

History

The Alans appear in Roman sources from the 1st century AD, raiding into Parthia and the Roman provinces south of the Caucasus. They formed a powerful confederation north of the Caucasus — the kingdom later known as Alania — which endured into the Middle Ages. In 375 AD the Huns defeated the Alans east of the Don; many Alans joined the Huns or migrated westward with Germanic peoples. Alans crossed the Rhine with Vandals and Suebi in 406, settled in Gaul and Hispania, and accompanied the Vandals to North Africa, where their kings styled themselves "King of the Vandals and Alans".

Alania and legacy

The Alans who remained in the Caucasus converted to Orthodox Christianity under Byzantine influence in the 10th century and maintained Alania until the Mongol invasions of the 13th century shattered it. Their direct descendants are generally identified as the Ossetians of the central Caucasus, whose Ossetian language continues the Alanic tongue. Alan influence has been proposed in figures of Arthurian legend (via Sarmatian troops in Roman Britain) and in place-names and words across Europe.

Culture

Classical writers describe the Alans as tall, blond horsemen and formidable warriors, worshipping a god of war represented by a naked sword planted in the ground — a cult Ammianus Marcellinus compared to that of Mars.
