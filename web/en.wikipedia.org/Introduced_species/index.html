Introduced species

Introduced species
Also calledAlien, exotic, non-native species
VectorsTrade, travel, ballast water, deliberate release
SubsetsNaturalized species, invasive species

An introduced species (also alien species, exotic species or non-native species) is a species living outside its native distributional range, typically having arrived there by human activity, either deliberate or accidental. Introduced species may or may not have a negative effect on the local ecosystem: those that spread aggressively and cause ecological or economic harm are termed invasive species, while many introductions remain localised or become integrated as naturalized species.

Pathways of introduction

Humans have moved species for millennia through agriculture, horticulture, forestry, the pet trade and biological control. Unintentional pathways include stowaways in shipping, organisms discharged in ballast water (such as the zebra mussel and comb jelly Mnemiopsis), contaminants of seed and soil, and escapees from gardens and aquaculture. Globalised trade has multiplied both the volume and speed of introductions.

Ecological effects

Notorious examples include the cane toad in Australia, introduced to control beetles and instead poisoning native predators; rabbits, foxes and feral cats, which devastated Australian wildlife; the brown tree snake on Guam, which eliminated most native forest birds; kudzu in the American South; and Nile perch in Lake Victoria, implicated in the loss of hundreds of cichlid species. Islands are especially vulnerable because their endemic species evolved without mainland predators or competitors.

Beneficial and neutral introductions

Most of the world's food crops and livestock are introduced species in the regions where they are farmed, and some deliberate introductions — such as classical biological control agents — provide major benefits. Management policy therefore focuses on risk assessment, quarantine, early detection and rapid eradication rather than on introductions per se.
