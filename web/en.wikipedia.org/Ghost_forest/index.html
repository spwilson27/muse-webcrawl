Ghost forest

A ghost forest is a stand of dead trees in a formerly healthy coastal or riverside forest that has been killed by rising salinity or inundation, most commonly caused by sea level rise, land subsidence, or earthquakes that suddenly lowered the land. The bleached, leafless trunks remain standing for years, giving the landscape a spectral appearance that gives the phenomenon its name.

Causes

Ghost forests form when salt water reaches soils that previously supported freshwater or low-salinity forest. Along low-lying coasts, gradual sea level rise pushes tides and storm surges farther inland, and salt in the soil and groundwater kills trees that cannot tolerate it. In the Pacific Northwest of North America, some ghost forests were created suddenly when great subduction-zone earthquakes — such as the 1700 Cascadia earthquake — dropped coastal land by a metre or more, drowning forests in salt marsh. The preserved stumps of these forests have been used to date past earthquakes.

Ecology

As trees die, the forest is typically replaced by salt marsh or open water. Standing dead trunks provide habitat for birds and insects for a time, and the transition zones are studied as examples of ecosystem migration in response to climate change. In regions such as the Chesapeake Bay and the Gulf Coast of the United States, researchers have documented expanding ghost forests as an indicator of accelerating sea level rise.

Ghost forests are also studied by dendrochronologists and geologists: the death dates of drowned trees, established by tree-ring analysis and radiocarbon dating, help reconstruct the timing of prehistoric earthquakes and tsunamis along subduction zones.
