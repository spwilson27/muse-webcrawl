Simon de Montfort, 6th Earl of Leicester (c. 1208 – 4 August 1265), sometimes also known as Simon V de Montfort, was an Anglo-Norman nobleman of French origin. He led the baronial opposition to King Henry III of England, and after defeating the king at the Battle of Lewes in 1264 became the de facto ruler of England. During his short rule he summoned two famous parliaments — most notably the parliament of January 1265, which for the first time included representatives of the shires and of the boroughs — for which he is often regarded as one of the progenitors of modern parliamentary democracy. He was killed at the Battle of Evesham in 1265.

Rise and conflict with Henry III

The younger son of the elder Simon de Montfort, a leader of the Albigensian Crusade, Simon came to England in 1230 to reclaim the family earldom of Leicester, and in 1238 he secretly married the king's sister Eleanor, widow of William Marshal the younger. His ruthless governorship of Gascony (1248–52) and quarrels over money and favour made him the king's enemy, and in 1258 he emerged as a leader of the barons who imposed the Provisions of Oxford on Henry, establishing a council to control the king. The reform movement fractured into civil war — the Second Barons' War — and on 14 May 1264 Montfort's forces defeated Henry at Lewes, capturing both the king and his son, the Lord Edward.

Rule, parliament and fall

Montfort governed through a triumvirate and council, and sought to broaden his support: his parliament of 1265 summoned, in addition to magnates and clergy, two knights from each shire and two burgesses from each borough — often described as the first English parliament with elected commoner representatives and echoed today in the name of De Montfort University and a statue on Leicester's clock tower. His regime, however, alienated key allies (notably the Earl of Gloucester), his sons behaved rapaciously, and his treatment of England's Jews — debts owed to Jewish moneylenders were cancelled during his rule, accompanied by massacres of Jewish communities in several towns — blackened his record. Prince Edward escaped custody in May 1265, raised an army, and on 4 August trapped Montfort at Evesham; Montfort was killed and his body mutilated. Miracles were soon reported at his tomb, and he was venerated unofficially as a saint, though never canonised.

Legacy

Historians debate whether Montfort was a principled constitutional reformer, a self-interested magnate, or both; his parliaments created precedents that Edward I would institutionalise a generation later, securing his lasting place in the history of Parliament.
