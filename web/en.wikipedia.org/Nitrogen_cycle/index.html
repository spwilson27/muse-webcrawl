Nitrogen cycle

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The nitrogen cycle is the biogeochemical cycle by which nitrogen is converted between its various chemical forms as it circulates among the atmosphere, terrestrial ecosystems, and marine ecosystems. Although molecular nitrogen (N2) makes up about 78% of the Earth's atmosphere, it is chemically inert and unavailable to most organisms; the cycle's transformations — largely mediated by microorganisms — convert it into biologically useful compounds such as ammonia, nitrite, and nitrate, and back again.

Processes

  • Nitrogen fixation: conversion of N2 into ammonia by nitrogen-fixing bacteria (free-living and symbiotic, e.g. Rhizobium in legume root nodules), by lightning, and industrially by the Haber–Bosch process.
  • Assimilation: uptake of ammonium and nitrate by plants and their incorporation into amino acids, proteins, and nucleic acids.
  • Ammonification (mineralisation): decomposition of organic nitrogen in dead organisms and waste by bacteria and fungi, releasing ammonia.
  • Nitrification: oxidation of ammonia to nitrite (by Nitrosomonas and relatives) and of nitrite to nitrate (by Nitrobacter and relatives).
  • Denitrification: reduction of nitrate back to gaseous N2 (and some N2O) by anaerobic bacteria, completing the cycle.
  • Anammox: anaerobic oxidation of ammonium with nitrite directly to N2, important in the oceans.

Human influence

Industrial fertiliser production through the Haber–Bosch process, cultivation of legumes, and combustion of fossil fuels now fix an amount of nitrogen comparable to all natural terrestrial fixation combined. Excess reactive nitrogen leaches into waterways, causing eutrophication and coastal "dead zones," acidifies soils, and contributes nitrous oxide — a potent greenhouse gas — to the atmosphere. Consequently, the nitrogen cycle is considered one of the planetary boundaries most severely transgressed by human activity.

Marine nitrogen cycle

In the oceans, nitrogen fixation by cyanobacteria such as Trichodesmium, nitrification in the water column and sediments, denitrification in oxygen-minimum zones, and anammox regulate the availability of nitrogen, which limits primary production in much of the surface ocean.
