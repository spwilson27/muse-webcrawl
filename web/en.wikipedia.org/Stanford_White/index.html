Stanford White (9 November 1853 – 25 June 1906) was an American architect and a partner in the firm McKim, Mead & White, the preeminent American architectural practice of the Gilded Age. A flamboyant figure in New York society, he was murdered in 1906 in one of the most sensational crimes of the era.

Career

White trained in the office of Henry Hobson Richardson before touring Europe and joining Charles Follen McKim and William Rutherford Mead in 1879. He brought a love of Renaissance classicism, rich ornament and sumptuous interiors to the firm's work, designing the Washington Square Arch, the second Madison Square Garden (topped by Augustus Saint-Gaudens's statue of Diana), the New York Herald Building, grand Newport and Long Island mansions, and clubs and churches across the Northeast.

Murder

White maintained a notorious private life, including an apartment with a red velvet swing where he entertained showgirls. On 25 June 1906 he was shot dead at Madison Square Garden's rooftop theatre by Harry Kendall Thaw, the mentally unstable husband of model and actress Evelyn Nesbit, whom White had allegedly seduced as a teenager. Thaw's trials — the first ending in a hung jury, the second in a verdict of not guilty by reason of insanity — were dubbed the "Trial of the Century".

Legacy

Despite the scandal, White's reputation as a designer of exuberant classical buildings and interiors has been restored, and many of his works are landmarked.
