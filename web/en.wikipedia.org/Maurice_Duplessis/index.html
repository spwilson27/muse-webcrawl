Maurice Le Noblet Duplessis (April 20, 1890 – September 7, 1959), known simply as Duplessis or by his nickname le Chef ("the Boss"), was a Canadian lawyer and politician who served as the 16th Premier of Quebec from 1936 to 1939 and again from 1944 until his death in 1959. As founder and lifelong leader of the Union Nationale party, he dominated Quebec politics for a generation.

Rise to power

Born in Trois-Rivières, Duplessis trained as a lawyer and entered the Quebec Legislative Assembly in 1927 as a Conservative. Joining with Liberals dissident from the Taschereau government, he formed the Union Nationale in 1935 and won the 1936 election on a platform of reform and provincial rights.

First ministry

His first government (1936–1939) enacted the notorious Padlock Law (1937), which allowed the attorney general to close premises used for propagating "communism" or Bolshevism, a measure used against unions, Jehovah's Witnesses and left-wing publications. Defeated in 1939 over his opposition to Canadian participation in the Second World War, Duplessis returned to power in 1944.

The Duplessis era

Duplessis's long second ministry combined rapid economic development — extensive rural electrification, highway construction, resource development with American capital, balanced budgets and low taxes — with social conservatism, an alliance with the Roman Catholic Church, aggressive anti-communism, patronage politics and suppression of labour unions, notably during the Asbestos strike of 1949. Critics later labelled the period the Grande Noirceur ("Great Darkness"), a characterization modern historians debate. His government's treatment of illegitimate and orphaned children — the "Duplessis Orphans" scandal — cast a lasting shadow. In the Roncarelli case, the Supreme Court of Canada ruled against his government's persecution of Jehovah's Witnesses, a landmark in Canadian civil liberties law.

Death and legacy

Duplessis died in office at Schefferville, Quebec, on September 7, 1959, while visiting mining developments in the north. His death, followed within months by that of his successor Paul Sauvé, opened the way to Quebec's Quiet Revolution.
