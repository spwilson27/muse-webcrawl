Cholecystectomy

A cholecystectomy is the surgical removal of the gallbladder. Cholecystectomy is a common treatment of symptomatic gallstones and other gallbladder conditions. In 2011, cholecystectomy was the eighth most common operating room procedure performed in hospitals in the United States. Cholecystectomy can be performed either laparoscopically or by open surgery.

Medical uses

The most common indications are painful gallstones causing biliary colic, acute or chronic cholecystitis, gallstone pancreatitis, biliary dyskinesia, and gallbladder cancer. The surgery is generally only indicated if the gallstones are causing problems; asymptomatic gallstones are usually managed expectantly.

Procedure

In the laparoscopic approach, the abdomen is inflated with carbon dioxide and four small incisions are made for a camera and instruments. The surgeon identifies the cystic artery and cystic duct (ideally obtaining the "critical view of safety"), clips and divides them, separates the gallbladder from the liver bed, and removes it through one of the ports. The open approach, now usually reserved for complicated cases or conversions, uses a right subcostal (Kocher) incision. Robotic and single-incision variants also exist.

Risks

Complications include bleeding, infection, bile leak, and injury to the common bile duct, which occurs in roughly 0.3–0.5% of laparoscopic cases and can be a life-altering injury requiring major reconstruction. After removal, bile flows continuously into the gut rather than being stored; most patients digest normally, though some experience postcholecystectomy syndrome with diarrhoea or abdominal pain.

History

The first successful cholecystectomy was performed by Carl Langenbuch in Berlin in 1882. Laparoscopic cholecystectomy was pioneered in the late 1980s by Erich Mühe (1985) and in France by Philippe Mouret (1987), and rapidly became the standard of care.
