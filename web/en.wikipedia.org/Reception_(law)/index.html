Reception (law)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In law, reception is the process by which a legal system adopts, in whole or in part, the law of another system — its statutes, precedents, concepts or codes. Reception may be imposed by conquest or colonisation, deliberately chosen by a legislature modernising its law, or occur gradually through the influence of jurists, courts and legal education. It is one of the principal ways legal systems have developed historically, and a central topic of comparative law.

The reception of Roman law

The classic reception is that of Roman law in medieval and early modern Europe. From the 11th-century revival of Justinian's Corpus Juris Civilis at Bologna, university-trained jurists carried Roman concepts into courts and chanceries across the continent — a process formalised in the Holy Roman Empire around 1495 with the creation of the Reichskammergericht, whose judges applied the "common law" (ius commune) of Roman and canon law. This received law, though adapted locally, formed the bedrock of the modern civil-law tradition and influenced even the codifications of the 19th century, such as the French Code civil and the German BGB.

Colonial and post-colonial reception

European expansion transplanted entire legal systems. English common law was "received" in the American colonies, Canada, Australia, India and much of Africa, often through formal reception statutes specifying the date up to which English law applied — for example, the reception of English law in the American states after independence, confirmed by state statutes and constitutional provisions adopting the common law as it stood at independence or at an earlier settlement date. Courts then decided which received rules were "applicable to local circumstances". Many post-colonial states retained received law while adapting it, producing hybrid systems.

Voluntary reception and transplants

States modernising their law have often borrowed wholesale: Meiji Japan adopted French- and German-inspired codes; the Republic of Turkey in 1926 adopted the Swiss Civil Code and Italian-style penal law; Ethiopia's 1960 Civil Code was drafted under French influence; and Louisiana, Quebec, Scotland and South Africa preserve mixed civil/common-law traditions from layered receptions. Legal theorist Alan Watson argued that such "legal transplants" are the most fertile source of legal change, though critics such as Pierre Legrand contend that rules cannot truly travel because their meaning is embedded in the culture that produced them — a debate at the heart of comparative law.
