Coalition government

A coalition government is a government formed when several political parties cooperate, agreeing to share executive power because no single party commands a majority in the legislature. Cabinet posts are distributed among the coalition partners according to negotiated agreements, and the parties commit to support a common programme. Coalition governments are the norm in parliamentary systems with proportional representation, and are rare in presidential systems and in majoritarian two-party democracies.

Formation

After an election leaves a hung parliament, party leaders negotiate a coalition agreement covering policy, portfolios, and dispute-resolution procedures. Political scientists distinguish minimum winning coalitions — containing just enough parties for a majority — from surplus (oversized) coalitions, which add partners for stability or special majorities, and minority coalitions, which govern with external support. Gamson's law observes that cabinet portfolios tend to be allocated in proportion to each party's contribution of legislative seats.

Examples

Germany has been governed by coalitions almost continuously since 1949, including "grand coalitions" of the CDU/CSU and SPD and the SPD–Green–FDP "traffic light" coalition of 2021–2024. Israel's proportional system produces multi-party coalitions as a matter of course, and India's coalition era (1989–2014) saw alliances such as the United Progressive Alliance manage large, diverse governments. In the United Kingdom, where single-party government is the norm, the wartime coalitions of 1915–1922 and 1940–1945 and the Conservative–Liberal Democrat coalition of 2010–2015 are the principal modern exceptions.

Advantages and disadvantages

Supporters argue that coalitions broaden representation, force compromise, and moderate policy. Critics contend they blur accountability, let small parties wield disproportionate leverage over particular issues, and can produce unstable governments — the rapid turnover of cabinets in the French Fourth Republic and Italy is frequently cited. Empirical research suggests coalition governments are not necessarily shorter-lived than single-party minority governments and that policy outputs are often similar in stability.
