Underpainting

Underpainting is an initial layer of paint applied to a ground, which serves as a base for subsequent layers of paint. Underpaintings are usually monochromatic and used to establish the composition and values (lights and darks) of a work before colour is added. A version executed in greys is called a grisaille; in greens, a verdaccio.

Technique

Working from the underpainting, the artist builds the image with glazes and opaque layers, letting the tonal structure show through thin colour to create luminous effects. The method was standard in medieval and Renaissance workshop practice, especially in Early Netherlandish and Italian painting, and remained common in academic painting into the 19th century. Infrared reflectography lets conservators and art historians see underpainting (and underlying drawing) beneath the surface of finished works, revealing changes of mind called pentimenti.

Variants

Related techniques include the imprimatura, a thin toned stain applied over the whole ground; dead colouring, a flat layer of local colour; and alla prima painting, which by contrast is executed directly without underpainting.
