Planetesimals are solid objects, thought to exist in protoplanetary disks and debris disks, formed when dust, rock, and ice grains stick together and grow until gravity can hold them together. Generally taken to be bodies of about a kilometre or larger, they are the building blocks of planets: through collisions and gravitational attraction they merge into protoplanets and eventually full-sized planets. In the Solar System the process occurred about 4.6 billion years ago; asteroids, comets, and Kuiper belt objects are surviving planetesimals that never grew into planets.

Formation theories

The classic problem is the "metre-size barrier": small particles collide too fast and drift into the star too quickly to grow steadily by sticking. Two broad solutions dominate modern research. In hierarchical coagulation, grains grow gradually by low-speed collisions, aided by sticky ices beyond the snow line. In gravitational-collapse models, aerodynamic effects such as the streaming instability concentrate pebbles into dense clouds that collapse directly into planetesimals tens to hundreds of kilometres across, skipping the difficult intermediate sizes — a picture supported by the gentle, bilobed shape of the Kuiper belt object Arrokoth, visited by New Horizons in 2019.

Role in planet formation

Once formed, planetesimals collide and accrete in a "runaway growth" phase dominated by the largest bodies, then slower "oligarchic growth" that yields planetary embryos. The term was popularised by Viktor Safronov and by the Chamberlin–Moulton planetesimal hypothesis of the early 1900s. Evidence from chondritic meteorites — which are fragments of undifferentiated planetesimals — dates the first solids of the Solar System (calcium–aluminium-rich inclusions) to 4,567 million years ago. Research in planetary science uses these relics to reconstruct how Earth and its neighbours were assembled.
