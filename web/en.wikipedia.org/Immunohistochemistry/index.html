Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) is a laboratory method that uses antibodies to detect specific antigens (markers) in cells of a tissue section. It is commonly used in the diagnosis of abnormal cells such as those found in cancerous tumors, and in basic research to understand the distribution and localization of biomarkers and proteins in biological tissues.

Technique

The method exploits the principle that antibodies bind specifically to antigens. A tissue section — usually formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded, or frozen — is incubated with a primary antibody against the target antigen. A secondary antibody, linked to a reporter such as the enzyme horseradish peroxidase or a fluorophore, is then applied. With chromogenic detection, the enzyme converts a substrate (commonly DAB) into a colored precipitate visible under a light microscope wherever the antigen is present. Antigen retrieval by heat or proteolysis is often needed to unmask epitopes in fixed tissue.

Applications

In diagnostic pathology, IHC panels help classify tumors of unknown origin, identify tumor subtypes, and detect prognostic and predictive markers — for example estrogen receptor, progesterone receptor, and HER2 in breast cancer, which guide therapy. IHC is also used to detect infectious agents in tissue and to study protein expression in neuroscience, developmental biology, and drug development.

History

The technique grew out of Albert Coons's immunofluorescence work in the 1940s; enzyme labels introduced in the 1960s–70s made bright-field IHC practical, and it became routine in pathology laboratories from the 1980s onward.
