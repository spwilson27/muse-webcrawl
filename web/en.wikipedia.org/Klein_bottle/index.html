In mathematics, the Klein bottle is a non-orientable surface: a two-dimensional manifold on which notions of "inside" and "outside", and of left and right, cannot be consistently defined. Described by Felix Klein in 1882, it is closely related to the Möbius strip and the real projective plane, and is a classic object in topology.

Construction

The Klein bottle can be constructed from a square by gluing the left and right edges in the same direction and the top and bottom edges in opposite directions — equivalently, by taking a cylinder, passing one end through its side, and joining it to the other end "from the other side". In three dimensions this immersion necessarily intersects itself; in four dimensions the bottle can be embedded smoothly with no self-intersection. Like the Möbius strip it is one-sided, but unlike the strip it is a closed surface with no boundary edge.

Properties

The Klein bottle has Euler characteristic 0, the same as a torus, but is non-orientable: an ant crawling on it could return to its starting point mirror-reversed. It can be decomposed into two Möbius strips glued along their edges, or obtained by gluing two cross-caps. It cannot hold a volume in the ordinary sense — a physical "Klein bottle" is only an immersion, and liquid poured into it comes back out of the same opening. Glass models have been produced since the 19th century, and hand-blown examples are sold as mathematical curiosities.

In culture

The Klein bottle appears frequently in mathematical humour and art. The name itself may partly rest on a pun: Klein's German Fläche (surface) is sometimes said to have been rendered Flasche (bottle). Limericks and poems about it circulate widely, and it is used as a symbol of paradoxical containment in design and fiction.
