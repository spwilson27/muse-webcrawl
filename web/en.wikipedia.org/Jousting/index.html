Jousting

Type: Martial game / tournament sport
Era: High Middle Ages to 16th century
Venue: The lists (tiltyard)
Equipment: Lance, shield, great helm, plate armour, tilt barrier

Jousting is a medieval and early modern martial game in which two horsemen riding toward each other at speed attempt to strike the opponent with a lance, typically to unhorse him or to break the lance on his shield or armour. A series of passes constitutes a single joust, and tournaments often centred on a series of jousts. It became the emblematic sport of chivalry.

Origins

The joust developed within the tournament culture of the 11th and 12th centuries as a one-on-one counterpart to the mêlée, a mock battle between groups of knights. By the 13th century the joust of peace — fought with blunted lances fitted with coronels to spread impact — was separated from the joust of war with sharp weapons, and specialized armour and equipment emerged.

The joust at its height

From the 14th to the 16th century, jousting was the centrepiece of courtly spectacle in Burgundy, France, England, and the Empire. Knights charged along opposite sides of a wooden tilt barrier, introduced in the 15th century to prevent collisions. Points were awarded for breaking lances, striking the helm, or unhorsing the opponent. Elaborate pageantry — heraldic displays, crested helms, and the favour of a lady — surrounded the combat. Royal jousts could still be lethal: Henry II of France died in 1559 from a lance splinter received while jousting, an event that hastened the sport's decline in France.

Decline and revival

As firearms and professional infantry transformed warfare, the joust lost its military training value and faded during the 17th century, surviving in ceremonial forms such as the carousel and the quintain. It is today revived by reenactors, at Renaissance fairs, and in competitive full-contact leagues, and since 2013 Maryland's long-standing ring-jousting tradition is joined by modern sporting competitions. Jousting remains the official state sport of Maryland in its ring form.
