Great Meteor hotspot track

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Great Meteor hotspot track, also called the New England hotspot track, is a chain of volcanic and intrusive igneous centres extending from northern Ontario and Quebec, across New England, and out into the Atlantic Ocean as the New England Seamounts. It records the passage of the North American Plate over a relatively stationary mantle hotspot — often identified with the present-day Great Meteor (or New England) hotspot south of the Azores — during the Mesozoic era.

Features along the track

The oldest recognised manifestations are kimberlite fields in Ontario and the Attawapiskat region (around 180 million years old). The track continues through the Jurassic–Cretaceous intrusions of the White Mountain magma series in New Hampshire and the Monteregian Hills of Quebec — including Mount Royal, which gives Montreal its name — formed roughly 125–100 million years ago. Offshore, the chain continues as the New England Seamounts, a line of extinct underwater volcanoes running southeast from Georges Bank toward Bermuda, dated to about 100–80 million years.

Significance

The track is a classic test of the hotspot hypothesis of J. Tuzo Wilson and W. Jason Morgan: the regular age progression along the chain matches the direction and rate of North American plate motion. A debated extension proposes that the hotspot earlier passed beneath western Canada, linking it to events such as the High Arctic large igneous province, while an alternative theory attributes the magmatism to edge-driven convection along the continental margin rather than a deep mantle plume.
