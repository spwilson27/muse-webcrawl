Ship of the line

A ship of the line was the principal type of naval warship from the 17th through the mid-19th century: a large, three-masted sailing vessel carrying dozens of smoothbore cannon on two or three gun decks, powerful enough to stand in the line of battle — the column formation in which fleets exchanged broadsides.

Development

The line-ahead tactic emerged in the Anglo-Dutch Wars of the 1650s, and warships were thereafter rated by gun count. First rates carried 100 or more guns (HMS Victory, Nelson's flagship at Trafalgar in 1805, is the surviving exemplar); the versatile 74-gun third rate was the backbone of the British and French fleets. Ships of the line were built massively of oak, crewed by several hundred men, and grew steadily in size over two centuries.

Decline

Steam propulsion, explosive shell guns, and iron armour rendered wooden line-of-battle ships obsolete in the 1850s–1860s, when they were superseded by steam frigates and ironclads.
