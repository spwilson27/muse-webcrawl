Denial-of-service attack

In computing, a denial-of-service attack (DoS attack) is a cyber-attack in which the perpetrator seeks to make a machine or network resource unavailable to its intended users by temporarily or indefinitely disrupting services of a host connected to a network. Denial of service is typically accomplished by flooding the targeted machine or resource with superfluous requests in an attempt to overload systems and prevent some or all legitimate requests from being fulfilled.

In a DDoS attack, many compromised devices flood a single target

Distributed denial of service

In a distributed denial-of-service attack (DDoS attack), the incoming traffic flooding the victim originates from many different sources, often a botnet of malware-infected computers and Internet of Things devices. A famous example is the 2016 attack on the DNS provider Dyn, launched from the Mirai botnet, which disrupted major websites across the United States and Europe.

Types of attack

Attacks may target bandwidth (volumetric floods), protocol weaknesses (such as SYN floods that exhaust connection tables), or application resources (slow or expensive requests, such as the Slowloris technique). Amplification attacks exploit services that return responses much larger than the requests, using spoofed source addresses to direct the flood at the victim.

Defences

Defences include over-provisioning bandwidth, filtering and rate limiting, anycast networks that diffuse traffic, scrubbing centres that filter attack traffic, and content delivery networks. Because attacks are hard to distinguish from legitimate traffic spikes, mitigation is a continuing arms race.

Legal status

DoS and DDoS attacks are crimes in most jurisdictions, including under the US Computer Fraud and Abuse Act and the UK Computer Misuse Act 1990. Motives range from extortion and protest ("hacktivism") to state-sponsored disruption.
