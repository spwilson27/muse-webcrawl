Tenkō

Tenkō (転向, literally "turning" or "reorientation") is a Japanese term referring to the ideological conversion of numerous Japanese socialists and communists who, between 1925 and 1945, renounced left-wing beliefs — often under police pressure — and announced support for the emperor system and the Japanese state.

History

Mass tenkō followed the arrests of Japanese Communist Party leaders under the Peace Preservation Law. In 1933, imprisoned party leaders Sano Manabu and Nabeyama Sadachika issued a joint statement renouncing the Comintern line, prompting hundreds of imprisoned activists to follow. The government encouraged tenkō through rehabilitation policies, and by the late 1930s the great majority of jailed communists had recanted.

Legacy

Tenkō became a major subject of postwar Japanese literature and scholarship, raising questions about coercion, belief, and collaboration under authoritarianism.
