Cellulose nitrate

Cellulose nitrate (also nitrocellulose) is a highly flammable compound formed by nitrating cellulose with a mixture of nitric and sulfuric acids. Depending on the degree of nitration it has been used as an explosive and propellant (guncotton), as the first synthetic plastic (celluloid), in lacquers and coatings, and as the base for early photographic and motion-picture film.

Properties

Highly nitrated cellulose (above about 12.5% nitrogen) is a powerful explosive that ignites readily and can decompose spontaneously when old or impure. Lower-nitrogen grades dissolve in solvents to form lacquers and collodion.

History

Christian Friedrich Schönbein discovered guncotton in 1846. Alexander Parkes and later John Wesley Hyatt developed celluloid, used for billiard balls, combs, and film stock. Nitrate film was the industry standard until the early 1950s, but its extreme flammability — nitrate fires cannot be extinguished once established and the film produces its own oxygen — caused many cinema and archive disasters, and surviving nitrate prints require special cold storage.

Modern uses

Nitrocellulose remains in use in smokeless powder, lacquers, inks, nail polish, membranes for laboratory blotting, and novelty items such as flash paper.
