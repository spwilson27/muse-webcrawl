Francis Herbert Bradley
Born: 30 January 1846, Clapham, Surrey
Died: 18 September 1924, Oxford
School: British idealism
Notable work: Appearance and Reality (1893)

Francis Herbert Bradley (30 January 1846 – 18 September 1924) was a British philosopher and the most influential figure of the British idealist movement. A fellow of Merton College, Oxford, for over fifty years, Bradley developed a monistic metaphysics in which ultimate reality — the Absolute — is a single, harmonious, supra-relational whole, of which finite things are only appearances.

Life

Bradley was educated at Cheltenham College and University College, Oxford, and elected to a fellowship at Merton in 1870. Afflicted by ill health for much of his life, he lived quietly in Oxford, rarely teaching but producing a steady series of works. He was the first British philosopher appointed to the Order of Merit, in 1924.

Philosophy

In Ethical Studies (1876) Bradley criticised utilitarianism and the doctrine of "my station and its duties" expressed his view that morality is realised through one's concrete social role. The Principles of Logic (1883) attacked psychologism and empiricist theories of judgement. His masterpiece, Appearance and Reality (1893), argued that relations, things, the self, space and time are all riddled with contradictions and hence merely apparent; reality is a single experience in which all distinctions are transcended and harmonised. His argument against relations later gave rise to the problem known as "Bradley's regress", still discussed in metaphysics.

Influence and criticism

Bradley and British idealism dominated Anglophone philosophy until the early twentieth century, when G. E. Moore and Bertrand Russell led the analytic revolt against idealism. T. S. Eliot wrote his doctoral dissertation on Bradley and quoted him in his poetry. Although his system fell from fashion, his work on logic, ethics and metaphysics continues to attract scholarly attention.
