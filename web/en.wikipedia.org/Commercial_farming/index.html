Commercial farming

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Commercial farming — also called commercial agriculture or agribusiness — is a method of agricultural production in which crops and livestock are raised primarily for sale in markets rather than for the farmer's own consumption. It contrasts with subsistence farming, and is characterised by large areas, mechanisation, significant capital investment, and integration into regional and global supply chains.

Characteristics

Commercial farms typically specialise in one or a few commodities — cereals, oilseeds, dairy, poultry, beef, cotton, fruit, or plantation crops such as tea, coffee, and rubber — and achieve economies of scale through machinery, irrigation, synthetic fertilisers and pesticides, improved or genetically modified seed, and hired labour. Output is often contracted in advance to processors and retailers, and prices are influenced by commodity exchanges.

Types

  • Intensive commercial farming — high inputs of capital and technology per hectare, common in densely populated or high-income regions (e.g. dairying and market gardening in northwestern Europe).
  • Extensive commercial farming — large holdings with lower input per hectare but high total output, such as wheat farming on the North American prairies and Australian stations.
  • Plantation agriculture — large estates, historically colonial, producing tropical cash crops for export.
  • Commercial livestock production — including feedlots and intensive poultry and pig units.

Impacts and criticism

Commercial farming underpins global food security and low consumer prices, but is criticised for environmental effects — deforestation, soil erosion, nutrient runoff, greenhouse gas emissions, and biodiversity loss — and for social effects including rural depopulation and the consolidation of land and seed markets. Responses include precision agriculture, certification schemes, regenerative practices, and policies aimed at internalising environmental costs.
