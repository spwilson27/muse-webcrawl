Alvarez hypothesis

The Alvarez hypothesis posits that the mass extinction of the non-avian dinosaurs and many other species at the end of the Cretaceous period, about 66 million years ago, was caused by the impact of a large asteroid or comet on Earth. The hypothesis is named for the father-and-son team of Luis Alvarez and Walter Alvarez, who first proposed it in 1980.

Evidence

In 1980 the Alvarezes, with Frank Asaro and Helen Michel, reported anomalously high concentrations of the rare metal iridium in the thin clay layer marking the Cretaceous–Paleogene (K–Pg) boundary at sites around the world. Because iridium is rare in Earth's crust but common in asteroids, they proposed that the layer was the debris of a roughly 10 km impactor. Subsequent discoveries strengthened the case: shocked quartz, tektites and tsunami deposits around the Gulf of Mexico, and in 1991 the identification of the Chicxulub crater beneath Mexico's Yucatán Peninsula, dated to the same time.

Effects and debate

The impact is thought to have injected sulfate aerosols and dust into the stratosphere, darkening the sky, halting photosynthesis and collapsing food chains — an "impact winter". Roughly 75% of species became extinct. Some scientists argue that the contemporaneous Deccan Traps volcanism in India also contributed; today the impact is widely regarded as the primary cause of the Cretaceous–Paleogene extinction event.
