Pompeii was an ancient Roman city near modern Naples in the Campania region of Italy. Together with Herculaneum and many surrounding villas, it was buried under 4 to 6 metres of volcanic ash and pumice in the eruption of Mount Vesuvius in 79 CE. Preserved beneath the ash, the city offers an extraordinarily detailed snapshot of Roman urban life, and has been a major tourist destination and archaeological site for some 250 years.

History

Pompeii was founded by the Oscan-speaking peoples around the 7th–6th century BCE, passed under Greek, Etruscan, and Samnite influence, and became a Roman colony in 80 BCE after siding against Rome in the Social War. It grew into a prosperous commercial town and resort, exporting wine and the fermented fish sauce garum, with a population estimated around 11,000–20,000. A major earthquake in 62 CE caused extensive damage that was still being repaired when the eruption came. The city had amphitheatre, theatres, baths, temples, a forum, and hundreds of shops, taverns, bakeries, and brothels.

The eruption of 79 CE

On 24 August 79 CE (the traditional date; some evidence suggests mid-October), Vesuvius erupted catastrophically. A column of ash and pumice rained on Pompeii for some 18 hours, collapsing roofs and burying the city; the following morning, pyroclastic surges and flows killed those who remained. The naturalist Pliny the Elder died during rescue efforts at Stabiae, and his nephew Pliny the Younger's letters to Tacitus give the classic eyewitness account. Perhaps 2,000 of the inhabitants died; most escaped. Unlike Herculaneum, which was sealed by hot flows, Pompeii's soft ash deposit eventually allowed bodies to decay, leaving cavities that excavators from 1863 filled with plaster to make the famous casts of victims — many now made with resin and studied with CT scans and DNA analysis.

Excavation and preservation

The site was rediscovered in the late 16th century during canal digging, but systematic excavation began only in 1748 under the Bourbon kings of Naples. Under Giuseppe Fiorelli in the 1860s the city was divided into the regiones and insulae still used today. Excavation has continued in episodes ever since, major recent work including the EU-funded Great Pompeii Project (2012 onward) and high-profile finds such as a ceremonial chariot (2021) and frescoed banqueting rooms. About two-thirds of the walled city (66 hectares) has been excavated; the remainder is deliberately left buried for future archaeologists. Together with Herculaneum and Torre Annunziata, Pompeii was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1997 and receives over 2.5 million visitors a year.
