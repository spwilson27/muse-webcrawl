A boy band is a vocal group consisting of young male singers, usually formed and managed as a commercial package, who perform pop music with close harmonies and choreographed dance routines. Members typically do not play instruments on stage, and each is marketed with a distinct persona — the "cute one", the "bad boy", and so on — aimed principally at a pre-teen and teenage audience.

History

The template descends from barbershop quartets, doo-wop groups, and Motown acts. The Jackson 5 (from 1964) and The Osmonds demonstrated the commercial power of wholesome family vocal groups; Puerto Rico's Menudo (1977) pioneered a constantly rotating line-up. In the late 1980s, Maurice Starr assembled New Kids on the Block after success with New Edition, launching the modern boy band boom.

The 1990s were the genre's commercial peak. Manager Lou Pearlman created the Backstreet Boys (1993) and *NSYNC (1995), who, along with Boyz II Men and Britain's Take That, sold hundreds of millions of records; the Backstreet Boys' Millennium (1999) is among the best-selling albums ever. After a lull in the 2000s, One Direction — assembled on The X Factor in 2010 — revived the format for the social media era, and South Korean groups such as BTS later took the model global, becoming the best-selling act in the world in 2020–2021 and addressing the UN General Assembly.

Characteristics and criticism

Boy bands are often formed by managers or labels through auditions rather than arising organically, and critics have long charged the format with prioritising image and merchandising over musicianship; accounts of exploitative contracts, notably Pearlman's, prompted legal reforms and greater scrutiny. Nonetheless many members (Justin Timberlake, Robbie Williams, Harry Styles, Beyoncé's counterpart groups aside) have gone on to substantial solo careers, and the format's pop craftsmanship is now broadly reassessed more favourably.
