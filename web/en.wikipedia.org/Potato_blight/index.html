Potato blight

Potato blight is a serious disease of potatoes caused principally by the oomycete (water mould) Phytophthora infestans, which causes late blight of potato and tomato. (A distinct early blight is caused by the fungus Alternaria solani.) Late blight destroys leaves, stems, and tubers, and can reduce a healthy crop to a foul, rotting mass within days under cool, wet conditions.

History

P. infestans originated in the Americas — modern studies point to central Mexico as a centre of diversity — and spread to the United States and then Europe in the 1840s. Its arrival in Ireland in 1845, combined with dependence on a single susceptible variety, the 'Lumper', produced the Great Famine (1845–1852), in which about a million people died and a million more emigrated. The disease was initially blamed on weather or static electricity; Miles Berkeley and Anton de Bary established its microbial cause, foundational work for plant pathology.

Biology and control

The pathogen produces sporangia spread by wind and rain splash; in wet weather sporangia release motile zoospores that infect through stomata and wounds. It overwinters in infected tubers. Control combines resistant varieties, clean seed, destruction of cull piles and volunteers, crop rotation, and protective fungicides — historically Bordeaux mixture, later synthetic fungicides — guided by weather-based forecasting ("blight warnings"). Late blight remains one of the most costly plant diseases worldwide, with losses and control costs estimated in billions of dollars annually, and the pathogen continues to evolve new aggressive lineages.
