Fresh water

Fresh water is naturally occurring liquid or frozen water containing low concentrations of dissolved salts and other total dissolved solids — conventionally less than 500 parts per million, and by some definitions up to 1,000 ppm — in contrast to seawater (about 35,000 ppm) and brackish water, which lies between them. Fresh water is found in lakes, rivers, streams, wetlands, groundwater aquifers, glaciers, and ice caps.

Distribution

Only about 2.5 percent of Earth's water is fresh. Of that, roughly 69 percent is locked in ice sheets, glaciers, and permanent snow (mainly Antarctica and Greenland), about 30 percent is groundwater, and only about 1 percent is surface water in lakes, rivers, and swamps — the fraction most accessible to ecosystems and human use. The largest freshwater lakes by volume include Lake Baikal in Siberia, which alone holds about a fifth of the world's liquid surface fresh water, and the Laurentian Great Lakes of North America.

Importance and pressures

Fresh water sustains terrestrial life and is essential for drinking, sanitation, agriculture (which accounts for roughly 70 percent of human withdrawals), and industry. Because renewable fresh water is unevenly distributed and increasingly stressed by population growth, pollution, and climate change, water scarcity affects billions of people for at least part of the year, and management of transboundary rivers and aquifers is a recurring source of international cooperation and tension.
