Pneumoconioses are lung diseases caused by inhaling dust in the workplace.

Pneumoconiosis is the general term for a group of interstitial lung diseases caused by the inhalation of certain mineral dusts and the lung tissue's reaction to them. The diseases are almost entirely occupational and develop after years of exposure; they cause pulmonary fibrosis and progressive breathlessness and are generally irreversible, making prevention through dust control the central strategy.

Types

The principal pneumoconioses are named for their dust: silicosis from crystalline silica (mining, quarrying, sandblasting, engineered-stone fabrication), coal workers' pneumoconiosis ("black lung") from coal dust, asbestosis from asbestos fibres (associated also with mesothelioma and lung cancer), berylliosis from beryllium, siderosis from iron, and byssinosis ("brown lung") from cotton dust, which is classified with the group in many schemes. The International Labour Organization classifies chest radiographs of pneumoconiosis on a standard scale used in worker surveillance.

Pathophysiology and symptoms

Respirable particles under about 10 micrometres reach the alveoli, where macrophages engulf them but cannot destroy mineral dusts such as silica; the resulting inflammation and cell death release fibrogenic signals, producing nodules and, in severe cases, progressive massive fibrosis. Symptoms — cough, exertional breathlessness, chest tightness — typically appear after 10–20 years of exposure, though intense silica exposure can cause acute silicosis within months. Complications include tuberculosis (silica impairs macrophage killing of mycobacteria), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease and cor pulmonale.

Prevention and treatment

There is no cure; treatment is supportive, with smoking cessation, vaccination, oxygen and, rarely, lung transplantation. Prevention relies on wet methods, ventilation, enclosed cabs, respirators, exposure limits and medical surveillance. Coal workers' pneumoconiosis resurged in central Appalachia in the 2010s, and an epidemic of silicosis among young engineered-stone workers in Australia and elsewhere has driven new bans and regulations.
