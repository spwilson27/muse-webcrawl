Atomic orbital

An atomic orbital is a function describing the location and wave-like behaviour of an electron in an atom. This function can be used to calculate the probability of finding any electron of an atom in any specific region around the atom's nucleus. The term may also refer to the physical region or space where the electron can be calculated to be present, as predicted by the particular mathematical form of the orbital.

Quantum numbers

Each orbital in an atom is characterized by a set of values of the three quantum numbers n, l, and ml, which respectively correspond to the electron's energy, angular momentum, and the component of that angular momentum along a chosen axis. Orbitals take letters denoting their angular momentum: s (l = 0), p (l = 1), d (l = 2), and f (l = 3). Each orbital can hold at most two electrons, which must have opposite spins according to the Pauli exclusion principle.

Shapes

The s orbitals are spherically symmetric. The p orbitals have a dumbbell shape with two lobes separated by a node. The d and f orbitals have more complex forms with multiple lobes and angular nodes. Orbitals are often visualized as boundary surfaces enclosing the region within which an electron is likely to be found, for example with 90% probability.

History

The orbital model arose from quantum mechanics in the 1920s, replacing the Bohr model's fixed circular orbits. The term "orbital" was coined by Robert Mulliken in 1932 as an abbreviation for "one-electron orbital wave function". Schrödinger's wave equation yields exact orbital solutions for the hydrogen atom; for multi-electron atoms, orbitals are approximated, for example by Hartree–Fock methods.
