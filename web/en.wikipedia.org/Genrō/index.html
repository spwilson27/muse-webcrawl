Genrō

The genrō were elder statesmen who guided imperial Japan.

Genrō (元老) is a designation for a group of retired elder statesmen in the Empire of Japan, who served as informal, extraconstitutional advisors to the Emperor during the Meiji and early Shōwa eras. The institution's influence peaked in the decades around the turn of the twentieth century, when the genrō effectively selected Japan's prime ministers and vetted major policy decisions.

Origins

The term derives from the genrōin, an early Meiji legislative chamber created in 1875. Although that body was soon marginalised, the men who had led the Meiji Restoration — figures such as Itō Hirobumi, Yamagata Aritomo, Matsukata Masayoshi, Inoue Kaoru, Ōyama Iwao, Katsura Tarō and Saionji Kinmochi — continued to meet and advise the throne in an unofficial capacity.

Role and influence

The genrō's most consequential power was the recommendation of candidates for prime minister, a prerogative they exercised from the 1880s until the 1920s. They also weighed in on foreign policy, military appointments and constitutional crises. Because their authority rested on custom and personal prestige rather than law, it was sometimes called the rule of "government by elder statesmen."

Decline

As the original Restoration leaders died, the circle was never replenished in a systematic way. After 1924 Saionji Kinmochi, the last surviving genrō, performed the advisory role largely alone, most notably during the selection of governments in the 1930s. His death in 1940 ended the institution.
