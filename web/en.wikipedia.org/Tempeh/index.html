Tempeh (Indonesian: tempe) is a traditional Indonesian food made by fermenting cooked soybeans with a fungus, Rhizopus oligosporus (or R. oryzae), which binds the beans into a firm, sliceable cake with a nutty, savoury flavour. Originating on the island of Java, it is a staple protein source in Indonesia and has become popular worldwide as a meat substitute in vegetarian and vegan cooking.

Production

Dehulled soybeans are soaked, cooked, inoculated with the starter (ragi tempe), wrapped or packed in perforated containers, and incubated at about 30 °C for 24–48 hours. The white mycelium knits the beans together and partially digests their proteins and fats, improving digestibility and reducing flatulence-causing oligosaccharides. Unlike most fermented soy products, tempeh retains the whole bean, giving it higher protein, fibre and vitamin content than tofu.

Culinary use

In Indonesia tempeh is fried, grilled, stewed in coconut curries, or crumbled into sambals; tempe goreng and tempe mendoan are common preparations. In Western cooking it is marinated, baked, or used in sandwiches and stir-fries. A serving of 100 g provides roughly 19 g of protein.
