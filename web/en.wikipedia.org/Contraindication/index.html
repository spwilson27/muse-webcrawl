Contraindication

In medicine, a contraindication is a condition or factor that serves as a reason to withhold a certain medical treatment due to the harm that it would cause the patient. Contraindication is the opposite of indication, which is a reason to use a certain treatment.

Absolute and relative contraindications

An absolute contraindication is a condition that makes a treatment entirely inadvisable because it could cause a life-threatening situation. For example, giving a live vaccine to a severely immunocompromised person, or using isotretinoin during pregnancy because of its teratogenicity, are absolute contraindications. A relative contraindication is a condition in which two drugs or procedures may still be used together or performed if the benefits outweigh the risks; caution is advised, and the decision is individualized. For instance, pregnancy is a relative contraindication to many radiographic examinations, which may still be justified when the diagnostic benefit is high.

In drug labelling

Contraindications appear in a dedicated section of official drug information (such as the U.S. package insert and the European Summary of Product Characteristics), alongside warnings and precautions. They may be based on known pharmacology, clinical trial exclusions, or post-marketing reports of harm. Clinicians also consider drug interactions — where the combination of two agents is contraindicated — and patient-specific factors such as allergies, organ impairment, age and pregnancy.
