Pulp magazine

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Pulp magazines (often referred to as "the pulps") were inexpensive fiction magazines widely published from 1896 through the late 1950s. The term "pulp" derives from the cheap wood pulp paper on which the magazines were printed; in contrast, magazines printed on higher-quality paper were called "glossies" or "slicks".

A typical pulp magazine was 128 pages, seven inches wide by ten inches long, with ragged, untrimmed edges. Pulps were the successors to the penny dreadfuls, dime novels and short-fiction magazines of the nineteenth century, and were an important incubator of modern genre fiction: detective fiction, science fiction, westerns, horror and adventure.

History

The first pulp was Frank Munsey's Argosy, converted to all-fiction on pulp paper in 1896. Street & Smith and other publishers followed, and by the 1920s and 1930s specialised genre titles flourished: Detective Story Magazine, Black Mask, Weird Tales, Amazing Stories, Astounding Science Fiction and The Shadow. Writers such as Dashiell Hammett, Raymond Chandler, H. P. Lovecraft, Robert E. Howard, Edgar Rice Burroughs and Isaac Asimov began or built careers in the pulps.

Decline

Paper shortages during the Second World War, competition from paperback books, comic books and television, and the collapse of the American News Company distribution network in 1957 killed most of the pulps. A few titles survive in digest format.

Legacy

The pulps' vivid painted covers, sensational subject matter and stable of heroic characters — The Shadow, Doc Savage, Conan the Barbarian — deeply influenced later popular culture; the term "pulp fiction" itself connotes fast-paced, lurid genre storytelling, echoed in the title of Quentin Tarantino's 1994 film.
