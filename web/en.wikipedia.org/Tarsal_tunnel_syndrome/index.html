Tarsal tunnel syndrome

Tarsal tunnel syndrome (TTS) is a compression neuropathy of the posterior tibial nerve as it passes through the tarsal tunnel, a fibro-osseous channel behind and below the medial malleolus of the ankle, beneath the flexor retinaculum. It is considered the lower-limb analogue of carpal tunnel syndrome in the wrist.

Symptoms

Typical symptoms include burning, tingling, numbness, and shooting pain along the sole of the foot and heel, sometimes radiating into the toes or up the calf. Symptoms often worsen with prolonged standing, walking, or at night, and may be reproduced by percussing the nerve behind the ankle (Tinel's sign) or by sustained inversion or eversion of the foot.

Causes and diagnosis

Any space-occupying or deforming process in the tunnel can cause TTS: ganglion cysts, varicosities, tenosynovitis, tumours, flatfoot (pes planus) and hindfoot valgus, ankle injuries, and systemic conditions such as diabetes and rheumatoid arthritis; in many cases no definite cause is found. Diagnosis is clinical, supported by nerve conduction studies and electromyography, and imaging (ultrasound or MRI) when a mass is suspected.

Treatment

Initial management is conservative: rest, ice, anti-inflammatory medication, footwear modification, orthotics, and physiotherapy. Persistent or severe cases may be treated with surgical decompression — release of the flexor retinaculum — with outcomes generally better when a definite compressive lesion is identified and treated.
