Kairos

Kairos (καιρός) is an ancient Greek word meaning the right, critical, or opportune moment. In classical rhetoric and philosophy it contrasts with chronos, which denotes sequential, quantitative time: while chronos measures duration, kairos signifies the qualitative instant at which action is most effective.

In rhetoric

For Greek rhetoricians such as Gorgias and Isocrates, kairos was the principle of aptness — saying the right thing at the right moment, adapted to the audience and occasion. A speech that ignored kairos might be true yet fail, because persuasion depends on timing and circumstance. The concept remains central to rhetorical theory today, where it is often listed alongside ethos, pathos, and logos.

In theology

In the New Testament, kairos is used for appointed or decisive moments in God's plan, as distinct from ordinary chronological time. Twentieth-century theologians such as Paul Tillich revived the term to describe moments of historical crisis and decision in which new possibilities break into history.

Personification

Kairos was also personified as a youthful god, depicted by the sculptor Lysippos with a forelock of hair and a bare back of the head — opportunity can be seized only as it approaches, not once it has passed. He was shown balancing on a wheel or razor's edge, holding scales.
