Johan de Witt

Johan de Witt (24 September 1625 – 20 August 1672) was a Dutch statesman and mathematician who, as Grand Pensionary of Holland from 1653 to 1672, effectively governed the Dutch Republic during the First Stadtholderless Period. His leadership coincided with the height of the Dutch Golden Age; he was lynched by an Orangist mob in the Rampjaar (Disaster Year) of 1672.

Early life

De Witt was born into a patrician family of Dordrecht. He studied law and mathematics at Leiden, publishing Elementa curvarum linearum, an early work on analytic geometry. After a grand tour of France and England he practised law at The Hague before becoming pensionary of Dordrecht in 1650 and Grand Pensionary of Holland in 1653.

Rule of the Republic

De Witt dominated Dutch politics for nearly two decades, defending the republican "True Freedom" against the Orangist party that wished to restore the stadtholderate for the young Prince William of Orange. He negotiated the Treaty of Westminster (1654) ending the First Anglo-Dutch War — with a secret clause, the Act of Seclusion, excluding the Prince of Orange from the stadtholderate — and led the Republic through the Second Anglo-Dutch War, which culminated in the daring Raid on the Medway in 1667. The Perpetual Edict of 1667 abolished the stadtholderate in Holland.

Downfall and death

In 1672 the Republic was simultaneously attacked by France, England, Münster and Cologne. The public blamed De Witt; he was wounded in an assassination attempt and resigned on 4 August. On 20 August, visiting his brother Cornelis in prison in The Hague, both men were seized by a civic militia mob and savagely murdered, their bodies mutilated. William of Orange, restored as stadtholder, did not punish the killers. De Witt also left important mathematical work on annuities, having in 1671 written one of the first treatises on life annuities and the pricing of risk.
