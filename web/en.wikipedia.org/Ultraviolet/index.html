Ultraviolet

The ultraviolet region of the electromagnetic spectrum.

Ultraviolet (UV) light is electromagnetic radiation with a wavelength shorter than that of visible light but longer than X-rays, in the range of about 10 nm to 400 nm, corresponding to photon energies from roughly 3 eV to 124 eV. It takes its name from the fact that it lies beyond the violet end of the visible spectrum (Latin ultra, "beyond"). UV radiation is invisible to the human eye, although some insects, birds, and other animals can perceive parts of it.

Discovery

Ultraviolet radiation was discovered in 1801 by the German physicist Johann Wilhelm Ritter, who observed that invisible rays beyond the violet end of the spectrum darkened silver chloride–soaked paper more quickly than visible light itself. The discovery followed William Herschel's detection of infrared radiation the previous year.

Subtypes

  • UVA (315–400 nm) — "long-wave" or "black light"; not absorbed by the ozone layer; penetrates deep into skin and contributes to ageing and some skin cancers.
  • UVB (280–315 nm) — "medium-wave"; mostly absorbed by the ozone layer; causes sunburn and is required for vitamin D synthesis in skin.
  • UVC (100–280 nm) — "short-wave" or "germicidal"; almost entirely absorbed by the atmosphere, but produced by artificial lamps for disinfection.
  • Extreme, far, and vacuum UV — wavelengths below about 200 nm, strongly absorbed by air and studied mainly in vacuum equipment.

Natural and artificial sources

The Sun is the dominant natural source of ultraviolet light, though most solar UV is absorbed by the Earth's atmosphere, particularly the ozone layer. Artificial sources include mercury-vapour lamps, tanning lamps, black lights, UV LEDs, welding arcs, and germicidal lamps.

Effects on health

Moderate UV exposure enables the skin to produce vitamin D, which is essential for bone health. Excessive exposure causes sunburn, photoageing, and increases the risk of skin cancers, including melanoma and basal-cell and squamous-cell carcinomas, as well as eye damage such as cataracts and photokeratitis ("snow blindness"). Tanning beds, which emit concentrated UVA, are classified as carcinogenic. Protective measures include sunscreen, clothing, shade, and sunglasses. The UV index is a standard international scale describing the intensity of sunburn-producing radiation at a given place and time.

Applications

Ultraviolet light has many practical uses:

  • Disinfection — UVC lamps sterilise water, air, and surfaces by damaging microbial DNA.
  • Fluorescence — UV excites fluorescent materials, exploited in black lights, mineralogy, forensics, and security features of banknotes and documents.
  • Photolithography and curing — UV is used to pattern semiconductors and to harden inks, adhesives, and resins.
  • Analysis and astronomy — UV spectroscopy identifies chemical composition; space telescopes observe the ultraviolet universe, which is blocked from the ground.
  • Biology — many flowers display UV patterns visible to pollinating insects but not to humans.
