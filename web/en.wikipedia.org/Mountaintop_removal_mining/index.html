Mountaintop removal mining

Mountaintop removal mining (MTR), also known as mountaintop mining, is a form of surface mining at the summit or summit ridge of a mountain. It is practiced mainly in the Appalachian Mountains of the eastern United States, especially in West Virginia, Kentucky, Virginia and Tennessee, to extract coal seams lying under a mountaintop.

Process

The mountain is first cleared of forest, and explosives are used to blast away up to 120 m (400 ft) of rock and soil (the "overburden") to expose coal seams. Huge dragline excavators remove the coal; excess rock and spoil is dumped into adjacent valleys in "valley fills", burying headwater streams. After mining, operators are required to reclaim and regrade the site, but the original elevation and ecology are not restored, and flat or rolling land replaces the peak.

Impacts and controversy

Studies by U.S. agencies have linked MTR to deforestation of large tracts of hardwood forest, burial of more than 3,000 km of streams, elevated levels of minerals and heavy metals in downstream waters, increased flooding, and higher rates of some health problems in nearby communities. Supporters point to jobs and low-cost coal, though employment in the practice is small compared with other mining methods. MTR expanded greatly in the 1990s and has declined with the falling demand for Central Appalachian coal; it remains one of the most contested environmental issues in the United States.
