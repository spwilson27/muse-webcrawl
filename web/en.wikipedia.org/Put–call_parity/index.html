Put–call parity

Put–call parity is a principle in financial mathematics that defines a relationship between the price of a European call option and a European put option, both with the same strike price and expiry date. In a frictionless market, the two prices are linked by the current price of the underlying asset and the present value of the strike: the call price plus the present value of the strike equals the put price plus the price of the underlying asset. Any deviation creates an arbitrage opportunity.

Statement

For European options on a non-dividend-paying stock, put–call parity states that C + K·e−rT = P + S, where C and P are the call and put prices, S is the spot price of the underlying, K is the strike price, r is the risk-free interest rate, and T is the time to expiry. The two sides correspond to portfolios with identical payoffs at expiry: a call plus cash equal to the discounted strike (a fiduciary call) replicates a put plus the underlying share (a protective put), so by the law of one price the portfolios must cost the same today.

History

Versions of the parity relationship were understood by option traders by the early twentieth century; it was described by Henry Deutsch in 1910 and popularized in academic form by Hans Stoll in 1969. Put–call parity is a foundational example of pricing by replication and no-arbitrage, and it underlies the construction of synthetic positions — synthetic long and short stock, conversions, and reversals — widely used by options market makers.

Extensions and limitations

The relationship is modified for dividend-paying stocks (subtracting the present value of expected dividends), for American options (where early exercise turns the equality into inequalities), and in real markets by transaction costs, taxes, and short-selling constraints, which allow prices to depart from parity within a no-arbitrage band. Deviations from parity in index options have been studied as indicators of market stress and funding constraints.
