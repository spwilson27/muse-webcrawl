Parthenogenesis

Parthenogenesis (from the Greek parthenos, "virgin", and genesis, "creation") is a natural form of asexual reproduction in which an embryo develops from an unfertilised egg cell, without fertilisation by a male. It occurs in many invertebrates — including water fleas, aphids, nematodes, some bees and wasps, stick insects, and scorpions — and in some vertebrates, including certain lizards, snakes, sharks, and birds. Offspring produced parthenogenetically may be clones of the mother or carry only part of her genetic variation, depending on the cellular mechanism involved.

Aphids reproduce parthenogenetically through much of the year, giving birth to live female young.

Mechanisms

In apomictic parthenogenesis, the egg is produced by mitosis rather than meiosis, so offspring are genetically identical to the mother. In automictic parthenogenesis, meiosis occurs but the diploid number is restored by fusion of the egg with a polar body or by duplication of the chromosomes, producing offspring that are partly homozygous. In many haplodiploid insects such as ants, bees, and wasps, unfertilised eggs develop into haploid males and fertilised eggs into females — a system called arrhenotoky.

Life cycles

Many parthenogenetic species alternate between asexual and sexual reproduction (cyclical parthenogenesis): aphids and water fleas produce many all-female generations during favourable seasons, then switch to sexual reproduction to lay hardy eggs before winter. Other lineages, such as bdelloid rotifers, appear to have reproduced without males for tens of millions of years. Obligate parthenogenesis in vertebrates is concentrated in squamates: more than fifty species of lizard, including entire all-female whiptail lineages, many of hybrid origin, reproduce this way, and facultative parthenogenesis — "virgin births" by females kept without males — has been documented in Komodo dragons, several sharks, and domestic turkeys.

Evolutionary significance

Parthenogenesis offers short-term advantages: a single female can found a population, and no energy is spent finding mates. Its long-term costs — reduced genetic diversity and the accumulation of harmful mutations — are one answer to the evolutionary puzzle of why sexual reproduction is so widespread. In plants, the analogous production of seeds without fertilisation is called apomixis, and inducing it in crops is a long-standing goal of plant breeding.
