The Washington Color School was a movement of abstract painters working in Washington, D.C., from the 1950s through the 1970s. Associated with color field painting, its best-known members were Morris Louis and Kenneth Noland, together with Gene Davis, Thomas Downing, Howard Mehring and Paul Reed. Their work favoured large expanses of pure, often stained, colour arranged in stripes, targets, veils and other simple formats, in deliberate contrast to the gestural brushwork of New York action painting.

Origins

The movement coalesced after Louis and Noland visited Helen Frankenthaler's New York studio in 1953 with the critic Clement Greenberg and saw her soak-stain technique in Mountains and Sea (1952). Both artists adopted staining unprimed canvas with thinned acrylic (Magna) paint, so that colour became integral to the canvas rather than sitting on its surface. Louis developed his "Veil" and later "Unfurled" and "Stripe" paintings; Noland painted concentric "targets" and chevrons.

Recognition

The name derives from the 1965 exhibition "The Washington Color Painters" at the Washington Gallery of Modern Art, which showed Davis, Downing, Louis, Mehring, Noland and Reed. The show helped establish Washington as a centre of post-painterly abstraction. Gene Davis became known for his precisely measured stripe paintings, including the enormous Franklin's Footpath painted on a Philadelphia street in 1972.

Although the group's prominence faded as other movements arose, its members' work is represented in major museum collections and has been the subject of renewed scholarly and exhibition interest in the twenty-first century.
