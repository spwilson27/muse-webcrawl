Warsaw Pact

The Warsaw Pact, formally the Treaty of Friendship, Cooperation and Mutual Assistance, was a collective defence treaty signed in Warsaw, Poland, on 14 May 1955 between the Soviet Union and seven Eastern Bloc socialist republics: Albania, Bulgaria, Czechoslovakia, East Germany, Hungary, Poland and Romania. The counterpart to NATO in the Cold War, it created a unified military command under Soviet leadership and formalised Soviet control over the armed forces of its satellite states. The pact's only joint military operations were against its own members: the suppression of the Hungarian Revolution (1956, just before the command structures took effect) and the invasion of Czechoslovakia in 1968. It was dissolved on 1 July 1991 as the Cold War ended.

Establishment

The pact was concluded in direct response to West Germany's entry into NATO and its rearmament in May 1955, which Moscow portrayed as a renewed Western military threat. Its text followed the pattern of Soviet bilateral treaties with the Eastern European states, pledging mutual assistance in the event of an armed attack in Europe. In practice, the alliance's Unified Armed Forces were always headed by a Soviet commander, and the Soviet Union concluded status-of-forces agreements stationing its troops across the bloc.

Interventions

In 1956 the USSR crushed the Hungarian Revolution with Soviet (not formally pact-integrated) forces after Imre Nagy announced Hungary's withdrawal from the pact and neutrality. In August 1968, five pact members invaded Czechoslovakia to end the liberalising reforms of the Prague Spring; the operation supplied the pretext for the Brezhnev Doctrine of "limited sovereignty" for socialist states. Albania, which had ceased participating in 1961 after the Sino-Soviet split, formally withdrew in 1968 in protest. Romania under Ceaușescu refused to join the 1968 invasion and limited its participation in joint exercises.

Dissolution

As communist governments fell across Eastern Europe in 1989, the pact lost its ideological basis. East Germany left in 1990 upon German reunification; the military structures were disbanded at a Budapest meeting on 25 February 1991, and the treaty itself was formally terminated in Prague on 1 July 1991. Most former members subsequently joined NATO — Poland, Hungary and the Czech Republic in 1999 — a development that would frame European security politics for decades.
