Social mobility

[Image placeholder: social mobility diagram]

Social mobility is the movement of individuals, families, households, or other categories of people within or between social strata in a society. It is a change in social status relative to one's current social location within a given society. Movement can be upward or downward, and it may occur over the course of a single lifetime (intragenerational mobility) or between generations (intergenerational mobility).

Social mobility is a central topic in sociology and economics and is closely related to concepts of equality of opportunity, social class, and the distribution of income and wealth. The openness of a society—how freely positions can change—varies greatly across countries and historical periods.

Types

  • Upward and downward mobility: movement to a higher or lower social position.
  • Intergenerational mobility: change in status between parents and their children, often measured by income or occupation.
  • Intragenerational mobility: change within a person's own working life.
  • Structural mobility: mobility produced by changes in the occupational structure itself, such as the shift from manufacturing to services.
  • Absolute and relative mobility: absolute mobility compares living standards across generations; relative mobility concerns a person's rank compared with others in their generation.

Measurement

Economists commonly measure intergenerational mobility using the intergenerational income elasticity—the percentage difference in children's earnings associated with a one-percent difference in parents' earnings. Lower elasticity indicates higher mobility. Sociologists have traditionally used occupational class schemes and mobility tables comparing fathers' and children's occupations. A widely discussed finding, sometimes called the "Great Gatsby curve," is the association between higher income inequality and lower intergenerational mobility across countries.

Factors

Access to education is among the strongest predictors of mobility, along with family resources, social networks, neighborhood conditions, health, and labor-market institutions. Inheritance, assortative mating, and residential segregation tend to reduce mobility, while public education, progressive taxation, and anti-discrimination law are often argued to increase it. Research by economists such as Raj Chetty has shown large geographic variation in mobility within the United States, correlated with factors including school quality, family structure, and social capital.

Criticism and debate

Debates concern whether meritocracy accurately describes high-mobility societies, whether mobility or equality of outcome should be the primary policy goal, and how mobility relates to well-being: some studies find that downward mobility carries significant psychological costs, and that belief in mobility can coexist with high measured inequality.
