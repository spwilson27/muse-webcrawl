Corpus Christi (feast)

The Feast of Corpus Christi (Latin for "Body of Christ"), also known as the Solemnity of the Most Holy Body and Blood of Christ, is a Catholic liturgical feast celebrating the real presence of Christ in the Eucharist. It is observed on the Thursday after Trinity Sunday (or, in many countries, transferred to the following Sunday), sixty days after Easter.

History

The feast originated in the diocese of Liège in the thirteenth century, promoted by the Augustinian nun Juliana of Liège, who reported visions urging a festival in honour of the Blessed Sacrament. The local bishop instituted the feast in 1246. After the reported Eucharistic miracle of Bolsena in 1263, Pope Urban IV extended the feast to the whole Latin Church by the bull Transiturus in 1264; tradition holds that Thomas Aquinas composed the office texts, including the hymns Lauda Sion, Pange lingua and Sacris solemniis.

Observance

The distinctive feature of Corpus Christi is the outdoor procession: the consecrated host is carried in a monstrance beneath a canopy through streets decorated with flowers and greenery, with stops at temporary altars for Benediction. Elaborate floral carpets are created in places such as Spello in Italy, Arundel in England, and many towns in Spain, Poland and Latin America. The feast is a public holiday in several predominantly Catholic countries, including Austria, Brazil, Colombia, Poland, Portugal and parts of Germany, Spain and Switzerland. Some Lutheran and Anglican traditions also observe a day of thanksgiving for the institution of Holy Communion.
