The Oceanic languages are a well-established branch of the Austronesian language family, comprising roughly 450 to 600 languages spoken by about two million people across the islands of Melanesia, Micronesia and Polynesia and along the northern coast of New Guinea. They descend from Proto-Oceanic, spoken by the makers of the Lapita culture some 3,000–3,500 years ago.

Subgroups

Linguists divide Oceanic into several primary subgroups, including the Admiralties languages, the St Matthias languages, the Western Oceanic linkage (of northern New Guinea and the Bismarck Archipelago), the Southeast Solomonic languages, and the large Central-Eastern Oceanic grouping, which contains the Central Pacific languages (Fijian and the Polynesian languages) and the languages of Vanuatu and New Caledonia. Vanuatu has the world's highest density of languages per capita, with over 100 indigenous Oceanic languages.

Features

Oceanic languages are known for features such as possessive classifiers (distinguishing, for example, how food, drink and general possessions are "owned"), inclusive and exclusive first-person pronouns ("we" including or excluding the listener), and in many languages a distinction between alienable and inalienable possession. Polynesian languages such as Hawaiian and Māori have notably small sound inventories.

History and scholarship

Reconstruction of Proto-Oceanic vocabulary has been used, alongside archaeology, to trace the settlement of the Pacific: terms for outrigger canoes, sails, and cultivated plants such as taro, yam and breadfruit illuminate the Lapita expansion from the Bismarck Archipelago eastward into Remote Oceania. Many Oceanic languages are endangered as speakers shift to English, French, or regional lingua francas such as Tok Pisin and Bislama, and documentation and revitalisation efforts — notably for Hawaiian and Māori — are ongoing.
