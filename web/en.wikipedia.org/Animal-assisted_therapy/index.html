Animal-assisted therapy

Animal-assisted therapy (AAT) is an alternative or complementary type of therapy that includes the use of animals in a treatment plan. It is a goal-oriented, structured intervention directed by a health or human-service professional, in which a trained animal meeting specific criteria is an integral part of the treatment process. AAT is distinguished from informal animal-assisted activities (such as volunteer pet-visitation programmes) by its documented therapeutic goals and evaluation.

History

Animals were used in therapeutic settings as early as the York Retreat in 1790s England, where patients cared for small animals. Florence Nightingale noted the value of pets for the chronically ill. The modern field is often dated to child psychologist Boris Levinson, who in the 1960s reported that his dog Jingles facilitated communication with a withdrawn child patient, and who coined the term "pet therapy".

Types and settings

Dogs are the most commonly used therapy animals, but programmes also involve cats, rabbits, and especially horses — in equine-assisted therapies and therapeutic riding (hippotherapy) used for physical rehabilitation and psychological treatment. AAT is applied in hospitals, nursing homes, schools, prisons, rehabilitation centres, and mental-health practice, including work with children with autism, people with dementia, patients with PTSD, and individuals in physical rehabilitation.

Evidence and criticism

Studies have reported benefits such as reduced anxiety, lowered blood pressure, increased social interaction, and improved mood; however, systematic reviews note that much of the literature consists of small studies with methodological weaknesses, and strong evidence for lasting therapeutic effects remains limited. Animal welfare, hygiene, allergies, and the proper training and certification of animals and handlers are practical concerns addressed by organizations that set standards for the field.
