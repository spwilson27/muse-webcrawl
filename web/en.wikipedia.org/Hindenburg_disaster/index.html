The Hindenburg disaster occurred on 6 May 1937, when the German passenger airship LZ 129 Hindenburg caught fire and was destroyed while attempting to dock with its mooring mast at Naval Air Station Lakehurst in Manchester Township, New Jersey. Of the 97 people on board, 35 died, as did one member of the ground crew. The disaster effectively ended the era of rigid airships as passenger transport.

Background

The Hindenburg, the largest airship ever built, had completed a successful 1936 season of transatlantic flights between Germany and the United States and Brazil. It had been designed to use non-flammable helium, but export restrictions by the United States — then the only major producer — forced the Germans to lift it with flammable hydrogen instead.

The disaster

Delayed by thunderstorms, the airship approached Lakehurst in the evening and began its landing procedure shortly after 19:00. At 19:25, as mooring lines were dropped, witnesses saw a small flame near the tail. The fire spread with tremendous speed through the hydrogen cells, and within about half a minute the ship's tail fell to the ground. Remarkably, 62 of those aboard survived, many by jumping as the hull settled. Newsreel footage and Sam Shere's photographs of the burning ship became iconic, as did radio reporter Herbert Morrison's anguished eyewitness broadcast — "Oh, the humanity!" — recorded for WLS in Chicago.

Cause

The official inquiries concluded that escaping hydrogen had been ignited, probably by a spark of static electricity. Later theories have emphasised the flammability of the airship's doped cotton skin; a sabotage theory was popular at the time but has never been substantiated.

Legacy

Public confidence in airships collapsed; the Hindenburg's sister ship, Graf Zeppelin II, never carried paying passengers, and the age of the great passenger zeppelins was over.
