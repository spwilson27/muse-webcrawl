Foley (filmmaking)

Foley is the reproduction of everyday sound effects that are added to films, videos, and other media in post-production to enhance audio quality. These reproduced sounds, named after sound-effects artist Jack Foley, can be anything from the swishing of clothing and footsteps to squeaky doors and breaking glass. Foley sounds are used to enhance the auditory experience of a movie: they add a sense of realism by creating a rich, smooth, and lifelike soundscape.

History

Jack Foley (1891–1967), a sound editor at Universal Studios, developed the technique during the transition to sound film, when effects were performed live to picture while dialogue was recorded. What is now called Foley was originated as adding sounds for live radio broadcasting in the 1920s; by the time of films such as Show Boat (1929), footsteps, movement, and props were performed in synchronisation with the projected image.

Process

Foley artists work in a Foley stage, a studio equipped with pits filled with gravel, sand, water, and other surfaces, and a vast collection of props. Watching the film, they perform three categories of sound: footsteps, movement (such as the rustle of clothing), and specifics — the bespoke sounds of objects handled on screen, from breaking celery standing in for breaking bones to coconuts simulating horses' hooves. The performances are recorded and mixed with production dialogue and other sound effects.
