Stress (biology)

In biology, stress is the body's method of reacting to a condition such as a threat, challenge, or physical or psychological barrier. Stimuli that alter the body's internal environment are called stressors. The stress response — coordinated by the nervous and endocrine systems — prepares the organism for "fight or flight," but chronic activation can damage health.

Physiology

Perception of a threat activates the sympathetic nervous system and the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis. The adrenal medulla releases adrenaline (epinephrine) and noradrenaline, raising heart rate, blood pressure and blood glucose; the HPA axis then drives release of cortisol from the adrenal cortex, sustaining energy mobilisation and modulating immune function. When the threat passes, feedback loops normally restore baseline — a return to homeostasis, or, in modern terms, a new state of allostasis.

General adaptation syndrome

Hans Selye, who introduced the biological concept of stress in the 1930s and 1950s, described a three-stage general adaptation syndrome: alarm (initial mobilisation), resistance (sustained coping) and exhaustion (depletion if the stressor persists). Selye also distinguished damaging distress from positive eustress.

Health effects

Acute stress is adaptive, but chronic stress is associated with cardiovascular disease, immunosuppression, metabolic disorders, depression and anxiety, and accelerated cellular ageing — measured, for example, in telomere shortening. The cumulative physiological cost of chronic adaptation is termed allostatic load. Psychological appraisal matters: the same event may be experienced as a challenge or a threat depending on perceived resources, as modelled by Richard Lazarus's transactional theory of stress and coping.

Stress in other organisms

Plants respond to drought, salinity and temperature extremes with hormonal signalling (notably abscisic acid) and protective compounds, while microorganisms mount heat-shock and general stress responses mediated by alternative sigma factors and proteins such as Hsp70.
