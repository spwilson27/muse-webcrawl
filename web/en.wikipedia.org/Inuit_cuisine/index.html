Inuit cuisine

Inuit cuisine is the traditional diet of the Inuit of the Arctic, based overwhelmingly on hunted and fished animal foods: seal, walrus, whale, caribou, polar bear, fish and seabirds, supplemented by gathered berries, seaweed and roots during the short summer. In Inuktitut, country food is called niqituinnaq ("real food"), in contrast to store-bought qallunaatitut food.

Characteristics

The diet is high in protein and fat and traditionally very low in carbohydrate. Much food was eaten raw or frozen — raw and frozen fish, seal meat and whale blubber (maktaaq/muktuk) retain vitamin C that cooking would destroy, protecting against scurvy in a region without fresh fruit or vegetables. Other preparations included boiling, drying (fish and caribou dried as pivsiit), and fermentation, as in igunaq, walrus or other meat aged in caches.

Seal was the staple across most regions, providing meat, blubber for lamps and heat, and skins for clothing. Whale, walrus and caribou hunts were communal events governed by strong traditions of sharing meat throughout the community.

Modern changes

From the mid-20th century, settlement life, wage labour and store-bought foods transformed the diet, bringing high rates of diabetes and cardiovascular disease. Country food nevertheless remains nutritionally and culturally central, though access is challenged by the high cost of hunting, climate change affecting ice and animal movements, and environmental contaminants such as mercury and persistent organic pollutants that accumulate in marine mammals.
