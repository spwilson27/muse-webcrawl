China–United States relations

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

China–United States relations, also known as US–China relations, are the international relations between China and the United States of America. The relationship between the two countries — the world's two largest economies and major military powers — is widely regarded as one of the most important bilateral relationships in the world, encompassing trade, security, human rights, Taiwan, the South China Sea, and global governance.

Historical outline

Contacts between the United States and Qing-dynasty China began with the voyage of the Empress of China to Canton in 1784. The Treaty of Wanghia (1844) established formal relations. In the late 19th century the United States advocated the Open Door Policy to preserve equal commercial access to China. The two countries were allies against Japan in the Second World War. After the Chinese Civil War, the People's Republic of China (PRC) was proclaimed in 1949, while the United States continued to recognize the Republic of China, which had retreated to Taiwan. The two sides fought directly in the Korean War (1950–1953).

Rapprochement and normalization

A turning point came with "ping-pong diplomacy" in 1971 and President Richard Nixon's visit to China in February 1972, which produced the Shanghai Communiqué. On 1 January 1979 the United States formally recognized the PRC and established diplomatic relations, severing official relations with Taipei while maintaining unofficial ties under the Taiwan Relations Act. Deng Xiaoping's visit to the United States the same year symbolized a new era of engagement.

Economic relations

China's accession to the World Trade Organization in 2001 deepened economic integration. China became one of America's largest trading partners and a major holder of US Treasury securities, while bilateral trade grew to hundreds of billions of dollars annually. Since 2018 the relationship has been strained by a trade war involving tariffs on large volumes of goods, technology restrictions, and disputes over industrial policy and market access.

Strategic competition

In the 21st century, relations have increasingly been characterized by strategic competition. Points of friction include Taiwan's status, Chinese military activity in the South and East China Seas, human rights in Xinjiang and Hong Kong, cybersecurity, and technology supply chains. At the same time, the two governments cooperate on issues such as climate change, public health, and nuclear non-proliferation. Successive US administrations have described China variously as a partner, competitor, and rival.
