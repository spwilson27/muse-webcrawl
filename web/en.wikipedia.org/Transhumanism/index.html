Transhumanism

Transhumanism is a philosophical and intellectual movement that advocates the enhancement of the human condition by developing and making widely available sophisticated technologies that can greatly enhance longevity, cognition, and well-being. Transhumanist thinkers study the potential benefits and dangers of emerging technologies that could overcome fundamental human limitations, as well as the ethics of using such technologies. Some transhumanists believe that human beings may eventually be able to transform themselves into beings of such greatly expanded abilities that they would merit the label of posthuman beings.

History

The term "transhumanism" was coined in its modern sense by biologist Julian Huxley in 1957, though antecedents can be found in the work of thinkers such as J. B. S. Haldane and his 1923 essay Daedalus: or, Science and the Future. In the 1980s, a group of futurists, including FM-2030 and Max More, formalized transhumanist philosophy; More's Principles of Extropy became a foundational text. The World Transhumanist Association (now Humanity+) was founded in 1998 by Nick Bostrom and David Pearce to provide a general organizational basis for transhumanist groups.

Themes

Central transhumanist themes include life extension through anti-aging medicine and cryonics, cognitive enhancement through nootropics and brain–computer interfaces, genetic engineering, nanotechnology, artificial intelligence, and mind uploading. Transhumanists distinguish between morphological freedom — the right to modify one's own body — and a concern that unregulated enhancement could produce existential risks, a theme explored by Bostrom in works on superintelligence.

Criticism

Critics, sometimes termed bioconservatives, argue that enhancement technologies threaten human dignity, equality, and what it means to be human. Francis Fukuyama called transhumanism "the world's most dangerous idea," while others object on religious, economic, or safety grounds.
