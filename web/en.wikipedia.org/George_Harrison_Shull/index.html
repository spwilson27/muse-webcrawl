George Harrison Shull (1874–1954) was an American botanist and geneticist whose experiments with maize at Cold Spring Harbor laid the scientific foundations of hybrid corn, one of the most consequential agricultural innovations of the twentieth century.

Maize experiments

Working from 1905 at the Station for Experimental Evolution in Cold Spring Harbor, New York, Shull inbred maize lines for successive generations, observing that the plants became progressively weaker and more uniform — the phenomenon of inbreeding depression. When two such inbred lines were crossed, however, the hybrid offspring were strikingly vigorous and high-yielding. His papers of 1908 and 1909 described both the method and the effect.

Heterosis

In 1914 Shull proposed the term heterosis for the increased vigour of hybrids, deliberately avoiding a commitment to any particular genetic mechanism. Although Shull's single-cross method was initially impractical for commercial seed production, it was adapted by Donald F. Jones and others into double-cross hybrids, and hybrid maize transformed American agriculture from the 1930s onward.

Later career

Shull became professor of botany and genetics at Princeton University in 1915. He founded the journal Genetics in 1916 and served as its first editor. He was elected to the National Academy of Sciences and received numerous honours for his contributions to genetics.
