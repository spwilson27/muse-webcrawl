Taishō period

The Taishō period (Japanese: 大正時代, Taishō jidai) is a period in the history of Japan dating from 30 July 1912 to 25 December 1926, coinciding with the reign of Emperor Taishō. It followed the Meiji period and preceded the Shōwa period. The period is often associated with "Taishō democracy", a flourishing of liberal and democratic movements in Japanese politics and culture.

Background

Emperor Taishō (Yoshihito) succeeded his father, Emperor Meiji, in 1912. Unlike his father, Taishō suffered from chronic ill health, and political power shifted from the oligarchic genrō toward the Imperial Diet and the democratic parties. Prince Katsura Tarō's attempt to rule without party support triggered the Taishō Political Crisis of 1912–13, an early milestone of the era's mass politics.

First World War

Japan entered the First World War on the side of the Allies in 1914, seizing German possessions in Shandong and the Pacific. The wartime boom greatly expanded Japanese industry and exports, though inflation sparked the Rice Riots of 1918, which helped bring the "commoner prime minister" Hara Takashi to power as the first party cabinet leader.

Taishō democracy

The 1920s saw the rise of party cabinets, universal male suffrage (enacted in 1925), labour unions, and a vibrant urban consumer culture. At the same time, the government passed the Peace Preservation Law of 1925, which suppressed radical movements. The Great Kantō earthquake of 1923 devastated Tokyo and Yokohama, killing over 100,000 people.

End of the period

Emperor Taishō died on 25 December 1926, and his son Hirohito ascended the throne, beginning the Shōwa period. The liberal currents of Taishō democracy weakened in the early 1930s amid economic depression and rising militarism.
