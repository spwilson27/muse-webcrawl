Sir Ernst Boris Chain (19 June 1906 – 12 August 1979) was a German-born British biochemist who shared the 1945 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine with Alexander Fleming and Howard Florey for the discovery of penicillin and its curative effect in various infectious diseases. Chain's biochemical work turned Fleming's laboratory observation into a practical drug.

Life and career

Chain was born in Berlin to a Russian-Jewish chemical manufacturer. He studied chemistry at the Friedrich Wilhelm University, graduating in 1930, but left Germany after the Nazi seizure of power in 1933. In England he worked at Cambridge and then, from 1935, at the Sir William Dunn School of Pathology at Oxford under Howard Florey, becoming a British citizen in 1939.

Surveying the literature on antimicrobial substances in 1938, Chain and Florey chose penicillin — described by Fleming in 1929 — for systematic study. Chain devised methods to extract, concentrate, and assay the unstable substance, worked out its basic chemical character, and with Norman Heatley and Edward Abraham produced enough material for the landmark Oxford trials of 1940–41, which showed dramatic curative effects in mice and in the first human patients. Chain was also an early advocate of large-scale fermentation production and of chemical work on penicillin's structure, carried through by Dorothy Hodgkin's crystallography.

After the war, disappointed by the failure to patent penicillin in Britain, Chain moved to Rome in 1948 to direct a new international centre for chemical microbiology at the Istituto Superiore di Sanità. He returned to Britain in 1961 as professor of biochemistry at Imperial College London, where he built up a major department. He was knighted in 1969. Chain also conducted important work on snake venoms, lysozyme, and insulin action.
