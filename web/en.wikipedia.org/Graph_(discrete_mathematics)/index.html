Graph (discrete mathematics)

[ Image placeholder: A drawing of a graph with six vertices and seven edges ]

In discrete mathematics, and more specifically in graph theory, a graph is a structure amounting to a set of objects in which some pairs of the objects are in some sense "related". The objects correspond to mathematical abstractions called vertices (also called nodes or points) and each of the related pairs of vertices is called an edge (also called a link or line). Typically, a graph is depicted in diagrammatic form as a set of dots joined by lines or curves.

Definitions

Formally, a graph is an ordered pair G = (V, E) of a set V of vertices and a set E of edges, each edge being associated with one or two vertices (its endpoints). In an undirected graph edges link two vertices symmetrically; in a directed graph (digraph) edges have a direction from one vertex to another. Vertices connected by an edge are called adjacent, and the number of edges incident to a vertex is its degree.

History

Graph theory began with Leonhard Euler's 1736 solution of the Seven Bridges of Königsberg problem. Graphs are now fundamental tools in computer science, network analysis, biology, linguistics and operations research, modelling everything from road networks to molecular structures.

Types and concepts

Important special cases include trees (connected acyclic graphs), complete graphs, bipartite graphs, planar graphs and weighted graphs. Core problems include finding shortest paths, traversals such as depth-first and breadth-first search, graph colouring and connectivity.
