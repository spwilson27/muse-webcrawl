Río de la Plata

The Río de la Plata (Spanish for "River of Silver"), called the River Plate in British English, is the estuary formed by the confluence of the Paraná and Uruguay rivers on the southeastern coast of South America. It flows into the Atlantic Ocean between Argentina to the southwest and Uruguay to the northeast, and at about 220 kilometres (140 mi) wide at its mouth is often cited as the widest river estuary in the world.

Geography

The estuary begins at the Paraná Delta near the Argentine city of La Plata and widens progressively toward the Atlantic. Its waters are characteristically brown and silty owing to the enormous sediment load carried by the Paraná. The northwestern portion is fresh water, grading to brackish and marine conditions at the mouth. Its shores host the capitals Buenos Aires (Argentina) and Montevideo (Uruguay), facing each other across the widest part of the estuary.

History

The first European to reach the estuary was Juan Díaz de Solís in 1516, who was killed by the indigenous Charrúa. Ferdinand Magellan passed through in 1520, and Sebastian Cabot explored it in the 1520s, seeking a rumoured "Sierra de la Plata" (Mountain of Silver) that gave the river its name. Buenos Aires was founded on its southern shore, first in 1536 and permanently in 1580. The estuary saw the first naval battle of the Second World War — the Battle of the River Plate in December 1939, ending with the scuttling of the German pocket battleship Admiral Graf Spee off Montevideo.

Ecology

The estuary supports rich fisheries and is home to the La Plata dolphin (franciscana), one of the smallest dolphins in the world. Its wetlands and the Samborombón Bay are important habitats for migratory birds.
