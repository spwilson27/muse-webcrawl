Carter v Canada (Attorney General)

CourtSupreme Court of Canada
Decided6 February 2015
Citation2015 SCC 5
IssueConstitutionality of the prohibition on assisted dying
ResultProhibition struck down in part; unanimous (per curiam)

Carter v Canada (Attorney General), 2015 SCC 5, is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of Canada in which the Court unanimously struck down the federal prohibition on physician-assisted dying for competent adults suffering from grievous and irremediable medical conditions, holding that it violated section 7 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms.

The Supreme Court of Canada, which decided Carter in 2015.

Background

The case was brought on behalf of Gloria Taylor, who suffered from amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS), and Lee Carter and Hollis Johnson, who had accompanied Carter's mother to an assisted-dying clinic in Switzerland. The British Columbia Civil Liberties Association supported the challenge. At trial in the Supreme Court of British Columbia, Justice Lynn Smith ruled the prohibition unconstitutional; the B.C. Court of Appeal reversed, bound by the Supreme Court's earlier 1993 decision in Rodriguez v British Columbia.

Decision

In an unsigned per curiam judgment, the Court held that the blanket prohibition deprived competent adults of life, liberty, and security of the person in a manner that was overbroad and grossly disproportionate: it forced some people to take their own lives prematurely, while others endured intolerable suffering. The Court declared the prohibition void insofar as it applies to a competent adult person who clearly consents to the termination of life and has a grievous and irremediable medical condition causing enduring and intolerable suffering, and suspended the declaration for 12 months (later extended by four months) for Parliament to respond.

Aftermath

Parliament responded in June 2016 with Bill C-14, which created a regulated regime of medical assistance in dying (MAID) for adults whose natural death was reasonably foreseeable. Further litigation and Bill C-7 (2021) expanded eligibility. Carter remains one of the Court's most significant Charter decisions.
