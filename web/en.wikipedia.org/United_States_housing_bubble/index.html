United States housing bubble

The United States housing bubble was a real estate bubble affecting over half of the U.S. states. It was the impetus for the subprime mortgage crisis. Housing prices peaked in early 2006 and started to decline in 2006 and 2007; by 2008, prices had fallen sharply, triggering a wave of mortgage delinquencies and foreclosures that cascaded into the global financial crisis of 2007–2008.

PeriodInflation c. 1997–2006; deflation 2006–2012
PeakEarly 2006 (Case–Shiller Index)
Key factorsLow interest rates, lax lending standards, subprime mortgages, securitization
ConsequencesGreat Recession, foreclosure crisis
Rapid suburban construction accompanied the run-up in prices.

Causes

Multiple factors inflated the bubble: historically low interest rates after the dot-com bust, abundant global capital, government policies promoting homeownership, and the widespread origination of subprime and adjustable-rate mortgages with minimal documentation. Mortgages were bundled into mortgage-backed securities and collateralized debt obligations whose risks were underestimated by rating agencies and investors. Land speculation was especially intense in Arizona, California, Florida, and Nevada, the "sand states."

Collapse

As prices stalled and interest rates reset higher, defaults surged. Housing starts and sales fell, inventory mounted, and by September 2008 the failure of Lehman Brothers turned the housing downturn into a systemic banking panic. Unemployment peaked at 10 percent in October 2009 during the ensuing Great Recession, millions of homes entered foreclosure, and national home prices declined by roughly a third from peak to trough before stabilizing in 2012. Reforms such as the Dodd–Frank Act of 2010 sought to tighten lending standards and oversight.
