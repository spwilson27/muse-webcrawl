Franz Clemens Honoratus Hermann Brentano (January 16, 1838 – March 17, 1917) was a German philosopher, psychologist, and former Catholic priest whose work profoundly influenced phenomenology and analytic philosophy. His best-known contribution is the revival of the concept of intentionality — the directedness of mental states toward objects — as the defining characteristic of the mental.

Life

Brentano, a nephew of the poet Clemens Brentano, studied philosophy at Munich, Würzburg, Berlin, and Münster, earning his doctorate in 1862. Ordained a priest in 1864, he taught at Würzburg, but left the priesthood and his chair in the 1870s after rejecting the newly proclaimed dogma of papal infallibility. From 1874 he taught at the University of Vienna, where his lectures attracted a remarkable generation of students. He spent his final years in Italy and Switzerland, continuing to write despite failing eyesight.

Philosophy

In his major work, Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint (1874), Brentano argued that mental phenomena are distinguished from physical phenomena by their "intentional inexistence" of an object — every thought is a thought of something, every desire a desire for something. He divided mental phenomena into presentations, judgements, and phenomena of love and hate. His descriptive psychology, emphasis on rigorous method, and school of students — including Husserl, Meinong, Twardowski, and Stumpf — seeded both phenomenology and the Lvov–Warsaw school of logic.
