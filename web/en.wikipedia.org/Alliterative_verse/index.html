Alliterative verse

The opening of Beowulf, the most famous alliterative poem in Old English.

Alliterative verse is a form of poetry that uses alliteration as the principal device to bind lines together, in place of the rhyme or syllable-counting metres of later traditions. It was the standard verse form of the early Germanic languages — Old English, Old Norse, Old Saxon and Old High German — and enjoyed a revival in Middle English during the fourteenth century.

Structure

The classic alliterative line is divided by a caesura into two half-lines, each containing two stressed syllables (lifts). In the first half-line, one or both lifts alliterate; in the second half-line, the first lift alliterates with those of the first half, while the final lift does not. Unstressed syllables (dips) vary more freely in number.

Old English and Old Norse forms

The Old English epic Beowulf (3,182 lines) is the best-known example, alongside poems such as The Battle of Maldon and Judith. Old Norse poetry used related metres, including fornyrðislag and the more compressed ljóðaháttr, in the poems of the Poetic Edda.

The Alliterative Revival

After the Norman Conquest, alliterative verse survived in the west and north of England and flowered again in the fourteenth century in works such as William Langland's Piers Plowman, the anonymous Sir Gawain and the Green Knight, and The Parlement of the Thre Ages. Scholars debate whether this was a true revival or an unbroken regional tradition.

Modern use

J. R. R. Tolkien wrote modern alliterative verse, including his version of Sir Gawain and The Fall of Arthur, and W. H. Auden and Ezra Pound experimented with the form in the twentieth century.
