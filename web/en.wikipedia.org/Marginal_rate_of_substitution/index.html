Marginal rate of substitution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

[Image: Indifference curves with a tangent budget line]

In economics, the marginal rate of substitution (MRS) is the rate at which a consumer is willing to give up some amount of one good in exchange for another good while maintaining the same level of utility. Geometrically it equals the (absolute value of the) slope of the consumer's indifference curve at a given bundle.

Definition

For goods X and Y, the MRS of X for Y is the amount of Y the consumer will surrender for one more unit of X with no change in satisfaction. In calculus terms it equals the ratio of marginal utilities: MRS = MUX / MUY. At an interior optimum with a budget constraint, the MRS equals the price ratio, PX / PY, so the indifference curve is tangent to the budget line.

Diminishing MRS

Standard preferences assume a diminishing marginal rate of substitution: as a consumer has more of good X, they value additional units of X less relative to Y, making indifference curves convex to the origin. Perfect substitutes have a constant MRS (straight-line indifference curves), while perfect complements have L-shaped curves.

Related concepts

The analogous concept on the production side is the marginal rate of technical substitution (MRTS), the slope of an isoquant.
