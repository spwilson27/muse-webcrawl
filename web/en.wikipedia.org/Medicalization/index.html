Medicalization

Medicalization is the process by which human conditions and problems come to be defined and treated as medical conditions, and thus become the subject of medical study, diagnosis, prevention, or treatment. Medicalization can be driven by new evidence or theories about a condition; by changing social attitudes; by economic interests; or by the expansion of the medical profession's jurisdiction. The concept was developed by sociologists such as Irving Zola, Ivan Illich and Peter Conrad.

Examples

Conditions whose medical framing has expanded include alcoholism, attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, menopause, childbirth, baldness, shyness (social anxiety disorder), and erectile dysfunction. Critics argue that medicalization can turn ordinary life problems into disorders, promote pharmaceutical solutions ("disease mongering"), and shift social control to medicine; supporters note it can legitimate suffering, unlock treatment and insurance coverage, and remove moral stigma. Demedicalization — the reverse process — occurred for homosexuality, removed from the DSM in 1973.

Related concepts

Medicalization is connected with pharmaceuticalization, geneticization, and the broader sociology of diagnosis. It overlaps with debates about overdiagnosis and the expansion of diagnostic categories in successive editions of the DSM and ICD.
