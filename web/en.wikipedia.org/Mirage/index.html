Mirage

A mirage is a naturally occurring optical phenomenon in which light rays bend via refraction to produce a displaced image of distant objects or the sky. Unlike an hallucination, a mirage is a real optical phenomenon that can be photographed: the observer sees light that has genuinely arrived along a curved path, and interprets it as coming from a different location.

Types

An inferior mirage occurs when the surface — desert sand or a sun-heated road — is much hotter than the air above, so light rays curve upward and the observer sees a shimmering image below the object, most famously the illusion of water on a dry road. A superior mirage occurs when colder air lies beneath warmer air, as over polar seas or ice; objects then appear above their true position, sometimes stretched or stacked in the complex, rapidly changing form known as a Fata Morgana.

Explanation

The cause is the temperature dependence of air's refractive index. Light passing through layers of different temperature bends toward the denser (cooler) air; when the bending becomes extreme, rays undergo total internal reflection within the layer. The brain, assuming light travels in straight lines, projects the image along the line of sight — hence ships appearing to float above the horizon or phantom lakes appearing in the desert.
