Entailment (linguistics)

In linguistics, entailment is a relation between statements in which the truth of one guarantees the truth of another: a sentence p entails a sentence q if, whenever p is true, q must also be true. For example, "John killed the spider" entails "The spider died," and "Mary is a sister of Tom" entails "Mary is female." Entailment is a central notion in semantics and the philosophy of language, distinguished from presupposition and conversational implicature.

Properties

Entailments hold in all circumstances in which the entailing sentence is true; they cannot be cancelled without contradiction ("John killed the spider, but the spider didn't die" is contradictory). Entailment is closely tied to negation: p entails q exactly when p is inconsistent with the negation of q. Sets of lexical relations such as hyponymy generate entailments: "It is a poodle" entails "It is a dog."

Entailment versus other inferences

Unlike conversational implicatures, which depend on context and the cooperative principle and can be cancelled ("Some of the students passed — indeed, all of them"), entailments are purely truth-conditional. Presuppositions differ because they are typically preserved under negation: "John stopped smoking" both entails and presupposes that John once smoked, but under negation ("John didn't stop smoking") only the presupposition survives. Natural language inference (NLI), a task in computational linguistics, asks systems to classify sentence pairs as entailment, contradiction, or neutral.
