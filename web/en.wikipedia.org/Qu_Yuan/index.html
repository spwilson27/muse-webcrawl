Qu Yuan (c. 340–278 BC) was a Chinese poet and aristocrat of the state of Chu during the Warring States period. He is known for his patriotism and for his contributions to classical Chinese poetry, particularly the verses collected in the Chu Ci anthology. His most famous work is the long political allegory Li Sao ("On Encountering Sorrow").

Tradition holds that Qu Yuan drowned himself in the Miluo River after the Chu capital fell to the Qin general Bai Qi in 278 BC. His death is commemorated annually by the Dragon Boat Festival (Duanwu Festival), during which dragon boat races are held and glutinous rice dumplings called zongzi are eaten.

Biography

The main source for Qu Yuan's life is the biography written by the Han dynasty historian Sima Qian in the Records of the Grand Historian (Shiji), composed roughly a century and a half after Qu Yuan's death. According to Sima Qian, Qu Yuan was a member of the Chu royal clan and served King Huai of Chu as a trusted minister, holding the office of zuotu (left minister).

Qu Yuan advocated a policy of alliance with the other states against the expansionist Qin. However, he fell victim to slander by corrupt courtiers and jealous officials, and King Huai banished him from the capital. Qu Yuan spent his exile wandering the countryside south of the Yangtze River, composing poetry expressing his loyalty, his grief at his country's decline, and his longing for a virtuous ruler.

Death and the Dragon Boat Festival

In 278 BC, the Qin general Bai Qi captured Ying, the capital of Chu. On hearing the news, Qu Yuan, in despair, clasped a large stone and drowned himself in the Miluo River in what is now Hunan province. According to legend, local people raced out in boats to try to save him or to recover his body, beating drums and splashing the water to frighten away fish and evil spirits. They also threw rice wrapped in leaves into the river so that the fish would eat the rice rather than the poet's body. These customs are said to be the origin of dragon boat racing and the eating of zongzi during the Duanwu Festival, held on the fifth day of the fifth month of the Chinese lunar calendar.

Works

Qu Yuan is regarded as the first major Chinese poet whose name is known. The Chu Ci ("Songs of Chu") anthology, compiled by Wang Yi in the second century AD, attributes numerous poems to him, though the authorship of some pieces is debated. Works traditionally attributed to Qu Yuan include:

  • Li Sao ("On Encountering Sorrow"), a long autobiographical and allegorical poem of 373 lines
  • Jiu Ge ("Nine Songs"), a cycle of shamanistic hymns
  • Tian Wen ("Questions to Heaven"), a series of cosmological and mythological questions in verse
  • Jiu Zhang ("Nine Pieces")
  • Yuan You ("Far-off Journey")
  • Bu Ju ("Divination") and Yu Fu ("The Fisherman"), prose dialogues
  • Zhao Hun ("Summons of the Soul")

Legacy

Qu Yuan became an enduring symbol of the loyal minister and patriotic poet in Chinese culture. His image as a man of integrity who chose death over compromising with corruption has been celebrated for more than two thousand years. In 1953, the World Peace Council named him one of four world cultural celebrities commemorated that year. In 2009, UNESCO added the Dragon Boat Festival to its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, the first Chinese festival so honoured.
