Robert Moses (December 18, 1888 – July 29, 1981) was an American urban planner and public official who built much of New York City's and the surrounding region's modern physical infrastructure. Sometimes called the "master builder" of mid-20th-century New York, he held as many as twelve official titles simultaneously — none of them elected — and used public authorities' toll revenues to finance hundreds of miles of parkways, dozens of bridges and tunnels, state parks, beaches, housing projects and the sites of two World's Fairs.

Career

After a doctorate from Columbia and work as a reform-minded civil servant, Moses rose under Governor Al Smith, building Long Island's state parks and the first American parkways in the 1920s. Under Mayor Fiorello La Guardia and a succession of governors, he reshaped New York City: the Triborough Bridge, Verrazzano-Narrows Bridge, Henry Hudson and Cross Bronx Expressways, Jones Beach State Park, Shea Stadium, Lincoln Center (built under his urban renewal programmes) and the 1964 World's Fair all bear his imprint. He also directed vast "slum clearance" projects that displaced hundreds of thousands of residents, disproportionately Black and Puerto Rican families.

Criticism and legacy

Moses's car-centred expressways, built through dense neighbourhoods while mass transit starved, sparked famous citizen revolts — most notably Jane Jacobs's campaign that killed the Lower Manhattan Expressway. Robert Caro's Pulitzer Prize-winning biography The Power Broker (1974) cemented a darker view of Moses as a racist and democratically unaccountable autocrat; later scholars have partly reassessed his record, noting his parks, playgrounds and pools, while the debate over his legacy continues to shape arguments about cities and infrastructure.
