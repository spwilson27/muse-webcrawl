The genetic code is the set of rules used by living cells to translate information encoded within genetic material (DNA or RNA sequences of nucleotide triplets, or codons) into proteins. Translation is accomplished by the ribosome, which links amino acids in an order specified by messenger RNA (mRNA), using transfer RNA (tRNA) molecules to carry amino acids and to read the mRNA three nucleotides at a time. The genetic code is nearly universal, shared with only minor variations by almost all organisms from bacteria to humans.

The standard code

There are 64 possible codons: 61 specify amino acids (sense codons) and three — UAA, UAG and UGA — are stop codons that terminate translation. AUG, specifying methionine, also serves as the usual start codon. The code is degenerate: most amino acids are encoded by more than one codon (leucine, serine and arginine each have six), with synonymous codons typically differing at the third position, which the wobble hypothesis of Francis Crick explains through flexible base pairing at the tRNA anticodon.

History

After the double-helix structure of DNA (1953), George Gamow proposed triplet codes; the code was cracked experimentally in 1961 when Marshall Nirenberg and Heinrich Matthaei showed that poly-U RNA directs synthesis of polyphenylalanine, and by 1966 the work of Nirenberg, Har Gobind Khorana and others had assigned all 64 codons — earning Nirenberg, Khorana and Robert Holley the 1968 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Variations and expansion

Variant codes occur in mitochondria (where, for example, UGA codes for tryptophan instead of stop) and in some ciliates and bacteria. Two additional amino acids, selenocysteine and pyrrolysine, are inserted at recoded stop codons in many organisms. Genetic engineers have further expanded the code with synthetic codons and unnatural amino acids, and have compressed it in synthetic genomes such as the recoded E. coli Syn61.
