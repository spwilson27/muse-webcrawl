1918 flu pandemic

The 1918–1920 flu pandemic, often misleadingly called the Spanish flu, was a devastating global influenza pandemic caused by an H1N1 subtype of influenza A virus. It infected an estimated 500 million people — about a third of the world's population at the time — and killed between 17 million and 50 million people, with some estimates considerably higher, making it one of the deadliest pandemics in history.

Course of the pandemic

The pandemic occurred in several waves. A first, relatively mild wave appeared in spring 1918 — early outbreaks were recorded at Camp Funston, Kansas, in March 1918, though the geographic origin remains debated. A far deadlier second wave swept the world from August to December 1918, spread by the movement of troops during the First World War. Third and fourth waves followed in 1919 and 1920 before the virus subsided, its descendants circulating as seasonal influenza for decades.

Unusual mortality

Unlike most influenza outbreaks, which kill mainly the very young and the very old, the 1918 pandemic caused unusually high mortality among healthy adults aged 20–40. Many deaths resulted from bacterial pneumonia following the viral infection; in some patients an overreaction of the immune system, a "cytokine storm," may have caused rapid death. The pandemic's "W-shaped" mortality curve is a lasting subject of epidemiological study.

Name and legacy

The name "Spanish flu" arose because wartime censorship in the belligerent countries suppressed news of the disease, while neutral Spain's press reported on it freely, including the illness of King Alfonso XIII. The pandemic overwhelmed health systems, prompted mask mandates and closures in many cities, and remains the benchmark against which later influenza pandemics — including those of 1957, 1968 and 2009 — are compared. Scientists reconstructed the 1918 virus's genome from preserved tissue samples in the 2000s.
