NAIRU

NAIRU is the acronym for non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment. It is the level of unemployment at which the rate of inflation does not change — that is, the unemployment rate consistent with stable inflation. When unemployment falls below the NAIRU, inflation tends to accelerate as employers bid up wages to attract scarce workers; when unemployment rises above it, inflation tends to decelerate. The concept is closely related to the "natural rate of unemployment" proposed by Milton Friedman and Edmund Phelps in the late 1960s, and to the expectations-augmented Phillips curve.

The NAIRU marks the unemployment rate at which inflation is stable.

History

The term NAIRU was introduced in the 1970s by Franco Modigliani and Lucas Papademos (as "NIRU", the non-inflationary rate of unemployment) and later popularized in its present form by economists including James Tobin. It arose as economists sought to reconcile the original Phillips curve trade-off between unemployment and inflation with the experience of stagflation, in which high inflation and high unemployment occurred together. In the expectations-augmented framework, any short-run trade-off depends on inflation being higher or lower than expected; in the long run the Phillips curve is vertical at the NAIRU.

Policy use

Central banks and fiscal authorities use estimates of the NAIRU to judge how much slack exists in the labor market. If actual unemployment is above the estimated NAIRU, monetary policy can generally be more expansionary without risking inflation. The difficulty is that the NAIRU is not directly observable and must be estimated with considerable uncertainty; estimates for the United States have commonly ranged between about 4 and 6 percent. The NAIRU can also shift over time with changes in labor market institutions, demographics, and productivity growth.

Criticism

Critics note the wide confidence bands around NAIRU estimates and argue that relying on them can lead to policy errors. The concept of hysteresis suggests that long periods of high unemployment may themselves raise the NAIRU by eroding workers' skills and attachment to the labor force.
