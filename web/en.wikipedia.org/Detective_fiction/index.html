Detective fiction

Detective fiction is a subgenre of crime fiction and mystery fiction in which an investigator — usually a detective, professional, amateur or retired — investigates a crime, typically a murder. The reader is invited to follow the clues and identify the perpetrator before or as the detective does. It is generally credited with beginning in 1841 with Edgar Allan Poe's "The Murders in the Rue Morgue", whose C. Auguste Dupin became the model for generations of fictional detectives.

History

Poe's Dupin stories, and elements in earlier works such as Voltaire's Zadig and E. T. A. Hoffmann's tales, established the rational amateur who out-thinks the police. Émile Gaboriau's Monsieur Lecoq and Wilkie Collins's The Moonstone (1868) extended the form, but the genre was transformed by Arthur Conan Doyle's Sherlock Holmes, whose adventures from 1887 onward fixed the detective-and-sidekick pattern. Between the wars came the "Golden Age" of the fair-play whodunit — Agatha Christie, Dorothy L. Sayers, G. K. Chesterton and others — governed by informal rules such as Ronald Knox's "Ten Commandments" that guaranteed the reader a fair chance. In parallel, American writers of the hardboiled school, including Dashiell Hammett and Raymond Chandler, moved the genre to the streets, with private eyes negotiating corrupt cities.

Subgenres

Major subgenres include the whodunit, the locked-room mystery, the police procedural (pioneered by Ed McBain's 87th Precinct novels), the cozy mystery, the inverted detective story or "howcatchem" (exemplified on television by Columbo), historical detective fiction, and legal and forensic thrillers. The detective novel also flourished outside the Anglophone world, from Georges Simenon's Maigret novels in French to Japanese honkaku mysteries and contemporary Scandinavian noir.

Conventions

Recurring devices include the brilliant eccentric detective, the less acute companion-narrator, the gathering of suspects for the final revelation, red herrings, and the closed circle of suspects. Modern criticism examines how the genre encodes attitudes to reason, order, class and justice.
