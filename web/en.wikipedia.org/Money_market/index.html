Money market

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The money market is a component of the economy that provides short-term funds, dealing in assets with maturities of one year or less. It enables governments, banks, corporations, and other large institutions to borrow and lend on a short-term basis and to manage their liquidity, and it is the market through which much of a central bank's monetary policy is transmitted.

Instruments

Common money market instruments include Treasury bills issued by governments, certificates of deposit issued by banks, commercial paper issued by companies, repurchase agreements (repos), in which securities are sold with an agreement to buy them back shortly afterward, and interbank lending such as federal funds in the United States. Instruments are usually sold in large denominations, trade over the counter rather than on exchanges, and carry low default risk and low returns.

Participants and functions

Participation is dominated by institutional investors. Money market funds pool the cash of smaller investors and lend it in this market. For treasurers, the money market serves as a parking place for surplus cash; for borrowers it fills seasonal or working-capital needs. Because the market underlies so much short-term funding, stress in it — as in September 2008, when a major fund "broke the buck" after Lehman Brothers failed — can rapidly become a systemic financial crisis, prompting central bank intervention.
