Texture (music)

Musical texture
Main typesMonophonic, homophonic, polyphonic, heterophonic
ConcernsNumber and relationship of simultaneous musical lines

In music, texture describes how melodic, rhythmic, and harmonic materials are combined in a composition, and thus the overall quality of the sound. It is determined by how many layers of sound are heard at once and how those layers relate to one another.

Types of texture

Monophony is a single melodic line without accompaniment, as in Gregorian chant or a lone flute solo; several voices singing the same line in unison remain monophonic. Homophony features one predominant melody supported by chordal accompaniment, the most common texture in Western popular music and much Classical-era music; the closely related homorhythmic texture has all voices moving in the same rhythm, as in a hymn. Polyphony combines two or more independent melodic lines simultaneously, as in a Renaissance motet or a Bach fugue, and is studied as counterpoint. Heterophony, found in many non-Western traditions, presents simultaneous variants of a single melody.

Description and analysis

Analysts also describe textures as thick or thin, light or heavy, depending on the number of parts, their spacing, and their registers. Composers vary texture within a work to shape form and expression — for instance, thinning to a solo line for intimacy or building to full orchestral polyphony at a climax.
