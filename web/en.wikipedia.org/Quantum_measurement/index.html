Quantum measurement

In quantum mechanics, quantum measurement is the testing or manipulation of a physical system to yield a numerical result. A fundamental feature of quantum theory is that the predictions it makes are generally probabilistic. The mathematical tools for making predictions about what measurement outcomes may occur were developed during the 20th century and make use of linear algebra and functional analysis.


Measuring a quantum system

Quantum measurement is an important topic in the interpretations of quantum mechanics, where it has given rise to the measurement problem: the question of how (or whether) the wave function collapses and a definite outcome occurs. In the conventional Copenhagen interpretation, measurement causes the instantaneous reduction of the wave function to one of the possible states of the system.

Mathematical formulation

In quantum mechanics, a measurement on a system in a state described by a density matrix is modelled by a set of observables or, more generally, by a positive operator-valued measure (POVM). The probability of obtaining a given outcome is given by the Born rule, according to which the probability is the squared modulus of the inner product of the state with the relevant eigenstate, for a pure state undergoing a projective measurement.

A measurement that projects the state onto an eigenbasis of a self-adjoint operator is called a projective or von Neumann measurement. More general measurement schemes, in which the measured system interacts with an ancilla that is subsequently measured projectively, are described by completely positive maps and the formalism of quantum operations.

Weak and continuous measurement

A weak measurement extracts only partial information about an observable, disturbing the system correspondingly less, and repeated weak measurements can track the gradual evolution of a quantum state. Continuous measurement theory describes systems monitored over extended periods, with applications in quantum optics, feedback control of quantum devices, and the readout of superconducting qubits.
