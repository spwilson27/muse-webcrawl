History of the Jews in Iraq


The shrine of the prophet Ezekiel, a pilgrimage site for Iraqi Jews

The history of the Jews in Iraq is one of the oldest of the Jewish diaspora, beginning with the Babylonian captivity in the 6th century BCE. For more than two and a half millennia, the Jewish community of Mesopotamia — "Babylonia" — produced the Babylonian Talmud, led world Jewry through the Geonim, and formed a major element of Baghdad's population. The community effectively ended with the mass emigration of 1950–52 (Operation Ezra and Nehemiah), which airlifted some 120,000 Iraqi Jews to Israel.

Babylonian exile and the Talmud

Nebuchadnezzar II deported thousands of Judahites to Babylon after conquering Jerusalem in 597 and 586 BCE. Many remained even after Cyrus the Great permitted return in 538 BCE. Under the Parthian and Sasanian empires, Babylonian Jewry flourished under its own leader, the Exilarch (Resh Galuta). The academies of Sura, Pumbedita and Nehardea produced the Babylonian Talmud, redacted around 500 CE, which became the central text of rabbinic Judaism. The community's scholars, the Geonim (7th–11th centuries), answered legal questions from across the Jewish world.

Islamic period

Under the Abbasid Caliphate, whose capital Baghdad was founded in 762, Jews participated as merchants, physicians and financiers; Saadia Gaon (10th century) translated the Bible into Arabic and wrote foundational philosophy. The Mongol sack of Baghdad in 1258 devastated the community. Under Ottoman rule (from 1534) conditions varied; by the 19th century Baghdad's Jews dominated much of the city's commerce, and families such as the Sassoons built trading networks to India and China. The Alliance Israélite Universelle opened schools from 1864, spreading French education.

British mandate and kingdom

After the First World War Iraq became a British mandate. Jews — roughly a quarter to a third of Baghdad's population — served prominently in the new state: Sassoon Eskell became the first finance minister. Iraq gained independence in 1932. However, rising Arab nationalism and Nazi propaganda worsened the climate, culminating in the Farhud, a pogrom in Baghdad on 1–2 June 1941 in which about 180 Jews were killed and much property looted, an event that shattered communal confidence.

Emigration and aftermath

After the 1948 Arab–Israeli War, Zionism was criminalised and Jews faced dismissal, arrests and a public hanging of Jewish businessman Shafiq Ades. In March 1950 Iraq passed a law permitting Jews to emigrate if they renounced citizenship, expecting a limited outflow; instead nearly the entire community registered. By 1952 about 120,000–124,000 had been airlifted to Israel, and a 1951 law froze the assets of those who left. A remnant of a few thousand remained, shrinking through further emigration, especially after the Ba'ath regime's public hangings of nine Jews in Baghdad in 1969. By the early 21st century only a handful of elderly Jews remained in Iraq.

Legacy

Iraqi Jews in Israel preserved distinctive liturgy, music (including the Iraqi maqam tradition) and cuisine. Iraqi-Jewish heritage centres and archives, such as the Iraqi Jewish Archive of seized communal documents, preserve a record of the community's long history.
