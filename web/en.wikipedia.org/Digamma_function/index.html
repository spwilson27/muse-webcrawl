Digamma function

The digamma function ψ(x) for positive real x.

In mathematics, the digamma function, denoted ψ(x), is defined as the logarithmic derivative of the gamma function:

ψ(x) = d/dx ln Γ(x) = Γ′(x)/Γ(x).

It is the first of the polygamma functions and is named after the Greek letter digamma (Ϝ), whose shape resembles two gammas. The digamma function is meromorphic on the complex plane with simple poles at the non-positive integers 0, −1, −2, …

Properties

The function satisfies the recurrence ψ(x + 1) = ψ(x) + 1/x, mirroring the gamma function's recurrence Γ(x + 1) = xΓ(x), and the reflection formula ψ(1 − x) − ψ(x) = π cot(πx). Its values at the positive integers are given by the harmonic numbers: ψ(n) = Hn−1 − γ, where γ ≈ 0.57721 is the Euler–Mascheroni constant; in particular ψ(1) = −γ and ψ(1/2) = −γ − 2 ln 2. It also admits the series representation ψ(x) = −γ + Σn=0∞ [1/(n+1) − 1/(n+x)].

Applications

The digamma function appears throughout analysis and statistics wherever the gamma function is differentiated: in the study of the zeta function and harmonic series; in Bayesian statistics, where it arises in inference for the Dirichlet and gamma distributions (for example in variational methods and topic models); and in physics, in calculations involving the beta function and Feynman diagrams. Derivatives of digamma give the trigamma and higher polygamma functions.
