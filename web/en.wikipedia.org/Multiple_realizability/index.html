Multiple realizability

Multiple realizability is a thesis in the philosophy of mind holding that a single mental state or kind can be realised by many different kinds of physical state or system. It was articulated most influentially by Hilary Putnam in the 1960s and became a central argument for functionalism and against the mind–brain identity theory.

The argument

Putnam argued that pain, for example, is unlikely to correspond to one specific neurochemical state across all creatures that feel it: mammals, octopuses, hypothetical Martians and even machines might each realise pain in radically different physical structures. If mental kinds cross-classify physical kinds in this way, then mental states cannot simply be identified with particular brain states; instead they are better understood by their functional or causal role — what they do, rather than what they are made of. Analogies commonly offered include mousetraps, corkscrews and computers, where the same functional kind is instantiated in diverse materials and mechanisms.

Criticism and influence

Critics such as Jaegwon Kim have argued that multiple realizability does not preclude local, species-specific reductions of mental kinds, and that truly heterogeneous realisations may be rarer than assumed. Nonetheless the thesis profoundly shaped cognitive science and the "special sciences" debate, underpinning the idea that psychology can be autonomous from neuroscience.
