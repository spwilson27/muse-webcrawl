Edward VI

Edward VI (12 October 1537 – 6 July 1553) was King of England and Ireland from 28 January 1547 until his death in 1553. He was crowned at the age of nine, the son of Henry VIII and Jane Seymour. Because he never reached adulthood, England was ruled by a regency council during his reign, led first by his uncle Edward Seymour, Duke of Somerset, and then by John Dudley, Duke of Northumberland.

Reign

Edward was a precociously intelligent, devoutly Protestant boy, educated by reform-minded tutors including Richard Cox and John Cheke. Under his reign the English Reformation advanced decisively: the Book of Common Prayer was introduced in 1549 (and revised in a more Protestant form in 1552), images and altars were removed from churches, clerical celibacy was abolished, and Archbishop Thomas Cranmer's Forty-two Articles laid foundations for Anglican doctrine.

The reign also saw serious social and economic turbulence, including inflation, debasement of the coinage, the Prayer Book Rebellion in the West Country and Kett's Rebellion in Norfolk in 1549 — uprisings suppressed with considerable bloodshed. Somerset's fall that year brought Northumberland to power.

Death and succession

Edward fell ill in early 1553, probably with tuberculosis. Fearing that his Catholic half-sister Mary's accession would undo the Reformation, he drew up a "Devise for the Succession" excluding both Mary and Elizabeth and naming his Protestant cousin Lady Jane Grey. Edward died on 6 July 1553, aged 15. Jane was proclaimed queen but was deposed by Mary after nine days.

Edward's detailed journal of his reign is a valuable historical source. Despite his youth and early death, the religious settlement of his reign shaped the Church of England permanently.
