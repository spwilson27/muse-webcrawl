Celtic polytheism

Celtic polytheism is the religious practice of the ancient Celts, characterised by belief in many deities. It is generally divided into the traditions of the Continental Celts of Gaul and Iberia, known mainly through archaeology and Roman accounts, and the Insular Celts of Britain and Ireland, whose mythology was recorded in medieval manuscripts.

Continental tradition

Inscriptions from the Roman period record hundreds of divine names, many occurring only once, suggesting strong local and tribal cults. Widely attested deities include Lugus, Taranis, Esus, Teutates, Epona, and the mother goddesses known as the Matres or Matronae. Under Roman rule many Celtic gods were paired with Roman equivalents, a process known as interpretatio romana.

Insular tradition

Irish mythology preserves cycles of tales about the Tuatha Dé Danann, a pantheon of gods who became the fairy people of later folklore. Welsh mythology, recorded in the Mabinogion and other texts, features figures such as Lleu Llaw Gyffes, Rhiannon, and Arawn. Scholars debate how far these literary figures reflect pre-Christian gods.

Practice

Worship took place at temples, sacred groves, springs, and river sources. Offerings included weapons, jewellery, animals, and occasionally human sacrifice, which Roman authors emphasised in hostile accounts. The ritual deposition of valuable objects in water continued a tradition reaching back to the Bronze Age.
