Convertible

A convertible or cabriolet is a passenger car that can be driven with or without a roof in place. The methods of retracting and storing the roof vary across manufacturers and eras: roofs may be made of soft vinyl or canvas ("soft tops"), or of rigid metal panels that fold into the trunk ("retractable hardtops"). Most convertibles are two-door body styles, often based on coupé platforms, though four-door examples exist.

History

Most early automobiles were open cars; enclosed bodies became common only in the 1920s, and by the mid-1930s the purpose-built convertible coupé had appeared. After World War II convertibles were marketed as stylish flagship models in the United States. Safety concerns and the spread of air conditioning caused sales to collapse in the 1970s — the 1976 Cadillac Eldorado was promoted as the "last convertible" — but the body style revived in the 1980s with models such as the Chrysler LeBaron and Saab 900 convertible. Modern designs use powered tops, rollover protection systems and improved sealing, though convertibles remain heavier and less rigid than equivalent coupés.

Variants

Related body styles include the roadster (a spartan two-seat open car), the targa top (with a removable panel over the front seats and a fixed roll bar), and the landaulet. Retractable hardtops offer coupé-like refinement but add weight and reduce luggage space.
