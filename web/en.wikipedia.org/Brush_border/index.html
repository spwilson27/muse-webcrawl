Brush border

StructureDense microvilli on epithelial cell apices
Found inSmall intestine, proximal tubule of kidney
FunctionAbsorption; surface-area amplification

A brush border is a dense covering of microvilli on the apical surface of certain epithelial cells, so named because under the light microscope the tightly packed projections resemble the bristles of a brush. Brush borders greatly increase the surface area available for absorption and are a hallmark of the lining of the small intestine and the proximal convoluted tubule of the kidney.

Structure

Each microvillus is a finger-like projection of the plasma membrane about 1 micrometre long, supported by a core of actin filaments anchored to the terminal web of the cell cortex. On intestinal absorptive cells (enterocytes) thousands of microvilli cover each cell, multiplying the surface area many times over the already folded villi and plicae of the gut wall.

Function

The brush border membrane of the intestine carries digestive enzymes—such as lactase, sucrase-isomaltase, and peptidases—that perform the final steps of digestion at the cell surface, together with transporters that absorb sugars, amino acids, fatty acids, ions, and water. In the kidney, the brush border of the proximal tubule reabsorbs the bulk of filtered glucose, amino acids, phosphate, and sodium.

Clinical significance

Damage to the intestinal brush border causes malabsorption: coeliac disease blunts villi and microvilli, and congenital microvillus inclusion disease disrupts microvillus formation in infants. Loss of specific brush border enzymes, such as lactase deficiency, leads to food intolerances.
