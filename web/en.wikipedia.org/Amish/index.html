Amish

The Amish are a group of traditionalist Anabaptist Christian church fellowships known for simple living, plain dress, pacifism, and a reluctance to adopt many conveniences of modern technology. They descend from Swiss German Anabaptists and are named after Jakob Ammann, whose followers split from the Swiss Brethren in 1693 over questions of discipline and the practice of shunning.

History and population

Fleeing persecution in Europe, Amish families settled in Pennsylvania from the 1730s, and later in Ohio, Indiana, and many other states and in Ontario, Canada. Today the Amish population is roughly 400,000 and growing rapidly because of large families and high retention. The largest settlements are in Holmes County, Ohio; Lancaster County, Pennsylvania; and northern Indiana (the "Amish Country" regions).

Beliefs and practices

Amish life is governed by the Ordnung, an unwritten set of local rules covering dress, technology, and behaviour. Adult baptism, non-resistance, and separation from the world are central. Most Old Order Amish forgo grid electricity, car ownership, and telephones in the home, travel by horse and buggy, and speak Pennsylvania German ("Pennsylvania Dutch") at home. Young people experience Rumspringa, a period of relative freedom before deciding whether to join the church. Formal schooling typically ends at eighth grade, a practice upheld by the US Supreme Court in Wisconsin v. Yoder (1972).
