Retroreflector

A retroreflector is an optical device or surface that reflects light back toward its source with minimal scattering, even when the light arrives over a range of angles. Retroreflection makes road signs, bicycle reflectors, survey targets, safety clothing, and spacecraft instruments appear bright to an observer located near the illumination source.

Corner reflectors

A corner-cube retroreflector uses three mutually perpendicular reflecting surfaces. A ray undergoes three reflections and emerges parallel to its incoming direction but displaced laterally. Arrays of small corner cubes are moulded into plastic reflectors. Precision hollow or solid corner cubes are used in interferometry, satellite tracking, and laser ranging.

Glass beads and prismatic sheeting

Microscopic glass beads embedded in a coating focus incident light near a reflective layer and return much of it toward the source. Microprismatic sheeting uses tiny cube corners and generally provides higher brightness and a wider or more controlled viewing geometry. Performance is described by coefficients of retroreflection and depends on entrance and observation angles.

Applications

Retroreflectors are used in traffic signs, lane markers, vehicle plates, marine navigation, runway markings, photogrammetry, and optical surveying. Apollo astronauts left lunar laser ranging arrays on the Moon; observatories still time laser pulses reflected from them to measure the Earth–Moon distance and test gravitation. Satellite laser ranging stations use similar arrays to determine orbits and geodetic positions.

Distinction from mirrors

An ordinary plane mirror returns a beam toward the source only when the beam is perpendicular to it; at other angles, the reflection leaves at an equal opposite angle. A retroreflector reverses the direction of travel over a broader range. Retroreflective material does not create light, so it appears most striking when the observer's eyes are close to a headlamp or other light source.
