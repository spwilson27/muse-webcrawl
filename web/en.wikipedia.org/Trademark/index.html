Trademark

A trademark (also written trade mark) is a sign capable of distinguishing the goods or services of one enterprise from those of others — typically a name, logo, slogan, shape, colour or sound. It is a form of intellectual property that protects consumers from confusion about the source of products and protects the goodwill businesses build in their brands. Registered trademarks are denoted by the ® symbol; unregistered marks may use ™, and service marks ℠.


Trademarks identify the commercial source of goods and services

History

Marks of origin are ancient — potters stamped Roman oil lamps, and medieval guilds required makers' marks — but modern trademark law developed in 19th-century Britain and the United States as mass production and advertising grew. The first registered trademark under the UK's 1875 Act was the Bass red triangle, registered on 1 January 1876.

Registration and rights

Rights arise through use and are strengthened by registration with offices such as the USPTO (under the Lanham Act of 1946), the UK IPO and the EUIPO; the Madrid Protocol enables international filings. A mark must be distinctive: coined words ("Kodak") are strongest, while merely generic terms ("apple" for fruit) cannot be monopolised. The owner may stop others from using identical or confusingly similar marks on related goods. Registration typically lasts ten years and is renewable indefinitely, provided the mark remains in use; marks can become generic (aspirin, escalator) and lose protection. Owners must also police dilution of famous marks and may license or assign their marks.

Infringement and defences

Infringement analysis centres on the likelihood of consumer confusion. Defences and limitations include descriptive fair use (using "apple" to describe fruit), nominative fair use (referring to a product for comparison or repair), and parody or free-speech interests. Counterfeiting — deliberate copying of marks on fake goods — is subject to seizure and criminal penalties in most jurisdictions.
