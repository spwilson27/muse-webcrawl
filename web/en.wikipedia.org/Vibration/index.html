Vibration

Vibration
TypeMechanical oscillation
Key quantitiesFrequency, amplitude, acceleration
Related fieldsAcoustics, structural dynamics, seismology

Vibration is a mechanical phenomenon whereby oscillations occur about an equilibrium point. The oscillations may be periodic, such as the motion of a pendulum or a plucked guitar string, or random, such as a tyre rolling over rough ground. Vibration can be desirable — as in a tuning fork, a reed instrument, or a loudspeaker — or undesirable, wasting energy and producing unwanted sound, fatigue, and structural damage in machines and buildings.

Types

Free vibration occurs when a system, once displaced, oscillates without external input, as a mass on a spring. Forced vibration results from a continuing external force; when the forcing frequency matches the system's natural frequency, resonance occurs and amplitudes can grow dangerously large (the 1940 Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse is a famous, if aerodynamically nuanced, example). Damped vibration loses energy through friction or viscous devices, causing oscillations to decay.

Analysis and control

The simplest model is the spring–mass–damper system governed by a second-order differential equation. Engineers characterise vibration by frequency (in hertz), amplitude, and acceleration (often in g or m/s²), and use spectral (Fourier) analysis to diagnose rotating machinery. Control methods include balancing, isolation mounts, tuned mass dampers (used in skyscrapers), and active cancellation. In humans, excessive hand-arm or whole-body vibration is a recognised occupational hazard.
