The Russian Arctic is the portion of the Arctic region lying within the Russian Federation. Russia is the largest Arctic state: its Arctic coastline stretches for roughly 24,000 kilometres along the Arctic Ocean and its marginal seas — the Barents, White, Kara, Laptev, East Siberian and Chukchi seas — and includes major archipelagos such as Franz Josef Land, Novaya Zemlya, Severnaya Zemlya, the New Siberian Islands and Wrangel Island. The officially defined Arctic Zone of the Russian Federation, expanded by presidential decrees in 2014 and 2019, comprises Murmansk Oblast, the Nenets, Yamalo-Nenets and Chukotka autonomous okrugs, and parts of Arkhangelsk Oblast, Karelia, Komi, Krasnoyarsk Krai and Yakutia (Sakha Republic).

Geography and population

The region is characterised by tundra, permafrost, and long, severe winters. Despite its size it is sparsely populated — about two million people, roughly half of all people living in the world Arctic — concentrated in cities such as Murmansk, Arkhangelsk, Norilsk, Yakutsk and Salekhard. Indigenous peoples of the Russian North include the Nenets, Chukchi, Evenki, Khanty, Mansi, Sami and others, many of whom practise reindeer herding, hunting and fishing.

Resources and shipping

The Russian Arctic holds vast reserves of oil and natural gas, both onshore (for example the Yamal Peninsula fields feeding the Yamal LNG project) and offshore, as well as nickel, apatite, diamonds and other minerals. The Northern Sea Route along the coast, administered with the help of the world's largest icebreaker fleet (including nuclear-powered icebreakers operated by Atomflot), has seen growing cargo traffic as sea ice declines. The region also hosts significant Russian military infrastructure, including the Northern Fleet based on the Kola Peninsula.

Environmental issues

The Arctic is warming several times faster than the global average, causing permafrost thaw that damages buildings and pipelines — a factor in the 2020 Norilsk diesel spill — and raising concerns over the "zombie fires", coastal erosion, and the release of methane and ancient pathogens. Conservation areas such as the Russian Arctic National Park protect polar bears, walrus and seabird colonies.
