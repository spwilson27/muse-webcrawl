Radar is a radiolocation system that uses radio waves to determine the distance (ranging), direction (azimuth and elevation angles), and radial velocity of objects relative to the site. The term is an acronym for Radio Detection and Ranging, coined by the United States Navy in 1940. A radar system consists of a transmitter producing electromagnetic waves in the radio or microwave spectrum, a transmitting antenna, a receiving antenna, a receiver and a processor to interpret the reflected signals.

Principles

A radar transmits pulses or a continuous waveform of radio energy. When the waves strike an object, a small fraction is scattered back toward the receiver. The round-trip travel time gives the range: R = c·t/2, where c is the speed of light. The Doppler shift of the returned signal reveals the target's radial velocity. Antenna beam direction at the moment of reception gives the target's bearing.

History

Early experiments by Heinrich Hertz showed radio waves reflect from metallic objects. Practical systems were developed secretly and in parallel by several nations in the 1930s. Britain's Chain Home network, operational by the Battle of Britain, provided early warning of air raids. The cavity magnetron, developed in Britain in 1940 and shared with the United States, enabled compact centimetric radars that transformed night fighting, anti-submarine warfare and bombing accuracy.

Applications

Modern radar is used in air traffic control, weather forecasting (Doppler weather radar), maritime and automotive navigation, speed enforcement, astronomy (planetary radar), remote sensing and missile guidance. Phased-array radars steer their beams electronically, tracking many targets simultaneously.
