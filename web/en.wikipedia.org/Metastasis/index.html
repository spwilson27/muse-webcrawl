Metastasis

Metastasis is the spread of a pathogenic agent from an initial or primary site to a different or secondary site within the host's body; the term is typically used when referring to metastasis by a cancerous tumor. Newly pathological sites are metastases ("mets"). Metastasis is the principal cause of death from cancer and corresponds to stage IV disease in most staging systems; it is distinguished from direct local invasion of neighbouring tissues.

Mechanism

The metastatic cascade involves local invasion of surrounding tissue, intravasation into blood or lymphatic vessels, survival in the circulation, arrest and extravasation at a distant site, and colonisation — growth of a secondary tumour in the new tissue, supported by angiogenesis. Only a tiny fraction of circulating tumour cells complete the process ("metastatic inefficiency"). Paget's "seed and soil" hypothesis (1889) noted that particular cancers preferentially colonise particular organs, a pattern explained by anatomy of drainage, adhesion molecules and the pre-metastatic niche.

Patterns and sites

Cancers of the gastrointestinal tract drain via the portal vein to the liver; prostate and breast cancers commonly spread to bone; lung and breast cancers may seed the brain. Lymphatic spread first reaches regional (sentinel) lymph nodes. Serous cavity seeding (transcoelomic spread) is typical of ovarian cancer and produces ascites and peritoneal deposits.

Diagnosis and treatment

Metastases are detected by imaging (CT, PET, MRI, bone scan) and confirmed by biopsy; histology generally resembles the primary tumour, aiding identification of cancers of unknown primary. Metastatic disease is usually treated systemically — chemotherapy, endocrine therapy, targeted therapy and immunotherapy — with surgery or radiotherapy reserved for selected oligometastatic or symptomatic cases, and palliative care playing a central role.
