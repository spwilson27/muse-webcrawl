An N95 mask, formally an N95 filtering facepiece respirator, is a particulate-filtering respirator certified by the U.S. National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) to filter at least 95% of airborne particles. The "N" indicates it is not resistant to oil. N95 respirators are widely used in healthcare and construction to protect wearers from dusts, aerosols, and infectious particles.

Design and filtration

N95s are made of layered nonwoven polypropylene fabric. The filter layer carries an electrostatic charge that attracts and traps particles, supplementing mechanical filtration mechanisms such as interception, impaction, and diffusion. Unlike loose surgical masks, N95s are designed to seal tightly to the face; fit testing is required in occupational settings because leakage around the edges undermines protection. Some models include an exhalation valve, which eases breathing but releases unfiltered exhaled air.

History and use

The modern disposable respirator descends from designs developed in the 1970s, including Sara Little Turner's work at 3M. N95 certification was introduced in 1995 when NIOSH updated its respirator regulations. Demand surged during the SARS outbreak, the 2009 H1N1 pandemic, and especially the COVID-19 pandemic, when global shortages led to rationing, extended use, decontamination protocols, and the widespread import of similar respirators such as China's KN95.

Standards and equivalents

Other jurisdictions maintain comparable standards: FFP2 in Europe (≥94%), KN95 in China, P2 in Australia and New Zealand, and KF94 in South Korea. NIOSH maintains a certified equipment list, and counterfeit respirators have been a recurring enforcement concern.
