Otto Diels


Born: 23 January 1876, Hamburg, German Empire
Died: 7 March 1954 (aged 78), Kiel, West Germany
Known for: Diels–Alder reaction
Awards: Nobel Prize in Chemistry (1950)

Otto Paul Hermann Diels (23 January 1876 – 7 March 1954) was a German chemist who shared the 1950 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with his former student Kurt Alder "for their discovery and development of the diene synthesis" — the reaction now universally known as the Diels–Alder reaction, one of the most important methods for forming carbon–carbon bonds in organic synthesis.

Career

Diels studied chemistry at the University of Berlin under Emil Fischer and taught there before becoming professor at the University of Kiel in 1916, where he remained until retirement. His earlier work included the discovery of carbon suboxide (1906) and a selenium-based dehydrogenation method that helped establish the carbon skeleton of steroids — the "Diels hydrocarbon" obtained from cholesterol degradation was a key clue to steroid structure.

The Diels–Alder reaction

In 1928 Diels and Alder published the reaction between a conjugated diene and a dienophile to give a cyclohexene derivative. The reaction's reliability, predictable stereochemistry and atom economy made it a cornerstone of synthesis, used in landmark total syntheses of compounds such as cortisone, morphine-related structures and many polymers and pharmaceuticals. Diels survived the destruction of his Kiel institute by bombing in the Second World War and rebuilt it before his death in 1954.
