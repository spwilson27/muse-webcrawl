Avant-garde jazz

Stylistic origins: Bebop, hard bop, modal jazz, free improvisation

Cultural origins: Late 1950s, United States

Key figures: Ornette Coleman, John Coltrane, Cecil Taylor, Sun Ra, Albert Ayler

Avant-garde jazz is a style of music and improvisation that combines avant-garde art music with elements of jazz. It originated in the late 1950s and developed through the 1960s, as musicians loosened or abandoned the conventions of bebop harmony, fixed chord changes, and regular metre in favour of collective improvisation and new timbres.

History

Pioneers included pianist Cecil Taylor, whose percussive playing drew on modernist classical music, and alto saxophonist Ornette Coleman, whose 1959 album The Shape of Jazz to Come and 1960 double-quartet recording Free Jazz gave the related free jazz movement its name. John Coltrane's late work, including Ascension (1965), and the music of Sun Ra, Albert Ayler, and the members of Chicago's AACM broadened the style's spiritual, political, and sonic range.

Characteristics

Avant-garde jazz often features improvisation outside diatonic harmony, unconventional instrumental techniques and extended sounds, instruments rarely heard in jazz (such as bassoon or cello), and structures borrowed from contemporary classical composition. Although closely related to free jazz, the avant-garde stream more often retains compositional frameworks and pre-planned structures.
