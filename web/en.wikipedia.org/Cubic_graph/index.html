Cubic graph

In the mathematical field of graph theory, a cubic graph is a graph in which every vertex has degree three — that is, a 3-regular graph. Cubic graphs form a rich class of examples and counterexamples in graph theory, including many famous named graphs.

Examples

Well-known cubic graphs include the utility graph K3,3, the cube graph, the Petersen graph, the Heawood graph, the Möbius–Kantor graph, the Pappus graph, and the Desargues graph. The Petersen graph in particular is a classic counterexample: it is non-Hamiltonian, has girth five, and is the smallest snark.

Properties

By the handshaking lemma, a cubic graph must have an even number of vertices. By a theorem of Petersen, every bridgeless cubic graph has a perfect matching. By Brooks' theorem, every cubic graph is 3-edge-colorable except those containing a component isomorphic to K4; cubic graphs that are not 3-edge-colorable are called snarks.

Applications

Cubic graphs model the carbon skeletons of many hydrocarbon molecules, appear as fullerene structures (where vertices correspond to carbon atoms), and arise in network design and the study of expander graphs.
