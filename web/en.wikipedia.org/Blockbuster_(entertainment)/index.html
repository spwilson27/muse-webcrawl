Blockbuster (entertainment)

A blockbuster is a work of entertainment — typically a feature film or video game — that is highly popular and financially successful. The term also describes any very large production intended for wide appeal, especially in its opening period. A blockbuster's production and marketing commonly cost tens or hundreds of millions of dollars, and its success is measured in very large box-office or sales figures.

Etymology

The word derives from the Second World War: a “blockbuster” was a large bomb capable of destroying an entire city block. The term entered entertainment trade journalism in the early 1950s to describe films with exceptional drawing power, and came into its modern sense in the 1970s.

History

The modern blockbuster era is generally dated to 1975 with Steven Spielberg's Jaws, whose saturation booking and television advertising made it the highest-grossing film of its time, followed in 1977 by George Lucas's Star Wars. The model — high-concept premises, summer releases, merchandising tie-ins and franchise sequels — reshaped Hollywood economics. By the 2010s, superhero franchises dominated global box-office charts.

Criticism

Critics argue that blockbuster economics crowd out mid-budget films, encourage formulaic storytelling and sequels, and concentrate risk. Defenders reply that blockbuster profits subsidise riskier productions and that spectacle films can also be artistic achievements.
