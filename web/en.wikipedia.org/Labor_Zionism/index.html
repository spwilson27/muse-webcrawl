Labor Zionism

Jewish agricultural pioneers in Mandatory Palestine, the embodiment of Labor Zionist ideals

Labor Zionism (Hebrew: ציונות סוציאליסטית, tziyonut sotzyalistit) is the left-wing, socialist tradition of the Zionist movement. It held that a Jewish state could only be created by Jewish workers settling and building the land themselves, combining national liberation with social justice, communal agriculture and workers' self-organisation. From the 1930s until the late 1970s, Labor Zionism was the dominant political force in the Yishuv and in the State of Israel.

Ideology

Labor Zionist thinkers argued that Jews in the diaspora were trapped in an "inverted pyramid" of occupations, excluded from manual and agricultural labour. Thinkers such as Nachman Syrkin, Ber Borochov and A. D. Gordon each gave the synthesis a different inflection: Borochov framed Zionism in Marxist terms of class struggle, while Gordon exalted physical labour itself as a near-spiritual value — the "religion of labor". The movement promoted the "conquest of labor" (Hebrew labor) and built institutions to embody it.

Institutions and history

During the Second Aliyah (1904–1914), Labor Zionist pioneers founded the first kibbutzim, beginning with Degania in 1909/1910, and parties such as Poale Zion and Hapoel Hatzair. In 1920 the movement established the Histadrut, the general federation of trade unions, which became an economic and social empire in its own right, and the Haganah defence organisation. In 1930 the major Labor parties merged to form Mapai, led by David Ben-Gurion, which led the Yishuv, proclaimed the State of Israel in 1948 and governed Israel until 1977. The Israeli Labor Party, formed in 1968, inherited this tradition, though its electoral dominance faded and the kibbutz movement later underwent extensive privatisation.
