Mauna Loa (Hawaiian for "Long Mountain") is one of five volcanoes that form the Island of Hawaii in the U.S. state of Hawaii. It is the largest shield volcano on Earth in terms of volume and area, and among the most active: it has erupted 34 times since its first well-documented eruption in 1843. Its summit rises 13,681 feet (4,170 m) above sea level, and its flanks descend to the sea floor an additional five kilometres, so that its total height from base to summit exceeds that of Mount Everest.

Geology

Mauna Loa is a shield volcano built by fluid basaltic lava flows issuing from its summit caldera, Moku'āweoweo, and from two rift zones extending to the northeast and southwest. The volcano is part of the Hawaiian–Emperor seamount chain produced by the Hawaii hotspot. Its enormous volume is estimated at about 75,000 cubic kilometres. Much of the volcano lies within Hawaii Volcanoes National Park.

Eruption history

Native Hawaiians witnessed eruptions for centuries before written records began. Documented eruptions occurred in 1843, 1855–56, 1880–81, 1926, 1935, 1942, 1950, 1975 and 1984, among others. The 1950 eruption sent fast-moving flows to the sea in a matter of hours; the 1984 eruption threatened the city of Hilo. After its longest recorded quiet period of 38 years, Mauna Loa erupted again on November 27, 2022, with lava fountains in the summit caldera and flows in the Northeast Rift Zone; the eruption ended in December 2022.

Monitoring and the Mauna Loa Observatory

The U.S. Geological Survey's Hawaiian Volcano Observatory, founded in 1912 by Thomas Jaggar, monitors Mauna Loa with networks of seismometers, GPS instruments and gas sensors. On the volcano's northern flank sits the Mauna Loa Observatory of NOAA, where Charles David Keeling began continuous measurements of atmospheric carbon dioxide in 1958; the resulting "Keeling Curve" is a cornerstone of climate science.

Hazards

Because its slopes are steep for a shield volcano, Mauna Loa's lava flows can advance rapidly toward populated areas on both the Kona and Hilo sides of the island, making it one of the volcanoes in the USGS Decade Volcano monitoring programme.
