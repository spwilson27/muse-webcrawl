Ploidy
FieldGenetics, cell biology
Symbolsn (haploid), 2n (diploid)
Key termsEuploidy, aneuploidy, polyploidy

Ploidy is the number of complete sets of chromosomes in a cell, and hence the number of possible alleles for autosomal and pseudoautosomal genes. A chromosome set is the complete complement of chromosomes derived from one parent. The ploidy of cells is denoted relative to the haploid number n: a cell with one set is haploid (1n), with two sets diploid (2n), with three triploid (3n), and so on.

Types

TermSetsExample
Haploid1Human gametes (eggs, sperm), many fungi
Diploid2Most somatic cells of animals and many plants
Triploid3Seedless bananas and watermelons
Tetraploid4Many cultivated potatoes, some amphibians
Hexaploid6Bread wheat (Triticum aestivum)

Cells and organisms with more than two sets are generically called polyploid. Variation in the number of whole sets is called euploidy, while gains or losses of individual chromosomes are aneuploidy — as in trisomy 21 (Down syndrome) in humans.

Life cycles

Sexually reproducing organisms typically alternate between haploid and diploid phases: meiosis halves the chromosome number to produce haploid gametes, and fertilisation restores the diploid state. Plants and algae show an "alternation of generations" between a multicellular haploid gametophyte and a diploid sporophyte, with either phase dominant depending on the lineage. Many fungi spend most of their lives haploid, while some organisms — for example certain insects — have haplodiploid sex determination.

Polyploidy

Whole-genome duplication is especially common and evolutionarily important in plants, where it can create new species in a single generation (allopolyploidy following hybridisation). Many crops are polyploid, including wheat, cotton, strawberries and sugarcane. Polyploidy is rarer in animals but occurs in some fish, amphibians and invertebrates.
