A fire engine (also known in some territories as a fire truck or fire appliance) is a vehicle designed to assist in fighting fires. Its primary purposes are transporting firefighters and having a water supply to set up an initial attack on a fire, and it typically carries a pump, a water tank, hoses, ground ladders, and a variety of other equipment needed for firefighting and rescue work.

Design

Modern fire engines are built on custom or commercial truck chassis and painted in highly visible colours — traditionally red, though lime-yellow and other colours are used for visibility. A typical pumper carries between 1,900 and 7,500 litres (500–2,000 US gallons) of water and a pump capable of delivering several thousand litres per minute. Equipment routinely carried includes attack and supply hose lines, self-contained breathing apparatus, thermal imaging cameras, forcible entry tools, and hydraulic rescue tools ("Jaws of Life").

Types

Related apparatus include aerial ladder trucks and turntable ladders for high-rise access and rescue, water tenders (tankers) for rural water supply, wildland fire engines for brush fires, and airport crash tenders equipped with foam cannons.

History

The earliest fire engines were hand pumps on carts, developed in the seventeenth century; Dutch inventor Jan van der Heyden's flexible hose (1673) and Richard Newsham's engines of the 1720s were key advances. Steam-powered pumpers appeared in the mid-nineteenth century, horse-drawn at first, and motorised fire engines replaced them in the early twentieth century.
