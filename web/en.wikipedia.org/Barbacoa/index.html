Barbacoa

Barbacoa (Spanish pronunciation: [baɾβaˈkoa]) is a form of cooking meat that originated in the Caribbean with the Taíno people, from which the English word "barbecue" derives. In contemporary Mexico, it usually refers to meats or a whole sheep or goat slow-cooked over an open fire or, more traditionally, in a hole dug in the ground covered with agave (maguey) leaves, a preparation also found in parts of South America.

Preparation

Traditional barbacoa involves digging a pit, lining it with heated stones, placing seasoned meat — often wrapped in maguey or banana leaves — inside, covering it with earth, and cooking it slowly for many hours. The result is tender meat and a rich broth (consomé) collected beneath it. In central Mexico, barbacoa de borrego (sheep) is a common weekend food, frequently served in tacos with salsa borracha. In northern Mexico, beef cuts such as cachete (cheek) and cabeza (head) are common, and in the state of Hidalgo the style is especially celebrated.

History

The word entered Spanish from the Taíno language of the Caribbean, where it described a wooden framework used for roasting or drying meat. Spanish colonists carried the term and the cooking tradition throughout the Americas, where it developed into regional styles of barbecue.
