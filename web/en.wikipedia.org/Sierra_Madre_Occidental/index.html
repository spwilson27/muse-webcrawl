The Sierra Madre Occidental is a major mountain range in northwestern Mexico, running roughly 1,500 kilometres from near the US border in Sonora and Chihuahua southward through Sinaloa, Durango, Zacatecas, Nayarit, and Jalisco, where it meets the Trans-Mexican Volcanic Belt. It is the largest of Mexico's three Sierra Madre ranges and forms the western rim of the Mexican Plateau.

Geography and geology

The range consists largely of an elevated volcanic plateau of ignimbrites, built up by massive silicic eruptions in the Eocene and Oligocene, and is deeply dissected on its western flank by rivers draining to the Gulf of California. These have carved enormous barrancas, most famously the Copper Canyon system (Barrancas del Cobre) in Chihuahua, whose combined extent rivals and in places exceeds the depth of the Grand Canyon. Average elevations are around 2,200–3,000 metres.

Ecology and people

The highlands support pine–oak forests of great biodiversity, part of the Madrean sky island region shared with the southwestern United States. The remote canyons are home to the Tarahumara (Rarámuri), renowned long-distance runners, as well as Huichol, Tepehuán, and Cora peoples. The Chihuahua al Pacífico railway ("El Chepe") crosses the range through the Copper Canyon and is celebrated as one of the world's great rail journeys.

History

The mountains long resisted Spanish colonization and later became a refuge for Apache raiders, revolutionaries — Pancho Villa operated in the region — and, in the modern era, a centre of both silver and gold mining and, notoriously, drug cultivation in the "Golden Triangle" where Sinaloa, Durango, and Chihuahua meet.
