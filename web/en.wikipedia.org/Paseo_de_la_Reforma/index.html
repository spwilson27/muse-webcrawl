Paseo de la Reforma is a wide avenue in downtown Mexico City, Mexico. It stretches diagonally across the city for about 14.7 kilometers and is lined with monuments, skyscrapers, hotels, embassies and the headquarters of many of Mexico's largest companies. Modeled on the great boulevards of Europe, it is one of the most important avenues in the country.

History

The avenue was conceived in the 1860s under Emperor Maximilian I of the Second Mexican Empire, designed by the Austrian officer and engineer Ferdinand von Rosenzweig to connect the Imperial Palace (now the National Palace) with Chapultepec Castle, the imperial residence. It was originally called Paseo de la Emperatriz (Promenade of the Empress), after Maximilian's wife Carlota. After the restoration of the republic, it was renamed in honor of the liberal reforms of Benito Juárez.

Monuments

The avenue's roundabouts (glorietas) hold some of Mexico City's best-known monuments: the Monument to Christopher Columbus, the Monument to Cuauhtémoc, the Angel of Independence (El Ángel, 1910), the Diana the Huntress Fountain, and the Monument to the Revolution nearby. Numerous statues of Mexican heroes line the avenue.

Modern development

The stretch through the Zona Rosa and the financial district contains many of Mexico City's tallest buildings, including Torre Reforma, Torre BBVA and the Torre Mayor (once Latin America's tallest building). In 2003 the city began a major renovation with new sidewalks, gardens and cycle paths. On Sundays the avenue is closed to motor traffic for cyclists and pedestrians as part of the Muévete en Bici program.

Events

Reforma is the traditional route for parades, celebrations after football victories at the Angel of Independence, and large political demonstrations and marches.
