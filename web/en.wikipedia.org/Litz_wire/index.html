Litz wire

Litz wire is a multistrand wire constructed of many thin, individually enamel-insulated strands twisted or woven together in a carefully designed pattern. It is used to carry alternating current at radio frequencies, where it reduces losses from the skin effect and proximity effect. The name derives from the German Litzendraht, "woven wire."

Principle of operation

At high frequencies, alternating current crowds toward the surface of a conductor (the skin effect), so the interior of a thick wire carries little current while adding resistance. Proximity effect — distortion of current distribution caused by neighboring conductors — adds further loss. In litz wire, each strand has a diameter smaller than about twice the skin depth at the operating frequency, and the weaving pattern makes every strand periodically occupy every position in the bundle, equalizing flux linkage so current shares evenly among strands. The result is an AC resistance much lower than that of a solid wire of the same cross-section, typically beneficial from a few kilohertz up to a few megahertz.

Construction

Strands of magnet wire are bundled in multiple twisting stages, sometimes with silk or nylon serving. Designs specify strand gauge, strand count, and bundle pattern; termination requires removing the enamel from all strands so that they are all soldered — otherwise the benefit is lost.

Applications

  • Coils and transformers in switch-mode power supplies and radio-frequency equipment
  • Inductors in induction heating and induction cooktops
  • Wireless power transfer systems, including Qi chargers
  • High-Q tuned circuits, antennas, and Tesla coils
