Eightfold way (physics)

In physics, the eightfold way is an organisational scheme for a class of subatomic particles known as hadrons, which led to the development of the quark model. It was proposed by American physicist Murray Gell-Mann and independently by Israeli physicist Yuval Ne'eman in 1961. The scheme uses flavour SU(3) symmetry to group mesons and baryons into multiplets — notably octets, giving the scheme its name (a nod to the Noble Eightfold Path of Buddhism) — according to their strangeness and electric charge.

The scheme

By the late 1950s, accelerators had produced a bewildering "particle zoo" of strongly interacting particles. Gell-Mann and Ne'eman realised that the hadrons fell into families described by representations of the group SU(3): the spin-1/2 baryons (nucleons, Λ, Σ, Ξ) formed an octet, the spin-0 mesons formed an octet (with a singlet), the spin-3/2 baryons formed a decuplet, and mass splittings within multiplets obeyed the Gell-Mann–Okubo mass formula.

Prediction of the Omega-minus

The scheme's crowning triumph was the prediction of a missing member of the baryon decuplet: a particle with strangeness −3, charge −1, and mass around 1,672 MeV/c², which Gell-Mann named the Ω⁻. In 1964, the particle was discovered at Brookhaven National Laboratory with exactly the predicted properties — a success comparable to Mendeleev's predictions in the periodic table, and a decisive vindication of the symmetry scheme.

The quark model

Gell-Mann (and independently George Zweig) explained the SU(3) pattern by proposing that hadrons are composed of fractionally charged constituents — quarks (or "aces" in Zweig's terminology) — in three flavours: up, down, and strange. Mesons are quark–antiquark pairs and baryons are three-quark states, and the eightfold way multiplets arise naturally from these combinations. The quark model developed into quantum chromodynamics (QCD), the modern theory of the strong interaction, in which flavour SU(3) is understood as an approximate symmetry arising from the small masses of the three lightest quarks.
