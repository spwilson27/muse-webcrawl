Tandava (Sanskrit: ताण्डव, Tāṇḍava) is the divine dance performed by the Hindu god Shiva. In Shaiva tradition, Shiva's dance is the source of the cycle of creation, preservation and dissolution of the universe. The vigorous, masculine Tandava contrasts with Lasya, the graceful, gentle dance of his consort Parvati.

Forms and symbolism

Texts describe several forms of the dance, from the blissful Ananda Tandava to the fierce Rudra Tandava performed in cremation grounds. The most famous depiction is Shiva as Nataraja, Lord of Dance: dancing within a flaming halo, he holds a drum (damaru) signifying creation and fire signifying destruction, tramples the dwarf Apasmara (ignorance), and raises one hand in the abhaya mudra of reassurance while another points to his raised foot, the refuge of the devotee. The Chola bronzes of Tamil Nadu, cast in the 10th–12th centuries, are the classic sculptural form. According to legend the dance was performed at Chidambaram, the "centre of the universe" in Shaiva geography.

In dance and scripture

The Natya Shastra, the foundational treatise on Indian performing arts, describes 108 karanas (dance units) of the Tandava, said to have been taught by Shiva's attendant Tandu—whence the name. These movements form a basis of classical Indian dance, especially Bharatanatyam.
