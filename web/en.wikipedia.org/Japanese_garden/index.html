Japanese garden

A Japanese garden is a traditional garden created in the aesthetic and philosophical ideas of Japanese culture. Unlike Western formal gardens, Japanese gardens emphasize natural landscapes in miniature, asymmetry, and the suggestion of age and tranquility. They developed under the influence of Chinese gardens and evolved through the Heian, Kamakura, Muromachi, and Edo periods.

History

Early Japanese gardens were influenced by Chinese models imported in the Asuka and Nara periods. During the Heian period (794–1185), aristocratic gardens featured large ponds for boating, described in The Tale of Genji. The introduction of Zen Buddhism transformed garden design, giving rise to the dry landscape garden (karesansui), exemplified by Ryōan-ji in Kyoto.

Elements

Core elements include water (real or represented by raked gravel), rocks, sand, islands, bridges, stone lanterns, and carefully pruned trees such as pine, maple, and cherry. Paths are often irregular to slow the visitor and reveal views gradually, a principle known as miegakure (hide and reveal). Borrowed scenery (shakkei) incorporates distant mountains or trees outside the garden into its composition.

Types

Major types include pond gardens, dry Zen gardens, tea gardens (roji) leading to a teahouse, stroll gardens of the Edo period, and small courtyard gardens (tsubo-niwa). Famous examples include Kenroku-en in Kanazawa, Kōraku-en in Okayama, and Kairaku-en in Mito — the Three Great Gardens of Japan.
