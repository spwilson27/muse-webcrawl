IS–LM model

Type: Macroeconomic model
Developed: John Hicks, 1937
Curves: IS (investment–saving), LM (liquidity–money)

The IS–LM model (investment–saving / liquidity preference–money supply) is a Keynesian macroeconomic model showing how the market for goods interacts with the loanable funds and money markets to determine output and interest rates in the short run. It was formalised by John Hicks in 1937 as an interpretation of Keynes's General Theory and extended by Alvin Hansen.

The curves

The IS curve slopes downward: higher interest rates reduce investment and hence aggregate demand and income. The LM curve slopes upward: higher income raises money demand, requiring higher interest rates to restore money-market equilibrium at a fixed money supply. Their intersection gives simultaneous equilibrium.

Policy analysis

Fiscal expansion shifts IS right, raising income and rates; monetary expansion shifts LM right, raising income and lowering rates. Special cases include the liquidity trap (flat LM, monetary policy ineffective) and the classical case (vertical LM). The model dominated textbooks for decades, though later macroeconomics criticised its fixed price level, static expectations, and lack of microfoundations, leading to IS–LM–AS and New Keynesian successors.
