The Semipalatinsk Test Site (Russian: Семипалатинский испытательный полигон), also known as "The Polygon", was the primary testing venue for the Soviet Union's nuclear weapons. It is located in northeastern Kazakhstan, on the steppe near the city of Semey (formerly Semipalatinsk), south of the valley of the Irtysh River. The site covered some 18,500 square kilometres (7,100 sq mi), an area roughly the size of Wales.

History

The site was selected in 1947 by Lavrentiy Beria, head of the Soviet atomic bomb project, who misleadingly claimed the vast steppe was "uninhabited". The first Soviet atomic test, RDS-1 — a plutonium weapon closely resembling the American Fat Man design — was detonated on a tower on 29 August 1949. Between 1949 and 1989, at least 456 nuclear tests were carried out at Semipalatinsk, including 340 underground and 116 atmospheric or surface explosions. The first Soviet thermonuclear device, RDS-6s, was tested there in 1953, and the first true two-stage hydrogen bomb, RDS-37, in 1955.

The scientific and administrative town serving the site was Kurchatov, named after physicist Igor Kurchatov, located at the northern edge of the polygon near the Irtysh.

Health and environmental impact

Fallout from the early atmospheric tests exposed nearby villages to high levels of radiation, often without adequate warning or evacuation. Studies have documented elevated rates of cancer and other health effects among the local population — sometimes called the "downwinders" of Kazakhstan. Anti-nuclear protests led by the Nevada–Semipalatinsk movement, founded in 1989 by poet Olzhas Suleimenov, helped press for an end to testing. The site was officially closed by Kazakh president Nursultan Nazarbayev on 29 August 1991.

Aftermath

After Kazakhstan became independent, large quantities of weapons-usable plutonium remained in tunnels at the site; a long-running cooperative programme between Kazakhstan, Russia and the United States secured and sealed much of this material by 2012. Parts of the site remain contaminated, while studies have examined its subsequent use and the long-term fate of its radioactive legacy.
