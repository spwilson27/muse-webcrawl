Tremolo

Tremolo (Italian for "trembling") is a musical effect produced by the rapid repetition of a single note, the rapid alternation between two notes, or — in electronic and recorded music — a rapid variation of volume. In notation, tremolo is indicated by strokes through the stem of a note, or by slashes above noteheads in measured tremolo, where the exact number of repetitions is specified.

Techniques

On string instruments, tremolo is produced by rapid up-and-down strokes of the bow and became a staple of orchestral writing, famously in Monteverdi's Combattimento di Tancredi e Clorinda (1624), where the agitated stile concitato required rapid repeated notes. Classical guitarists and mandolinists achieve tremolo by rapid alternation of fingers or plectrum strokes, as in Tárrega's Recuerdos de la Alhambra. On piano, tremolo consists of rapid octave or chord repetitions. On electric guitar, "tremolo" is also the (technically inaccurate) name for the vibrato bridge system, while amplifier tremolo — an amplitude modulation effect — is sometimes conversely labelled "vibrato."

Distinctions

Tremolo (amplitude or repetition) differs from vibrato, a periodic variation in pitch, though the terms are frequently confused in popular music terminology.
