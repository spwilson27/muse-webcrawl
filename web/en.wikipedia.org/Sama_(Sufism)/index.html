Sama (Sufism)

Sama (Arabic: سماع, samāʿ, "listening") is a Sufi ceremony of spiritual listening to music and poetry, performed as a form of dhikr (remembrance of God) intended to induce ecstatic states of devotion. It is most famously embodied in the whirling dance of the Mevlevi order founded by the followers of the poet Jalal al-Din Rumi.

Practice

In the Mevlevi sema, dervishes in long white skirts and tall camel-hair hats spin in precise, meditative rotation to the music of the ney (reed flute), rebab, drums and sung hymns, including verses from Rumi's Masnavi. The turning symbolises the planets revolving around the sun and the soul's journey toward divine love; one palm is raised to receive blessings from heaven, the other turned down to pass them to earth. The ceremony is highly structured, with set movements honouring the sheikh, the Prophet and the divine.

History and debate

Sama gatherings existed by the 9th–10th centuries in Baghdad and Khorasan, and their lawfulness was long debated among Islamic scholars: al-Ghazālī defended music and audition under proper conditions in his Book of the Etiquette of Sama and Ecstasy, while stricter jurists condemned sama as innovation or frivolity. Orders such as the Chishtiyya of India cultivated sama and qawwali singing, while others avoided music entirely.

Recognition

In 2005 UNESCO proclaimed the Mevlevi Sema Ceremony a Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage of Humanity. In Turkey the ceremony was suppressed when Sufi lodges were closed in 1925, but it survived as cultural performance and is now staged in Konya each December for Rumi's festival.
