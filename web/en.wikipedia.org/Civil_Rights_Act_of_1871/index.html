Civil Rights Act of 1871

Other name: Ku Klux Klan Act
Enacted: April 20, 1871
Signed by: President Ulysses S. Grant
Codified: 42 U.S.C. § 1983 (Section 1)

The Civil Rights Act of 1871, also known as the Ku Klux Klan Act or the Third Enforcement Act, is a United States federal statute enacted during Reconstruction to protect the civil and political rights of African Americans in the South against violence by the Ku Klux Klan and similar groups.

President Ulysses S. Grant requested the legislation after state authorities proved unable or unwilling to suppress Klan terrorism. The act gave the President power to suspend the writ of habeas corpus and use federal troops to enforce the Fourteenth Amendment, powers Grant used in parts of South Carolina.

Section 1983

The most enduring part of the act is its first section, now codified as 42 U.S.C. § 1983. It creates a civil cause of action against any person who, acting under color of state law, deprives another of rights secured by the Constitution or laws of the United States. After decades of relative disuse, the Supreme Court revived the provision in Monroe v. Pape (1961), and it has since become a principal vehicle for civil rights litigation against state and local officials.

Other provisions

Section 2 of the act, codified in part at 42 U.S.C. § 1985, addresses conspiracies to deprive persons of equal protection of the laws. Other sections imposed federal criminal penalties for conspiracies to interfere with voting and public office.
