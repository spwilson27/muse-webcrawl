The Ming dynasty was the ruling dynasty of China from 1368 to 1644, founded after the collapse of the Mongol-led Yuan dynasty. The last native Chinese imperial dynasty, it governed one of the largest and most prosperous states of the pre-modern world and left an enduring mark on Chinese culture — from the Forbidden City to blue-and-white porcelain.

Founding and early Ming

Zhu Yuanzhang, a former peasant and monk turned rebel leader, defeated rival warlords and expelled the Mongols, proclaiming the Ming ("Brilliant") dynasty in 1368 with his capital at Nanjing. As the Hongwu Emperor he rebuilt the agrarian economy, codified laws, and ruled autocratically. His son, the Yongle Emperor (r. 1402–1424), seized the throne in a civil war, moved the capital to Beijing, built the Forbidden City, recompiled the Great Wall's defences, and sponsored the great treasure voyages of the eunuch admiral Zheng He, which reached East Africa between 1405 and 1433.

Height and culture

Ming China was a centre of commerce and craftsmanship: Jingdezhen porcelain, silk, and printed books circulated worldwide, and huge quantities of silver flowed in from Japan and the Spanish Americas in exchange for tea, silk and ceramics. The dynasty compiled the immense Yongle Encyclopedia, and produced classics of the novel such as Journey to the West and Water Margin. Civil service examinations on Neo-Confucian orthodoxy staffed the bureaucracy, while emperors increasingly withdrew, leaving government to grand secretaries and palace eunuchs.

Decline and fall

By the early 1600s fiscal crisis, corruption, famine, and epidemics weakened the state. The rebel Li Zicheng captured Beijing in April 1644 and the Chongzhen Emperor hanged himself; the Manchu Qing armies then swept in and conquered China, though Ming loyalist regimes (the Southern Ming) resisted until 1662, and Zheng Chenggong (Koxinga) held Taiwan to 1683.
