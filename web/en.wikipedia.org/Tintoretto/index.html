Jacopo Robusti (late September or early October 1518 – 31 May 1594), best known as Tintoretto, was an Italian Renaissance painter of the Venetian school. His contemporaries both admired and criticised the speed with which he painted and the unprecedented boldness of his brushwork; he was nicknamed Il Furioso ("the Furious"). His nickname Tintoretto means "little dyer", after his father's trade as a dyer of silk.

Life and career

According to tradition, the young Tintoretto was briefly apprenticed to Titian, who dismissed him after a few days upon seeing his drawings. He thereafter studied on his own, modelling small figures in wax and clay and suspending them in box-like stage sets to work out complex foreshortenings. A motto attributed to him set his programme: "the drawing of Michelangelo and the colour of Titian". Working rapidly and cheaply, he undercut rivals for altarpieces and confraternity commissions across Venice.

Major works

From 1564 Tintoretto painted the vast cycle for the Scuola Grande di San Rocco, eventually comprising more than fifty canvases, among them the Crucifixion (1565). For the Doge's Palace he painted the Paradiso (1588–92), one of the largest oil paintings ever executed, with the help of his son Domenico. His late Last Supper (1592–94) in San Giorgio Maggiore places the table diagonally into depth, filling the scene with swirling servants and angels. His daughter Marietta Robusti, "La Tintoretta", was a noted portraitist in his workshop.

Style

Tintoretto's figures are elongated and dramatically lit, arranged in plunging, theatrical perspectives; his rapid, visible brushwork — he often sketched compositions directly in paint — helped define the Mannerist phase of Venetian painting and influenced artists from El Greco to the Baroque.
