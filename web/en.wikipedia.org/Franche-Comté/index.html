Franche-Comté (French pronunciation: [fʁɑ̃ʃ kɔ̃te]; Frainc-Comtou: Fraintche-Comtè; German: Freigrafschaft Burgund) is a former administrative region of eastern France and a traditional province. Since 1 January 2016 it has formed part of the region of Bourgogne-Franche-Comté. Its capital and largest city is Besançon, and its population is roughly 1.18 million.

Geography

The region is bounded by Switzerland to the east and is dominated by the Jura Mountains, which run along the Swiss border. Much of the terrain is mountainous and forested, with plateaus, limestone valleys and numerous lakes. The Doubs river winds through the region, at times forming deep gorges. The Vosges mountains touch the north of the region near Belfort.

History

The name means "Free County" (of Burgundy): the County of Burgundy, centred on Dole, was part of the Holy Roman Empire and distinct from the Duchy of Burgundy to the west. In 1678, under the Treaties of Nijmegen, the territory was annexed by France under Louis XIV, and Besançon became its capital. The region is noted for the Royal Saltworks at Arc-et-Senans, designed by Claude Nicolas Ledoux and now a UNESCO World Heritage Site, and for the Citadel of Besançon built by Vauban.

Economy and culture

Franche-Comté has strong traditions in watchmaking and microtechnology, centred on Besançon, and in the automobile industry around Montbéliard and Sochaux, home of Peugeot. It is famous for its cheeses, above all Comté, as well as Morbier and Mont d'Or, and for wines from the Jura, including the distinctive vin jaune. The region is also associated with figures such as Victor Hugo, Gustave Courbet and Louis Pasteur, all born within its borders.
