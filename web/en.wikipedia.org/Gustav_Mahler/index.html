Gustav Mahler (1860–1911) was an Austro-Bohemian composer and conductor of the late Romantic era. In his lifetime he was celebrated mainly as one of the greatest conductors of his generation; his vast symphonies and orchestral song cycles, neglected for decades after his death, came to be recognised in the later 20th century as cornerstones of the repertoire, bridging Romanticism and modernism.

Life and career

Born to a Jewish family in Bohemia, Mahler studied at the Vienna Conservatory and rose through conducting posts in Prague, Leipzig, Budapest and Hamburg before becoming director of the Vienna Court Opera in 1897 — a post for which he formally converted to Catholicism. His exacting decade in Vienna transformed the opera's standards. Driven out by intrigue, he moved to New York in 1908 to conduct at the Metropolitan Opera and the New York Philharmonic. Diagnosed with a heart condition, and shaken by the death of his daughter Maria in 1907, he died in Vienna in 1911 shortly after completing the sketched Tenth Symphony.

Music

Mahler's nine completed symphonies expand the form to unprecedented scale — the Eighth, the "Symphony of a Thousand", employs enormous forces — and several incorporate voices and song. Works such as Das Lied von der Erde and the Ninth Symphony confront mortality with searing intensity. Leonard Bernstein's advocacy in the 1960s drove the Mahler revival.
