Fan fiction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fan fiction or fanfiction (also abbreviated to fan fic, fanfic, fic, or ff) is fictional writing written in an amateur capacity by fans, unauthorized by, but based on an existing work of fiction. The author uses copyrighted characters, settings, or other intellectual properties from the original creator(s) as a basis for their writing. Fan fiction ranges from a couple of sentences to an entire novel, and fans can retain their creator's characters and settings and/or add their own. It is a form of fan labour. Fan fiction can be based on any fictional (and occasionally non-fictional) subject. Common sources include novels, movies, musical groups, TV shows, video games, and anime.

Fan fiction is rarely commissioned or authorized by the original work's creator or publisher and is rarely professionally published. It may infringe on the original author's copyright, depending on the jurisdiction and on legal questions such as whether or not it qualifies as "fair use" (see Legal issues with fan fiction). Attitudes of authors and copyright owners of original works to fan fiction have ranged from encouragement to indifference to rejection. Copyright owners have occasionally responded with legal action.

The term came into use in the 20th century as copyright laws began to distinguish between stories using established characters that were written by fans and submitted for publication, and stories written both by fans and by professionals. It differs from pastiche — fiction written by professionals, often commissioned and authorized by the creator — such as Sherlock Holmes pastiches and licensed franchise novels.

Definition

Fan fiction is defined by the utilization and dependency on another author's creations. The premise of such a system is that authors build a community around shared likes and dislikes; such a community allows for expansion of knowledge of a particular story world and helps fill perceived gaps or change story outcomes. Because fan fiction is seldom published and therefore is of varying quality, writers in this genre tend to be self-motivated by enjoyment of their work and interest in expanding on the source material rather than by monetary gain.

Common subgenres include alternate universe (AU) stories, which transplant characters into different settings or timelines; "shipping" stories, which focus on romantic or sexual relationships between characters (the term derives from "relationship"); hurt/comfort stories; Mary Sue or self-insert stories, in which the author inserts an idealized version of themselves into the narrative; and crossover fiction, which merges the worlds of two or more source texts.

History

Before about 1965, the term "fan fiction" in science fiction fandom referred to original, roughly science-fictional work published in amateur science fiction publications; it did not yet imply the use of another's characters. The modern form of fan fiction — stories based on existing media properties — grew out of Star Trek fanzines in the late 1960s. The first Star Trek fanzine, Spockanalia (1967), contained fan-written fiction, and the Kirk/Spock pairing popularized the "slash" genre, named for the "/" character placed between the characters' names.

Throughout the 1970s and 1980s, fan fiction circulated primarily through print fanzines sold at conventions or by mail. The arrival of the World Wide Web transformed distribution: mailing lists, Usenet newsgroups, and personal websites gave way to large centralized archives. FanFiction.Net, founded in 1998, became the largest general archive for many years; LiveJournal communities were prominent homes for fandom activity in the 2000s. In 2007 the Organization for Transformative Works (OTW), a fan-run non-profit, was founded, and in 2009 it launched Archive of Our Own (AO3), a non-commercial, fan-owned archive that won the Hugo Award for Best Related Work in 2019. Wattpad and other platforms also host large quantities of fan fiction, particularly real-person fiction and stories based on musicians and celebrities.

Categories

Fan works are commonly tagged by rating, by genre, by the relationship pairings they contain, and by their relationship to canon: whether they are canon-compliant, canon-divergent, or entirely alternate universe. Readers navigate archives using these folksonomy tags, and platforms such as AO3 have built extensive tagging and filtering systems to make very large collections searchable. Fan fiction may also be produced as fan films, fan art, or audio dramas, though written fiction remains the most common form.

Communities and archives

Fan fiction communities are organized around fandoms — communities of fans of a particular source. Within them, authors exchange feedback, participate in fic exchanges and challenges, and work with beta readers who edit stories before posting. Gift exchanges, prompt fests, and big bang challenges (in which authors and artists collaborate on long works) are common community structures. Fan fiction has been the subject of academic study in media and cultural studies; Henry Jenkins's Textual Poachers (1992) is a foundational work describing fans as active "poachers" who remake media texts.

Legal issues

Fan fiction's legal status turns on copyright and trademark law: unauthorized derivative works may infringe, but many fan works are non-commercial and arguably transformative, potentially qualifying as fair use under United States law. No definitive court ruling addresses fan fiction generally. Some creators, such as J. K. Rowling, have publicly welcomed non-commercial fan fiction; others, such as Anne Rice, historically requested that archives remove stories based on their work. Some franchises have created sanctioned channels: Amazon's Kindle Worlds (2013–2018) licensed fan fiction for certain properties before closing.

Several commercially published works began as fan fiction with identifying details removed ("filing off the serial numbers"), most famously Fifty Shades of Grey, which originated as Twilight fan fiction.
