Tongass National Forest

LocationSoutheast Alaska, United States
AreaApproximately 16.7 million acres (67,000 km²)
EstablishedSeptember 10, 1907
Administered byU.S. Forest Service

The Tongass National Forest, in Southeast Alaska, is the largest national forest in the United States at 16.7 million acres (26,100 sq mi; 6,800,000 ha; 68,000 km²). Most of its area is a temperate rain forest and is remote enough to be home to many species of endangered and rare flora and fauna. The Tongass, which is managed by the United States Forest Service, encompasses islands of the Alexander Archipelago, fjords and glaciers, and peaks of the Coast Mountains.

An international border with Canada (British Columbia) runs along the crest of the Boundary Ranges of the Coast Mountains. The forest is administered from Forest Service offices in Ketchikan. There are local ranger district offices located in Craig, Hoonah, Juneau, Ketchikan, Petersburg, Sitka, Thorne Bay, Wrangell, and Yakutat.

History

The Alexander Archipelago Forest Reserve was established by Theodore Roosevelt in a presidential proclamation of August 20, 1902. Another presidential proclamation made by Roosevelt, on September 10, 1907, created the Tongass National Forest. On July 1, 1908, the two forests were joined, and the combined forest area encompassed most of Southeast Alaska. Further proclamations of February 16, 1909 (during Roosevelt's last days in office), expanded the forest's area.

Ecology

The Tongass includes part of the Pacific temperate rainforest ecoregion as defined by the World Wildlife Fund. The forest is home to large populations of brown bears, black bears, Sitka black-tailed deer, wolves, and moose. More than 300 species of birds inhabit the region, including the largest known concentration of nesting bald eagles. The waters of the Tongass support all five species of Pacific salmon, as well as steelhead and cutthroat trout.

Flora

Common tree species include Sitka spruce, western hemlock, mountain hemlock, Alaska yellow cedar, and western red cedar. Old-growth forest is estimated to cover between 4.5 and 5.5 million acres of the forest.

Management and controversy

The Tongass has long been at the center of controversy over logging of its old-growth forests. Long-term logging contracts with pulp mills in Sitka and Ketchikan dominated the second half of the 20th century. The Tongass Timber Reform Act of 1990 modified practices, and the application of the federal roadless rule to the forest has been repeatedly contested in litigation and changing federal policy.
