Canalisation (genetics)

Canalisation (also spelled canalization) is a measure of the ability of a population to produce the same phenotype regardless of variability in its environment or genotype. The term was coined by C. H. Waddington in 1942, drawing an analogy with development following a canal-like path in his "epigenetic landscape." Canalised traits are robust: they vary little despite mutations or environmental perturbations, until stress exceeds a buffering threshold.

Concept

Waddington proposed that natural selection favours developmental pathways that reliably produce a fit phenotype. His epigenetic landscape pictured a ball (the developing cell) rolling down valleys (developmental trajectories); the deeper the valleys, the more strongly development is canalised against disturbance. Related ideas include developmental stability, phenotypic plasticity (its inverse in some respects), and genetic assimilation.

Genetic assimilation

In famous experiments with Drosophila, Waddington showed that an environmentally induced phenotype — such as crossveinless wings after heat shock — could, after generations of selection, appear without the environmental stimulus. He interpreted this as selection on cryptic genetic variation revealed by stress, gradually canalising the new phenotype.

Mechanisms

Canalisation arises from redundancy and feedback in gene regulatory networks, distributed robustness, and molecular chaperones. The heat-shock protein Hsp90 acts as a "capacitor" of morphological evolution: by buffering mutant proteins it hides genetic variation, which is released under stress, as shown by Rutherford and Lindquist (1998). Gene duplication and network motifs such as feed-forward loops also contribute to robustness.

Significance

Canalisation explains why wild-type phenotypes are so uniform and why many mutations are recessive or silent. In evolutionary developmental biology it helps resolve how variation can accumulate cryptically and be released in bursts, potentially accelerating adaptive change when environments shift.
