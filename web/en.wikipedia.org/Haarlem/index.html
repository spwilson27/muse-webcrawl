Haarlem (Dutch pronunciation: [ˈɦaːrlɛm]) is a city and municipality in the Netherlands. It is the capital of the province of North Holland. Haarlem is situated at the northern edge of the Randstad, 20 kilometres (12 mi) west of Amsterdam, on the river Spaarne. Haarlem was granted city status in 1245, making it one of the oldest cities in Holland, and it preserves a celebrated historic centre of canals, gabled houses, and almshouse courtyards (hofjes).

History

Haarlem received its city charter from Count William II of Holland in 1245. In the Dutch Golden Age it flourished as a centre of painting — the Haarlem school included Frans Hals, Jacob van Ruisdael, and Judith Leyster — as well as of linen and silk manufacture, printing, and tulip bulb trading; the Harlem neighbourhood of New York was named after it by Dutch settlers (Nieuw Haarlem, founded 1658). The city endured the seven-month Siege of Haarlem (1572–73) during the Eighty Years' War against Spain, an episode remembered for the resistance of its citizens, including the figure of Kenau Simonsdochter Hasselaer.

Culture and landmarks

Landmarks include the Grote Markt square, the Gothic St. Bavochurch (Grote Kerk) with its famous Christian Müller organ of 1738 once played by Mozart and Handel, the Frans Hals Museum, the Teylers Museum (the Netherlands' oldest museum, founded 1778), and the Corrie ten Boom House. Haarlem is known as a shopping and market city, with a major weekly market on the Grote Markt and the annual Bloemencorso flower parade passing through from the bulb fields.

Transport

Haarlem station, opened in 1839 on the Amsterdam–Haarlem line — the oldest railway in the Netherlands — is an art-deco-influenced building serving frequent trains to Amsterdam (15 minutes) and the coast at Zandvoort.
