Mass shootings in the United States

Mass shootings in the United States are incidents of firearm violence in which several people are shot, typically defined by researchers and the media as events with three or four or more victims, not including the perpetrator. The United States experiences more public mass shootings than any other country, a fact attributed by researchers to its high rate of civilian gun ownership, the availability of high-capacity firearms, and cultural and social factors.

Definitions

There is no single agreed definition. The FBI has historically used "mass murder" for four or more killings in one event; the Gun Violence Archive counts incidents in which four or more people are shot, whether killed or injured; criminologists such as Grant Duwe use narrower definitions focused on public places. Counts therefore vary widely, from a handful of public rampages per year to several hundred incidents annually under the broadest measures.

History

Notable early incidents include the University of Texas tower shooting in 1966, in which Charles Whitman killed 14 people. The 1980s and 1990s saw workplace and school shootings that drew national attention, including San Ysidro (1984), Stockton (1989), and Columbine High School (1999), where 13 victims were killed. The 2007 Virginia Tech shooting (32 killed) was for a time the deadliest in US history, surpassed by Orlando nightclub (2016, 49 killed), Las Vegas (2017, 60 killed), Sutherland Springs (2017), El Paso (2019), Uvalde (2022), and others.

Characteristics

Most perpetrators are male, often act alone, and frequently die at the scene by suicide or police action. Many obtain their weapons legally. Researchers study warning signs, leakage of intent, grievance, and imitation effects. Targets include schools, workplaces, houses of worship, retail stores, concerts, and military installations.

Policy debate

Mass shootings intensify debates over gun control, universal background checks, red-flag laws, assault-weapon and magazine restrictions, mental-health services, school security, and media coverage of perpetrators. Federal responses have included the 1994 assault weapons ban (expired 2004) and the 2022 Bipartisan Safer Communities Act, the first major federal gun-safety law in nearly three decades.
