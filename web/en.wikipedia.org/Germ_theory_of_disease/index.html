Germ theory of disease

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The germ theory of disease is the currently accepted scientific theory for most infectious diseases: it states that microorganisms — including bacteria, viruses, fungi and prions — are the cause of many diseases. These pathogens invade a host, grow and reproduce, and produce illness directly or through toxins. The theory displaced earlier explanations such as miasma ("bad air") and humoral imbalance.

History

Early anticipations came from thinkers such as Girolamo Fracastoro (1546), who proposed contagion by "seeds" of disease, and from microscopists like Antonie van Leeuwenhoek, who observed bacteria in the 1670s. In the 1840s Ignaz Semmelweis showed that hand disinfection dramatically cut childbed fever in maternity wards, and John Snow's investigation of the 1854 Broad Street cholera outbreak demonstrated waterborne transmission.

The theory was firmly established in the 1860s–1880s by Louis Pasteur, whose experiments refuted spontaneous generation and linked microbes to fermentation and disease, and by Robert Koch, who isolated the bacteria causing anthrax (1876), tuberculosis (1882) and cholera (1883) and formulated Koch's postulates for proving that a specific microbe causes a specific disease. Joseph Lister applied the theory to surgery through antiseptic technique, drastically reducing post-operative infection.

Consequences

Acceptance of germ theory transformed medicine and public health: it led to sterilisation, aseptic surgery, pasteurisation, vaccination programmes, antibiotics and modern sanitation, and remains the foundation of infectious-disease science, though it is complemented today by knowledge that host genetics, immunity and environment also shape disease.
