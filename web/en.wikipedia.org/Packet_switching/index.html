Packet switching is a method of grouping data into packets that are transmitted over a digital network. Each packet consists of a header, which carries addressing and control information, and a payload containing the data itself. Packets from many different communications are interleaved (statistically multiplexed) over shared links and are routed independently through the network, where they may arrive out of order and be reassembled at the destination.

Packet switching is the dominant basis for data communications worldwide and underlies the Internet. It contrasts with traditional circuit switching, in which a dedicated end-to-end path is reserved for the duration of a communication, as in the classic telephone network.

History

The concept was developed independently in the early 1960s. Paul Baran at the RAND Corporation proposed a distributed, survivable communications network for the United States Air Force, publishing his work in 1964. Donald Davies at the United Kingdom's National Physical Laboratory developed a similar design and coined the term "packet" in 1965; his NPL network became operational in 1969. Leonard Kleinrock contributed the queueing-theory analysis of message switching that informed the design of the ARPANET, which began operation in 1969 and adopted packet switching throughout.

In the 1970s, packet-switched public data networks such as Telenet and the X.25 standard spread the technology commercially, while the work of Vint Cerf and Bob Kahn on TCP/IP created the protocols that knit packet networks together into the Internet.

How it works

A sending host divides a message or data stream into packets. Each packet's header contains a source and destination address and, in connection-oriented protocols such as TCP, sequence numbers that allow the receiver to reorder packets and detect losses. Intermediate nodes called routers examine each packet's destination and forward it along the best available path, a process known as store-and-forward switching.

Because links are shared statistically, packet switching uses bandwidth far more efficiently than circuit switching for bursty data traffic. The trade-off is that delay and throughput can vary as network load changes, a property managed by congestion control and quality-of-service mechanisms.

Modes

Packet switching is implemented in two broad modes. In connectionless (datagram) service, exemplified by the Internet Protocol, each packet is routed independently with no prior setup. In connection-oriented service, exemplified by X.25, Frame Relay and ATM, a virtual circuit is established first and packets follow the same path in order.

Advantages and disadvantages

  • Efficiency: network resources are shared among many users.
  • Resilience: traffic can be rerouted around failed links or nodes.
  • Flexibility: a single network carries many kinds of traffic.
  • Variable delay: jitter and packet loss must be handled by higher-layer protocols, which matters for real-time voice and video.
