Beatrice Portinari

Beatrice "Bice" di Folco Portinari (c. 1265/66 – 8 June 1290) was an Italian woman from Florence who is best known as the principal inspiration for Dante Alighieri's early work the Vita Nuova and as the character Beatrice who guides Dante through Paradise in the Divine Comedy. Dante claimed to have met her only twice — once when both were children, and once as adults — yet she became the supreme literary emblem of courtly and spiritual love, the "glorious lady of my mind" whose greeting was held to confer salvation.

Life

Beatrice was the daughter of the banker Folco Portinari, a wealthy and charitable citizen of Florence. According to Dante's Vita Nuova, he first saw her in 1274, when he was nine and she about eight, dressed in crimson at her father's May Day party, and fell instantly in love. They met again nine years later, when she greeted him in the street — a greeting he treasured. She married the banker Simone dei Bardi in 1287 and died young, in 1290. Little else is securely documented; her tomb is traditionally placed in the church of Santa Margherita dei Cerchi in Florence.

In Dante's works

In the Vita Nuova (c. 1294) Dante collects his poems for Beatrice and explains their meaning, ending with a vow to write of her "what was never written of any woman." In the Divine Comedy, Beatrice sends Virgil to guide Dante through Hell and Purgatory, then herself leads him through the spheres of Paradise, rebuking his failings and expounding theology, before yielding her place to St Bernard for the final vision of God. Scholars debate how much of the figure is historical person and how much an allegory of theology and grace; the identification with Beatrice Portinari, made already by Boccaccio, is generally accepted.

Legacy

Beatrice became a favourite subject of the Pre-Raphaelites — Dante Gabriel Rossetti translated the Vita Nuova and painted her repeatedly, often modelling her on his wife Elizabeth Siddal. The asteroid 83 Beatrix is named in her honour.
