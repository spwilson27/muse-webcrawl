Auckland (Māori: Tāmaki Makaurau) is a large metropolitan city in the North Island of New Zealand. It is the most populous urban area in the country, home to roughly a third of the national population, and the centre of New Zealand's largest commercial and transport hub. Auckland straddles an isthmus between the Pacific Ocean and the Tasman Sea, giving it harbours on two coasts.

History

The Auckland volcanic field was settled by Māori around 1350, and fortified pā (hillforts) were built on its volcanic cones. After the signing of the Treaty of Waitangi in 1840, Governor William Hobson chose the site as New Zealand's capital — a role it held until 1865, when Wellington became capital. The city grew steadily as a port and commercial centre, and in 2010 its local authorities were amalgamated into a single "super city" governed by Auckland Council.

Geography

Auckland lies between the Waitematā Harbour to the east and the Manukau Harbour to the southwest. Its landscape is shaped by some 53 extinct volcanoes, including Rangitoto Island, an iconic symmetrical cone in the Hauraki Gulf. The climate is subtropical, with warm, humid summers and mild, wet winters.

Demographics and culture

Auckland is one of the most ethnically diverse cities in the world, with large Māori, Pacific Islander and Asian communities; it has the largest Polynesian population of any city. Landmarks include the Sky Tower — the tallest freestanding structure in the Southern Hemisphere at 328 metres — the Auckland War Memorial Museum in the Domain, and the Harbour Bridge. The "City of Sails" nickname reflects the very high rate of boat ownership and its sailing culture, including hosting the America's Cup.

Economy and transport

The city is New Zealand's economic powerhouse, hosting the country's largest port and international airport, and most major companies' head offices. Its transport network is centred on motorways, an expanding commuter rail network (including the City Rail Link), ferries and cycleways.
