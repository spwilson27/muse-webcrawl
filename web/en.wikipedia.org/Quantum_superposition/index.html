Quantum superposition

Quantum superposition
FieldQuantum mechanics
Related conceptsWave function, entanglement, decoherence
Famous illustrationSchrödinger's cat (1935)

Quantum superposition is a fundamental principle of quantum mechanics which states that a physical system — such as an electron — can exist partly in multiple states at once, and that any two (or more) quantum states can be added together ("superposed") to yield another valid quantum state. Conversely, every quantum state can be represented as a sum of two or more other distinct states.

The principle follows from the linearity of the Schrödinger equation: if ψ1 and ψ2 are solutions, then any linear combination is also a solution:

|ψ> = α|0> + β|1>,   with |α|2 + |β|2 = 1

Superposition of two quantum states in a double-well system

Examples

The classic experimental demonstration of superposition is the double-slit experiment, in which particles such as electrons produce an interference pattern even when sent through the apparatus one at a time, as though each particle passes through both slits simultaneously. A photon passing through a beam splitter is likewise placed in a superposition of the transmitted and reflected paths, a fact exploited in interferometers and in many quantum information protocols.

Superposition of spin states is the basis of the qubit: a spin-½ particle can be in any combination of "spin up" and "spin down," represented by a point on the Bloch sphere.

Measurement and interpretations

When a measurement is performed, only one of the superposed outcomes is ever observed, with probabilities given by the Born rule — the squared magnitudes of the coefficients. What happens to the superposition at measurement is the subject of the measurement problem. In the Copenhagen interpretation, the wave function is said to "collapse" to a single outcome. The many-worlds interpretation holds instead that all outcomes occur in branching universes. Erwin Schrödinger devised his famous cat thought experiment in 1935 to highlight the apparent absurdity of applying superposition to macroscopic objects.

Decoherence

Superpositions of large systems are extremely fragile. Interaction with the environment rapidly entangles the system with its surroundings, suppressing interference between the components in a process called quantum decoherence. This explains why everyday objects are never seen in superpositions of positions, and it is the main obstacle to building quantum computers, which rely on maintaining coherent superpositions of many qubits.
