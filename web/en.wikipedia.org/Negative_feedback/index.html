Negative feedback

Negative feedback
FieldControl theory, electronics, biology, physiology
MechanismOutput is subtracted from input, counteracting change
EffectsStability, accuracy, reduced distortion, homeostasis
ContrastPositive feedback (self-reinforcing)

Negative feedback (or balancing feedback) occurs when some function of the output of a system, process, or mechanism is fed back in a manner that tends to reduce fluctuations in the output, whether caused by changes in the input or by other disturbances. It is a cornerstone of control engineering and of self-regulation in biology, where it underpins homeostasis.

Principle

In a negative feedback loop, a sensor measures the output, a comparator computes the error — the difference between the measured output and a desired reference — and a controller acts to reduce that error. Because the correction opposes the deviation, the loop is stabilizing: if the output rises too high, the feedback drives it down, and vice versa. The loop gain determines how tightly the output is controlled; excessive gain combined with delays can, however, produce oscillation or instability, a central concern in control theory (analyzed with tools such as the Nyquist stability criterion and Bode plots).

History

Self-regulating devices are ancient — float valves were known in antiquity, and centrifugal governors were used on windmills and, famously, on James Watt's steam engines. The theory was placed on firm ground in the 20th century: Harold Black conceived the negative-feedback amplifier in 1927 while working at Bell Labs, greatly reducing distortion in telephone amplifiers, and Harry Nyquist developed the stability theory of feedback systems in the early 1930s. Norbert Wiener's cybernetics (1948) generalized feedback as a unifying concept across machines and organisms.

Applications

Electronics

Negative feedback in amplifiers trades gain for linearity, wider bandwidth, and controlled input/output impedances. Operational amplifier circuits are almost always configured with negative feedback to set precise gains.

Biology and physiology

Body temperature regulation, blood glucose control by insulin and glucagon, blood pressure control via the baroreceptor reflex, and the hypothalamic–pituitary control of hormone levels (e.g., thyroid hormone and cortisol) all operate by negative feedback: a rise in the controlled variable inhibits the processes that produce it.

Other domains

Negative feedback appears in climate systems (some cloud and weathering feedbacks), economics (price mechanisms), ecology (predator–prey regulation), and engineering servomechanisms from thermostats to cruise control.
