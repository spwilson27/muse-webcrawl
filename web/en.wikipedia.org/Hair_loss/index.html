Hair loss, also known as alopecia or baldness, refers to a loss of hair from part of the head or body. Typically at least the head is involved. The severity of hair loss can vary from a small area to the entire body. Normally humans shed 50 to 100 hairs per day as part of the natural hair growth cycle; visible thinning or bald patches occur when shedding exceeds regrowth or follicles are destroyed.

Types and causes

The most common cause is androgenetic alopecia — male-pattern or female-pattern hair loss — a hereditary condition influenced by the hormone dihydrotestosterone (DHT), which progressively miniaturises susceptible hair follicles. In men it typically presents as a receding hairline and thinning at the crown; in women as diffuse thinning over the top of the scalp.

Other causes include alopecia areata, an autoimmune disorder causing patchy loss; telogen effluvium, temporary shedding after stress, illness, childbirth or nutritional deficiency; fungal infections such as tinea capitis; thyroid disorders; iron deficiency; and scarring (cicatricial) alopecias, in which follicles are permanently destroyed. Chemotherapy and radiation commonly cause temporary loss (anagen effluvium).

Diagnosis

Diagnosis is usually based on the pattern of loss, medical history and examination of the scalp. A pull test, dermoscopy (trichoscopy), blood tests for thyroid function and iron stores, and occasionally scalp biopsy may be used to identify the cause.

Treatment

Treatment depends on the cause. For androgenetic alopecia, the best-established medications are topical minoxidil and oral finasteride (in men), which slow loss and can partially regrow hair. Alopecia areata may be treated with corticosteroids or immunotherapy. Hair transplantation redistributes follicles from resistant areas of the scalp. Wigs, hairpieces and cosmetic camouflage are non-medical options. Many treatments have limited or temporary effects, and no cure exists for pattern hair loss.

Psychological effects

Although usually medically harmless, hair loss can cause significant psychological distress, affecting self-esteem and quality of life — a burden historically underestimated, particularly for women and younger patients.
