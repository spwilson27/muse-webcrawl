Francoist Spain

Francisco Franco ruled Spain as a dictatorship from 1939 until his death in 1975.

Francoist Spain (Spanish: España franquista), officially the Spanish State (Estado Español), was the period of Spanish history between 1939 and 1975, when Spain was ruled as a dictatorship by General Francisco Franco following the Nationalist victory in the Spanish Civil War.

Consolidation and repression

The regime was built on the single party FET y de las JONS (the Falange), the army, and National Catholicism — a close alliance with the Catholic Church. The early years saw severe repression of Republicans, including tens of thousands of executions, concentration camps and forced labour. Spain remained neutral (formally non-belligerent) in the Second World War, though it sent the Blue Division of volunteers to fight alongside Germany on the Eastern Front.

Isolation and opening

After 1945 Spain was diplomatically isolated, but the Cold War brought rehabilitation: a 1953 agreement gave the United States bases in Spain, and Spain joined the United Nations in 1955. The 1959 Stabilization Plan, designed by technocrats linked to Opus Dei, ended the failed autarkic policies and opened the economy, producing the "Spanish miracle" — rapid growth through the 1960s driven by industry, tourism and emigrant remittances.

Final years and transition

The regime's last years were marked by growing labour and student unrest, Basque separatist violence (Franco's designated prime minister, Luis Carrero Blanco, was assassinated by ETA in 1973), and cautious liberalisation. Franco died on 20 November 1975; his designated successor, King Juan Carlos I, led the transition to democracy that dismantled the Francoist state by 1978.
