Flap (surgery)

In surgery, a flap is a unit of tissue that is transferred from one site (the donor site) to another (the recipient site) while maintaining its own blood supply. This distinguishes a flap from a skin graft, which is placed on a wound bed and must acquire a new blood supply from it. Flaps may consist of skin and subcutaneous tissue alone, or may include fascia, muscle, bone, or combinations of tissues (composite flaps).

Classification

Flaps are commonly classified by their blood supply, their composition, and the method and distance of transfer. A pedicled flap remains attached to the donor site by a pedicle carrying its vessels, and is rotated, advanced or tunnelled into the defect; a free flap is completely detached, and its artery and vein are reconnected to vessels at the recipient site using microsurgical technique. By circulation pattern, flaps may be random-pattern (supplied by the subdermal plexus) or axial (supplied by a named vessel running along the flap's axis, permitting greater length). Modern perforator flaps are based on individual vessels that pierce deep fascia to reach the skin, allowing thin flaps with minimal donor muscle sacrifice.

Uses

Flap surgery is central to reconstructive and plastic surgery. Flaps are used to close wounds where grafts would fail (for example over exposed bone, tendon, cartilage or hardware), to rebuild defects after cancer excision — including breast reconstruction using abdominal tissue (such as the DIEP or TRAM flap) and head-and-neck reconstruction using the radial forearm or fibula free flap — and to treat pressure sores and traumatic injuries. The forehead flap, described in ancient Indian medicine for nasal reconstruction and refined in the Sushruta tradition, is among the oldest recorded operations.

Complications

The principal risk is flap failure from inadequate blood flow — arterial insufficiency, venous congestion, haematoma or infection — which can lead to partial or total flap necrosis. Careful flap design, microsurgical technique, and post-operative monitoring of colour, temperature and capillary refill are used to detect and rescue compromised flaps.
