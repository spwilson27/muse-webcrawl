In Greek mythology, Icarus (Greek: Ἴκαρος) was the son of the master craftsman Daedalus, the architect of the labyrinth of Crete. After Theseus escaped from the labyrinth, King Minos imprisoned Daedalus and Icarus in a tower to prevent Daedalus from revealing its secrets. The two escaped by means of wings that Daedalus constructed from feathers held together with beeswax. Daedalus warned Icarus first not to fly too low, where the sea's dampness would clog his wings, nor too high, where the sun's heat would melt the wax.

The myth

Icarus ignored his father's instructions not to fly too close to the sun. The wax in his wings melted, the feathers came apart, and he tumbled out of the sky, fell into the sea and drowned. The sea near the island of Samos where he fell was named the Icarian Sea in his honour, and the nearby island took the name Icaria. The myth is told most fully in Ovid's Metamorphoses (Book 8), where Daedalus buries his son's body on Icaria.

Interpretation

The story is commonly read as a warning against hubris and reckless ambition — "flying too close to the sun" — as well as a warning against disobedience and the folly of neglecting the "middle course" between extremes. Some scholars interpret it as a tale about human invention and its limits; in antiquity, alternative rationalising versions held that Daedalus invented sails, and Icarus fell from a ship.

In art and culture

The fall of Icarus is the subject of Landscape with the Fall of Icarus, a painting once attributed to Pieter Bruegel the Elder, in which Icarus is an almost unnoticed pair of legs disappearing into the sea while a ploughman carries on with his work. W. H. Auden's poem "Musée des Beaux Arts" (1938) meditates on the painting's portrayal of suffering ignored by everyday life. The Icarus story lends its name to the "Icarus paradox" in business, to NASA-related uses, and to countless songs, novels and games.
