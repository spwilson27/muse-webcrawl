Ergot sclerotia on rye

Claviceps purpurea is an ergot fungus that grows on the ears of rye and related cereal and forage plants. Consumption of grains or seeds contaminated with the survival structure of this fungus, the ergot sclerotium, can cause ergotism in humans and other mammals.

Life cycle

An ergot kernel, called a sclerotium, develops when a spore of fungal species of the genus Claviceps infects a floret of flowering grass or cereal. The infection process mimics a pollen grain growing into an ovary during fertilization. Infection requires that the fungal spore have access to the stigma; consequently, plants infected by Claviceps are mainly outcrossing species with open flowers, such as rye and ryegrass.

The proliferating fungal mycelium then destroys the plant ovary and connects with the vascular bundle originally intended for seed nutrition. The first stage of ergot infection manifests itself as a white soft tissue (sphacelia) producing sugary honeydew, which often drops out of the infected grass florets. This honeydew contains millions of asexual spores (conidia) which insects disperse to other florets.

Later, the sphacelia convert into a hard dry sclerotium inside the husk of the floret. At this stage, alkaloids and lipids accumulate in the sclerotium. The sclerotium overwinters on the ground and germinates in the spring, producing small mushroom-like fruiting bodies (stromata) that eject ascospores into the air to begin the cycle anew.

Effects on humans and animals

The sclerotia contain significant concentrations of ergot alkaloids, including ergotamine. Historically, consumption of rye products contaminated with the fungus caused outbreaks of ergotism, known in the Middle Ages as St. Anthony's fire, which could manifest in convulsive or gangrenous forms. Ergot alkaloids have also been used pharmaceutically, and ergotamine is a precursor in the synthesis of lysergic acid.

Host range

Claviceps purpurea infects more than 400 plant species, including economically important cereals such as rye, wheat, barley, oats, sorghum and millet, as well as many wild and forage grasses. See also blood coagulation, which ergot alkaloids can influence through vasoconstriction.
