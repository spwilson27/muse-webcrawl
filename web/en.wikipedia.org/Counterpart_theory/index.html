Counterpart theory

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In philosophy, specifically in metaphysics and philosophical logic, counterpart theory is an interpretation of modal talk developed by David Lewis. It was first presented in Lewis's 1968 paper "Counterpart Theory and Quantified Modal Logic" and forms part of his modal realism, the view that all possible worlds are as real as the actual world.

Whereas standard quantified modal logic (following Kripke) treats modal statements as being about the very same individuals existing in different possible worlds ("transworld identity"), counterpart theory denies that anything exists in more than one world. Instead, when we say that some individual could have been different, we mean that it has a counterpart — a sufficiently similar individual — in another world who is different in the relevant way. Humphrey could have won the election because there is another world containing a counterpart of Humphrey who wins there.

The theory

Lewis's system rests on four postulates: anything is a part of one and only one world; nothing is part of two worlds; for someone to have a counterpart is for that counterpart to resemble them closely in important respects and not resemble anything else in its world more closely; and certain primitive notions (worldhood, spatiotemporal relatedness, resemblance) underpin the analysis. The counterpart relation is a similarity relation: it is neither transitive nor symmetric in general, and it can be context-dependent, which Lewis took to explain the flexibility of ordinary modal judgements.

Motivations and objections

Lewis argued counterpart theory avoids puzzles about transworld identity, such as how one object could have incompatible properties in different worlds, and how identity across worlds could be non-conventional. Kripke famously objected with the "Humphrey objection": when we say Humphrey could have won, we seem to care about Humphrey himself, not about whether some other person, however similar, wins in another world. Lewis replied that modal talk, properly analysed, never required transworld identity in the first place. Critics also question the context-dependence of the counterpart relation; defenders see in it a virtue that mirrors ordinary language.
