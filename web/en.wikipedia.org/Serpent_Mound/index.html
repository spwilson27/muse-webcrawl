Serpent Mound is a 1,348-foot-long (411 m), three-foot-high prehistoric effigy mound located in Peebles, Adams County, Ohio. Intended to resemble a giant serpent with a curled tail at one end and an open mouth at the other — apparently about to swallow an oval object, often interpreted as an egg — it is the largest serpent effigy in the world. The mound lies within Serpent Mound State Memorial on a plateau overlooking the valley of Ohio Brush Creek.

Design

The effigy winds back and forth in seven coils across the plateau, following the contours of the bluff. Its head aligns to the summer solstice sunset, and researchers have proposed that curves in the body align with other solar and lunar events, suggesting an astronomical function. Unlike many mounds, it contains no burials or grave goods.

Age and attribution

The mound's builders remain disputed. Frederic Ward Putnam, who excavated and restored the mound in the 1880s after raising funds to save it (it became one of Harvard University's preservation efforts and later an Ohio state park), attributed it to the Adena culture. Later scholars assigned it to the Fort Ancient culture, around 1070 AD, while radiocarbon work published in the 2010s obtained dates from the mound's core suggesting an Early Woodland (Adena-era) construction around 320 BC, possibly with later Fort Ancient remodelling. The debate continues.

Preservation

Serpent Mound was designated a National Historic Landmark in 1964 and has been placed on the United States' tentative list for UNESCO World Heritage inscription. It is managed by the Ohio History Connection together with the Arc of Appalachia Preserve System. The site draws visitors for archaeology, astronomy gatherings at the solstices, and its striking landscape.
