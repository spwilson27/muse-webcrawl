New Objectivity

New Objectivity (German: Neue Sachlichkeit) was an art movement that emerged in Germany in the 1920s as a reaction against Expressionism. The term was coined by Gustav Friedrich Hartlaub, director of the Kunsthalle Mannheim, for a 1925 exhibition titled Neue Sachlichkeit: Deutsche Malerei seit dem Expressionismus. The movement favored unsentimental, realistic, often satirical observation of Weimar society.

Characteristics

Hartlaub distinguished two tendencies: the "verists," such as Otto Dix and George Grosz, whose work was bitingly satirical and critical of social corruption; and the "classicists" or magic realists, such as Christian Schad and Alexander Kanoldt, who pursued a smooth, timeless realism. Portraiture was central, with sitters rendered in cool, precise detail against plain backgrounds.

Decline

The movement lost momentum with the fall of the Weimar Republic and the rise of Nazism after 1933, when much of the work was condemned as "degenerate art." Its influence nevertheless persisted in later realist painting and photography.
