Tracery

Tracery is an architectural device by which windows (or screens, panels, and vaults) are divided into sections of various proportions by stone bars or ribs of moulded stone. It is an essential decorative element of Gothic architecture, allowing ever larger windows to be glazed while their stone skeletons carried the structural loads, and filling interiors with patterned light.

Development

The earliest form, plate tracery (from about the 1190s), punched openings through a solid stone plate. It was superseded by bar tracery, first used at Reims Cathedral in the 1210s, in which slender moulded bars form the pattern; bar tracery made possible the great geometric windows of the thirteenth century. Later styles developed curvilinear or "flowing" tracery with ogee curves (the Decorated style in England) and the flame-like forms of French Flamboyant Gothic, while English Perpendicular Gothic favoured strong vertical mullions and grid-like patterns.

Other uses

Tracery patterns were applied beyond windows: to blind arcades, choir screens, tombs, furniture, and the rib patterns of vaults. The term is also used for similar interlaced ornament in other traditions, and for the intersecting rib work in fan vaulting. In the Gothic Revival of the nineteenth century, tracery returned in stone, cast iron, and wood for churches, universities, and railway stations.
