Biblical infallibility

Biblical infallibility is the belief that what the Bible says regarding matters of faith and Christian practice is wholly useful and true. It is closely related to the doctrine of biblical inerrancy, though the two are sometimes distinguished: inerrancy holds that Scripture is entirely without error, while infallibility holds that Scripture cannot fail in its purpose, particularly in matters of faith and practice.

History

Claims about the truthfulness of Scripture have roots in the writings of the Church Fathers. Augustine of Hippo argued that apparent contradictions in the Bible should be resolved by assuming a copyist's error or a misunderstanding by the reader. The terms "infallibility" and "inerrancy" gained prominence in the 19th and 20th centuries, especially among evangelical Protestants responding to higher criticism and modernist theology.

Statements and confessions

The Chicago Statement on Biblical Inerrancy (1978), produced by the International Council on Biblical Inerrancy, affirmed that Scripture, in its original manuscripts, is without error in all that it affirms. Many denominations and seminaries adopted statements affirming infallibility, sometimes deliberately choosing the word over "inerrancy" to allow more nuance.

Distinction from inerrancy

Some theologians argue that infallibility is a stronger term than inerrancy because it speaks not only to truth but to the Bible's unfailing effectiveness in accomplishing God's purposes. Others treat the terms as roughly synonymous, or prefer one for historical and confessional reasons.

Criticism

Critics contend that doctrines of infallibility impose modern standards of precision on ancient texts and complicate dialogue with science and history. Others within the tradition respond that the doctrine concerns the theological claims of the text read on its own terms.
