Surrealism is an art and cultural movement that developed in Europe in the aftermath of World War I, in which artists aimed to allow the unconscious mind to express itself, often resulting in the depiction of illogical or dreamlike scenes and ideas. The movement is best known for its visual artworks and writings, but also encompassed film, theatre, photography, and music.

Origins

The writer Guillaume Apollinaire coined the word "surrealism" in 1917. The movement crystallised in Paris around André Breton, a former member of the Dada movement influenced by Sigmund Freud's theories of the unconscious and dream interpretation. In the Manifesto of Surrealism (1924), Breton defined surrealism as "pure psychic automatism by which one proposes to express the actual functioning of thought... in the absence of any control exercised by reason".

Techniques and themes

Surrealists employed automatic drawing and writing, frottage, collage, decalcomania, and the "exquisite corpse" game to bypass rational control. Their imagery juxtaposed incongruous objects in dreamlike settings: Dalí's melting clocks in The Persistence of Memory (1931), Magritte's pipe that declares "Ceci n'est pas une pipe", and Ernst's collaged dream-worlds are canonical. Central themes include dream, desire, eroticism, chance, and the liberation of the mind from bourgeois conventions.

Spread and influence

Centre initially in Paris, the movement spread internationally, particularly after many artists fled to New York during World War II, where surrealism strongly influenced the emerging Abstract Expressionists. Its legacy persists in contemporary art, cinema (Buñuel, Lynch), advertising, and popular culture.
