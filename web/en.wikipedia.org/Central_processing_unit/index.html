Central processing unit

A central processing unit (CPU), also called a central processor or main processor, is the primary processor in a given computer. Its electronic circuitry executes instructions of a computer program, such as arithmetic, logic, controlling, and input/output operations specified by the instructions in the program.

History

Early computers such as ENIAC had to be physically rewired to perform different tasks. The stored-program concept, associated with the von Neumann architecture, allowed instructions to be held in memory alongside data. Transistors replaced vacuum tubes in the 1950s and 1960s, and integrated circuits later placed an entire CPU on a single chip — the microprocessor, beginning with devices such as the Intel 4004 in 1971.

Operation

The fundamental operation of most CPUs is to execute a sequence of stored instructions called a program. The cycle involves fetching an instruction from memory, decoding it, executing it, and writing back results. Registers hold small amounts of fast storage, the arithmetic logic unit (ALU) performs arithmetic and logic operations, and the control unit directs the operation of the processor.

Design and performance

Modern CPUs use pipelining, superscalar execution, out-of-order execution, branch prediction, and multiple cache levels to improve performance. Clock rate, instructions per cycle, and core count all influence throughput. Since the mid-2000s, power constraints have shifted design toward multi-core processors.
