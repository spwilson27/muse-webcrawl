Earl Marshal (alternatively Marshal of England) is a royal office and one of the Great Officers of State of England, ranking in precedence immediately below the Lord High Constable and above the Lord High Admiral. Originally a military office responsible for the sovereign's horses and, by extension, cavalry and army ordnance — "marshal" derives from the Old High German marah-scalc, "horse servant" — it evolved into a primarily ceremonial and heraldic role.

History

The office of Marshal of England was held by the celebrated William Marshal, 1st Earl of Pembroke (died 1219), and passed through the Bigod earls of Norfolk and the Mowbrays to the Howard family, dukes of Norfolk. In 1672 the earl marshalship was made hereditary in the Howard dukes of Norfolk, in whose family it remains; because the dukes were often Roman Catholics and thus barred from exercising functions under the penal laws, Protestant deputies acted for them for long periods.

Functions

The Earl Marshal is responsible for organising major state ceremonial, including coronations, state funerals and the State Opening of Parliament. He heads the College of Arms and has authority over heraldry in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, including the granting of coats of arms, and he is the judge of the Court of Chivalry, which sat in 1954 for the first time in more than two centuries. The office carries a gold baton as its symbol, and the Earl Marshal is one of the hereditary officers permitted to retain a seat entitlement associated with the House of Lords under the 1999 reforms.
