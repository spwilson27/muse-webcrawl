Ribosomal RNA

Ribosomal ribonucleic acid (rRNA) is the RNA component of the ribosome, the molecular machine that synthesises proteins in all living cells. It is the most abundant type of RNA in a typical cell, accounting for roughly 80% of total RNA, and it provides both the structural scaffold of the ribosome and the catalytic activity (peptidyl transferase) that links amino acids into proteins — making the ribosome a ribozyme.

Types

Ribosomes consist of a large and a small subunit, each containing rRNAs of characteristic sizes, historically measured in Svedberg (S) units. In bacteria, the small (30S) subunit contains 16S rRNA, and the large (50S) subunit contains 23S and 5S rRNA. In eukaryotes, the 40S subunit contains 18S rRNA and the 60S subunit contains 28S, 5.8S and 5S rRNA. The 16S and 18S sequences evolve slowly and are widely used to infer evolutionary relationships and to identify organisms in microbiome studies.

Synthesis

In eukaryotes most rRNA is transcribed in the nucleolus from tandemly repeated ribosomal DNA genes by RNA polymerase I (with 5S rRNA transcribed separately by polymerase III), then processed and assembled with ribosomal proteins into pre-ribosomal subunits.

Function in translation

During protein synthesis, the small subunit's rRNA guides messenger RNA and checks codon–anticodon pairing, while the large subunit's rRNA catalyses peptide bond formation.
