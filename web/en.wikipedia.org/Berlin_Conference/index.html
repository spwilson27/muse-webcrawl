Berlin Conference

The Berlin Conference of 1884–85, also known as the Congo Conference (Kongokonferenz) or West Africa Conference, met on 15 November 1884 and, after an adjournment, concluded on 26 February 1885 with the signing of the General Act. Convened by German Chancellor Otto von Bismarck at the request of Portugal, the conference regulated European colonization and trade in Africa during the New Imperialism period, coinciding with Germany's sudden emergence as an imperial power.

The General Act

The General Act established principles of free trade throughout the Congo Basin and navigation on the Congo and Niger rivers. It required any power taking possession of territory along the African coasts to notify the other signatories and to establish an effective authority in the occupied area — the doctrine of "effective occupation". These rules accelerated the Scramble for Africa: by 1914, European powers controlled nearly the entire continent except Ethiopia and Liberia.

Significance

No African representatives took part in the conference. The arbitrary colonial boundaries drawn in this era, grouping and dividing peoples without regard to ethnic or linguistic realities, left a lasting legacy for post-colonial Africa. The conference also endorsed the Congo Free State as the personal possession of King Leopold II of Belgium, whose administration became notorious for atrocities against the Congolese population.
