Dark energy

In physical cosmology, dark energy is an unknown form of energy that affects the universe on the largest scales. Its primary effect is to drive the accelerating expansion of the universe. Assuming the standard model of cosmology, the Lambda-CDM model, dark energy accounts for about 68% of the total energy content of the universe, with dark matter making up about 27% and ordinary matter less than 5%.

Evidence

The first observational evidence came in 1998 from observations of Type Ia supernovae by two teams — the Supernova Cosmology Project and the High-Z Supernova Search Team — which found that distant supernovae were fainter, and thus farther away, than expected in a decelerating universe. The discovery earned Saul Perlmutter, Brian Schmidt, and Adam Riess the 2011 Nobel Prize in Physics. Independent lines of evidence include measurements of the cosmic microwave background, baryon acoustic oscillations, and the large-scale structure of the universe.

Explanations

The simplest explanation is the cosmological constant (Λ), introduced by Einstein in 1917 and interpretable as the intrinsic energy density of empty space — "vacuum energy." Its observed value is extraordinarily small compared with theoretical estimates from quantum field theory, a discrepancy known as the cosmological constant problem. Alternatives include dynamical scalar fields such as quintessence, and modifications of general relativity on cosmological scales.

Implications for the fate of the universe

If dark energy is a cosmological constant, the universe will expand forever at an accelerating rate, growing ever colder and emptier. Other models permit different endings, from a "Big Rip," in which phantom energy tears structures apart, to a future reversal of acceleration.

Surveys

Major efforts to characterize dark energy include the Dark Energy Survey, the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI), the Vera C. Rubin Observatory's LSST, and the European Space Agency's Euclid mission, launched in 2023. Some DESI results have been interpreted as hints that dark energy may evolve with time, though this remains debated.
