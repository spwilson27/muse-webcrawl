The Parthenon crowns the Acropolis of Athens.

The Parthenon (Ancient Greek: Παρθενών) is a former temple on the Athenian Acropolis, Greece, dedicated to the goddess Athena, whom the people of Athens considered their patroness. Its name derives from Athena Parthenos, "Athena the Virgin". Construction began in 447 BCE, when the Athenian empire was at its height, and was completed by 432 BCE. It is the most important surviving building of classical Greece and is regarded as the finest example of the Doric order.

Architecture and sculpture

Designed by the architects Iktinos and Kallikrates under the artistic direction of the sculptor Phidias, the temple is a peripteral octastyle building measuring about 30.9 by 69.5 metres at the stylobate, with 8 columns at each end and 17 along the sides. It embodies subtle refinements — slight curvature of the platform, inward inclination of the columns, and thickening of the corner columns (entasis) — that correct optical illusions. Inside stood Phidias's colossal chryselephantine statue of Athena. The sculptural programme included 92 carved metopes (the Centauromachy, Gigantomachy, Amazonomachy and sack of Troy), a continuous Ionic frieze depicting the Panathenaic procession, and pediments showing the birth of Athena and her contest with Poseidon.

Later history

The Parthenon remained a temple for about a thousand years. In late antiquity it was converted into a church dedicated to the Virgin Mary, and after the Ottoman conquest into a mosque. In 1687, during the Venetian siege of Athens, an Ottoman powder magazine stored in the building exploded when struck by a shell, destroying the roof and much of the interior. In the early 19th century Lord Elgin removed many surviving sculptures — the Parthenon Marbles — which are now displayed in the British Museum, a removal whose legitimacy Greece continues to dispute. Since 1975 a major restoration programme has conserved the structure, and the nearby Acropolis Museum displays the frieze with plaster casts of the pieces held abroad.
