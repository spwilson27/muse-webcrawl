The Emirate of Granada (Arabic: إمارة غرناطة), also known as the Nasrid Kingdom of Granada, was an Islamic polity in the southern Iberian Peninsula during the Late Middle Ages. Founded in 1238 by Muhammad ibn Yusuf ibn Nasr, known as Muhammad I, it was the last Muslim state of al-Andalus. Its capital was the city of Granada, and its most famous monument, the Alhambra palace complex, was built largely under its rulers.

History

Following the Almohad collapse and the Christian advances of Ferdinand III of Castile, Muhammad I established his emirate in 1238 and secured its survival by accepting vassalage to Castile in 1246, even assisting Castile in the conquest of Seville. Thereafter the emirate survived for nearly 250 years as a tributary buffer state, sustained by its mountainous terrain, diplomacy, trade through the port of Málaga, and silk production. The Nasrid dynasty produced more than twenty emirs, frequently divided by civil wars. In 1482 the Catholic Monarchs, Ferdinand II of Aragon and Isabella I of Castile, began the Granada War; the last emir, Muhammad XII (Boabdil), surrendered the city on 2 January 1492, ending Muslim rule in Iberia and coinciding with the beginning of Spanish overseas expansion.

Culture

Nasrid Granada was a major centre of Islamic art and learning. The Alhambra and Generalife gardens exemplify its sophisticated court architecture, with intricate stucco, muqarnas vaulting and tilework (zellij). Its population included Muslims, Christians and a notable Jewish community; the scholar and physician families of the period maintained intellectual links across the Mediterranean.

Legacy

The surrender of Granada became a defining moment in Spanish history. The Alhambra, later partly rebuilt by Charles V, is today a UNESCO World Heritage Site and one of Spain's most visited monuments. The emirate's motto, "Wa lā ghāliba illā Allāh" ("There is no victor but God"), is inscribed throughout the Alhambra.
