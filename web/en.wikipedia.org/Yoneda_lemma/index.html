Yoneda lemma

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, specifically in category theory, the Yoneda lemma is a fundamental result about the embedding of a category into a category of functors defined on that category. It is named after the Japanese mathematician Nobuo Yoneda. The lemma shows that any object of a category is completely determined by the network of morphisms relating it to all other objects.

Statement

Let C be a locally small category, A an object of C, and F a functor from C to the category of sets. Then there is a bijection, natural in both A and F:

Nat(Hom(A, –), F) ≅ F(A)

between the natural transformations from the hom-functor Hom(A, –) to F, and the elements of the set F(A). The bijection sends a natural transformation to its component at A applied to the identity morphism of A.

Consequences

Taking F itself to be a hom-functor Hom(B, –) gives the corollary that morphisms A → B correspond bijectively to natural transformations Hom(A, –) → Hom(B, –); hence the Yoneda embedding of C into the functor category is full and faithful. The lemma underlies the philosophy that an object is known by all of its relationships — "tell me your morphisms and I will know your object" — and it is used throughout algebraic geometry, representation theory, and logic, for example in the theory of representable functors and in the study of presheaves.

History

Yoneda communicated the result in the 1950s; Saunders Mac Lane famously recalled learning of it from Yoneda at Paris's Gare du Nord railway station, and the lemma appeared in print in Yoneda's 1960 work on abelian categories. It is sometimes described as "the hardest lemma to prove and the easiest to use", since its proof is short but its implications are far-reaching.
