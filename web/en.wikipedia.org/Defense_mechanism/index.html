In psychoanalytic theory, a defense mechanism (British spelling: defence mechanism) is an unconscious psychological operation that functions to protect a person from anxiety-producing thoughts and feelings related to internal conflicts and external stressors. Defenses are a normal part of psychological functioning; they become clinically significant when used rigidly or excessively.

Theoretical background

Sigmund Freud proposed that the ego mediates between the instinctual demands of the id, the moral constraints of the superego, and external reality. When this mediation threatens to fail and anxiety arises, the ego deploys defenses. Anna Freud's The Ego and the Mechanisms of Defence (1936) catalogued the mechanisms systematically and emphasised their adaptive as well as defensive roles.

Vaillant's hierarchy

Psychiatrist George Eman Vaillant organised defenses into four levels of maturity. Level I (pathological) defenses, such as delusional projection and psychotic denial, grossly distort reality. Level II (immature) defenses include acting out, hypochondriasis, passive aggression, and fantasy; they are common in adolescence and in some personality disorders. Level III (neurotic) defenses include intellectualization, repression, displacement, and reaction formation. Level IV (mature) defenses — altruism, humor, sublimation, suppression, and anticipation — integrate conflicting feelings and are associated with good long-term functioning in Vaillant's longitudinal studies of adult development.

Clinical and research use

Defensive functioning is assessed in psychodynamic case formulation and was included in DSM-IV as a proposed axis and scale. Outside psychoanalysis, research psychologists have studied specific defenses experimentally — for example, perceptual defense, projection of traits, and motivated reasoning — and the management of anxiety more broadly under concepts such as coping strategies. Critics argue that some classic mechanisms, especially repression of traumatic memory, lack strong empirical support, while others translate readily into mainstream cognitive and social psychology.
