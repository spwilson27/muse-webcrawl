Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225 – 7 March 1274) was an Italian Dominican friar and priest, the foremost scholastic thinker of the Middle Ages, whose synthesis of Christian theology and Aristotelian philosophy — Thomism — became the foundation of Catholic doctrine. He is honoured as a saint and Doctor of the Church.

Life

Born at Roccasecca in the Kingdom of Sicily, Thomas joined the Dominican Order against his family's wishes — relatives detained him for over a year to dissuade him. He studied under Albertus Magnus at Paris and Cologne, taught at the University of Paris, and spent his final years in Naples organising a Dominican studium.

Works and thought

His vast, unfinished Summa Theologiae treats the whole of theology in a rigorous question format (objections, sed contra, response, replies). Aquinas argued that faith and reason are complementary, and presented five proofs of God's existence — the "Five Ways" — from motion, causation, contingency, degrees of perfection and final causes. His natural law ethics, doctrine of the analogy of being, and accounts of the virtues and the sacraments shaped Catholic thought for centuries; Pope Leo XIII's encyclical Aeterni Patris (1879) revived Thomism as official philosophy.

Legacy

Canonised in 1323 and named a Doctor of the Church in 1567, Aquinas remains one of the most studied of all medieval philosophers, influential in metaphysics, ethics, law and political theory.
