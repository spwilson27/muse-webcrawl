Tarmacadam

Tarmacadam, commonly shortened to tarmac, is a road-surfacing material made of crushed stone coated with tar. It takes its name from its inventor, the Scottish engineer John Loudon McAdam, whose "macadam" method of road building — using layers of angular crushed stone compacted into a firm, drained surface — revolutionised road construction in the early nineteenth century.

History

McAdam's original roads used no binder; the stone layers locked together under traffic. To reduce dust and stone loss, road builders began coating the surface with tar. In 1901 the Welsh engineer Edgar Purnell Hooley, after noticing that spilled tar mixed with slag had produced a dust-free, durable surface near an ironworks, patented tarmacadam — crushed slag or stone bound with tar — and founded the company that gave "Tarmac" its brand fame.

Modern usage

In Britain and Ireland "tarmac" remains the everyday word for paved road and airport surfaces, although most modern surfaces are technically asphalt concrete, in which bitumen (a petroleum product) rather than coal tar binds the aggregate. Airport aprons and runways are still widely called "the tarmac", especially in journalism, a usage aviation professionals sometimes resist. The material's advantages include rapid laying, smooth finish, and ease of repair; its weaknesses include softening in extreme heat and susceptibility to fuel spills on airfields.
