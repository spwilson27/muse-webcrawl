Biotherapy

Biotherapy is the medical use of living organisms, or substances derived from them, to treat disease. The term covers a wide range of practices, from the ancient use of maggots and leeches in wound care to modern immunotherapies and biopharmaceutical drugs produced by living cells. In some contexts "biotherapy" is used as a synonym for immunotherapy or biological therapy, particularly in oncology.

Medicinal leeches remain in clinical use for relieving venous congestion after surgery.

Forms

Maggot therapy

Maggot debridement therapy introduces disinfected larvae of the green bottle fly (Lucilia sericata) into non-healing wounds, where they consume necrotic tissue, disinfect the wound and stimulate healing. It was cleared for medical use in the United States in 2004.

Leech therapy

Medicinal leeches (Hirudo medicinalis) are used in plastic and reconstructive surgery to relieve venous congestion in grafted tissue; their saliva contains the anticoagulant hirudin.

Helminthic and other therapies

Experimental approaches include helminthic therapy for autoimmune conditions, bacteriophage therapy against antibiotic-resistant infections, and faecal microbiota transplantation for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection. In oncology, biological therapies include monoclonal antibodies, cytokines such as interleukin-2 and interferon, cancer vaccines and engineered T-cell (CAR-T) treatments.
