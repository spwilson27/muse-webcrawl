W. T. Tutte


Born: William Thomas Tutte, 14 May 1917, Newmarket, England
Died: 2 May 2002, Waterloo, Ontario, Canada
Fields: Mathematics, cryptanalysis
Known for: Graph theory; breaking the Lorenz cipher; Tutte polynomial

William Thomas Tutte (14 May 1917 – 2 May 2002) was a British and Canadian codebreaker and mathematician. During the Second World War he made a brilliant and fundamental advance in the cryptanalysis of the Lorenz cipher, a major German cipher system, and after the war he became one of the founders of modern graph theory.

Codebreaking

Working at Bletchley Park from 1941, Tutte was assigned to the Lorenz SZ40/42 teleprinter cipher used by the German Army high command. From a single intercepted message that had been retransmitted with the same settings, Tutte deduced the entire internal structure of the machine — its twelve wheels, their cam patterns and stepping behaviour — without ever seeing the device. His statistical method (the "1+2 break in") exploited non-randomness in the cipher's output and led to the construction of the Colossus computers, used to break Lorenz traffic at scale. The work remained secret for decades; Tutte's contributions were publicly recognised only late in his life.

Graph theory

Tutte's undergraduate work on "squaring the square" led to a lifelong focus on combinatorics. At the University of Toronto and later the University of Waterloo, where he helped build the Faculty of Mathematics, he proved foundational results including the Tutte theorem on perfect matchings, the Tutte–Berge formula, work on graph colouring and flows (including his flow conjectures), and the Tutte polynomial, now a central invariant in combinatorics. He was a Fellow of the Royal Society of London and of the Royal Society of Canada.
