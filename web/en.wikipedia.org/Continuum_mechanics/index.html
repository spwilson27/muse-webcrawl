Continuum mechanics

Continuum mechanics is a branch of mechanics that deals with the mechanical behavior of materials modelled as a continuous mass rather than as discrete particles. The French physicist Augustin-Louis Cauchy was the first to formulate such models in the 19th century. In continuum mechanics a body is idealized as filling the space it occupies, ignoring the fact that matter is made of atoms, provided the length scales of interest are much larger than atomic dimensions (i.e., the Knudsen number is small).

Basic concepts

The motion of a continuum is described by a displacement field, and its internal state by the deformation gradient. Deformation is quantified by strain tensors, and internal forces by the stress tensor introduced by Cauchy. The governing equations combine the balance laws — conservation of mass, balance of linear and angular momentum, and the laws of thermodynamics — with constitutive equations that describe the specific response of a material.

Branches

Continuum mechanics divides into the study of solids and fluids. Solid mechanics uses a reference configuration and includes elasticity, plasticity and viscoelasticity. Fluid mechanics adopts the current configuration and includes the study of Newtonian and non-Newtonian fluids; its best-known equations, the Navier–Stokes equations, govern viscous flow. Rheology studies materials with both solid and fluid characteristics. The framework underlies most of structural, geotechnical, aeronautical and biomedical engineering analysis.
