George Ellery Hale

George Ellery Hale (June 29, 1868 – February 21, 1938) was an American astrophysicist, best known for his discovery of magnetic fields in sunspots, and as the leader or key figure in the planning and construction of several world-leading telescopes: the 40-inch refracting telescope at Yerkes Observatory, the 60-inch and 100-inch reflecting telescopes at Mount Wilson Observatory, and the 200-inch Hale Telescope at Palomar Observatory.

Career

Born in Chicago to a wealthy family, Hale invented the spectroheliograph as an undergraduate at MIT, allowing astronomers to photograph the Sun in the light of a single element. He founded Yerkes Observatory for the University of Chicago in 1897 and later persuaded the Carnegie Institution to fund Mount Wilson. In 1908 he demonstrated that sunspots are regions of intense magnetic field — the first detection of a magnetic field beyond Earth — and later characterized the polarity laws of sunspot pairs known as Hale's law.

Institution building

Hale was a tireless organizer of science. He founded The Astrophysical Journal, helped transform the California Institute of Technology into a leading research university, co-founded the Huntington Library's research programme, and was instrumental in creating the National Research Council during the First World War. He also championed the Pasadena Civic Center and other civic projects. The 200-inch telescope at Palomar, completed after his death and dedicated in 1948, was named the Hale Telescope in his honour; for three decades it was the largest effective telescope in the world.
