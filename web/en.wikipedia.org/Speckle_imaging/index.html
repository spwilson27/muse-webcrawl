Speckle imaging

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Speckle imaging comprises a range of high-resolution astronomical imaging techniques based on the analysis of large numbers of short-exposure images through the shifting atmosphere. They can be divided into two categories: those that use speckle masks (aperture masking and speckle interferometry), and those that use image reconstruction algorithms such as shift-and-add and lucky imaging. These techniques can dramatically increase the resolution attainable with ground-based telescopes, approaching the diffraction limit.

Principles

Atmospheric turbulence distorts the wavefront of light from a star, breaking its image into many shifting "speckles" in exposures of a few milliseconds. Although any single short exposure looks noisy, the speckles contain information at spatial frequencies up to the telescope's diffraction limit. Statistical analysis of many frames — for example, computing autocorrelation functions or power spectra (Labeyrie's speckle interferometry, proposed in 1970) — recovers high-resolution information lost in a long exposure.

Methods

Speckle interferometry recovers the modulus of the object's Fourier transform, permitting measurement of binary star separations and stellar diameters. Bispectral analysis and speckle masking recover phase information and produce true images. Shift-and-add aligns the brightest speckle of each frame before stacking, while lucky imaging selects only the sharpest fraction of frames.

Applications

Speckle imaging has been used to resolve close binary stars, measure asteroid shapes and sizes, image the surfaces of giant stars, and study the cores of galaxies. Modern speckle cameras on large telescopes routinely reach resolutions of tens of milliarcseconds in visible light, complementing adaptive optics systems.
