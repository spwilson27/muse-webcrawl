A studio backlot is a large outdoor area at the rear of a film studio containing permanent or semi-permanent exterior sets used for filming movies and television shows. Backlots typically feature streets of false-front buildings — facades supported by scaffolding with little or no interior — that can be redressed to represent different cities, eras, and countries without the cost and disruption of location shooting.

History

Backlots grew up with the Hollywood studio system in the 1910s–1930s, as major studios bought large tracts of land in the Los Angeles area and built standing sets: New York streets, western towns, European villages, and jungle lagoons, often adjacent to movie ranches used for open landscapes. Iconic sets include the courthouse square used in To Kill a Mockingbird and Back to the Future, and the suburban street and "Colonial Street" on the Universal lot, home to the houses of Desperate Housewives' Wisteria Lane.

Design and use

Backlot buildings are usually shells: only the camera-facing side is finished, and interiors are shot separately on sound stages. Sets are designed for forced perspective and easy redressing — changing signage, paint, and set dressing can turn the same street from 1890s Chicago to modern New York. Backlots also include water tanks, process screens, and open "sky backing" areas.

Decline and preservation

From the 1950s onward, rising land values, the breakup of the studio system, and the fashion for location realism led many studios to sell or redevelop backlot land; the MGM backlot, for example, was largely turned into housing. Surviving backlots remain working production spaces and popular studio-tour attractions, and several have been recognized as historically significant.
