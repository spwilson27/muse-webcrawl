OriginAppalachia and the rural American South; roots in British, Irish and African traditions
InstrumentsFiddle, banjo (clawhammer), guitar, mandolin, double bass, dulcimer

Old-time music is a genre of North American folk music rooted in the traditions of the Appalachian Mountains and the rural South. It developed from the dance tunes, ballads and songs brought by English, Scottish and Irish settlers, blended with African American musical practices — most notably the banjo, an instrument of African origin. Old-time is primarily dance and participatory music, centred on the fiddle.

Characteristics

Old-time ensembles ("string bands") typically combine fiddle, five-string banjo played in the clawhammer or "frailing" style, guitar and often mandolin or bass. Tunes are short, repetitive and played for square dances; regional styles — from Round Peak, North Carolina, to the Ozarks and West Virginia — differ in bowing, repertoire and rhythm. Unlike bluegrass, which emerged from old-time in the 1940s, old-time emphasises ensemble groove over solo virtuosity.

History and revival

Commercial recordings of old-time musicians such as Fiddlin' John Carson (1923) and the Skillet Lickers launched the early country music industry. The folk revival of the 1950s and 1960s, through figures like the New Lost City Ramblers, carried the music to new audiences, and festivals and fiddlers' conventions — such as those at Galax, Virginia, and Clifftop, West Virginia — sustain a thriving participatory culture today.
