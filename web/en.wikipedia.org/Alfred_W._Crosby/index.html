Alfred Worcester Crosby Jr. (15 January 1931 – 14 March 2018) was an American historian and professor of history, geography and American studies at the University of Texas at Austin. A pioneer of environmental history, he is best known for The Columbian Exchange (1972), which introduced the concept of the vast transfer of plants, animals and diseases between the Old and New Worlds after 1492.

Career and works

Crosby studied at Harvard and Boston University and taught at Washington State University before moving to Texas in 1982. The Columbian Exchange: Biological and Cultural Consequences of 1492 showed how Eurasian diseases such as smallpox devastated Native American populations, while American crops such as maize and potatoes reshaped diets and demography worldwide. He extended the argument in Ecological Imperialism: The Biological Expansion of Europe, 900–1900 (1986), explaining European colonisation of the temperate "Neo-Europes" — the Americas, Australia and New Zealand — through the accompanying portmanteau biota of crops, livestock, weeds and pathogens.

Other work

Crosby also wrote America's Forgotten Pandemic (1976; later Epidemic and Peace, 1918), a landmark history of the 1918 influenza pandemic, Throwing Fire (2002), on projectile technology, and Children of the Sun (2006), a history of human energy use. His work helped make the environment a central subject of historical inquiry and influenced later popular histories of disease and empire.
