Mahamudra

Mahāmudrā (Sanskrit: महामुद्रा, literally "the Great Seal" or "Great Symbol") is a system of meditation and associated teachings within Indian and Tibetan Buddhism aimed at direct realization of the nature of mind. The term denotes that all phenomena are sealed by, or inseparable from, their true nature — emptiness and luminosity. Mahāmudrā became especially central to the Kagyu school of Tibetan Buddhism, while a parallel tradition was preserved in the Gelug school through the Gelug–Kagyu transmission of Panchen Losang Chökyi Gyaltsen.

Origins

The tradition traces its Indian sources to the mahāsiddhas of the eighth to twelfth centuries, particularly Tilopa, who is said to have received teachings from the Buddha Vajradhara, and his disciple Nāropa, followed by Marpa the Translator, who brought the teachings to Tibet, and Milarepa. The Ganges Mahāmudrā instruction attributed to Tilopa and the songs (dohā) of Saraha are foundational texts.

Practice

Mahāmudrā practice is commonly organised into ground, path, and fruition. Preliminary practices (ngöndro) are followed by śamatha (calm abiding) and vipaśyanā (insight) directed at mind itself: the practitioner rests in awareness, investigates the nature of thoughts and of the one who thinks, and recognises mind's empty, luminous nature. The Kagyu presentation of Gampopa distinguishes sūtra Mahāmudrā, tantra (mantra) Mahāmudrā, and essence Mahāmudrā, allowing the teaching to be given outside full tantric empowerment in some lineages — a point of historical controversy.

Relationship to Dzogchen

Mahāmudrā is often compared with Dzogchen, the highest teaching of the Nyingma school; both emphasise direct recognition of mind's nature, and modern teachers frequently present them as converging in practice while differing in terminology and framework.
