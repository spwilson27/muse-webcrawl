Lübeck (German: [ˈlyːbɛk]), officially the Hanseatic City of Lübeck, is a city in Northern Germany. With around 217,000 inhabitants, it is the second-largest city on the German Baltic coast and in the state of Schleswig-Holstein. From the 14th century it was the leading city — the "Queen" — of the Hanseatic League, and its medieval old town is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

History

Lübeck was founded in 1143 by Adolf II of Holstein on the site of an earlier Slavic settlement and received its town charter from Henry the Lion in 1159. Its law (Lübeck law) was adopted by over 100 Baltic towns. As the hub of Hanseatic trade between the North Sea, the Baltic and Novgorod, Lübeck grew rich on salt, herring and cloth. It remained a free imperial city and later a sovereign state of the German Confederation until 1937, when the Greater Hamburg Act stripped it of statehood and incorporated it into Prussia's Schleswig-Holstein province.

The Palm Sunday bombing raid of March 1942 destroyed about a fifth of the old town. Lübeck also marked the inner-German border during the Cold War, its eastern edge directly adjoining East Germany.

Sights and culture

Landmarks include the Holstentor brick gate, the seven spires of its five main churches (including St Mary's, a model for Baltic brick Gothic), the salt warehouses (Salzspeicher), Thomas Mann's Buddenbrookhaus, and the European Hansemuseum. Lübeck is famed for its marzipan, produced by firms such as Niederegger since the 19th century.
