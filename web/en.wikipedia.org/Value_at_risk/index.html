Value at risk

Value at risk (VaR) is a measure of the risk of loss of investment. It estimates how much a set of investments might lose (with a given probability), given normal market conditions, in a set time period such as a day. VaR is typically used by firms and regulators in the financial industry to gauge the amount of assets needed to cover possible losses.

Definition

For a given portfolio, time horizon, and probability p, the p VaR is defined as a threshold loss value, such that the probability that the loss on the portfolio over the given time horizon exceeds this value (assuming normal markets and no trading in the portfolio) is the given probability level. For example, a one-day 95% VaR of $1 million means there is a 5% chance the portfolio will lose more than $1 million over one day.

Pr(Loss > VaR) = 1 − p

Calculation methods

VaR can be computed by three principal methods: the historical method, which replays past returns; the variance–covariance (parametric) method, which assumes returns are normally distributed; and Monte Carlo simulation, which generates many hypothetical scenarios. Each approach involves trade-offs between simplicity, data requirements, and the ability to capture nonlinear risks.

Criticism

VaR has been widely criticised, particularly after the financial crisis of 2007–2008. It fails to describe losses in the tail beyond the quantile, and it is not a coherent risk measure because it can violate subadditivity: the VaR of a combined portfolio can exceed the sum of the VaRs of its parts. Regulators have increasingly supplemented or replaced VaR with expected shortfall, the average loss beyond the VaR threshold.
