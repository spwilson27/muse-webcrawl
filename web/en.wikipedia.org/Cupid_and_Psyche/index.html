Cupid and Psyche is a story originally from Metamorphoses (also called The Golden Ass), written in the 2nd century AD by Lucius Apuleius Madaurensis (or Platonicus). The tale concerns the love between the god Cupid and the mortal princess Psyche, whose Greek name means "soul" — making the story, in later interpretation, an allegory of the soul's trials and eventual union with divine love.

Plot

Psyche, the youngest of three daughters of a king, is so beautiful that worshippers neglect Venus, who in jealousy orders her son Cupid to make Psyche fall in love with a monster. Instead Cupid falls in love with her himself and has her carried to a secluded palace, where he visits her only at night, forbidding her to look at him. Her jealous sisters persuade her that her husband is a serpent; Psyche lights a lamp while he sleeps, and a drop of hot oil wakes him. Cupid flees, and Psyche wanders the earth searching for him.

The tasks

Venus imposes four impossible tasks: sorting a heap of mixed grains (accomplished with the help of ants); gathering golden wool from fierce sheep; filling a flask with water from the Styx (an eagle helps); and descending to the underworld to fetch a box of Persephone's beauty. Psyche completes the first three but opens the box on her return and falls into a deathlike sleep. Cupid rescues her, Jupiter grants Psyche immortality, and the two are married on Olympus; their daughter is Voluptas (Pleasure).

Influence

The tale is one of the most retold in Western art and literature, inspiring sculptures by Canova and Rodin, paintings by Gérôme and Waterhouse, and C. S. Lewis's novel Till We Have Faces. Folklorists classify its structure within the "search for the lost husband" cycle (Aarne–Thompson type 425), related to tales such as "Beauty and the Beast" and "East of the Sun and West of the Moon".
