Born coordinates

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In relativistic physics, the Born coordinate chart is a coordinate chart for (part of) Minkowski spacetime, the flat spacetime of special relativity. The chart describes observers who rotate rigidly around an axis in the sense of Born rigidity, and it was first discussed by Max Born and by Georges Lemaître and others in the context of the Ehrenfest paradox of a rotating disk.

Definition

Starting from an inertial frame with cylindrical coordinates (t, r, φ, z), Born coordinates (t, r, φ', z) are defined by allowing the azimuthal coordinate to rotate uniformly: φ = φ' + Ωt, where Ω is a constant angular velocity. The worldlines of observers at fixed r, φ', z are helices in spacetime. The chart is valid only inside the light cylinder at r = 1/Ω (in units where c = 1), beyond which the putative observers would have to move faster than light.

Born rigidity and the rotating disk

A material disk cannot be spun up from rest while maintaining Born rigidity — the requirement that the distance between neighboring points, measured in their instantaneous rest frames, remains constant — because the circumference and radius transform differently. This is the heart of the Ehrenfest paradox of 1909, and its analysis clarified that in relativity a "rigid body" in the classical sense is impossible. Observers on a rotating platform also experience the Sagnac effect: light signals sent around the rim in opposite directions return at different coordinate times, so clocks on the rim cannot be consistently synchronized.

The Langevin metric

By quotienting Minkowski spacetime by the timelike helical Killing field of the rotating observers — eliminating the time coordinate — one obtains the Langevin–Landau–Lifschitz metric, a spatial geometry of the rotating platform that is non-Euclidean: the ratio of circumference to radius measured by the rotating observers exceeds 2π. This construction underlies the relativistic treatment of rotating frames such as the Earth, relevant to GPS corrections and ring laser gyroscopes.
