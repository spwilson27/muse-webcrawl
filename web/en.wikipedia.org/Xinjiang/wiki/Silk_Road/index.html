Silk Road

Silk Road
Active2nd century BCE – mid-15th century CE
SpanChang'an (Xi'an), China to Constantinople and Europe

The Silk Road was a network of trade routes connecting East Asia with Central Asia, the Middle East and Europe for some fifteen centuries. Named in the 19th century by geographer Ferdinand von Richthofen for the lucrative Chinese silk trade, it carried far more than silk.

Routes

From Chang'an the road reached Dunhuang and split around the Taklamakan Desert — northern and southern routes through oasis towns such as Turpan, Kashgar and Khotan in present-day Xinjiang, home to peoples including the Uyghurs — then crossed the Pamirs to Samarkand, Bukhara, Persia and the Mediterranean. Maritime routes linked China by sea to India, Arabia and East Africa.

Exchange

Along the routes moved goods (silk, spices, glass, paper, gunpowder, jade), technologies, religions — Buddhism, Islam, Nestorian Christianity, Manichaeism — art, music, and unfortunately disease, including plague. The Han envoy Zhang Qian's journeys (138–125 BCE) are traditionally credited with opening the route; the Tang dynasty and later the Pax Mongolica brought periods of flourishing trade, recounted in the West by Marco Polo.

Decline and revival

Sea trade, the fall of Constantinople in 1453 and political fragmentation reduced the overland routes. In the 21st century China revived the name in its Belt and Road Initiative, and UNESCO lists Silk Roads heritage corridors, including sites near Ürümqi and Turpan.
