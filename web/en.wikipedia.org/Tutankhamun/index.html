Tutankhamun (c. 1341 – c. 1323 BC), commonly referred to as King Tut, was an Egyptian pharaoh of the 18th Dynasty who reigned from about 1332 BC until his death at about eighteen or nineteen. Son of the "heretic king" Akhenaten, he ascended the throne as a child under the name Tutankhaten and presided over the abandonment of his father's Atenist revolution and the restoration of the traditional gods—reflected in his change of name to Tutankhamun, "Living Image of Amun".

Reign and death

Guided by officials including the vizier Ay and the general Horemheb, the young king issued a Restoration Stela, reopened temples and returned the court from Amarna to Thebes. The cause of his early death remains debated: studies of his mummy have reported a leg fracture, malaria infection and congenital conditions, possibly aggravated by inbreeding within the royal family. He was succeeded by Ay and then Horemheb.

The tomb

Tutankhamun was a minor king whose fame rests on his tomb, KV62 in the Valley of the Kings, discovered largely intact by Howard Carter in November 1922 under the patronage of Lord Carnarvon. Its more than 5,000 objects—including the solid gold funerary mask, nested shrines, chariots and a dagger of meteoritic iron—gave an unmatched view of New Kingdom royal burial and sparked a worldwide Egyptomania. Most of the treasure is now displayed at the Grand Egyptian Museum near Giza. Stories of a "pharaoh's curse" followed the deaths of several excavation associates, though statistics do not support an unusual mortality rate.
