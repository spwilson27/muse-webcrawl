The 1876 United States presidential election was the 23rd quadrennial presidential election, held on Tuesday, November 7, 1876. It was one of the most contentious presidential elections in American history. Democratic nominee Samuel J. Tilden of New York won a majority of the popular vote, but Republican nominee Rutherford B. Hayes of Ohio ultimately prevailed in the Electoral College by a single vote, 185 to 184.

The dispute

Twenty electoral votes from Florida, Louisiana, South Carolina, and Oregon were disputed, with rival slates of electors submitted from the Southern states. With Tilden holding 184 undisputed votes — one short of a majority — and Hayes 165, the outcome hinged entirely on the contested returns.

Congress created a bipartisan Electoral Commission of fifteen members — five senators, five representatives, and five Supreme Court justices — which awarded all twenty disputed votes to Hayes in a series of 8–7 party-line decisions.

Compromise of 1877

Democrats accepted the outcome in what became known as the Compromise of 1877: in exchange for recognizing Hayes as president, Republicans agreed to withdraw the remaining federal troops from the South, effectively ending Reconstruction. Hayes was inaugurated on March 5, 1877.

The election remains the only one in which a candidate won an outright majority of the popular vote but lost the presidency, and Tilden's popular-vote margin was the largest ever recorded by a losing candidate until 2020 by percentage in some reckonings.
