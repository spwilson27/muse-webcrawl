Elias James Corey (born July 12, 1928) is an American organic chemist. In 1990, he won the Nobel Prize in Chemistry "for his development of the theory and methodology of organic synthesis", specifically retrosynthetic analysis. Widely regarded as one of the greatest synthetic chemists of the twentieth century, he has developed numerous synthetic reagents, methodologies, and total syntheses of complex natural products, and trained generations of leading chemists.

Career

Corey studied at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, completing his bachelor's degree in 1948 and his PhD in 1951. After a faculty position at the University of Illinois at Urbana–Champaign, where he became a full professor at 27, he moved to Harvard University in 1959, where he remains an emeritus professor.

Contributions

Retrosynthetic analysis is Corey's formalised strategy for planning the synthesis of complex molecules: the target is mentally disassembled, step by step, into simpler precursors through "disconnections", until commercially available starting materials are reached. His 1969 synthesis of several prostaglandins, built around the versatile "Corey lactone" intermediate, demonstrated the power of the approach and remains a landmark. His group went on to synthesise scores of complex targets, including ginkgolide B (1988), a total synthesis celebrated for its elegance, and later developed a practical route to the anti-influenza drug oseltamivir (Tamiflu).

Named reactions and methods associated with Corey include the Corey–House synthesis of alkanes, the Corey–Kim oxidation, the Corey–Fuchs reaction, the Corey–Itsuno (CBS) reduction for enantioselective ketone reduction, and the Corey–Chaykovsky reaction. His work helped establish computer-assisted synthesis design (the LHASA program) and advanced the logic of chemical synthesis as a discipline in its own right.
