History of painting

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The history of painting reaches back to prehistoric cave art—some of the oldest known figurative paintings, in Sulawesi and in caves such as Chauvet and Lascaux in France, are between roughly 17,000 and 45,000 years old—and continues through the art of the ancient world, the Byzantine and medieval traditions, the Renaissance, the Baroque, and the succession of movements that defines modern and contemporary art. Painting developed in parallel in East Asia, above all China, with its own traditions of ink and colour on silk and paper.

Ancient and medieval painting

Egyptian tomb painting, Minoan and Roman frescoes (preserved at Pompeii), early Christian catacomb painting, and Byzantine icons and mosaics established conventions of representation that lasted for centuries. In the Latin West, painting flourished in illuminated manuscripts and, from the 12th century, in panel painting for altarpieces. Giotto di Bondone (c. 1267–1337) is traditionally credited with breaking from the Byzantine manner toward naturalism, mass, and emotion.

The Renaissance

In 15th-century Florence, the invention of linear perspective (Brunelleschi, Alberti) and the study of anatomy and light transformed painting: Masaccio, Piero della Francesca, Botticelli, Leonardo da Vinci, Michelangelo, and Raphael produced the High Renaissance synthesis of classical ideals and observed nature. In Northern Europe, Jan van Eyck perfected oil painting, enabling unprecedented detail and luminosity; Dürer, Holbein, and Bruegel carried Renaissance art north of the Alps. Venice, with Titian, Giorgione, and Tintoretto, prized colour (colorito) over Florentine drawing (disegno).

Baroque to Romanticism

The 17th century's Baroque painting—Caravaggio's dramatic chiaroscuro, Rubens's exuberant flesh, Rembrandt's introspective mastery, Velázquez's court realism, Poussin's classical order—served Counter-Reformation churches and absolutist courts. The 18th century moved from the decorative Rococo (Watteau, Fragonard, Boucher) to Neoclassicism (David) reacting to revolution and antiquity. Romanticism (Géricault, Delacroix, Friedrich, Turner, Goya) exalted emotion, nature, and the sublime, while academic painting and the Pre-Raphaelites offered rival paths.

Modern painting

From the 1860s, Impressionism (Monet, Renoir, Degas, Cézanne in a different direction) broke with academic finish to capture light and modern life; Post-Impressionism (Van Gogh, Gauguin, Seurat) pushed toward expression and structure. The early 20th century exploded into movements: Fauvism, Expressionism, Cubism (Picasso and Braque), Futurism, abstraction (Kandinsky, Mondrian, Malevich), Dada, and Surrealism. After 1945 the centre shifted to New York with Abstract Expressionism (Pollock, Rothko, de Kooning), followed by Pop art (Warhol, Lichtenstein), Minimalism, and Conceptual art's challenge to painting itself.

Non-Western traditions

Chinese painting, documented from the Han dynasty and theorised by Xie He in the 6th century, developed the scholar-amateur ideal of ink painting, landscape (shanshui), and calligraphy. Japanese painting adapted Chinese models into screen painting and ukiyo-e prints, which in turn influenced European modernism. Indian miniature painting, Persian miniatures, Islamic manuscript illumination, and the painting traditions of Africa, Oceania, and the pre-Columbian Americas each constitute major histories of their own.

Contemporary painting

Despite repeated declarations of the "death of painting", the medium remains central to contemporary art, encompassing photorealism, neo-expressionism (Basquiat, Kiefer), appropriation, and digitally assisted practice, alongside revivals of figuration in the 21st century and a globalised art market in which painters from every continent participate.
