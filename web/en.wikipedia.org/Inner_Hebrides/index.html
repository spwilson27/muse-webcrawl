The Inner Hebrides (Scottish Gaelic: Na h-Eileanan a-staigh) are an archipelago off the west coast of mainland Scotland, to the south east of the Outer Hebrides. Together the two island chains form the Hebrides. The Inner Hebrides comprise 35 inhabited islands and 44 uninhabited islands with an area greater than 30 hectares (74 acres).

Geography

The largest islands are Skye, Mull and Islay. Other notable islands include Jura, Rum, Eigg, Coll, Tiree, Colonsay, Lismore, Iona and Staffa. The terrain ranges from the jagged Cuillin mountains of Skye to the low, fertile machair of Tiree. The climate is mild for the latitude, influenced by the North Atlantic Drift, though wet and windy.

History

The islands were settled in prehistoric times and later formed part of the Gaelic kingdom of Dál Riata. From the 9th century they came under Norse influence as part of the Kingdom of the Isles, passing to Scotland by the Treaty of Perth in 1266. The Lordship of the Isles, centred on Islay, was a semi-independent Gaelic power until its forfeiture in 1493. Iona was an early centre of Christianity, founded by St Columba in 563, and a major pilgrimage site. In the 18th and 19th centuries many inhabitants emigrated or were displaced during the Highland Clearances.

Economy and culture

Tourism, whisky distilling (notably on Islay and at Talisker on Skye), fishing, aquaculture and crofting are the mainstays of the island economies. Scottish Gaelic remains spoken by a significant minority. The islands are noted for wildlife, including golden eagles, sea eagles, red deer and marine life around the Sound of Mull.
