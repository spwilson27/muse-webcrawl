Shōmyō

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Shōmyō (Japanese: 声明) is the traditional style of Japanese Buddhist chant, in which sutras and ritual texts are sung to elaborate melodic formulas. The word derives from the Sanskrit śabda-vidyā, one of the five traditional sciences of India. Introduced to Japan from China — itself transmitting Indian practice — from the Nara period onward, shōmyō became the liturgical music of the Tendai and Shingon schools and remains the foundation of Japanese Buddhist ritual sound.

History

Chant arrived with Buddhism in the sixth century and was systematised in the Heian period, notably by Ennin (Jikaku Daishi) after his study in Tang China, and later by Ryōnin, founder of the Yūzū Nembutsu school. The great centres of Mount Hiei (Tendai) and Mount Kōya (Shingon) preserved distinct repertories and styles of notation and performance.

Styles and notation

Shōmyō is performed a cappella by monks in unison or responsorial chant. Texts are sung in Sanskrit (in Siddhaṃ-derived pronunciation), Chinese, or Japanese, according to the class of piece — bonsan, kansan, and wasan respectively. Melodies are recorded in neumatic notation called hakase, curved lines and dots drawn beside the characters which indicate melodic movement to those already trained in the oral tradition. Performance styles are slow, highly melismatic, and governed by transmitted formulas rather than fixed scales.

Influence

Shōmyō is regarded as the root of much later Japanese music: its modal theory intersects with gagaku court music, and its vocal practices influenced narrative chant and the singing of Noh theatre. In the twentieth century composers such as Mayuzumi Toshirō drew on shōmyō sonorities in modern works.
