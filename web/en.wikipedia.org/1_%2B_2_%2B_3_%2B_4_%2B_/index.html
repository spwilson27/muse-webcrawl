1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + ⋯

The infinite series whose terms are the natural numbers 1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + ⋯ is a divergent series: the partial sums 1, 3, 6, 10, 15, … (the triangular numbers) grow without bound, so the series has no finite sum in the ordinary sense. Nevertheless, methods of summation for divergent series — in particular Ramanujan summation and zeta function regularisation — assign it the value −1/12, a result that appears in number theory and theoretical physics and has attracted wide popular attention.

Partial sums

The nth partial sum is the triangular number

1 + 2 + … + n = n(n + 1) / 2,

which increases without bound as n grows. The series therefore diverges by the term test (its terms do not tend to zero) and by unbounded partial sums.

Summability

The value −1/12 arises rigorously as ζ(−1), the value of the Riemann zeta function at −1 via analytic continuation: since ζ(s) = Σ n−s for Re(s) > 1, formal substitution of s = −1 gives 1 + 2 + 3 + ⋯, and the analytic continuation takes the value −1/12. Srinivasa Ramanujan recorded the result in his first letter to G. H. Hardy in 1913 and developed it in his notebooks using what is now called Ramanujan summation, remarking that if he told Hardy the sum was −1/12, Hardy would think him bound for the lunatic asylum. Simpler heuristics using the related alternating series 1 − 2 + 3 − 4 + ⋯ (whose Abel sum is 1/4) can reproduce −1/12, but these manipulations are not valid proofs, since the series are not absolutely convergent.

Applications in physics

The regularised value appears in physics in the Casimir effect, where ζ(−3) enters the calculation of the attractive force between conducting plates, and in bosonic string theory, where the sum 1 + 2 + 3 + ⋯ helps determine the critical dimension of 26. In these contexts the zeta-function value arises from analytic continuation of physical quantities rather than from literal addition of the divergent series.

In popular culture

A 2014 Numberphile video demonstrating the −1/12 result with informal manipulations was viewed tens of millions of times and drew both enthusiasm and criticism from mathematicians, prompting published clarifications about the precise meaning of the equality.
