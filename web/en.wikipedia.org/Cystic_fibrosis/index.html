Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a genetic disorder inherited in an autosomal recessive manner that impairs the normal clearance of mucus from the lungs, which facilitates the colonisation and infection of the lungs by bacteria, with cycles of inflammation and scarring. Symptoms include persistent coughing, wheezing, exercise intolerance, repeated chest infections, and poor weight gain despite a normal or increased appetite.

Cause

CF is caused by mutations in both copies of the gene encoding the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) protein, a chloride and bicarbonate ion channel. The most common mutation, ΔF508 (F508del), deletes one amino acid and causes the protein to misfold. Defective ion transport leads to dehydrated, thick secretions in the lungs, pancreas, sweat glands, and other organs. Pancreatic insufficiency, diabetes, liver disease, and male infertility are common complications.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is usually by a sweat chloride test and genetic testing; many countries include CF in newborn screening. There is no cure, but treatment includes airway clearance techniques, inhaled therapies, antibiotics, pancreatic enzyme replacement, and, since 2012, CFTR modulator drugs such as ivacaftor and the triple combination elexacaftor/tezacaftor/ivacaftor, which have substantially improved prognosis. Median life expectancy in developed countries has risen into the 50s and beyond.
