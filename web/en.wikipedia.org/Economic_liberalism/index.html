Economic liberalism

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Adam Smith (1723–1790), whose Wealth of Nations (1776) founded modern economic liberalism.

Economic liberalism is a political and economic ideology that supports a market economy based on individualism and private property in the means of production. An economically liberal government intervenes minimally in the economy, limiting itself to protecting property rights, enforcing contracts, and providing some public goods, while allowing prices and production to be determined by supply and demand.

Origins

The philosophy emerged in the Enlightenment, in reaction against mercantilism and feudal privilege. Adam Smith argued in An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations (1776) that individuals pursuing their own interest are led by an "invisible hand" to promote the public good. Nineteenth-century liberals such as Richard Cobden campaigned for free trade, culminating in the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846.

Principles

Central principles include private property, freedom of contract, the rule of law, competition, free trade and laissez-faire — the doctrine that government should leave markets alone. Economic liberals hold that voluntary exchange is mutually beneficial and that economic freedom is both productive and a component of individual liberty.

Variants and criticism

Classical liberalism, neoliberalism and ordoliberalism differ in the role they assign the state in maintaining competition and social stability. Critics argue that unregulated markets produce inequality, instability and monopoly, and that public intervention is needed to correct market failures such as externalities.
