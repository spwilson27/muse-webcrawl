Herman Hollerith

BornFebruary 29, 1860, Buffalo, New York, U.S.
DiedNovember 17, 1929 (aged 69), Washington, D.C., U.S.
OccupationStatistician, inventor, businessman
Known forPunched-card tabulating machine; founder of a predecessor of IBM

Herman Hollerith (February 29, 1860 – November 17, 1929) was a German-American statistician, inventor, and businessman who developed an electromechanical tabulating machine for punched cards to assist in summarizing information. His invention launched the punched-card data processing industry, and the company he founded became a core component of what is now IBM.

The tabulating machine

While working for the United States Census Office, Hollerith was inspired by the observation that train conductors punched tickets to record passenger characteristics. His system encoded data as holes in paper cards; pins in the tabulator passed through the holes to complete electrical circuits and advance counting dials. The machines were used for the 1890 U.S. Census, which was completed far faster than the manually tabulated 1880 census and saved the government millions of dollars. His patents of 1889 were among the foundational patents of information processing.

Company

In 1896 Hollerith founded the Tabulating Machine Company. In 1911 it was merged with three other companies to form the Computing-Tabulating-Recording Company, which in 1924 was renamed International Business Machines (IBM). Hollerith sold his patents to the new firm and served as a consulting engineer until his retirement in 1921.

Legacy

Punched cards — for decades standardized in an 80-column format — remained a dominant data-input medium well into the computer era, making Hollerith a pivotal figure in the prehistory of computing.
