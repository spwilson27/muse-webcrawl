Radiometric dating (or radioactive dating) is a technique used to date materials such as rocks and organic remains by comparing the observed abundance of a naturally occurring radioactive isotope with that of its decay products, which accumulate at a known constant rate. Because each isotope decays with a characteristic half-life, the measured parent-to-daughter ratio yields the time elapsed since the material formed or was last reset.

Methods

Radiocarbon dating uses carbon-14 (half-life 5,730 years) to date once-living material up to about 50,000 years old. Potassium–argon and argon–argon dating date volcanic rocks from thousands to billions of years. Uranium–lead dating of zircon crystals is the most precise method for ancient rocks and established the age of the Earth at about 4.54 billion years (Clair Patterson's 1956 measurement on the Canyon Diablo meteorite). Rubidium–strontium, samarium–neodymium and fission-track methods cover other materials and ranges.

Assumptions and validation

The method requires that decay rates are constant, that the system has remained closed to parent and daughter migration, and that initial daughter content can be estimated — isochron methods test these assumptions internally. Concordant results from independent isotope systems on the same sample give radiometric dating its reliability, underpinning the geological time scale, archaeology and planetary science.
