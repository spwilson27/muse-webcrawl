Peruvian cuisine

Peruvian cuisine reflects local practices and ingredients — including influences from the indigenous Inca and cuisines brought by immigrants from Europe (mainly Spain and Italy), Asia (predominantly China and Japan) and Africa. It is widely regarded as one of the world's great fusion cuisines, and Lima is frequently described as a gastronomic capital of the Americas.

Key ingredients

Peru is the centre of origin of the potato, with several thousand native varieties, and of crops such as quinoa, maize (including giant-kernelled choclo), and many varieties of chili pepper (ají), notably ají amarillo and rocoto. Other staples include sweet potato, yuca, and Andean grains.

Signature dishes

  • Ceviche — raw fish "cooked" in lime juice with onion, chili and salt; Peru's national dish, usually served with sweet potato and corn
  • Lomo saltado — stir-fried beef with onions, tomatoes and chips, a product of Chinese-Peruvian chifa cuisine
  • Ají de gallina — shredded chicken in a creamy, spicy walnut and cheese sauce
  • Anticuchos — grilled skewers, traditionally of beef heart
  • Pachamanca — meats and tubers cooked in an earth oven
  • Papa a la huancaína — potatoes in a spicy cheese sauce

The classic cocktail is the pisco sour, made with pisco brandy. Japanese-Peruvian (Nikkei) fusion produced dishes such as tiradito. In recent decades, chefs such as Gastón Acurio and Virgilio Martínez (whose Lima restaurant Central topped the World's 50 Best list in 2023) have brought global fame to Peruvian cooking.
