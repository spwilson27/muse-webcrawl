Private Finance Initiative

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


A hospital built under the Private Finance Initiative

The Private Finance Initiative (PFI) was a United Kingdom government procurement policy aimed at creating "public–private partnerships" (PPPs) where private firms are contracted to complete and manage public projects. Initially launched in 1992 by Prime Minister John Major, and expanded considerably by the Blair government, PFI is part of the wider programme of privatisation valued by the governments of Margaret Thatcher and John Major in the 1980s and 1990s.

Under PFI, the private sector borrows the money to build a hospital, school, road or other public facility, and the public sector repays the loan over a period of typically 25 to 35 years, together with payments for associated services such as cleaning, catering and maintenance. The total cost of many PFI contracts ran into tens of billions of pounds, and the policy became a major source of controversy over value for money, transparency and flexibility.

History

The initiative was announced in the Autumn Statement of November 1992 by the Chancellor of the Exchequer, Norman Lamont. Early PFI projects included the second Severn Crossing, the Skye Bridge and the first privately financed prisons. Under the Labour governments of Tony Blair and Gordon Brown, PFI expanded rapidly: hundreds of hospitals, schools, roads and defence projects were procured under the model, and PFI accounted for a substantial share of all public capital investment.

In 2012 the Coalition government replaced PFI with PF2, intended to address criticisms by giving the public sector an equity stake and increasing transparency. In October 2018, Chancellor Philip Hammond announced that the government would abolish the use of PFI and PF2 for new projects, although existing contracts, with a capital value of around £60 billion, would continue to 2050 and beyond.

Criticism

Critics, including the National Audit Office and parliamentary committees, argued that PFI projects were frequently more expensive than conventional procurement because private borrowing costs more than government borrowing, and that the long contracts were inflexible. The collapse of the construction and facilities management company Carillion in January 2018, a major PFI contractor, intensified scrutiny of the model. Supporters countered that PFI delivered projects on time and on budget and transferred construction and operational risk to the private sector.
