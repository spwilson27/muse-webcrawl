Divestment

Divestment or divestiture is the reduction of some kind of asset for financial, ethical or political objectives, or the sale of an existing business by a firm. A divestment is the opposite of an investment. In finance, divestment typically refers to a company's sale of a subsidiary, business unit, product line or other asset, often as part of a strategy to focus on core activities, raise capital, or satisfy regulators. In an ethical or political context, divestment refers to the withdrawal of investments from companies or countries whose activities are considered objectionable, as a form of protest or pressure for change.

Corporate divestment

Companies divest assets for many reasons: to raise funds, to eliminate poorly performing or non-core businesses, to reduce debt, to respond to changes in strategy, or to comply with antitrust requirements. The proceeds may be returned to shareholders, reinvested, or used for acquisitions. Divestitures can take several forms:

  • Spin-off — a business unit is separated into a new independent company whose shares are distributed to existing shareholders.
  • Equity carve-out — a minority stake in a subsidiary is sold through an initial public offering.
  • Outright sale — the asset or division is sold to another company or to a private equity buyer, sometimes through a management buyout.

Divestment campaigns

Divestment has frequently been used as a tool of social and political movements. One of the best-known campaigns targeted apartheid South Africa in the 1970s and 1980s, when universities, pension funds, churches and governments sold holdings in companies doing business there. Similar tactics have been used in campaigns concerning tobacco, Sudan, private prisons, and the Israeli–Palestinian conflict (including the Boycott, Divestment and Sanctions movement). In the 2010s, the fossil fuel divestment campaign, encouraged by activists and organisations such as 350.org, pressed institutions to withdraw investments from oil, gas and coal companies on climate-change grounds; by the early 2020s, institutions controlling tens of trillions of dollars in assets had announced some form of commitment.

Effectiveness

The practical effect of divestment is debated. Selling shares transfers ownership to other buyers and may have little direct impact on a company's share price or access to capital, particularly for large firms with liquid stock. Supporters argue that divestment works mainly by stigmatising an industry, shifting public opinion and influencing policymakers; critics argue that engagement as shareholders, or regulation, may be more effective. Economists distinguish between divestment's limited financial effect and its potentially significant political and reputational effects.
