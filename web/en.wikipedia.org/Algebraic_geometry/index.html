Algebraic geometry

Algebraic geometry is a branch of mathematics that studies the geometry of solutions to systems of polynomial equations. Its objects of study, called algebraic varieties, are the sets of points in affine or projective space where a collection of polynomials simultaneously vanish. The subject unifies abstract algebra — in particular commutative algebra, the theory of polynomial rings and their ideals — with geometric intuition about curves, surfaces, and their higher-dimensional analogues.

History

The subject grew out of analytic geometry: René Descartes and Pierre de Fermat showed in the 17th century that geometric curves could be described by algebraic equations. In the 19th century, Bernhard Riemann's study of algebraic functions of one variable, and the Italian school of algebraic surfaces (Castelnuovo, Enriques, Severi), developed rich geometric methods. David Hilbert's Nullstellensatz (1893) connected ideals in polynomial rings with geometric zero sets, laying the algebraic foundations.

In the 20th century Oscar Zariski and André Weil rebuilt the subject on commutative algebra to handle fields of arbitrary characteristic. Alexander Grothendieck's theory of schemes (1960s), developed in the Éléments de géométrie algébrique, generalized varieties so dramatically that algebraic geometry merged with much of number theory; scheme-theoretic methods were essential to Pierre Deligne's proof of the Weil conjectures and to Andrew Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem (via elliptic curves and modular forms).

Basic notions

Affine varieties

Given polynomials f1, …, fk in n variables over a field K, the affine variety V(f1, …, fk) is the set of common zeros in Kn. Hilbert's Nullstellensatz states that ideals of the polynomial ring correspond, in a precise way, to such zero sets when K is algebraically closed. The coordinate ring of a variety — polynomials modulo the ideal of functions vanishing on it — encodes the variety completely.

Projective varieties

Projective varieties are defined by homogeneous polynomials in projective space; they compactify affine varieties by adding "points at infinity," which resolves many pathologies in intersection theory (for example, Bézout's theorem counts intersections correctly in projective space).

Schemes

A scheme is built by gluing spectra of commutative rings, topological spaces whose points correspond to prime ideals and which carry a sheaf of rings. Schemes allow nilpotents (capturing infinitesimal and multiplicity information) and base fields — indeed base rings, such as the integers — so arithmetic and geometry can be treated uniformly in arithmetic geometry.

Applications

Algebraic geometry underlies elliptic-curve cryptography, coding theory (algebraic-geometry codes), robotics (kinematics as polynomial systems), and string theory (Calabi–Yau manifolds, mirror symmetry). Computational methods such as Gröbner bases make many of its constructions effectively computable.
