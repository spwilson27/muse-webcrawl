Maschke's theorem

Maschke's theorem, named after the German mathematician Heinrich Maschke (1853–1908), is a fundamental result in the representation theory of finite groups. It states that if G is a finite group and F is a field whose characteristic does not divide the order of G, then every finite-dimensional representation of G over F is completely reducible — that is, a direct sum of irreducible representations. Equivalently, the group algebra F[G] is semisimple.

Statement

Let V be a finite-dimensional representation of a finite group G over a field F with char(F) ∤ |G|. If W is a G-invariant subspace of V, then there exists a G-invariant subspace W′ such that V = W ⊕ W′. Hence V decomposes as a direct sum of irreducible (simple) representations.

Proof idea

Given any linear projection p : V → W, one averages it over the group to obtain a G-equivariant projection:

P(v) = (1/|G|) Σg∈G g · p(g−1 · v)

The image of P is W and its kernel is the desired invariant complement. The division by |G| is exactly why the hypothesis that the characteristic of F does not divide |G| is essential. When char(F) does divide |G| — the modular case — the theorem fails: for example, the cyclic group of order p over a field of characteristic p has indecomposable representations that are not irreducible.

Significance

Maschke's theorem underlies the classical theory of representations over the complex numbers: it guarantees that studying irreducible representations suffices to understand all finite-dimensional ones, leading to the orthogonality relations for characters and the complete classification of representations of finite groups over ℂ.
