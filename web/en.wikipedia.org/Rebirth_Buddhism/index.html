Rebirth (Buddhism)

The bhavacakra (wheel of life) depicts the six realms of rebirth within cyclic existence, saṃsāra.

In Buddhism, rebirth or reincarnation is the teaching that the actions of a sentient being lead to a new existence after death, in an endless cycle called saṃsāra. This cycle is considered to be dukkha — unsatisfactory and painful. The cycle stops only if one achieves insight into reality and the extinction of craving, attaining nirvana. Rebirth is one of the foundational doctrines of Buddhism, along with karma, nirvana and moksha.

Doctrine

Unlike the Hindu doctrine of reincarnation, Buddhist rebirth does not involve the transmigration of a permanent soul or self. Buddhism teaches anattā (no-self): what continues from one life to the next is not an unchanging essence but a stream of consciousness and karmic dispositions, classically compared to one candle flame lighting another — neither identical nor wholly different. The nature of the next existence is determined by the karma, the intentional actions, of previous lives.

Traditional cosmology describes rebirth into one of several realms: the hells, the realm of hungry ghosts (pretas), animals, humans, asuras, and the heavens of the gods. Human rebirth is held to be especially precious because humans, experiencing both suffering and pleasure, are best placed to hear the dharma and pursue liberation. When an arhat or Buddha dies, they are not reborn, having achieved parinirvana and escaped the cycle.

Evidence and interpretation

Within traditional Buddhism, the Buddha's own recollection of his past lives during the night of his enlightenment is cited as the model for knowledge of rebirth. Some modern and western Buddhists interpret rebirth metaphorically, as moment-to-moment change of the mind, or treat it agnostically; others maintain the traditional literal understanding. Scholars note that rebirth doctrines in Buddhism developed in dialogue with earlier Indian religions that shared the ideas of saṃsāra and karma.
