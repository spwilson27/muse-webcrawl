Milpa

A milpa is a traditional Mesoamerican crop-growing system used throughout Mexico and Central America. In its best-known form it is a polyculture in which maize (corn), beans and squash — the "Three Sisters" — are grown together on the same plot, often with additional crops such as chiles, tomatoes, avocados and agave. The word comes from Nahuatl milli ("field") and pan ("upon"). Milpas have sustained Maya, Nahua and other Indigenous communities for thousands of years and remain central to regional food culture and biodiversity.

In a milpa, beans climb the maize stalks while squash shades the soil.

How it works

The three main crops complement one another ecologically. Maize provides tall stalks for the beans to climb; the beans, as legumes, fix nitrogen in the soil through symbiotic bacteria; and the broad leaves of squash shade the ground, conserving moisture and suppressing weeds. Maize and beans also complement each other nutritionally, together supplying the essential amino acids limiting in each alone. A milpa may pass through stages over years: freshly cleared land is planted, and as soil fertility declines the plot may be converted to orchard or agroforestry before returning to fallow forest — a managed succession rather than abandonment.

Traditional practice

Historically, milpas were often prepared with slash-and-burn (swidden) cultivation: vegetation is cut, dried and burned, and planting follows the first rains. Plots are farmed for a few years and fallowed for longer periods to restore fertility, though population pressure has shortened fallows in many areas. The milpa is embedded in ritual and social life — offerings to rain and earth deities, communal labour exchanges, and seed-keeping traditions that maintain hundreds of native maize landraces.

Ecological and cultural significance

Milpa systems are recognised as reservoirs of agrobiodiversity: Mexico is the centre of origin of maize, and milpas preserve landraces of maize, beans and squash adapted to local conditions, along with numerous semi-wild edible plants (quelites). FAO has recognised the milpa of the Yucatán Peninsula Maya as a Globally Important Agricultural Heritage System. Researchers increasingly study milpa polyculture as a model for sustainable, low-input agriculture and climate resilience.
