Compulsory purchase

Legal doctrineState acquisition of private land without owner's consent
RequirementPublic purpose; compensation to the owner
UK frameworkCompulsory purchase orders; Land Compensation Acts; Acquisition of Land Act 1981
EquivalentsEminent domain (US), expropriation (Canada and others), resumption (Australia)

Compulsory purchase is the power of a public authority to acquire rights over, or ownership of, private land without the agreement of the owner, for purposes authorized by law, in return for compensation. In the United Kingdom and other Commonwealth jurisdictions the term is standard; the closely related doctrine is called eminent domain in the United States and expropriation in Canada.

Land acquired for public infrastructure

Procedure in the United Kingdom

Acquiring authorities — government departments, local councils, and certain statutory undertakers — obtain the power through a compulsory purchase order (CPO) made under an authorizing statute (for example, for highways, housing, planning, or major infrastructure under the Planning Act 2008). The order must be publicized, objections may trigger a public inquiry, and a minister or inspector confirms the order. Title is then transferred, typically by general vesting declaration, and disputes over compensation are determined by the Upper Tribunal (Lands Chamber).

Compensation

The compensation code, largely consolidated in the Land Compensation Acts 1961 and 1973 and the Compulsory Purchase Act 1965, aims to put the owner in the position they would have occupied had the land not been taken ("the principle of equivalence"). Compensation generally comprises the open market value of the land, disturbance payments for losses caused by displacement, severance and injurious affection where only part of a holding is taken, and home-loss or basic-loss payments in defined cases. The "no-scheme" principle disregards increases or decreases in value attributable to the acquiring authority's own project.

Purposes and controversies

Compulsory purchase enables roads, railways, utilities, schools, hospitals, and urban regeneration that could otherwise be blocked by a single hold-out owner or impossible land assembly. It remains controversial where powers are used for private-led regeneration or where displaced owners regard market-value compensation as inadequate — debates echoed internationally, as in the US controversy following Kelo v. City of New London (2005).
