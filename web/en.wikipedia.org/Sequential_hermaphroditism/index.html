Sequential hermaphroditism

Sequential hermaphroditism (called dichogamy in botany) is a type of hermaphroditism that occurs in many fish, gastropods, and plants. Sequential hermaphroditism occurs when the individual changes its sex at some point in its life. In animals, the different types of change are male to female (protandry), female to male (protogyny), female to hermaphrodite, male to hermaphrodite, or hermaphrodite to either single sex.

In fish

Many teleost fish are sequential hermaphrodites. Clownfish are protandrous: social groups consist of a breeding pair in which the dominant individual is female and the second-ranking is male; if the female dies, the male changes sex and the next-largest fish matures as the new male. Wrasses, parrotfish, and many groupers are protogynous: large terminal-phase males are derived from females, and social cues such as the loss of a dominant male trigger the change. Such changes involve complete gonadal restructuring and can occur within days to weeks.

In invertebrates and plants

Common slipper limpets (Crepidula fornicata) live in stacks in which large basal individuals are female and smaller individuals on top are male, transforming as they grow. In plants, dichogamy — the maturation of male and female reproductive organs at different times within a flower or plant — reduces self-pollination; protandrous flowers shed pollen before their stigmas become receptive, while protogynous flowers do the reverse.

Evolutionary explanation

The size-advantage model explains sequential hermaphroditism: if reproductive success increases with size more rapidly for one sex than the other, an individual maximizes lifetime reproduction by beginning life as the sex that does well when small and switching to the other sex when large.
