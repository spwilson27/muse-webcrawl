Cultural anthropology

Cultural anthropology is a branch of anthropology focused on the study of cultural variation among humans. It examines how people in different places live, organise their societies, and make sense of the world, typically through long-term fieldwork and ethnography. In the United States it forms one of the four traditional fields of anthropology, alongside biological anthropology, archaeology and linguistic anthropology; in Britain the closely related discipline is usually called social anthropology.

Methods

The hallmark method of cultural anthropology is participant observation: living within a community for an extended period, learning its language, and taking part in daily life while recording observations. The written product of such research is the ethnography, a detailed account of a particular culture. Bronisław Malinowski's work in the Trobriand Islands and Franz Boas's research among Indigenous peoples of the Pacific Northwest are foundational examples.

Key concepts

Central concepts include culture itself, kinship, ritual, myth, exchange and reciprocity, symbolism, and cultural relativism — the principle that a people's beliefs and practices should be understood in their own context. Major figures include Boas, Malinowski, Ruth Benedict, Margaret Mead, Claude Lévi-Strauss and Clifford Geertz, whose notion of "thick description" shaped interpretive approaches to culture.

Subfields

Subfields include medical anthropology, economic anthropology, political anthropology, anthropology of religion, visual anthropology, feminist anthropology and the anthropology of globalisation.
