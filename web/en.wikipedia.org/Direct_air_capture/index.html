Direct air capture (DAC) is a technology that uses chemical or physical processes to extract carbon dioxide directly from the ambient air. The captured CO₂ can be permanently stored in deep geological formations, a process known as direct air carbon capture and sequestration (DACCS), or used to produce fuels, chemicals, and building materials. When paired with permanent storage, DAC is a form of carbon dioxide removal.

Methods

Two broad approaches dominate. Liquid-solvent systems pass air through alkaline solutions such as potassium hydroxide; the CO₂ is later released by heating a calcium carbonate loop to around 900 °C. Solid-sorbent systems use filters coated with amines or metal-organic frameworks that adsorb CO₂ at ambient temperature and release it under moderate heat and vacuum (temperature-vacuum swing adsorption), as used by the Swiss company Climeworks.

History and deployment

The concept was proposed for climate mitigation by Klaus Lackner in 1999. Climeworks opened the Orca plant in Iceland in 2021 with a capacity of about 4,000 tonnes of CO₂ per year, storing the gas via Carbfix mineralisation in basalt. Its larger Mammoth plant followed in 2024. Occidental Petroleum's subsidiary 1PointFive began building the Stratos plant in Texas, designed for about 500,000 tonnes per year. Costs remain high — commonly estimated at several hundred dollars per tonne — and the energy required is substantial, so climate benefit depends on low-carbon energy supplies.

Criticism

Critics note the enormous scale required relative to annual emissions of roughly 40 billion tonnes, and the risk that DAC is used to justify continued fossil fuel production. Supporters argue that durable removals will be needed to counterbalance residual emissions and eventually reduce atmospheric CO₂ concentrations.
