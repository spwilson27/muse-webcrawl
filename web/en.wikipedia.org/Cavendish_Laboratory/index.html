Cavendish Laboratory

The Cavendish Laboratory is the Department of Physics at the University of Cambridge. It was founded in 1874 under the supervision of James Clerk Maxwell, its first Cavendish Professor, and is named in honour of the experimental physicist Henry Cavendish.

History

The laboratory began on the New Museums Site in central Cambridge and moved to its present West Cambridge site in 1974. Its early professors included Lord Rayleigh, J. J. Thomson, and Ernest Rutherford, under whom the laboratory became a world centre for experimental nuclear physics.

Discoveries

Discoveries made at the Cavendish include the electron (Thomson, 1897), the neutron (James Chadwick, 1932), and the splitting of the atom by accelerated particles (Cockcroft and Walton, 1932). Working at the laboratory, James Watson and Francis Crick determined the double-helix structure of DNA in 1953, drawing on Rosalind Franklin's X-ray diffraction data from King's College London. Researchers associated with the laboratory have won roughly thirty Nobel Prizes.

Present day

The modern laboratory hosts research groups in areas including astrophysics, biological physics, quantum matter, and the physics of sustainability, and a new building, the Ray Dolby Centre, has been constructed to house it.
