Market design is a practical methodology of economics and game theory devoted to creating and fixing markets — designing the rules and institutions by which goods, services, and opportunities are allocated. Unlike traditional economic analysis, which takes market rules as given, market design treats those rules as objects of study that can be engineered to produce desirable outcomes such as efficiency, stability, and fairness. Alvin E. Roth, a leading practitioner, likens market designers to engineers who must make theory work under real-world constraints.

Matching markets

Many important allocations cannot use prices alone: students are matched to schools, medical residents to hospitals, and kidney patients to donors. Building on the Gale–Shapley deferred acceptance algorithm, designers created systems such as the National Resident Matching Program in the United States, centralized school-choice mechanisms in New York City and Boston, and kidney exchange networks that pair incompatible donor–patient pairs across hospitals.

Auctions

Market design also transformed how governments sell assets. Economists including Paul Milgrom and Robert Wilson designed spectrum auctions — notably the simultaneous multiple-round auction used by the U.S. Federal Communications Commission from 1994 — which raised large revenues while assigning licenses efficiently. Auction design has since been applied to electricity markets, treasury securities, online advertising, and emissions permits.

Recognition

The field's founders have received multiple Nobel Memorial Prizes in Economic Sciences: Lloyd Shapley and Alvin Roth (2012) for matching theory, and Paul Milgrom and Robert Wilson (2020) for auction theory.
