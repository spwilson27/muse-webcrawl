Christian unity

Christian unity is the reconciled wholeness of the church of Jesus Christ, understood variously as the visible institutional union of all churches, full communion among separated denominations, or the spiritual unity of all believers. The aspiration is grounded in Jesus's prayer in John 17 "that they may all be one," and in the confession of "one, holy, catholic and apostolic Church" in the Nicene Creed.

The pursuit of Christian unity is the goal of the ecumenical movement

Ecumenism

The modern ecumenical movement is the organized pursuit of Christian unity. It traces its beginnings to the 1910 World Missionary Conference in Edinburgh and developed through the Faith and Order and Life and Work movements, culminating in the formation of the World Council of Churches in 1948. The Roman Catholic Church entered the movement formally with the Second Vatican Council's decree Unitatis Redintegratio (1964).

Approaches

Strategies for unity include bilateral doctrinal dialogues aimed at consensus on historic disputes — such as the 1999 Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification between the Lutheran World Federation and the Catholic Church — full-communion agreements between denominations, and organic church unions, exemplified by the Church of South India (1947) and other united and uniting churches. The landmark Faith and Order text Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry (1982) sought convergence on three classic obstacles to unity. "Reconciled diversity" describes a model in which churches recognize one another's sacraments and ministry without full structural merger.

Obstacles

Persistent obstacles include disagreements over papal authority, apostolic succession, the ordination of women, Eucharistic doctrine, and moral teaching. Pentecostal growth and continuing fragmentation in global Christianity pose further challenges, even as the Week of Prayer for Christian Unity is observed annually by Christians of many traditions.
