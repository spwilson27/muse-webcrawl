Indirect speech

Indirect speech (also called reported speech, indirect discourse or oratio obliqua) is a grammatical mechanism for reporting the content of another utterance without directly quoting it. Thus, "Jane said, 'I am going'" may be reported indirectly as "Jane said (that) she was going." In indirect speech, the form of the reported clause typically changes from its directly quoted form: pronouns shift according to the reporter's perspective, and in English the tense often undergoes "backshift" (present to past, past to past perfect), while time and place expressions change accordingly ("now" to "then", "here" to "there").

Forms across languages

Languages handle indirect speech differently. In Latin, indirect statements take the accusative and infinitive construction and indirect questions the subjunctive. German uses the Konjunktiv I mood for neutrality in reported speech, especially in journalism. In many languages, including Turkish and Quechua, reported information is instead marked by evidential morphology. Free indirect speech (or free indirect discourse) is a literary technique, used by authors such as Jane Austen and James Joyce, which blends third-person narration with the character's own voice, vocabulary and deictic centre, without reporting clauses or quotation marks.
