States' rights

In American political discourse, states' rights (or states rights) are political powers held for the state governments rather than the federal government of the United States, based on the Constitution and its interpretation in certain Supreme Court decisions. Drawing on the Tenth Amendment and the compact theory of the Union, the doctrine holds that powers not delegated to the federal government, nor prohibited to the states, are reserved to the states or the people.

Constitutional basis

The Tenth Amendment to the United States Constitution, ratified in 1791, provides that "The powers not delegated to the United States by the Constitution, nor prohibited by it to the States, are reserved to the States respectively, or to the people." Anti-Federalists and later theorists such as Thomas Jefferson and James Madison, in the Kentucky and Virginia Resolutions of 1798, argued that the federal government was the product of a compact among sovereign states, which retained the right to judge the constitutionality of federal acts.

Slavery and the Civil War

From the 1820s onward, states' rights doctrine was most prominently deployed by Southern states to defend the institution of slavery and to resist federal tariffs, notably in John C. Calhoun's theory of nullification and the Nullification Crisis of 1832–33. The secession of the Confederate states in 1860–61 was justified in the language of states' rights, though Confederate declarations of secession emphasized the preservation of slavery. The Union victory in the Civil War and the Reconstruction Amendments (Thirteenth, Fourteenth, and Fifteenth) substantially expanded federal power over the states.

Twentieth century and beyond

In the twentieth century, states' rights rhetoric was invoked by segregationist politicians resisting federal civil-rights enforcement, including the Dixiecrat campaign of 1948 and "massive resistance" to school desegregation after Brown v. Board of Education (1954). The concept has also been invoked in other contexts — by states legalizing marijuana contrary to federal law, in sanctuary-city disputes over immigration, and in federalism cases concerning healthcare, firearms, and environmental regulation.
