Hobbit

Hobbits are a fictional human-like race that inhabit the lands of Middle-earth in the epic fantasy works of J. R. R. Tolkien. About half average human height (roughly 3 feet 6 inches, or 1.07 m), hobbits live barefoot and inhabit underground houses with windows, as their land is usually hilly. Their feet have naturally tough leathery soles and they generally have brown curly hair.

Hobbits first appeared in the 1937 children's novel The Hobbit, whose titular hobbit is the protagonist Bilbo Baggins, who is thrown into an unexpected adventure involving a dragon. In its sequel, The Lord of the Rings, the hobbits Frodo Baggins, Sam Gamgee, Pippin Took, and Merry Brandybuck are primary characters who all play key roles in fighting to save their world. Hobbits live in the Shire, described as a bucolic area in the northwest of Middle-earth. Some live in the village of Bree further east.

Characteristics

Hobbits are between two and four feet (0.6–1.2 m) tall, the average height being three feet six inches (1.07 m). They are likely to be fat in the stomach, dress in bright colours favouring yellow and green, go barefoot, and have slightly pointed ears. They have a fondness for food, drink and pipe-smoking, and a pronounced dislike of adventures. Tolkien described them as having faces that are "good-natured rather than beautiful". They are adept at throwing stones, and they have a keen sense of hearing and sharp eyesight.

The hobbits of the Shire are divided into three types, or breeds: Harfoots, Stoors, and Fallohides. The Harfoots, who are browner of skin and smaller, are the most numerous. The Stoors, stronger and heavier, prefer living near water. The Fallohides, fairer and taller, are more adventurous.

History in Middle-earth

The origins of hobbits are obscure; they appear to have lived first in the Vale of Anduin. By the Third Age most hobbits had migrated westward across the Misty Mountains into Eriador. In about 1601 of the Third Age, the brothers Marcho and Blanco obtained official permission from the King of Arthedain to settle the land that became the Shire. The hobbits' calendar, the Shire Reckoning, counts its years from that settlement.

During the War of the Ring, played out in The Lord of the Rings, the hobbit Frodo Baggins bore the One Ring to its destruction in Mount Doom, accompanied by his gardener Sam Gamgee, while Merry Brandybuck and Pippin Took played roles in the wars in Rohan and Gondor. Afterwards the hobbits returned home to find the Shire industrialised under Saruman's influence, and scoured it clean.

Origins of the concept

Tolkien wrote in a letter that the word "hobbit" occurred to him suddenly when he wrote on a blank page of an exam script: "In a hole in the ground there lived a hobbit." He denied that the word was derived from "rabbit", though hobbits share some rabbit-like qualities. Earlier authors had used the word; a list of folkloric beings by Michael Aiken Denham in the 19th century includes "hobbits" among boggarts and hobgoblins. Similar fictional races include the hob-like halflings of later fantasy literature and games.

In popular culture

Hobbits are a central element of the film adaptations of The Hobbit and The Lord of the Rings directed by Peter Jackson. The village of Matamata in New Zealand, where the Hobbiton film set was built, has become a tourist attraction. Because "hobbit" is a trademark of the Tolkien estate, other works often use the term "halfling" for similar beings. In 2014, "hobbit" was widely used in the media as a nickname for the small fossil hominin Homo floresiensis, discovered in Indonesia.
