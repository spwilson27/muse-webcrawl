The Serbian Empire (Serbian: Српско царство, Srpsko carstvo) was a medieval Serbian state that existed from 1346 to 1371, created by the expansion of the Kingdom of Serbia under King Stefan Dušan, who crowned himself "Emperor of the Serbs and Greeks" at Skopje on Easter 1346. For a quarter-century it was one of the largest and most powerful states in southeastern Europe, encompassing much of the Balkans; after Dušan's death it rapidly fragmented among regional lords, in a period historians call the fall of the Serbian Empire.

Rise under Stefan Dušan

Stefan Dušan (r. 1331–1355) exploited the Byzantine civil wars of the 1340s to conquer Macedonia, Albania, Epirus, and Thessaly from the Byzantine Empire, roughly doubling Serbia's territory. In 1346 he had Archbishop Joanikije raised to Patriarch and was crowned emperor, claiming succession to the Byzantine imperial dignity. In 1349 (and in an expanded version, 1354) he promulgated Dušan's Code (Zakonik), one of the most important legal monuments of medieval Serbia, which combined Byzantine law with Serbian customary law and church canons. His court patronised church building — notably the Visoki Dečani and the Monastery of the Holy Archangels near Prizren — and mining towns such as Novo Brdo flourished on silver production.

Decline and dissolution

Dušan died suddenly on 20 December 1355, reportedly while marching toward Constantinople. He was succeeded by his son Stefan Uroš V ("the Weak"), under whom central authority waned and powerful nobles — Vukašin Mrnjavčević in Macedonia, Nikola Altomanović, Lazar Hrebeljanović, and the Balšić family — became effectively autonomous. In 1365 Uroš made Vukašin co-ruler; Vukašin and his brother Uglješa were killed by the Ottomans at the Battle of Maritsa in 1371, the same year Uroš V died childless, ending the Nemanjić dynasty and the empire. The successor lordships were gradually absorbed by the expanding Ottoman Empire over the following century, a process capped by the fall of Smederevo in 1459.

Administration and society

The empire adopted much of the Byzantine court and administrative apparatus: imperial titles (despot, sebastokrator, caesar) were granted to leading nobles, Greek was used alongside Serbian in administration of the conquered south, and land was held through pronoia-style grants. The Serbian Orthodox Church, headed by its own patriarchate at Peć, was the empire's ideological pillar; Serbian law, coinage (the silver dinar), and a Serbian redaction of church literature flourished under Dušan's rule.

Legacy

The Serbian Empire under Dušan is remembered in Serbian historiography and national tradition as the zenith of the medieval Serbian state. Dušan's Code, the patriarchate of Peć, and the memory of the empire informed Serbian statehood claims in the 19th century and remain prominent in Serbian historical memory.
