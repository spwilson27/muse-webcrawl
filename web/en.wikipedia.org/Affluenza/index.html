Affluenza

Affluenza is a portmanteau of affluence and influenza, and a term used by critics of consumerism. It is not a medically recognized condition. The word is used to describe the psychological and social malaise supposedly produced by the pursuit of material wealth: unsustainable addiction to economic growth, overconsumption, indebtedness, anxiety, and a sense of emptiness despite prosperity.

History of the term

The term gained wide currency with the 1997 PBS documentary Affluenza and the 2001 book Affluenza: The All-Consuming Epidemic by John de Graaf, David Wann, and Thomas H. Naylor, which defined it as "a painful, contagious, socially transmitted condition of overload, debt, anxiety, and waste resulting from the dogged pursuit of more." Oliver James popularized the term in Britain with his 2007 book Affluenza, arguing that materialistic values correlate with higher rates of mental distress.

The Ethan Couch case

In December 2013 the term drew international attention when Texas teenager Ethan Couch, who had killed four people while driving drunk, received a sentence of probation after a psychologist testified that Couch suffered from "affluenza" — that his wealthy parents had rendered him unable to understand consequences. The defense and the lenient sentence provoked widespread public outrage.

Criticism

Critics note that "affluenza" lacks any diagnostic standing and functions mainly as a rhetorical device — either as social criticism of consumer culture or, in the courtroom, as a provocative framing of privilege.
