Arabesque

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The arabesque is a form of artistic decoration consisting of "surface decorations based on rhythmic linear patterns of scrolling and interlacing foliage, tendrils or plain lines, often combined with other elements." Another definition is "foliate ornament, used in the Islamic world, typically using leaves, derived from stylised half-palmettes, which were combined with spiralling stems". It usually consists of a single design which can be 'tiled' or seamlessly repeated as many times as desired. Within the very wide range of Eurasian decorative art which includes motifs matching this basic definition, the term "arabesque" is used consistently as a technical term by art historians to describe only elements of the decoration found in two phases: Islamic art from about the 9th century onwards, and European decorative art from the Renaissance onwards.

Islamic arabesque

The arabesque developed out of the long-established traditions of plant-based ornament in cultures taken over by the early Islamic conquests, and of Byzantine art in particular. Early development took place in Umayyad art, drawing on Coptic and Sasanian models. A distinctive feature is the avoidance of a single central focus: the pattern implies infinity, extending beyond the edges of the object it decorates, which is often interpreted as reflecting the Islamic view of a world beyond the visible. Arabesques appear in carved stucco, woodwork, tile mosaics, metalwork, and illuminated manuscripts, notably in the great mosques of Samarra, Córdoba, and Isfahan.

Techniques and motifs

Common motifs include the split-palmette, the acanthus scroll, vine tendrils, and stylised leaves that bifurcate and recombine. Artists often combined arabesque with geometric patterns (girih) and calligraphy, the three together forming the characteristic decorative programme of Islamic religious buildings. Mistakes in pattern are sometimes said to be deliberately introduced by craftsmen as a sign that only God can produce perfection, though art historians treat this claim with caution.

Western arabesque

The term entered European usage in the Renaissance, when Italian artists adapted Islamic and classical scrolling ornament in grotesque decoration — the word "arabesque" being applied loosely to the fanciful hybrids of foliage, masks, and figures popularised by Raphael's workshop in the Vatican Loggie. In the 18th and 19th centuries the arabesque became a staple of wallpaper, textiles, and book ornament. William Morris and the Arts and Crafts movement drew directly on Islamic examples.

Other uses

"Arabesque" is also the name of a pose in ballet, a short lyrical piano piece (such as those by Schumann and Debussy), and a genre of Turkish popular music. These senses share the connotation of graceful, flowing intricacy.
