Skyr (Icelandic pronunciation: [ˈscɪːr̥]) is an Icelandic cultured dairy product. It has the consistency of strained yogurt (Greek yogurt), but a milder flavour. Skyr can be classified as a fresh sour milk cheese, but it is consumed like a yogurt. It has been a part of Icelandic cuisine for over a thousand years.

Skyr is made from skimmed milk inoculated with previous skyr cultures — typically Streptococcus thermophilus and Lactobacillus delbrueckii subsp. bulgaricus — and a small amount of rennet, then incubated and strained through cloth to remove the whey. Roughly four times as much milk goes into a given quantity of skyr as into the same quantity of ordinary yogurt, giving it a high protein content (around 11%) and very low fat.

History

The product is mentioned in medieval Icelandic sagas and was brought to Iceland by Norse settlers from Norway, where the tradition later died out. In Iceland, skyr-making knowledge was preserved through generations of farm households; starters were carefully handed down as a prized culture. A related product survived in Norway under names such as skjørost.

Uses

Skyr is traditionally served with milk and a topping of sugar, often mixed with porridge or served with berries and cream as a dessert (skyr og rjómi). Since the 2000s it has been marketed internationally — particularly in Scandinavia, the United Kingdom and North America — as a high-protein food, sold plain and in flavoured varieties by Icelandic and licensed producers.
