A chef is a professional cook who has been trained in all aspects of food preparation, usually specialising in a particular cuisine. The word comes from the French chef de cuisine, "head of the kitchen", reflecting the leadership role of the head chef in a professional kitchen.

The brigade system

Auguste Escoffier formalised the brigade de cuisine in the late 19th century, dividing the kitchen into stations: the executive chef directs the whole operation; the sous-chef is second in command; chefs de partie run sections such as the saucier (sauces), pâtissier (pastry), garde manger (cold dishes) and poissonnier (fish); commis chefs assist, and apprentices learn at the bottom. Large hotels may add an aboyeur (expediter) who calls orders.

Training and uniform

Chefs train through culinary schools, apprenticeships, or by working up the brigade. The traditional uniform — tall white toque, double-breasted jacket, neckerchief, apron and chequered trousers — was popularised by Marie-Antoine Carême and serves practical purposes: the toque's pleats were said to represent a hundred ways to cook an egg, and the jacket's double breast protects against heat and spills. In popular culture, celebrity chefs and televised competitions have made the profession highly visible, while guides such as Michelin award prestige to leading chefs and restaurants.
