Distributed denial-of-service

A distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attack is a malicious attempt to disrupt the normal traffic of a targeted server, service or network by overwhelming the target or its surrounding infrastructure with a flood of internet traffic. DDoS attacks achieve effectiveness by utilizing multiple compromised computer systems as sources of attack traffic, which may include computers and other networked resources such as Internet of Things devices.

How it works

Attackers typically assemble a botnet — a network of machines infected with malware and controlled remotely through command-and-control servers. When instructed, each bot sends requests to the target's IP address, potentially overwhelming the target and causing a denial of service to normal traffic. Because the traffic arrives from many sources, it is difficult to block by filtering a single address.

Types of attacks

  • Volume-based attacks — UDP and ICMP floods that saturate the target's bandwidth.
  • Protocol attacks — SYN floods and similar attacks that exhaust server connection tables or stateful middleboxes.
  • Application-layer attacks — floods of seemingly legitimate HTTP requests ("HTTP floods") that exhaust server resources with comparatively little traffic.
  • Amplification attacks — the attacker spoofs the victim's address and triggers large responses from open services such as DNS or NTP servers, multiplying the traffic delivered to the victim.

Notable incidents

Large attacks have been recorded against spam-fighting services, banks, governments and technology companies. The 2016 attack on the DNS provider Dyn, powered by the Mirai botnet of compromised IoT devices, knocked major websites offline across the United States. Cloud providers have since reported attacks exceeding several terabits per second and, for application-layer floods, hundreds of millions of requests per second.

Defences

Mitigations include over-provisioning bandwidth, anycast distribution of traffic across many points of presence, rate limiting, upstream filtering by internet service providers, and commercial DDoS-mitigation services that scrub traffic through filtering centres. Reducing the population of insecure devices and closing open amplification services address the problem at its source. DDoS attacks are criminal offences in most jurisdictions, under laws such as the U.S. Computer Fraud and Abuse Act and the UK Computer Misuse Act.
