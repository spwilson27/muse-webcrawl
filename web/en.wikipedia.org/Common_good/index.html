Common good

The common good is a concept in philosophy, theology, and political theory referring to what is shared by and beneficial to all or most members of a community — whether material conditions such as clean air and public infrastructure, or the social conditions that allow a community and its members to flourish. It is distinct from the mere sum of individual goods: a common good is enjoyed jointly and is diminished for each if it is lost by any.

Classical thought

Aristotle held that the polis exists for the sake of the good life of its citizens. Thomas Aquinas developed the bonum commune as the aim of law and human society, a tradition continued in Catholic social teaching, where the common good means "the sum total of social conditions which allow people, either as groups or as individuals, to reach their fulfilment more fully and more easily."

Modern debates

Jean-Jacques Rousseau's "general will" sought the common good against the mere "will of all". Utilitarians identify it with the greatest total welfare. Economists use a narrower sense: public goods and common-pool resources whose provision markets under-supply. Critics warn the concept can be invoked to override individual rights; defenders answer that many goods — language, security, trust — are irreducibly shared.
