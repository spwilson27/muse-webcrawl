Evangeline

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Evangeline, A Tale of Acadie is an epic poem by the American poet Henry Wadsworth Longfellow, published in 1847. The poem follows an Acadian girl named Evangeline and her search for her lost love Gabriel, set during the time of the Expulsion of the Acadians, the forced removal by the British of the French-speaking inhabitants of present-day Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, and Prince Edward Island during the French and Indian War (1755–1764).

Plot

In the village of Grand-Pré, Evangeline Bellefontaine is betrothed to Gabriel Lajeunesse. On their wedding day, British soldiers round up the Acadian men and announce the deportation of the whole population. The lovers are separated and placed on different ships. Evangeline spends the rest of her life wandering America — through the bayous of Louisiana, the prairies, and the settlements of the eastern seaboard — searching for Gabriel. She finally finds him, decades later, on his deathbed in a Philadelphia almshouse where she works as a Sister of Mercy; he dies in her arms, and she dies soon after. They are buried together in the city.

Composition and influence

Longfellow heard the story from the Reverend Horace Conolly, who had himself heard it from an Acadian parishioner; Hawthorne, a college friend, had first considered and declined using it. The poem, written in dactylic hexameters, was an immediate and enormous success in the English-speaking world and made Longfellow the most famous American poet of his century.

Evangeline profoundly shaped the identity of both the Acadian diaspora and Louisiana's Cajuns: the town of St. Martinville, Louisiana claims her as its own, with an "Evangeline Oak" and statue, while Grand-Pré is now a National Historic Site and UNESCO World Heritage landscape. The poem was adapted into several films, including a 1929 version starring Dolores del Río.
