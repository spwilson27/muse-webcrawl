Eicosapentaenoic acid

Eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA; also icosapentaenoic acid) is an omega-3 fatty acid. In physiological literature, it is given the name 20:5(n-3), denoting a 20-carbon chain with five cis double bonds, the first at the third carbon from the omega end. EPA is a polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA) that acts as a precursor for prostaglandin-3, thromboxane-3 and leukotriene-5 eicosanoids.

Sources

Humans obtain EPA mainly from eating oily fish such as salmon, herring, mackerel, sardines and menhaden, or from fish oil supplements. Fish do not synthesise large amounts themselves but accumulate EPA from the algae and phytoplankton they consume. Human milk also contains EPA. The body can convert some alpha-linolenic acid (ALA) into EPA, but the conversion is inefficient, and EPA can be retroconverted from docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) to a limited degree.

Biochemistry

EPA competes with arachidonic acid for the cyclooxygenase and lipoxygenase pathways, yielding eicosanoids that are generally less inflammatory than those derived from arachidonic acid. It is also the precursor of E-series resolvins, lipid mediators involved in resolving inflammation.

Clinical significance

EPA and DHA have been extensively studied for cardiovascular health. The purified EPA ethyl ester icosapent ethyl (Vascepa) reduced cardiovascular events in the REDUCE-IT trial (2019) in patients with elevated triglycerides already on statins, and is approved as an adjunct therapy in several countries. Evidence for benefits in depression and other conditions remains mixed.
