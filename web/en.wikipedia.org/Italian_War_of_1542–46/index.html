The Italian War of 1542–1546 was a conflict in the Italian Wars between Francis I of France and the allied forces of Holy Roman Emperor Charles V and Henry VIII of England. Francis, allied with the Ottoman sultan Suleiman the Magnificent, sought once more to break Habsburg power in Italy; the war ended in stalemate and exhaustion, with France abandoning its claims in Italy in exchange for concessions in the north.

Origins

The truce that ended the previous war in 1538 broke down after the murder of two French envoys travelling to Venice and Constantinople by Imperial agents in 1541, and over Charles V's investiture of his son Philip with the Duchy of Milan. Francis declared war in July 1542, attacking Luxembourg and Roussillon, and renewed the Franco-Ottoman alliance — the "impious alliance" that scandalized Christian Europe.

Course of the war

In 1543 a joint Franco-Ottoman fleet under the Ottoman admiral Hayreddin Barbarossa and French commanders besieged and partly captured Nice, then a Savoyard city allied with the Emperor; the Ottoman fleet afterwards wintered at Toulon, whose cathedral was converted into a mosque for the season. On land, French forces won the war's largest pitched battle in Italy at Ceresole Alba (14 April 1544), where the Comte d'Enghien's army — French, Swiss, and Italian infantry with heavy cavalry — defeated an Imperial-Spanish army under Alfonso d'Avalos; the victory, however, could not be exploited because France itself was invaded. In 1544 Charles V advanced deep into Champagne to within reach of Paris while Henry VIII landed in the north and captured Boulogne.

Settlement

Bankrupt and overextended, Charles made peace first: by the Treaty of Crépy (September 1544) Francis renounced his claims to Naples, Flanders, and Artois and agreed to abandon Milan, while Charles renounced his claim to the Duchy of Burgundy; a dynastic marriage was to settle Milan's fate. Fighting with England continued until the Treaty of Ardres (June 1546), under which France agreed to pay a large indemnity and pension for Boulogne, which England retained until 1550. The settlement essentially restored the status quo and marked the effective end of France's Italian ambitions, though one final Habsburg–Valois war followed in 1551–59.
