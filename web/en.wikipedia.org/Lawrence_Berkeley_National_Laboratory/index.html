Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory (Berkeley Lab) is a federally funded research and development center in the hills above Berkeley, California, United States. Established in 1931 by Ernest Lawrence as the Radiation Laboratory of the University of California, Berkeley, it is the oldest of the United States Department of Energy's national laboratories and is managed and operated by the University of California. Berkeley Lab research has produced 16 Nobel Prize laureates affiliated with the laboratory, and its scientists discovered sixteen chemical elements — more than any other institution — including plutonium, berkelium and lawrencium.

History

Ernest Lawrence founded the Radiation Laboratory in 1931 to develop his invention, the cyclotron, which won him the 1939 Nobel Prize in Physics. The lab became a center of the wartime Manhattan Project: Lawrence's electromagnetic separation method (the calutrons) was scaled up at Oak Ridge, and Glenn Seaborg's team discovered plutonium in 1940–41. After the war the lab moved to its present hillside site, built the Bevatron proton synchrotron (where the antiproton was discovered in 1955), and expanded from nuclear physics into materials science, chemistry, biology, computing and earth and energy sciences.

Research areas

Berkeley Lab is organized into research areas including physics, nuclear science, chemical sciences, materials sciences, biosciences, earth and environmental sciences, energy sciences, computing sciences and accelerator technology. It operates major user facilities for the DOE Office of Science: the Advanced Light Source (a synchrotron light source), the National Energy Research Scientific Computing Center (NERSC), the Molecular Foundry (nanoscience), the Joint Genome Institute, and the Energy Sciences Network (ESnet).

Element discoveries

Elements first synthesized or identified at Berkeley include technetium (in part), astatine, neptunium, plutonium, curium, americium, berkelium, californium, einsteinium, fermium, mendelevium, nobelium, lawrencium, dubnium and seaborgium — a record commemorated by the elements berkelium and lawrencium named for the city and the founder.

Nobel laureates

Sixteen scientists have been associated with Berkeley Lab as Nobel laureates, including Ernest Lawrence, Glenn Seaborg, Edwin McMillan, Emilio Segrè, Owen Chamberlain, Donald Glaser, Melvin Calvin, Luis Alvarez, Yuan T. Lee, Steven Chu, George Smoot, Saul Perlmutter and Jennifer Doudna.
