A tin can is a container for the distribution or storage of goods, made of thin metal. Although most tin cans are made of tinplate steel — steel coated with a thin layer of tin — many are now made of aluminium or other metals, and the term is used generically for metal cans. Cans are widely used for food preservation through canning, as well as for beverages, paints, aerosols and many other products.

History

The canning process was developed by the French inventor Nicolas Appert, who in 1809 won a prize offered by the French government for a method of preserving food; Appert used glass jars. In 1810 the English merchant Peter Durand patented the use of tin-coated iron cans instead of bottles. The patent was acquired by Bryan Donkin, John Hall and John Gamble, who in 1813 established the first commercial cannery in Bermondsey, London, supplying preserved food to the British Army and Royal Navy.

Early cans were made by hand from wrought iron coated with tin, and could only be opened with a hammer and chisel. The can opener was not invented until 1855, decades after cans themselves, because early cans were too thick for a dedicated tool. As steel became cheaper and cans thinner in the later nineteenth century, canning spread rapidly. The American Civil War and later the two World Wars greatly increased demand for canned rations.

Construction and materials

Modern food cans are typically made from tinplate: low-carbon steel electroplated with a thin layer of tin, which resists corrosion and is non-toxic. Many cans are additionally lacquered inside with an epoxy or polymer coating to prevent the contents reacting with the metal. Beverage cans are usually made of aluminium. A typical modern can has a cylindrical body with a double-seamed lid; the double seam folds the lid and body edges together with a sealing compound to produce a hermetic seal.

Canning process

In commercial canning, food is prepared, filled into cans with liquid such as brine or syrup, exhausted of air, sealed, and then heat-processed in retorts to destroy microorganisms, including the spores of Clostridium botulinum. Properly canned food can remain safe for years at ambient temperature, though quality gradually declines.

Recycling

Steel and aluminium cans are among the most recycled packaging materials. Steel is recovered magnetically from waste streams, while aluminium recycling saves about 95% of the energy needed to produce primary aluminium from bauxite.
