Multiplication

Multiplication (often denoted by the cross symbol ×, a mid-line dot ·, or an asterisk *) is one of the four elementary operations of arithmetic, alongside addition, subtraction and division. The multiplication of two numbers a and b, written a × b, is equivalent to adding a to itself b times; a and b are called factors, and the result is the product. For example, 3 × 4 = 4 + 4 + 4 = 12.

Properties

Multiplication of real numbers is commutative (a × b = b × a), associative, and distributive over addition: a × (b + c) = a × b + a × c. The multiplicative identity is 1, and every non-zero number has a reciprocal (multiplicative inverse). Multiplication by zero always yields zero, and division by zero is undefined.

Notation and computation

The × sign was introduced by William Oughtred in the seventeenth century; Leibniz preferred the dot to avoid confusion with the letter x. In algebra, factors are often simply juxtaposed (2x means 2 × x). Standard algorithms for multi-digit multiplication include the lattice method and long multiplication; ancient Egyptian and Roman methods used repeated doubling. Large-integer multiplication in computing uses fast algorithms such as Karatsuba and FFT-based methods.

Generalisations

The operation extends beyond numbers: fractions, real and complex numbers, matrices (where multiplication is not commutative), vectors (dot and cross products), polynomials and abstract algebraic structures such as groups and rings.
