Benjamin Franklin (January 17, 1706 – April 17, 1790) was an American polymath: a leading writer, printer, political philosopher, politician, Freemason, postmaster, scientist, inventor, humourist, civic activist, statesman, and diplomat. As a scientist he is a major figure in the American Enlightenment and in the history of physics for his studies of electricity. As a Founding Father, he helped draft the Declaration of Independence, secured the French alliance that made independence possible, and negotiated the Treaty of Paris (1783). He is the only Founding Father to have signed all four key founding documents: the Declaration, the Treaty of Alliance with France, the Treaty of Paris, and the United States Constitution.

Printer and writer

Born in Boston, the fifteenth child of a soap- and candle-maker, Franklin was apprenticed to his brother James's printing shop and educated himself by reading. He wrote the "Silence Dogood" letters as a teenager and ran away to Philadelphia in 1723. There he built a successful printing business, published the Pennsylvania Gazette and the hugely popular Poor Richard's Almanack (1732–1758) under the pseudonym Richard Saunders, and retired wealthy at 42. He founded the Library Company of Philadelphia, the American Philosophical Society, and the academy that became the University of Pennsylvania.

Science and inventions

Franklin's experiments with electricity in the 1740s–50s — including the (partly legendary) kite experiment of 1752 — established principles of charge conservation and positive and negative charge, coined terms such as battery, and led to his invention of the lightning rod. His inventions also include bifocal glasses, the Franklin stove, a flexible urinary catheter, and the glass armonica; he studied ocean currents, charting the Gulf Stream, and never patented any of his inventions, holding that knowledge should serve the public.

Statesman and diplomat

Franklin served as colonial agent in London, where his testimony helped secure repeal of the Stamp Act (1766), but the Hutchinson letters affair destroyed his hopes of reconciliation. Elected to the Second Continental Congress, he sat on the Committee of Five that produced the Declaration of Independence (1776). As minister to France (1776–1785), the 70-year-old Franklin became a celebrity of the French Enlightenment and secured the loans, supplies, and military alliance essential to American victory. He then helped negotiate peace with Britain in 1783.

Final years and legacy

Franklin served as president (governor) of Pennsylvania (1785–88) and, aged 81, as the eldest delegate to the Constitutional Convention of 1787, where he urged compromise and unanimity. In his last public act he signed an abolitionist petition to Congress (1790). He died on 17 April 1790; some 20,000 mourners attended his funeral in Philadelphia. Franklin's image appears on the U.S. $100 bill, and his autobiography — left unfinished — remains a classic of American literature.
