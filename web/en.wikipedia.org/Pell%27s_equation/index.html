Pell's equation, also called the Pell–Fermat equation, is any Diophantine equation of the form x2 − n y2 = 1, where n is a given positive nonsquare integer. Solutions in integers (x, y) are sought; besides the trivial solution x = 1, y = 0, there are infinitely many, generated from a single "fundamental solution" by recurrence.

History

The equation was studied extensively by Indian mathematicians: Brahmagupta (7th century) developed the chakravala-style composition method, and Bhāskara II (12th century) gave the cyclic chakravala algorithm, famously solving x2 − 61y2 = 1, whose smallest solution is x = 1766319049. In Europe, Fermat challenged others to solve such equations in 1657; Euler later attached the name of John Pell, apparently by mistake. Lagrange proved in the 18th century that a solution always exists, using continued fractions: the fundamental solution comes from a convergent of the continued fraction of √n.

Structure of solutions

All positive solutions are given by xk + yk√n = (x1 + y1√n)k, where (x1, y1) is the fundamental (smallest) solution — equivalently, the units of norm 1 in the ring ℤ[√n]. The equation is also related to Archimedes' cattle problem, whose solution is an astronomically large number.
