New York Times Co. v. Sullivan, 376 U.S. 254 (1964), is a landmark decision by the Supreme Court of the United States ruling that the First Amendment to the U.S. Constitution restricts the ability of American public officials to sue for defamation. The decision established the actual malice standard: a public official cannot recover damages for a defamatory falsehood relating to official conduct unless the statement was made with knowledge that it was false or with reckless disregard of whether it was false.

The case arose from the civil rights era. L. B. Sullivan, a police commissioner in Montgomery, Alabama, sued The New York Times over a 1960 full-page fund-raising advertisement, "Heed Their Rising Voices", which described abuses against civil rights protesters and contained several minor factual errors. An Alabama jury awarded Sullivan $500,000 in libel damages.

Decision

Justice William J. Brennan Jr., writing for a unanimous Court, held that debate on public issues should be "uninhibited, robust, and wide-open", and that erroneous statements are inevitable in free debate and must be protected if freedom of expression is to have the "breathing space" it needs. The Court reversed the judgment. The actual malice standard was later extended to "public figures" in cases such as Curtis Publishing Co. v. Butts (1967) and refined for private figures in Gertz v. Robert Welch, Inc. (1974).

Legacy

The decision is regarded as a cornerstone of American press freedom, enabling vigorous reporting on public affairs. It has nevertheless drawn periodic criticism, and in recent years some justices have called for the actual malice framework to be reconsidered; as of the mid-2020s the Court had not taken up such a challenge.
