Second Industrial Revolution

The Second Industrial Revolution, also known as the Technological Revolution, was a phase of rapid industrialization and standardization generally dated from about 1870 to 1914, ending with the outbreak of the First World War. It built on the foundations of the first Industrial Revolution and was characterized by the expansion of railroads and telegraph networks, cheap steel, electricity, and the rise of mass production.

Cheap steel from the Bessemer process underpinned railways, ships, and construction.

Key developments

The Bessemer process (patented 1856) and the open-hearth process made steel cheap and abundant, enabling larger bridges, ships, skyscrapers, and vast railway mileage. Petroleum became a major industry after 1859, supplying kerosene and later gasoline for the internal combustion engine. A new chemical industry produced synthetic dyes, fertilizers, and explosives. Electrical power spread after the 1880s with dynamos, electric lighting, motors, and streetcars, while the telegraph and telephone transformed communication. In manufacturing, interchangeable parts and the moving assembly line—most famously at Henry Ford's Highland Park plant in 1913—drove mass production of consumer goods.

Social and economic effects

Industrialization concentrated production in large corporations and trusts, spurred rapid urbanization, and transformed labour, prompting the growth of trade unions and new regulatory politics. Germany and the United States emerged as industrial leaders, overtaking Britain in several sectors. Rising standards of living for many coexisted with harsh factory conditions, child labour, and widening inequality, feeding progressive and socialist movements.
