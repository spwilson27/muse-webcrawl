John Heartfield (19 June 1891 – 26 April 1968), born Helmut Herzfeld, was a German artist and pioneer of photomontage. A founder of Berlin Dada, he turned the cut-and-pasted photograph into a weapon of political satire, above all in his anti-Nazi covers for the Arbeiter-Illustrierte-Zeitung (AIZ) in the 1930s, which made him one of the Nazi regime's most hated propagandists.

Early life and Dada

Herzfeld anglicized his name in 1916 in protest against anti-British wartime chauvinism. With his brother Wieland Herzfelde he ran the Malik publishing house, and with George Grosz he developed photomontage — they playfully disputed its invention for the rest of their lives. At the First International Dada Fair of 1920 the pair displayed radical collage works, and Heartfield went on to design strikingly modern book jackets for Malik-Verlag.

The AIZ montages

Heartfield joined the Communist Party of Germany in 1918. Between 1930 and 1938 he produced more than 200 covers for the mass-circulation AIZ, including the famous Adolf the Superman: Swallows Gold and Spouts Junk (1932), an X-ray image of Hitler swallowing coins, and The Meaning of the Hitler Salute (1932), showing a giant industrialist hand slipping money into the Führer's outstretched palm. His images were printed in huge numbers and pasted up in public.

Exile and later life

When the Nazis took power in 1933 Heartfield escaped arrest and fled to Prague, continuing his work there until 1938, when he escaped again to London ahead of the German occupation. Interned briefly as an enemy alien, he worked in British publishing during the war. In 1950 he returned to East Germany, where he designed books, posters and stage sets with Bertolt Brecht's Berliner Ensemble circle. His reputation as the founder of political photomontage was restored through major exhibitions before his death in 1968.
