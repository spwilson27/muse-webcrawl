Chinese art

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Chinese art has arguably the oldest continuous tradition in the world, stretching back to the Neolithic period, with surviving masterpieces from the Shang and Zhou dynasties in bronze, jade, and pottery. It is marked by an unusual degree of continuity within a tradition, and by conscious imitation of the past as the highest form of artistic praise.

Painting

Traditional Chinese painting (guohua) uses a brush dipped in ink or coloured pigments on paper or silk. Landscape painting (shanshui, "mountains and waters") reached its heights in the Tang, Song, and Yuan dynasties — masters such as Fan Kuan, Guo Xi, Mi Fu, Ni Zan, and Dong Qichang. Calligraphy, governed by strict rules for brush handling, was considered the highest art form, practiced by scholar-officials; painters inscribed poems directly on their works, merging the three arts of poetry, calligraphy, and painting.

Bronzes, jade, and ceramics

Ritual bronze vessels of the Shang and Zhou (taotie-decorated ding tripods), carved jade burial suits and the Hetian jade tradition, and ceramics — from Tang sancai to Song celadon and Ming blue-and-white porcelain (Jingdezhen ware) — are among the most prized art forms. Chinese porcelain was traded globally along the Silk Road and through maritime routes from the Tang dynasty onward.

Buddhist art

The arrival of Buddhism from the 1st century CE produced the cave temple complexes of Mogao (Dunhuang), Longmen, and Yungang, with thousands of Buddha statues and frescoes blending Indian, Central Asian, and Chinese styles. Chan (Zen) Buddhist ink painting produced distinctive spontaneous works by masters such as Liang Kai and Muqi.

Modern and contemporary

The 20th century saw fusion with Western media: Xu Beihong combined Chinese subjects with European realism; after 1949, socialist realism dominated, then reform-era China produced globally prominent contemporary artists such as Ai Weiwei, Zhang Xiaogang, Cai Guo-Qiang, and Xu Bing.
