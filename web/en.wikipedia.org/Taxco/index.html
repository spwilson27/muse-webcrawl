Taxco

Taxco de Alarcón (usually referred to simply as Taxco) is a small city and administrative center of the municipality of the same name located in the Mexican state of Guerrero. The city lies on steep hillsides at an elevation of about 1,800 metres in the Sierra Madre del Sur. It is heavily associated with silver mining and silverwork, both mining dating from the colonial period and the modern silver-jewelry trade, and it is designated a Pueblo Mágico for its preserved colonial character.

History

The area was inhabited by the Tlahuica people before the Spanish conquest, and Hernán Cortés established one of New Spain's earliest mining camps there in the 1520s after hearing of the region's metal wealth. The 18th-century silver boom under José de la Borda financed the city's landmark, the Church of Santa Prisca, built between 1751 and 1758 in an exuberant Mexican Churrigueresque style with twin towers of pink stone.

Silver

When the old mines declined, American silversmith William Spratling revived Taxco's fortunes in the 1930s by founding a workshop that trained local artisans and made the city famous for handcrafted silver jewelry and decorative objects. Hundreds of silver shops still line its cobbled streets, and the National Silver Fair is held each year around the end of November.

Tourism

Taxco's whitewashed houses with red tile roofs, narrow winding streets, and plazas are protected by federal heritage rules. Holy Week processions are a major attraction, as is the cable car to the Monte Taxco lookout. The city is about 170 km southwest of Mexico City.
