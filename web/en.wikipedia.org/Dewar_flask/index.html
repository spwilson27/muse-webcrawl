A laboratory Dewar flask used for storing cryogenic liquids.

A Dewar flask (also called a Dewar bottle, Dewar vessel, or simply a dewar) is a specialized insulating storage vessel that greatly slows the transfer of heat between its contents and the environment. It was invented by the Scottish physicist Sir James Dewar in 1892 in the course of his research into cryogenics, and it remains the standard container for liquefied gases such as liquid nitrogen and liquid helium.

Design

A Dewar consists of two vessels, one nested inside the other and joined only at the neck. The gap between them is evacuated to a near-vacuum, eliminating heat transfer by conduction and convection through air. The inner surfaces facing the gap are silvered or coated to reflect thermal radiation, minimizing radiative transfer as well. Laboratory dewars are typically made of borosilicate glass; large industrial vessels use stainless steel with additional multilayer insulation.

Uses

Dewars store and transport cryogenic liquids for laboratories, medicine, and industry, and are used in scientific instruments to cool detectors. Because a completely sealed dewar would build up dangerous pressure as liquid boils off, vessels are vented or fitted with loose-fitting stoppers and pressure-relief devices.

History and the Thermos

Dewar never patented his invention and received no royalties when the design was commercialized. The German glassblowers Reinhold Burger and Albert Aschenbrenner adapted the flask for domestic use and, with Gustav Robert Paalen, founded the Thermos company in 1904; "thermos" became a genericized trademark for vacuum flasks in many countries.
