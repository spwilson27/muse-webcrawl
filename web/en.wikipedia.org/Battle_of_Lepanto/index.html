The Battle of Lepanto was a naval engagement fought on 7 October 1571 in the Gulf of Patras, in which the fleet of the Holy League—a coalition of Catholic maritime states assembled by Pope Pius V under the command of Don John of Austria—inflicted a decisive defeat on the main fleet of the Ottoman Empire. It was the largest naval battle of the sixteenth century and one of the last major engagements fought primarily between rowing vessels.

Background

The Ottoman conquest of Cyprus in 1570–71, crowned by the fall of Famagusta and the atrocities that followed, drove the Pope to assemble the Holy League of Venice, Spain, the Papal States, Genoa, Savoy and the Knights of Malta. The allied fleet concentrated at Messina and sailed east in search of the Ottoman fleet based at Lepanto (Naupaktos).

The battle

The fleets met on the morning of 7 October. The Christian line deployed six heavily armed Venetian galleasses ahead of its three squadrons; their concentrated cannon fire broke up the Ottoman formations as they closed. In hours of brutal close combat—boarding actions fought deck to deck—the Christian arquebusiers' superior firepower told. The Ottoman commander Ali Pasha was killed and his flagship taken; only the Algerian corsair squadron of Uluç Ali broke through, captured the Maltese flagship, and escaped with some 30–40 galleys. By late afternoon the Ottoman fleet was effectively destroyed.

Aftermath

News of the victory was greeted with rejoicing across Christian Europe, and the feast of Our Lady of Victory (later Our Lady of the Rosary) was instituted on 7 October. Strategically the effects were limited: Cyprus remained Ottoman, and the sultan rebuilt a fleet within a year, remarking that the Christians had shaved his beard while he had lopped off Venice's arm. Yet Lepanto shattered the aura of Ottoman invincibility at sea and marked the high-water point of galley warfare in the Mediterranean.
