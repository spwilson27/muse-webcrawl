Dirichlet convolution

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In mathematics, the Dirichlet convolution is a binary operation defined for arithmetic functions; it is important in number theory. It was developed by Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet.

If f and g are two arithmetic functions from the positive integers to the complex numbers, the Dirichlet convolution f ∗ g is a new arithmetic function defined by

(f ∗ g)(n) = Σd | n f(d) g(n/d) = Σab = n f(a) g(b)

where the sum extends over all positive divisors d of n, or equivalently over all distinct pairs (a, b) of positive integers whose product is n.

Properties

The set of arithmetic functions forms a commutative ring under pointwise addition and Dirichlet convolution, called the Dirichlet ring. The multiplicative identity is the unit function defined by ε(n) = 1 if n = 1 and ε(n) = 0 otherwise.

The units (invertible elements) of this ring are the arithmetic functions f with f(1) ≠ 0. An arithmetic function f has a Dirichlet inverse if and only if f(1) is nonzero; the inverse can be computed recursively.

Examples

Important identities involving Dirichlet convolution include:

  • μ ∗ 1 = ε, where μ is the Möbius function, 1 is the constant function, and ε is the identity — this is the basis of Möbius inversion.
  • φ ∗ 1 = id, where φ is Euler's totient function and id(n) = n.
  • σ = 1 ∗ id, expressing the divisor function σ(n) as a convolution.
  • λ ∗ |μ| = 1, where λ is Liouville's function.

Dirichlet series

The Dirichlet convolution is closely connected with Dirichlet series. If F(s) and G(s) are the Dirichlet series of f and g, then the Dirichlet series of the convolution f ∗ g is the product F(s)G(s). This correspondence is a principal reason for the usefulness of the construction in analytic number theory.
