A dolphinarium is an aquarium for dolphins, and sometimes other cetaceans such as orcas and beluga whales. Dolphins are usually kept in a large pool, though occasionally they may be kept in pens in the open sea, either for research or for public performances. Some dolphinariums consist of one large pool where dolphins perform for the public, others are part of larger marine mammal parks and zoos.

History

The first commercial dolphinarium, Marine Studios (later Marineland of Florida), opened in 1938. The popularity of the television series Flipper in the 1960s and the growth of marine parks such as SeaWorld drove a boom in captive dolphin display and in the capture of wild dolphins, particularly in Taiji, Japan, and in the Solomon Islands. Modern facilities increasingly rely on captive breeding.

Animal welfare debate

Dolphinariums are controversial. Critics argue that pools cannot reproduce the ranging, social, and acoustic environment of wide-ranging predators, and point to stress behaviours, reduced lifespan (contested by the industry), and the ethics of capture and performance. Defenders cite education, conservation funding, research, and "swim with dolphins" therapy programmes. Several jurisdictions — including India, which in 2013 declared dolphins "non-human persons" whose captivity for entertainment was banned, and Canada, which legislated a phase-out in 2019 — have restricted or prohibited cetacean captivity.

Regulation

In the United States, dolphinariums are licensed under the Animal Welfare Act and the Marine Mammal Protection Act. In the European Union, they fall under the zoo directive. Trade in dolphins is regulated by CITES.
