The World Social Forum (WSF) is a regular gathering of civil society organizations, first held in Brazil, which seeks to develop an alternative future through the championing of counter-hegemonic globalization. It can be considered a manifestation of the global justice movement, and it meets annually at the same time as the World Economic Forum in Davos, Switzerland, to which it was conceived as a counter-summit.

History

The first WSF was held in Porto Alegre, Brazil, in January 2001, drawing some 12,000–20,000 registered participants. Porto Alegre hosted again in 2002, 2003 and 2005, when attendance reached roughly 155,000, making it the largest forum. Later world editions were held in Mumbai, India (2004); as a polycentric forum across Caracas, Bamako and Karachi (2006); Nairobi, Kenya (2007); Belém, Brazil (2009); Dakar, Senegal (2011); Tunis, Tunisia (2013 and 2015); Montreal, Canada (2016) — the first in the global North; Salvador, Brazil (2018); and, after virtual editions during the COVID-19 pandemic, Kathmandu, Nepal (2024).

Charter of Principles

The WSF is governed by a Charter of Principles adopted in 2001. It defines the Forum as an "open meeting place" for reflective thinking, democratic debate and the building of alternatives by social movements and NGOs opposed to neoliberalism and to domination of the world by capital and imperialism. The Forum issues no binding declarations and takes no positions as a body: participants may issue their own calls, such as the Porto Alegre Manifesto of 2005, proposed by a group of intellectuals. Political party representations and armed movements are excluded, and no one body may speak in the Forum's name.

Related forums

The WSF spawned regional, national and thematic gatherings, including the European Social Forum (first held in Florence in 2002), the United States Social Forum (Atlanta 2007, Detroit 2010) and hundreds of local social forums. Critics have questioned the Forum's effectiveness as it issues no unified programme, while supporters credit it with networking movements behind campaigns against free-trade agreements such as the FTAA, the Iraq War protests of 2003, and later climate-justice and debt-relief initiatives.
