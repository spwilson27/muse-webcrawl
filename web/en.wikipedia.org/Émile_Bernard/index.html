Émile Bernard

Émile Henri Bernard Born: 28 April 1868, Lille, France
Died: 16 April 1941 (aged 72), Paris, France
Movement: Post-Impressionism, Cloisonnism, Synthetism, Pont-Aven School
Known for: Painting, art theory
Émile Bernard, co-founder of Cloisonnism.

Émile Henri Bernard (28 April 1868 – 16 April 1941) was a French Post-Impressionist painter and writer who, while still in his teens, developed with Louis Anquetin the style known as Cloisonnism — flat areas of colour separated by bold dark outlines, inspired by stained glass and Japanese prints. At Pont-Aven in Brittany in 1888 he worked alongside Paul Gauguin, and the two forged Synthetism, a style aiming to synthesise outward appearance, the artist's feelings and pure aesthetics.

Expelled from the atelier of Fernand Cormon for insubordination, the young Bernard befriended Toulouse-Lautrec and, briefly, Vincent van Gogh, with whom he exchanged paintings and letters. His Breton Women in the Meadow (1888) is credited with prompting Gauguin's Vision of the Sermon, a debt Gauguin never properly acknowledged, embittering Bernard.

Later career

After travelling in Italy and spending a decade in Egypt, Bernard returned to France, quarrelled with Gauguin's legend, founded the journal La Rénovation esthétique, published important correspondence with Van Gogh and Cézanne, and turned toward a more classical, religious painting influenced by the Old Masters. Though his later work fell from fashion, art historians now recognise his crucial role in launching the Pont-Aven school and Post-Impressionist theory.
