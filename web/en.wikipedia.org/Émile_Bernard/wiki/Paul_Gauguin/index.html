Eugène Henri Paul Gauguin (7 June 1848 – 8 May 1903) was a French Post-Impressionist artist whose bold use of colour and Synthetist style set him apart from Impressionism. Unappreciated during his lifetime, he is now recognised for his experimental work, which influenced the avant-garde, modern art, and artists including Henri Matisse and Pablo Picasso.

Life and work

Gauguin spent part of his childhood in Lima, Peru, and worked as a stockbroker in Paris before the 1882 crash turned him to painting full-time. In Pont-Aven, Brittany, he developed Synthetism with the young Émile Bernard, producing Vision of the Sermon (1888). That autumn he joined Vincent van Gogh in Arles for nine turbulent weeks that ended with van Gogh's breakdown.

Seeking a more "primitive" setting, Gauguin sailed to Tahiti in 1891, painting works such as Ia Orana Maria and his monumental testament Where Do We Come From? What Are We? Where Are We Going? (1897). Impoverished and ill, he moved to the Marquesas Islands in 1901 and died at Atuona in 1903.

Legacy and criticism

His woodcuts, ceramics, and carved reliefs, as well as his paintings, fed into Fauvism and Primitivism. Modern reassessment also confronts his conduct in French Polynesia, including his relationships with young Tahitian girls, and the colonial framing of his imagery.
