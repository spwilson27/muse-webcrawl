DNA gyrase

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

DNA gyrase, or simply gyrase, is an enzyme within the class of topoisomerases (specifically, a type II topoisomerase) that reduces topological strain in DNA while double-stranded DNA is being unwound by elongation factors or helicases. Found in bacteria and some archaea, gyrase is unique among topoisomerases in its ability to introduce negative supercoils into DNA, a reaction driven by the hydrolysis of ATP.

Structure

Bacterial gyrase is a tetramer composed of two GyrA and two GyrB subunits. The GyrB subunit contains the ATPase activity, while GyrA carries the active-site tyrosine responsible for DNA cleavage and religation. The enzyme wraps a segment of DNA of roughly 120–150 base pairs around itself before passing another segment through a transient double-strand break.

Function

During replication and transcription, positive supercoils accumulate ahead of the advancing polymerase or replication fork. Gyrase removes these and maintains the chromosome in a slightly underwound (negatively supercoiled) state, which facilitates strand opening for replication initiation and transcription. It is thus essential for bacterial growth.

As a drug target

Because gyrase is absent from human cells, it is an attractive antibacterial target. Fluoroquinolone antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin trap the gyrase–DNA cleavage complex, leading to double-strand breaks and cell death. The coumarin antibiotics, such as novobiocin, instead inhibit the ATPase activity of GyrB. Resistance arises through mutations in gyrA and gyrB and through protection proteins such as Qnr.

History

Gyrase was discovered in 1976 by Martin Gellert and colleagues at the National Institutes of Health, who identified an E. coli activity that introduced supercoils into relaxed closed-circular DNA in an ATP-dependent manner.
