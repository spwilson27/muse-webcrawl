Fritz Thyssen

Fritz Thyssen (9 November 1873 – 8 February 1951) was a German industrialist, head of the Thyssen steel and mining empire founded by his father August Thyssen. He was one of the wealthiest men in Germany and is best known for his early financial support of Adolf Hitler and the Nazi Party, followed by his break with the regime and imprisonment in a concentration camp.

Life

Thyssen inherited control of the family business and expanded it, helping form the Vereinigte Stahlwerke steel trust in 1926. A nationalist and opponent of the Weimar Republic, he began funding the Nazis in the early 1920s and joined the party in 1931, encouraging other industrialists to support Hitler's appointment as Chancellor. His 1939 book "I Paid Hitler" (published 1941) publicised his role.

Disillusioned by Nazi economic policy, anti-Christian measures, and Kristallnacht, Thyssen broke with the regime at the outbreak of war in 1939 and fled to Switzerland and then France. The Vichy government handed him to Germany; he spent the remainder of the war as a privileged but confined prisoner in concentration camps, including Sachsenhausen and Dachau. After liberation he was tried in denazification proceedings, fined, and died in Argentina in 1951. The Fritz Thyssen Foundation, established by his widow and daughter in 1959, became a major funder of academic research in postwar Germany.
