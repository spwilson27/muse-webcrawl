Group A

Group A was a set of motor racing regulations introduced by the FIA in 1982, covering production-derived vehicles intended for competition in touring car racing and rallying. It replaced the earlier Group 2 (touring cars) and Group 4 (grand touring) categories within the FIA's Appendix J classification, and formed part of the system that also included Group B and Group N.

Regulations

Group A required a minimum production run of 2,500 examples of the competing model in a twelve-month period (5,000 overall for the base model in early years), four seats, and limited modifications compared with the loosely regulated Group B. Rules governed engine capacity classes, turbocharger equivalence factors, wheel and tyre sizes, and permitted changes to suspension, brakes, and aerodynamics. The more restricted Group N category covered near-standard production cars.

Touring cars

Group A became the premier formula for touring car racing worldwide. It defined the World Touring Car Championship of 1987, the European Touring Car Championship, the British Touring Car Championship, Australia's touring car series, and Japan's JTCC. Iconic Group A cars include the BMW M3 (E30), Ford Sierra RS500 Cosworth, Nissan Skyline GT-R (R32) — nicknamed "Godzilla" for its dominance in Australia — Holden Commodore, and Volvo 240 Turbo. The formula was phased out in the early 1990s in favour of Supertouring and other classes.

Rallying

After Group B was abolished at the end of 1986 following fatal accidents, Group A became the top class of the World Rally Championship from 1987 until 1996. Cars such as the Lancia Delta Integrale, Toyota Celica GT-Four, Subaru Impreza, Mitsubishi Lancer Evolution, and Ford Escort RS Cosworth carried on famous rivalries. In 1997 the WRC moved to the World Rally Car formula derived from, but looser than, Group A.
