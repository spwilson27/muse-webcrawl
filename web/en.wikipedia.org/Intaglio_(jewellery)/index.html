Intaglio (jewellery)

An intaglio is a gemstone or other hard material carved with a design incised below the surface, so that the image is recessed into the material. In jewellery the term applies to engraved gems — made of carnelian, agate, jasper, amethyst, rock crystal or glass — set in rings, seals, pendants and other ornaments. The opposite technique is the cameo, in which the design is carved in relief above the background.

History

Engraved seals were used in Mesopotamia and Egypt from the fourth millennium BC; stamp seals and cylinder seals impressed into clay served as signatures and marks of ownership. Greek and Etruscan craftsmen perfected gem engraving in the archaic and classical periods, and Roman intaglios — cut with drills and abrasive wheels — were produced in huge numbers, serving simultaneously as jewellery, personal seals and amulets. Subjects included portraits of rulers and gods, mythological scenes, animals and magical inscriptions (the so-called Abraxas stones of late antiquity).

Renaissance and later revival

Ancient gems were eagerly collected in the Renaissance, and engravers revived classical styles so successfully that their work was sometimes mistaken for antiquity. Interest peaked again in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries, when collectors such as the Duke of Marlborough assembled famous cabinets and artists like the Pichler family produced celebrated intaglios. Tassie's glass-paste impressions made engraved gem designs available to a wide audience.

Technique

The engraver works the stone in reverse with a lathe-driven set of small wheels and drills fed with abrasive slurry (historically emery or diamond dust), cutting the design as a mirror image so that an impression in wax or clay reads correctly. Because the design is cut into the stone, an intaglio ring can be pressed into sealing wax to authenticate documents — the original purpose from which signet rings descend.
