Chartered company

A chartered company is an association of investors formed under a charter granted by a government, historically giving the company a monopoly of trade in a region and often quasi-governmental powers to administer territory, raise armies and make treaties.

History

European powers used chartered companies to project trade and colonization from the 16th century onward. Prominent examples include the English East India Company (chartered 1600), the Dutch East India Company or VOC (1602), the Hudson's Bay Company (1670), and the French East India Company.

Powers and decline

Many companies governed vast territories — the East India Company ruled much of India until the British Crown assumed direct rule in 1858. By the late 19th and 20th centuries most charters were revoked or the companies wound up as states took over administration, though the Hudson's Bay Company survived as a retailer.
