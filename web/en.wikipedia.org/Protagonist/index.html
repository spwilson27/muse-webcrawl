Protagonist

A protagonist (from Ancient Greek prōtagōnistḗs, "one who plays the first part, chief actor") is the main character of a story. The protagonist makes key decisions that affect the plot, primarily influencing the story and propelling it forward, and is often the character with whom the audience is meant to identify or empathize. The protagonist's fortunes are followed most closely, and they are typically opposed by an antagonist, who embodies obstacles or opposition to the protagonist's goals.

Origins

The term derives from ancient Greek theatre, where the protagonist was the lead actor, distinguished from the deuteragonist (second actor) and tritagonist (third actor). According to tradition, Aeschylus expanded the number of actors on stage, allowing the protagonist to interact with other characters rather than only with the chorus, a development fundamental to Western drama. In modern narrative theory the protagonist is defined by function — the character whose choices drive the narrative — rather than by virtue: protagonists can be heroic, flawed, or outright villainous, as in antihero-centred stories.

Characteristics

Protagonists are generally involved in the central conflict of the work and undergo change — the character arc — in response to it. Stories may have a single protagonist, an ensemble sharing the role, or even a false protagonist who is displaced partway through. The perspective character and the narrator frequently coincide with the protagonist, but need not: in The Great Gatsby, for example, Nick Carraway narrates while Jay Gatsby is the protagonist. Critics caution against conflating "protagonist" with "hero," since the term describes structural position, not moral quality.
