Life Safety Code

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Life Safety Code, published as NFPA 101, is a consensus code published by the National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) that establishes minimum requirements for the design, construction, operation, and maintenance of buildings to protect occupants from fire, smoke, and related emergencies. It is the most widely used source for strategies to protect people from fire in the United States and is adopted or referenced by federal agencies, including the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services for health care facilities, as well as by many states and local jurisdictions.

History

The code's origins lie in the NFPA's Committee on Safety to Life, formed in 1913 in the wake of catastrophic fires such as the Triangle Shirtwaist fire of 1911. The committee's early publications on exit design were consolidated in 1927 as the Building Exits Code. Repeated revisions followed in response to disasters such as the Cocoanut Grove fire (1942), the Winecoff Hotel fire (1946), and the Our Lady of the Angels school fire (1958). In 1966 the document was restructured and renamed the Code for Safety to Life from Fire in Buildings and Structures, and it later became the Life Safety Code, republished in a three-year cycle.

Content

Unlike building codes that treat a building largely by its construction type, NFPA 101 is organized primarily by occupancy classification — assembly, educational, day care, health care, residential, mercantile, business, industrial, storage, and others — with separate chapters for new construction and existing buildings. Its central concepts include:

  • Means of egress: the exit access, exit, and exit discharge; requirements for number, capacity, width, travel distance, illumination, and marking of exits.
  • Features of fire protection: construction, compartmentation, smoke barriers, and vertical opening protection.
  • Protection systems: fire alarm, detection, automatic sprinklers, and standpipes.
  • Operating features: drills, staff training, and maintenance in occupancies such as health care, where "defend in place" strategies replace total evacuation.

Since the 2000 edition, the code has included performance-based design options alongside its prescriptive requirements, allowing engineers to demonstrate equivalent safety through fire modeling and analysis.

Adoption

NFPA 101 is enforced by authorities having jurisdiction in much of the United States and is used internationally. Together with NFPA 1 (Fire Code) and model building codes such as the International Building Code, it forms the framework of U.S. fire-safety regulation for buildings.
