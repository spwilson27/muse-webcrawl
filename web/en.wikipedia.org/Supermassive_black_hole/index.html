Supermassive black hole

A supermassive black hole (SMBH) is the largest type of black hole, containing hundreds of thousands to billions of times the mass of the Sun. Nearly every large galaxy, including the Milky Way, is thought to host one at its centre. The Milky Way's central black hole, Sagittarius A*, has about 4 million solar masses; the black hole in Messier 87 has about 6.5 billion.

Evidence and measurement

The existence of supermassive black holes is inferred from the orbits of stars and gas near galactic centres, from maser disks that give precise masses, and from the enormous energy output of quasars and active galactic nuclei, which is best explained by accretion onto such objects. Tight empirical relations — notably the M–sigma relation between black hole mass and the velocity dispersion of the host galaxy's bulge — suggest black holes and galaxies grow together.

Formation

How supermassive black holes formed so early in cosmic history is an open question. Proposed channels include the collapse of the first stars leaving heavy "seed" black holes, direct collapse of pristine gas clouds, runaway mergers in dense star clusters, and subsequent rapid accretion and galaxy mergers.

Imaging

In 2019 the Event Horizon Telescope collaboration released the first image of a black hole's shadow, that of M87* in the galaxy Messier 87, showing a bright ring of hot gas around a dark centre. In 2022 it released an image of Sagittarius A*, confirming the Milky Way's central object.
