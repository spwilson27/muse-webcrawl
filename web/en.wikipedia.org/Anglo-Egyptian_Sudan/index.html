Anglo-Egyptian Sudan

Anglo-Egyptian Sudan was a condominium of the United Kingdom and Egypt between 1899 and 1956, corresponding broadly to modern-day Sudan and South Sudan. It was established after the reconquest of Sudan from the Mahdist State and ended with Sudanese independence on 1 January 1956.

Background

Sudan had been conquered by Egypt under Muhammad Ali in the 1820s. The Mahdist revolt of 1881–1898 overthrew Turco-Egyptian rule, killing General Charles Gordon at Khartoum in 1885. An Anglo-Egyptian army under Herbert Kitchener reconquered the country in 1896–1898, culminating in the Battle of Omdurman.

The condominium

The agreement of 19 January 1899 established a joint administration: supreme military and civil command was vested in a governor-general appointed by Egypt on the recommendation of Britain, and the British and Egyptian flags were flown together. In practice, senior posts were dominated by British officials of the Sudan Political Service. The capital was Khartoum.

Administration and economy

The administration pursued "indirect rule" through local chiefs, especially after policies favouring native administration in the 1920s and 1930s. The Gezira Scheme, irrigated by the Sennar Dam from 1925, made cotton the colony's principal export. The south was administered largely separately from the north under the "Southern Policy", a divide that left deep legacies.

Towards independence

After the Second World War, Sudanese nationalism grew, divided between union with Egypt and full independence. Following the Egyptian revolution of 1952, Britain and Egypt agreed in 1953 to Sudanese self-government and a plebiscite on the country's future. The Sudanese parliament opted for independence, and the Republic of Sudan was proclaimed on 1 January 1956.
