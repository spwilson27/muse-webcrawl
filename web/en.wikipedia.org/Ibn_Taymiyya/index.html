Ibn Taymiyyah

Taqi al-Din Ahmad ibn Taymiyyah (January 22, 1263 – September 26, 1328), known simply as Ibn Taymiyyah, was a Sunni Muslim scholar, theologian and logician of the Mamluk era. A member of the Hanbali school of jurisprudence, he is famous for his voluminous writings on theology, law and politics, his criticism of practices he regarded as innovations in Islam, and his repeated imprisonment by the authorities of his day. His works have exerted an enormous — and contested — influence on later Islamic movements, including Wahhabism and Salafism.

Ibn Taymiyyah's writings fill dozens of volumes of fatwas and treatises.

Life

Ibn Taymiyyah was born in Harran in 1263 into a family of Hanbali scholars; fleeing the Mongol advance, the family settled in Damascus when he was a child. He mastered Qur'an, hadith, jurisprudence, Arabic grammar and mathematics, and began teaching in his late teens. He took part in the defence of Syria against the Mongol Ilkhanate, issuing fatwas that declared jihad against the Mongols obligatory even though their rulers professed Islam, on the ground that they ruled by the Mongol Yassa code rather than the Sharia.

Controversies and imprisonments

Ibn Taymiyyah's positions brought him into repeated conflict with both the religious establishment and the Mamluk state. He attacked the visitation of saints' tombs and other devotional practices as un-Islamic innovations (bid'ah), criticised Ash'ari theology and Ibn Arabi's Sufism, and wrote on divorce oaths and other legal questions in ways that defied consensus. He was imprisoned several times in Cairo and Damascus, and died in 1328 while confined in the Citadel of Damascus; thousands reportedly attended his funeral.

Thought and works

His best-known works include the 37-volume collection of fatwas known as Majmu' al-Fatawa, the theological treatise Al-'Aqidah al-Wasitiyyah, and the monumental Dar' Ta'arud al-'Aql wa al-Naql ("Refutation of the Contradiction between Reason and Revelation"), which argued that reason and scripture cannot genuinely conflict. He rejected Aristotelian logic's definitions and syllogisms as foundations for knowledge, championed the Qur'an and the practice of the earliest generations (al-salaf al-salih) as the standard of religion, and influenced his student Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya and the historian al-Dhahabi.

Legacy

Long a marginal figure guarded by Hanbali admirers, Ibn Taymiyyah was revived by Muhammad ibn Abd al-Wahhab in the eighteenth century and became a central authority for modern Salafi movements. His fatwas on the Mongols have also been cited by militant groups, a use contested by many scholars who emphasise their specific historical context.
