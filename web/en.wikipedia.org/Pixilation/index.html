Pixilation

Pixilation (frequently misspelled as "pixelation") is a stop-motion animation technique in which live actors are used as frame-by-frame subjects, in the same way that puppets or objects are used in conventional stop motion. By photographing actors in slightly different poses one frame at a time, filmmakers make people appear to slide along the ground, vanish, or perform impossible movements.

History

The technique was pioneered in the early years of cinema and was developed extensively by the Canadian filmmaker Norman McLaren, whose film Neighbours (1952), made with pixilated actors, won an Academy Award. McLaren also used the technique in A Chairy Tale (1957) and other National Film Board of Canada productions.

Notable uses

Pixilation appears in the work of Jan Švankmajer, the music videos of the 1980s such as Peter Gabriel's "Sledgehammer" (which combined pixilation with claymation), and in numerous experimental short films and title sequences. The technique remains popular with independent animators because it requires no sets or puppets, only a camera and patient performers.

Technique

Actors hold a pose while a single frame (or a few frames) is exposed, then shift slightly and hold the next pose. Played back at normal speed, the incremental changes produce jerky, dreamlike motion. Objects as well as people may be animated in the same shot to create apparent interaction between them.
