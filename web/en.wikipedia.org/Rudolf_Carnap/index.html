Rudolf Carnap (18 May 1891 – 14 September 1970) was a German-language philosopher who was active in Europe before 1935 and in the United States thereafter. He was a leading member of the Vienna Circle and a central figure of logical positivism.

Life and work

Carnap studied physics, mathematics and philosophy at Jena, where he attended Gottlob Frege's lectures on mathematical logic. After serving in the First World War he completed a doctorate on the concept of space. His Der logische Aufbau der Welt (1928, The Logical Structure of the World) attempted to show how all scientific concepts could be constructed from a basic phenomenal vocabulary. In The Logical Syntax of Language (1934) he advanced the principle of tolerance: in logic, there are no morals — everyone is free to choose the language suited to their purposes.

Emigrating to the United States in 1935, Carnap taught at the University of Chicago and later at UCLA. His later work developed formal semantics, inductive logic and a theory of probability as degree of confirmation in Logical Foundations of Probability (1950). His distinction between "internal" and "external" questions in "Empiricism, Semantics, and Ontology" (1950) shaped later debates about metaphysics, and his philosophy was a principal target and stimulus for W. V. O. Quine.
