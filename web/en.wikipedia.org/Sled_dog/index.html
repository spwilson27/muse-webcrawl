Sled dog


A sled dog team at work

Sled dogs are domestic dogs trained and used to pull land vehicles in a harness, most commonly a sled over snow. They were bred and kept by many Arctic peoples of North America and Siberia for transport, hunting, and survival in harsh environments, and remain used in remote communities as well as in the sport of mushing.

Breeds and characteristics

Traditional breeds include the Siberian Husky, Alaskan Malamute, Canadian Eskimo Dog, Greenland Dog, and Samoyed, while modern racing teams are dominated by the Alaskan husky, a purpose-bred crossbreed. Sled dogs are selected for endurance, tough feet, a dense double coat, efficient gait, and a strong appetite and work ethic; a team is organised with lead dogs at the front, point and swing dogs behind, and powerful wheel dogs nearest the sled.

History

Archaeological evidence from Zhokhov Island suggests dog sledding in Siberia some 9,000 years ago. Sled dogs were essential to polar exploration — Roald Amundsen's successful 1911 South Pole expedition relied on them — and to events such as the 1925 serum run to Nome, Alaska, in which diphtheria antitoxin was relayed by dog teams, commemorated by the statue of Balto in New York's Central Park.

Sport

Dog sled racing ranges from sprints to long-distance events such as the Iditarod Trail Sled Dog Race (about 1,000 miles from Anchorage to Nome) and the Yukon Quest. Animal welfare in the sport is debated, with race organisations enforcing veterinary checks and rest rules.
