Oligosaccharide

An oligosaccharide is a carbohydrate polymer made of a small number of monosaccharide (simple sugar) units — conventionally from three to ten — joined by glycosidic bonds. With fewer units a molecule is a disaccharide; with many more, a polysaccharide. Oligosaccharides are widespread in plants, milk, and on the surfaces of cells, where they play roles in energy storage, digestion and molecular recognition.

Common examples

The raffinose family of oligosaccharides — raffinose (a trisaccharide), stachyose (a tetrasaccharide) and verbascose — accumulates in beans, lentils and other legumes. Humans lack the enzyme α-galactosidase needed to digest them, so gut bacteria ferment them, producing gas. Fructooligosaccharides and galactooligosaccharides occur in vegetables and milk and are marketed as prebiotics. Human milk oligosaccharides (HMOs) are the third most abundant solid component of human breast milk and nourish the infant gut microbiome while discouraging pathogens.

Functions in the body

Oligosaccharides attached to proteins and lipids (glycans) decorate cell surfaces and determine blood group antigens — the ABO antigens are oligosaccharides. N-linked glycosylation of proteins begins with a conserved 14-sugar oligosaccharide in the endoplasmic reticulum. Pathogens and toxins often bind specific host oligosaccharides, and soluble milk oligosaccharides can act as decoy receptors.

Analysis

Because oligosaccharide structures branch and vary in linkage, their analysis is more demanding than that of linear biopolymers; mass spectrometry and enzymatic digestion are standard tools in the field known as glycomics.
