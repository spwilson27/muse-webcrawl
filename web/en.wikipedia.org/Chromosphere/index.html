Chromosphere

The chromosphere is the second layer of the Sun's atmosphere, lying above the photosphere and below the corona. It extends roughly 2,000 kilometres above the visible surface and is named for its reddish color (Greek chroma, color), produced by hydrogen-alpha emission at 656.3 nanometres. The chromosphere is normally hidden by the photosphere's brightness but becomes visible as a thin red rim during total solar eclipses and can be studied at any time with narrow-band filters and spectrographs.

Structure and features

Temperature in the chromosphere first falls to a minimum of about 4,100 K, then rises outward — counterintuitively — toward the corona, a heating puzzle still studied today. The layer is textured by spicules, jets of gas lasting minutes that shoot thousands of kilometres upward, and by a network pattern tracing supergranulation cells. Dark filaments (called prominences when seen at the limb) are cool, dense ribbons of plasma suspended by magnetic fields; bright plages overlie active regions and sunspots.

Observation

Spectroscopic study of the chromosphere began with the eclipse observations of 1868, during which a yellow emission line led to the discovery of helium. Modern solar observatories — ground telescopes with H-alpha filters and spacecraft such as SDO and IRIS — monitor the chromosphere continuously, since flares and filament eruptions there can precede coronal mass ejections that affect space weather at Earth.

Other stars

Chromospheres exist on most cool stars, where they are detected through emission in the cores of strong spectral lines such as calcium H and K. Chromospheric activity varies with stellar rotation and age and is used to estimate the ages of stars.
