Fon people

The Fon people, also called the Fon nu, Agadja, or Dahomeans, are a Gbe ethnic group. They are the largest ethnic group in Benin, concentrated in the south of the country, and are also found in southwestern Nigeria and Togo. Their language, Fon, is the most widely spoken Gbe language. The Fon are historically associated with the Kingdom of Dahomey, a powerful West African state that flourished from the seventeenth to the late nineteenth century.

History

According to Fon tradition, the people migrated to the Abomey plateau and founded their kingdom around 1600. Under kings such as Wegbaja, Akaba, and Agaja, Dahomey expanded to the Atlantic coast, becoming a major participant in regional trade — including, notoriously, the Atlantic slave trade through the port of Ouidah. The kingdom was also famed for its women soldiers, called Ahosi (the "Dahomey Amazons" of European accounts). Dahomey resisted French colonisation in the wars of 1890–1894, after which it was incorporated into French West Africa.

Culture and religion

Fon culture is known for appliqué textiles, brass and iron sculpture, and elaborate royal palaces at Abomey, now a UNESCO World Heritage Site. The Fon are the originators of Vodun (Voodoo), a religion centred on spirits known as vodun, which spread to the Americas through the African diaspora and influenced Haitian Vodou and related traditions. Alongside Vodun, many Fon today practise Christianity or Islam.
