War on cancer

The "war on cancer" is the effort to increase knowledge about causes of cancer and to develop more effective treatments, such as targeted drug therapies, in order to reduce deaths from the disease. The phrase is associated with the United States National Cancer Act of 1971, signed by President Richard Nixon on December 23, 1971, which greatly expanded the budget, authority, and autonomy of the National Cancer Institute (NCI).

Origins

Advocates such as philanthropist Mary Lasker and researcher Sidney Farber pressed for a concerted national effort modeled on the Manhattan Project and the Apollo program. Nixon announced the initiative in his 1971 State of the Union address, declaring it time to launch an intensive campaign to find a cure for cancer.

Progress and criticism

The program massively increased research funding and built infrastructure such as cancer centers and clinical-trial networks. Mortality from several cancers — childhood leukemias, Hodgkin lymphoma, testicular cancer — fell dramatically, and overall age-adjusted cancer death rates in the US have declined since the early 1990s, driven by reduced smoking, screening, and better treatment. Critics have long argued that the "war" framing promised a single cure for what is a family of hundreds of diseases, and that progress against metastatic solid tumors was slower than hoped.

Later initiatives

The metaphor has been repeatedly revived, notably in the 2016 Cancer Moonshot initiative led by Vice President Joe Biden following the death of his son Beau from brain cancer, which sought to accelerate research through data sharing and new funding.
