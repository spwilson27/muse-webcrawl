Found footage (appropriation)

Found footage is a filmmaking technique and genre of experimental cinema in which all or most of a film is built from pre-existing material originally shot for another purpose — archival newsreels, home movies, industrial films, surveillance recordings or discarded prints — and recontextualized by the new filmmaker. It is distinct from the found footage horror technique, in which newly shot material merely imitates amateur recordings.

History

The practice dates to the 1930s, when Joseph Cornell re-edited the B-movie East of Borneo into the surrealist collage film Rose Hobart (1936). Bruce Conner's A MOVIE (1958), assembled from newsreels, stag films and B-pictures, established the modern compilation film, and his Report (1963–67) meditated on the Kennedy assassination through repeated television footage.

Techniques and themes

Found-footage filmmakers employ montage, looping, optical printing, scratching and re-photography of the source material. The recontextualization often produces media criticism: works such as Craig Baldwin's collage features and Ken Jacobs's Tom, Tom, the Piper's Son (1969) interrogate the ideologies embedded in mass-media imagery. The appropriation raises questions of copyright and authorship that parallel those of appropriation art.
