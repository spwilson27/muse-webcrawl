Hepatotoxicity

Liver injury caused by a chemical substance

Hepatotoxicity (from hepatic toxicity) implies chemical-driven liver damage. Drug-induced liver injury (DILI) is a cause of acute and chronic liver disease and is the most common reason for a drug to be withdrawn from the market after approval. More than 900 drugs, toxins and herbs have been reported to cause liver injury.

Patterns and mechanisms

Liver injury may be hepatocellular (predominant rise in aminotransferases), cholestatic (predominant alkaline phosphatase rise), or mixed. Classic hepatotoxins include paracetamol (acetaminophen), whose overdose is the leading cause of acute liver failure in many Western countries; the antibiotic amoxicillin-clavulanate, the most common cause of idiosyncratic DILI; isoniazid; methotrexate; and herbal products such as kava and concentrated green tea extract. Mechanisms include direct toxicity, formation of reactive metabolites, and immune-mediated injury. The relation between ALT and alkaline phosphatase elevations (the R ratio) classifies the pattern, and Hy's law identifies cases at high risk of fatal outcome.

Diagnosis and management

Diagnosis is largely one of exclusion, correlating the timing of drug exposure with the onset of injury and its resolution after withdrawal (dechallenge). Management centres on stopping the offending agent; N-acetylcysteine is the antidote for paracetamol poisoning and is also used in some other acute liver failure. Severe cases may require liver transplantation. Regulatory agencies screen new drugs for hepatotoxicity in preclinical and clinical testing, and resources such as the LiverTox database catalogue drug-specific risk.
