Pernicious anemia

Pernicious anemia is a type of vitamin B12 deficiency anemia caused by impaired absorption of vitamin B12 due to a lack of intrinsic factor. It is most often due to autoimmune destruction of the gastric parietal cells that produce intrinsic factor (autoimmune gastritis).

Mechanism and symptoms

Without intrinsic factor, dietary B12 cannot be absorbed in the ileum. Deficiency impairs DNA synthesis, producing megaloblastic anemia with large red blood cells, fatigue, pallor and glossitis, and can damage the spinal cord and peripheral nerves (subacute combined degeneration), causing numbness, gait disturbance and cognitive changes. Neurological damage may occur even without anemia.

History

The disease was once fatal — hence "pernicious". In 1926 George Whipple, George Minot and William Murphy showed that eating large amounts of liver cured it, work that won the Nobel Prize; William Castle then identified intrinsic factor, and vitamin B12 itself was isolated in 1948.

Treatment

Treatment is lifelong vitamin B12 replacement, traditionally by intramuscular injection, though high-dose oral preparations can also be effective. Antibodies to intrinsic factor or parietal cells support the diagnosis, and patients require monitoring for associated autoimmune diseases and gastric cancer risk.
