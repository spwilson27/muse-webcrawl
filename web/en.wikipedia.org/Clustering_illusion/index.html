Clustering illusion

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The clustering illusion is the intuition of many people that inevitable streaks or clusters in small samples from random processes are not random. It is a cognitive bias in which a person underestimates the amount of variability likely to appear in a small sample of random or semi-random data. People tend to see patterns of meaning in random clusters — a form of apophenia — and to infer causes where only chance is at work.

Examples

The illusion takes its name from the example of a person who sees apparent "clusters" of bomb hits on a map of London during the Second World War and concludes that certain districts were deliberately targeted and others deliberately spared, when statistical analysis (by R. D. Clarke) showed the distribution was consistent with randomness. Similar reasoning applies to apparent cancer clusters near industrial facilities, which often turn out to be chance concentrations.

In sports, the clustering illusion underlies beliefs about the "hot hand" in basketball: players, coaches, and fans perceive streaks of successful shots as evidence that a shooter has temporarily become more likely to score. A famous 1985 study by Thomas Gilovich, Robert Vallone, and Amos Tversky found little evidence for such streakiness in shooting data, arguing that perceived streaks reflect the same tendency to see clusters in random sequences — although later statistical work has revisited the size of the effect.

Related biases

The clustering illusion is related to the Texas sharpshooter fallacy (drawing the target around the hits after the fact), the gambler's fallacy, and the illusion of control. It reflects a general human expectation that random sequences should "look" random — evenly spread and free of runs — when true randomness frequently produces striking local order.
