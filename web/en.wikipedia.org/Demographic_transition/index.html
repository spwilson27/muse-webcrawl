Demographic transition

Demographic transition is a phenomenon and theory in demography which refers to the historical shift from high birth rates and high death rates in societies with minimal technology, education (especially of women), and economic development, to low birth rates and low death rates in societies with advanced technology, education, and economic development, as well as the stages between these two extremes.

The classic model: mortality falls first, fertility later, producing rapid growth between

Stages

The classic model has four stages. In stage one, pre-industrial societies have high and fluctuating birth and death rates and slow population growth. In stage two, death rates fall — thanks to improved food supply, sanitation, and medicine — while birth rates remain high, so population grows rapidly. In stage three, birth rates fall as urbanization, education, contraception, and women's employment spread. In stage four, both rates are low and population stabilizes or declines slowly. Some demographers add a stage five of below-replacement fertility.

Origins

The theory was developed from observations of European population history, notably by Warren Thompson (1929) and Frank W. Notestein (1945), who coined the term "demographic transition".

Effects

The transition transforms age structure: falling fertility and longer lives produce population ageing, changing labour markets, pension burdens, and patterns of migration. The temporary bulge of working-age people can produce a demographic dividend if employment keeps pace.

Criticism

Scholars note the model is descriptive rather than predictive, that transitions have varied widely in timing and sequence, and that some countries have stalled mid-transition or experienced mortality reversals, for example from HIV/AIDS.
