Irish nationalism

Irish nationalism is a nationalist political movement which, in its broadest sense, asserts that the people of Ireland should govern Ireland as a sovereign state. Since the mid-19th century, Irish nationalism has largely taken the form of cultural nationalism based on the principles of national self-determination and popular sovereignty.

History

Early expressions include resistance to English rule from the Tudor conquest onward, the United Irishmen's rebellion of 1798, and Daniel O'Connell's mass campaigns for Catholic emancipation (achieved 1829) and repeal of the Act of Union. The Young Ireland movement and the Fenian Brotherhood combined cultural revival with revolutionary separatism.

Home Rule and independence

From the 1870s, the constitutional nationalism of Isaac Butt and Charles Stewart Parnell sought Home Rule within the United Kingdom. After the Easter Rising of 1916 and Sinn Féin's victory in the 1918 election, the Irish War of Independence led to the Anglo-Irish Treaty (1921), partition of Ireland, and the Irish Free State; Ireland became a republic in 1949.

Northern Ireland and reunification

Nationalists in Northern Ireland, who formed a large minority there, generally seek a united Ireland. During the Troubles (c. 1968–1998), republican paramilitaries such as the Provisional IRA pursued this by force, while the SDLP pursued constitutional means. The Good Friday Agreement (1998) recognized the principle of consent: Northern Ireland's status can change only by majority vote. Support for Irish reunification remains a central issue in Irish politics on both sides of the border.
