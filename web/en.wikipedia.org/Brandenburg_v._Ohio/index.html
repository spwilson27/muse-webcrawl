Brandenburg v. Ohio

Brandenburg v. Ohio, 395 U.S. 444 (1969), is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States establishing the modern standard for when speech advocating illegal action may be punished under the First Amendment. The Court held that the government cannot forbid advocacy of the use of force or of law violation "except where such advocacy is directed to inciting or producing imminent lawless action and is likely to incite or produce such action."

Background

Clarence Brandenburg, a Ku Klux Klan leader in Ohio, was convicted under the state's Criminal Syndicalism Act for speeches at a televised Klan rally that included racist and antisemitic remarks and vague threats of "revengeance" against the government. The statute criminalised advocating violence as a means of accomplishing political reform.

Decision

In a per curiam opinion, the Court reversed the conviction and effectively overruled Whitney v. California (1927). The new two-part test — intent to incite, and likelihood of producing, imminent lawless action — replaced the looser "clear and present danger" approaches of the earlier 20th century and sharply narrowed the circumstances in which advocacy itself can be criminalised. Justices Hugo Black and William O. Douglas concurred, arguing for even broader protection of speech.

Legacy

The Brandenburg test remains the governing standard in U.S. free-speech law and is regarded as one of the most speech-protective doctrines in the world; it was applied in cases such as Hess v. Indiana (1973) and considered in later disputes over protest and online speech.
