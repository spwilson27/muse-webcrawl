Spectrometer

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia


An optical spectrometer

A spectrometer is a scientific instrument used to separate and measure the spectral components of a physical phenomenon. In its most familiar form, an optical spectrometer disperses light into its component wavelengths using a prism or diffraction grating and measures the intensity at each wavelength, producing a spectrum.

Optical spectrometers

A typical optical spectrometer consists of a light source, a slit to define the beam, a collimator, a dispersing element (prism or grating), focusing optics, and a detector such as a CCD array. Spectrometers are used in astronomy to determine the composition, temperature and radial velocity of stars; in chemistry for absorption and emission spectroscopy; and in physics for the study of atomic and molecular structure.

Other types

The term is also applied to instruments that sort particles or radiation by other properties: mass spectrometers separate ions by mass-to-charge ratio, neutron spectrometers measure neutron energies, and electron spectrometers analyse electron energies. All share the principle of resolving a measured quantity into a distribution or "spectrum".

History

Spectroscopy began with Isaac Newton's prism experiments and was developed in the nineteenth century by Joseph von Fraunhofer, Gustav Kirchhoff and Robert Bunsen, whose work led to the identification of elements by their spectral lines.
