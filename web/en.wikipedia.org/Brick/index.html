A brick is a type of construction material used to build walls, pavements, and other elements in masonry. Typically rectangular, bricks are made of clay-bearing soil, sand, or concrete, and are hardened by drying, firing in a kiln, or chemical setting. One of humanity's oldest building materials, bricks are laid in courses with mortar in patterns called bonds, producing durable, fire-resistant structures.

History

Sun-dried mudbricks were used in the Neolithic Near East by about 7500 BC, and fired bricks appear in Mesopotamia and the Indus Valley by the third millennium BC. The Romans spread brick-making across their empire — stamping bricks with legion marks — and after a decline in early medieval northern Europe, brick Gothic architecture flourished in the Baltic region. Industrial kilns in the nineteenth century made bricks cheap and uniform.

Manufacture

Clay is prepared, moulded or extruded into shape, dried, and fired at around 900–1,200 °C, giving the brick its strength and red colour from iron oxide. Modern types include engineering bricks (dense and water-resistant), facing bricks, calcium silicate bricks, and concrete blocks.

Use

Brickwork is characterised by bonds such as stretcher bond, Flemish bond, and English bond. Bricks are also used for paving, chimneys, and refractory linings (firebricks). Standard sizes vary by country; a common metric brick measures 215 × 102.5 × 65 mm.
