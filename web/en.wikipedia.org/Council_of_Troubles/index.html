The Council of Troubles (Dutch: Raad van Beroerten; Spanish: Tribunal de los Tumultos), popularly known in the Netherlands as the Council of Blood (Bloedraad), was a special tribunal instituted on 9 September 1567 by the Duke of Alba, governor of the Habsburg Netherlands on behalf of Philip II of Spain, to prosecute those responsible for the religious and political unrest of 1566–67.

Background

After the iconoclastic fury (Beeldenstorm) of 1566 and growing resistance to Spanish religious policy, Philip II sent the Duke of Alba with an army to restore order. Alba set aside the ordinary courts and created the Council, whose members included the infamous prosecutor Juan de Vargas, to try cases of treason and heresy with summary procedures.

Prosecutions

The Council investigated around 12,000 people, of whom some 8,957 were banished or had property confiscated and roughly 1,000 were executed; many others fled abroad, including William of Orange. Its most famous victims were the Counts of Egmont and Horn, executed in Brussels on 5 June 1568 despite their loyalty to the king and status as Knights of the Golden Fleece.

Legacy

The Council's severity helped ignite the Dutch Revolt and the Eighty Years' War. Alba was recalled in 1573 and the Council was formally abolished in 1576.
