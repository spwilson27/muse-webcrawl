Rhizobium


Root nodules formed by Rhizobium symbiosis on a legume

Rhizobium is a genus of Gram-negative soil bacteria that fix atmospheric nitrogen in symbiosis with leguminous plants. After infection of the roots, the bacteria induce the formation of root nodules, within which they convert inert nitrogen gas (N2) into ammonia that the plant can assimilate — a process of enormous agricultural and ecological importance. The name derives from the Greek rhiza (root) and bios (life).

Symbiosis

Free-living rhizobia in the soil are attracted to legume root hairs by flavonoid compounds secreted by the plant. In response the bacteria produce signalling molecules called Nod factors, which cause the root hair to curl and the root cortex to divide, forming a nodule. Bacteria enter through an infection thread and are released into plant cells, where they differentiate into bacteroids enclosed in plant-derived membranes (symbiosomes). Supplied with sugars by the plant, the bacteroids use the enzyme nitrogenase to fix nitrogen, exporting fixed nitrogen compounds to the host in exchange.

The oxygen-sensitive nitrogenase is protected by the plant protein leghemoglobin, which, like animal haemoglobin, binds oxygen and gives active nodules their characteristic pink colour.

History

In 1888 the Dutch microbiologist Martinus Beijerinck isolated and cultured the nodule bacteria, naming them Bacillus radicicola; Frank later gave the genus the name Rhizobium. Hermann Hellriegel and Hermann Wilfarth had shown shortly before that legumes obtain nitrogen from the air through their nodules.

Taxonomy and importance

The genus belongs to the family Rhizobiaceae in the Alphaproteobacteria. Related nitrogen-fixing symbiont genera include Bradyrhizobium, Sinorhizobium (Ensifer) and Mesorhizobium; collectively they are often called rhizobia. Because legume crops inoculated with effective rhizobia need little nitrogen fertiliser, rhizobial inoculants are widely used in agriculture, and the symbiosis is a model system for plant–microbe interactions.
