Investment trust

TypeClosed-ended collective investment vehicle
StructurePublic limited company
Traded onStock exchanges (e.g. London Stock Exchange)
First founded1868 (Foreign & Colonial)
Key featureShares may trade at a premium or discount to NAV

An investment trust is a form of investment fund found mostly in the United Kingdom and Japan. Investment trusts are constituted as public limited companies and are therefore closed-ended: they issue a fixed number of shares that are traded on a stock exchange, and the trust invests the pooled money in a portfolio of assets.

Structure

Unlike open-ended funds such as unit trusts or OEICs, an investment trust does not continuously issue and redeem shares. Investors buy and sell shares in the trust itself on the market, so the share price is set by supply and demand and may stand at a premium or, more commonly, a discount to the underlying net asset value (NAV) per share.

Gearing

Because they are companies, investment trusts may borrow money to invest — a practice called gearing — which can amplify gains and losses. They may also retain up to 15% of income in reserve in good years to sustain dividends in leaner years, a feature that has helped some trusts increase their dividends for many consecutive decades.

History

The first investment trust, Foreign & Colonial Investment Trust, was founded in 1868 to give investors of moderate means access to diversified portfolios of foreign and colonial government bonds. Investment trusts grew rapidly in Britain and the United States in the 1920s, and the modern sector covers equities, property, private equity, infrastructure and renewable energy.

Governance

Each trust has an independent board of directors, elected by shareholders, which appoints and supervises an external fund manager. In the UK the industry is represented by the Association of Investment Companies (AIC), founded in 1932.
