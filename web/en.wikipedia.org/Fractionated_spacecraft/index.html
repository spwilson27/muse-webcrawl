Fractionated spacecraft

A fractionated architecture distributes one satellite's functions across multiple free-flying modules linked wirelessly.

A fractionated spacecraft is a satellite architecture in which the functions of a traditional monolithic spacecraft are distributed among multiple physically separate, free-flying modules that communicate wirelessly and cooperate to perform the mission as a single "virtual" satellite. Subsystems such as power generation, communications, data processing, attitude control and the mission payload each reside on their own module.

Rationale

Fractionation aims to make space systems more flexible and resilient. Because modules are smaller and more standardised, they can be launched on different vehicles, replaced or upgraded individually, and added to incrementally as technology or funding allows. If one module fails, only its function is lost, and a replacement can be launched without redesigning the whole system. The concept trades these benefits against the complexity of wireless inter-module links, formation flying, distributed computing and shared resource management (for example, wireless power and data transfer between modules).

History

The idea was studied in the 1980s and popularised in the 2000s, notably by DARPA's System F6 programme ("Future, Fast, Flexible, Fractionated, Free-Flying Spacecraft"), begun in 2007, which sought to demonstrate wireless networking, distributed computing and resource sharing among modules — though F6 was cancelled in 2013 before flight. Related ideas appear in cluster and formation-flying missions and in later work on satellite servicing and on-orbit assembly.
