The Mulberry harbours were two temporary portable harbours developed by the British Admiralty and War Office during the Second World War to facilitate the rapid offloading of cargo onto beaches during the Allied invasion of Normandy in June 1944. They were built in sections in Britain, towed across the English Channel, and assembled off the Normandy coast as part of Operation Neptune.

Design and construction

Each harbour consisted of an outer breakwater of floating steel "Bombardons" and sunken concrete caissons ("Phoenixes"), blockships ("Gooseberries"), floating piers and pierheads ("Spuds"), and floating roadways ("Whales") supported on pontoon units ("Beetles"). Some 600,000 tons of concrete and 31 miles of floating roadway went into the two harbours. More than 250 tugs were needed to tow the components across the Channel at roughly four knots.

Deployment

Mulberry A, assembled off Omaha Beach for the American forces, was wrecked by a severe storm on 19–22 June 1944 and abandoned. Mulberry B ("Port Winston") at Arromanches in the British sector survived and was used for the following eight months; over that period some 2.5 million men, 500,000 vehicles and 4 million tons of supplies were landed through it — far longer than the 90 days originally intended.

Background

The failure of the Dieppe Raid in 1942 had shown that the Allies could not rely on capturing a French port intact at the outset of an invasion, and that artificial harbours would be needed to sustain the invasion force until a major port such as Cherbourg could be taken and repaired. The concept is often credited in part to engineer Hugh Iorys Hughes and was championed by Winston Churchill, whose 1942 minute demanded piers that could "float up and down with the tide."

Legacy

Remnants of Mulberry B, including Phoenix caissons and sections of Whale roadway, are still visible at Arromanches today, and several caissons survive around the British coast where they were built or stranded.
