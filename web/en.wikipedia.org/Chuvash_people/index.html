Chuvash people

The Chuvash people (Chuvash: чӑвашсем, chavashsem; Russian: чуваши, chuvashi) are a Turkic ethnic group native to an area stretching from the Idel-Ural region to Siberia in Russia. Most of them live in the Chuvash Republic and the surrounding areas, although Chuvash diaspora communities exist throughout Russia. They speak the Chuvash language, the only surviving member of the Oghur branch of the Turkic languages.

History

The Chuvash are commonly regarded as descendants of the Volga Bulgars, whose medieval state on the middle Volga adopted Islam in the tenth century. After the Mongol invasions and the fall of Kazan in 1552, the region came under Russian rule. Mass Christianization campaigns in the eighteenth century converted most Chuvash to Orthodox Christianity, though elements of the traditional Chuvash faith (Vattisen Yaly) survived and have seen a modern revival.

Demographics

At the 2010 Russian census, about 1.4 million people identified as Chuvash, making them one of the largest ethnic groups in the Russian Federation. Roughly half live in the Chuvash Republic, whose capital is Cheboksary; sizeable communities live in Tatarstan, Bashkortostan, and Samara and Ulyanovsk oblasts.

Culture

Chuvash culture is noted for its rich geometric embroidery, in which every motif carries symbolic meaning, and for distinctive folk music and wooden architecture. Chuvash traditional religion centers on a supreme god, Tura, and a pantheon of spirits associated with nature and ancestors.
