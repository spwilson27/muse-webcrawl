Radioactive decay
Nuclear process
An unstable atomic nucleus loses energy by radiation

Radioactive decay (also known as nuclear decay, radioactivity, radioactive disintegration or nuclear disintegration) is the process by which an unstable atomic nucleus loses energy by radiation. A material containing unstable nuclei is considered radioactive. Three of the most common types of decay are alpha decay, beta decay and gamma decay, all of which involve emitting one or more particles.

Decay modes

In alpha decay the nucleus ejects a helium-4 nucleus, reducing its atomic number by two. In beta decay, a neutron converts to a proton (β−) or a proton to a neutron (β+), emitting an electron or positron and a neutrino. Gamma decay releases excess energy as a high-energy photon without changing the nucleus's composition. Other modes include spontaneous fission, neutron emission and electron capture.

Decay law and half-life

Decay is a random process at the level of single atoms, but the ensemble follows exponential decay characterised by a constant half-life: the time for half of a sample to decay. Half-lives range from fractions of a second to billions of years and are essentially unaffected by temperature, pressure or chemical state. Decay chains link sequences of radioactive progeny, as in the uranium and thorium series.

Discovery and applications

Henri Becquerel discovered the phenomenon in 1896; Marie and Pierre Curie and Ernest Rutherford developed its science. Applications include radiometric dating, medical imaging and radiotherapy, smoke detectors and nuclear power. See Radioactivity.
