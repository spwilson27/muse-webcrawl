Command responsibility

Command responsibility
FieldInternational humanitarian law
Key instrumentsAdditional Protocol I (1977), Rome Statute (1998), art. 28
Landmark caseIn re Yamashita (1946)

Command responsibility is an omission-based form of individual criminal responsibility under international law, whereby a superior — military commander or civilian leader — may be held criminally liable for crimes committed by subordinates under their effective command and control, where the superior knew or should have known that the subordinates were committing or about to commit such crimes and failed to take all necessary and reasonable measures to prevent them or to punish the perpetrators.

History

Early formulations appear in the Hague Conventions and in the work of the 1919 Commission on the Responsibility of the Authors of the War. The doctrine was prominently applied after the Second World War in the trial of Japanese general Tomoyuki Yamashita, who was convicted by a U.S. military commission for atrocities committed by troops under his command in the Philippines, establishing the "Yamashita standard" that a commander has a duty to control subordinates and may be liable for failing to discover and stop their crimes.

Modern law

Articles 86 and 87 of Additional Protocol I to the Geneva Conventions (1977) codified the duty of commanders. The ad hoc tribunals for Yugoslavia and Rwanda applied the doctrine in cases such as Čelebići, and Article 28 of the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court distinguishes military commanders (a "knew or should have known" standard) from civilian superiors (a higher "consciously disregarded information" standard). The ICC's first command-responsibility conviction was that of Jean-Pierre Bemba in 2016, which was overturned on appeal in 2018.

Elements

  • A superior–subordinate relationship with effective command and control;
  • The superior knew, or owing to the circumstances should have known, of the crimes;
  • The superior failed to take necessary and reasonable measures to prevent, repress, or report the crimes.
