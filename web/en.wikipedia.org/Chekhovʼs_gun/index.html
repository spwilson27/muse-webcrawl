Chekhov's gun

Chekhov's gun (Russian: Чеховское ружьё) is a narrative principle that states that every element in a story must be necessary, and irrelevant elements should be removed. The principle is named after the Russian playwright and short story writer Anton Chekhov, who stated it in several forms in his letters and notebooks. In its most famous formulation, if a rifle is hung on the wall in the first act of a play, it must be fired in a later act; conversely, if a rifle is to be fired later, it should be introduced early.

A rifle on the wall: the emblem of Chekhov's principle of narrative economy.

Origins and formulations

Chekhov expressed the idea repeatedly over his career. In a letter to Aleksandr Semenovich Lazarev dated 1 November 1889, he wrote that one must not put a loaded rifle on the stage if no one is thinking of firing it, adding that it is wrong to make promises one does not mean to keep. A related passage appears in the diary of the playwright's contemporary records of his table talk, and in a letter of 1889 Chekhov advised removing everything that has no relevance to the story: "If in the first act you have hung a pistol on the wall, then in the following one it should be fired. Otherwise don't put it there."

The principle also appears in Chekhov's own dramatic practice. His plays, including The Seagull, Uncle Vanya, and The Cherry Orchard, are noted for their economy of detail, in which apparently casual objects and remarks acquire significance as the action develops. In The Seagull, the dead bird Konstantin presents to Nina becomes a recurring symbol, and the gun Konstantin keeps is ultimately used in his offstage suicide.

Interpretation

Chekhov's gun is generally understood as a principle of narrative economy rather than a rigid rule that every object must literally be used. Its core claims are twofold. First, prominent details create expectations in the audience; an author who draws attention to an element implicitly promises that it will matter. Second, elements that never contribute to the story dilute its force and may mislead or frustrate the audience. The principle is closely related to the idea of foreshadowing, though it is broader: foreshadowing plants information for later payoff, while Chekhov's gun is also a discipline of revision, directing the writer to cut what does not pay off.

The principle has a corollary sometimes called "Chekhov's gun" in reverse: elements that prove crucial late in a story should be established earlier, so that their importance does not feel arbitrary. A sudden solution introduced at the climax without prior establishment is an example of deus ex machina, which the principle discourages.

Applications and criticism

The concept is widely taught in creative writing, screenwriting, and game design. In film, the principle is often invoked in editing: scenes and props that survive the final cut are expected to earn their place. Writers such as Ernest Hemingway, whose "iceberg theory" similarly prized omission, worked in a compatible tradition of economy.

Critics have cautioned against applying the principle mechanically. Red herrings in mystery fiction deliberately introduce elements that mislead, though defenders reply that a red herring still performs a function and so satisfies the deeper requirement that every element be necessary. Atmospheric detail, characterisation, and thematic resonance can also justify an element that has no direct plot function. The playwright's own works contain rich, seemingly incidental detail that builds mood and milieu, suggesting that Chekhov's standard was purposefulness rather than bare plot utility.
