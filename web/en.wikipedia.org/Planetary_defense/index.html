Planetary defense seeks to detect hazardous asteroids and comets and, if necessary, deflect them.

Planetary defense (also planetary defence) comprises the detection, tracking and potential deflection of near-Earth objects (NEOs) — asteroids and comets whose orbits bring them close to Earth and which could cause catastrophic damage on impact. It is coordinated internationally through bodies such as NASA's Planetary Defense Coordination Office, established in 2016, and the International Asteroid Warning Network (IAWN) and Space Missions Planning Advisory Group (SMPAG), both endorsed by the United Nations.

Detection

Survey telescopes such as Pan-STARRS, the Catalina Sky Survey and ATLAS scan the sky nightly for moving objects. By the early 2020s more than 30,000 near-Earth asteroids had been catalogued, including over 90 percent of those larger than one kilometre — the size capable of global effects — but a much smaller fraction of the estimated 25,000 objects larger than 140 metres, a completeness goal set by the U.S. Congress in 2005. The planned NEO Surveyor space telescope aims to close that gap from the late 2020s. Impact probabilities are expressed on the Torino and Palermo scales and monitored by systems such as NASA's Sentry.

Deflection methods

Proposed mitigation techniques include the kinetic impactor, which changes an asteroid's velocity by collision; the gravity tractor, a spacecraft that tows the object gravitationally over years; ion-beam shepherds; and, as a last resort, nuclear explosive devices. All require warning times of years to decades, which is why early detection is paramount. Fragmenting a large object is generally disfavoured, since it can convert one impact into many.

Missions and events

NASA's DART mission deliberately crashed into Dimorphos, the moon of asteroid Didymos, on 26 September 2022 and shortened its orbital period by about 33 minutes — the first demonstration that humanity can alter the motion of a celestial body. ESA's Hera mission, launched in 2024, is characterising the impact crater and aftermath. Real events underscore the risk: the 2013 Chelyabinsk meteor injured around 1,500 people, and the 1908 Tunguska event flattened some 2,000 square kilometres of Siberian forest.
