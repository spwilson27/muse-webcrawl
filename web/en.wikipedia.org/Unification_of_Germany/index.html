The unification of Germany was the process in which the German states — previously fragmented into dozens of kingdoms, principalities, and free cities — were merged into a single nation-state, the German Empire, proclaimed in 1871. It was achieved under the leadership of Prussia and its Minister President, Otto von Bismarck, through diplomacy and three wars.

Background

After the Congress of Vienna (1815), the German-speaking states formed the loose German Confederation dominated by Austria and Prussia. The economic union of the Zollverein (1834) integrated most states under Prussian leadership, while the liberal revolution of 1848–49 failed to unite Germany "from below" when the Frankfurt Parliament's crown was refused by the Prussian king.

Wars of Unification

Bismarck engineered unification "from above" through three wars: the Second Schleswig War against Denmark (1864), the Austro-Prussian War (1866), which expelled Austria from German affairs and created the North German Confederation, and the Franco-Prussian War (1870–71), whose nationalist fervour brought the south German states into the union. On 18 January 1871, in the Hall of Mirrors at Versailles, King Wilhelm I of Prussia was proclaimed German Emperor.

Legacy

The empire created in 1871 was a federal state of 25 states in which Prussia held a dominant position. The "lesser German" (kleindeutsch) solution left German-speaking Austria outside, a question unresolved until the 20th century. The unified Germany became continental Europe's leading industrial and military power, reshaping the European balance of power. Germany was divided again after 1945 and reunified in 1990.
