Hückel's rule

In organic chemistry, Hückel's rule predicts that a planar ring molecule will have aromatic properties if it has 4n + 2 π electrons, where n is a non-negative integer. The rule was proposed by German physicist and physical chemist Erich Hückel in 1931, based on quantum mechanical calculations using the method now known as Hückel molecular orbital theory.

Criteria for aromaticity

For the rule to apply, a molecule must be (1) cyclic, (2) planar or nearly planar, (3) fully conjugated, with a p orbital on every atom of the ring, and (4) contain 4n + 2 π electrons. Benzene (6 π electrons, n = 1) is the archetype; the cyclopentadienyl anion (6) and the tropylium cation (6) are classic ionic examples, while naphthalene satisfies the rule with 10 π electrons (n = 2). Systems with 4n π electrons that meet the other criteria are termed antiaromatic and are unusually unstable — cyclobutadiene being the standard example.

Refinements

The rule is most reliable for monocyclic hydrocarbons. Extensions and refinements include Möbius aromaticity (in which a twisted π system follows a reversed 4n rule), Baird's rule for excited states, and spherical aromaticity for fullerenes (the 2(n+1)² rule). Modern formulations also address homoaromaticity, in which through-space interaction bypasses a saturated centre.
