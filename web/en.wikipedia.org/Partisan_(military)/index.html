Partisan (military)

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Partisans conducted guerrilla operations against occupying forces in the Second World War

A partisan is a member of an irregular military force formed to oppose control of an area by a foreign power or by an occupying army. Partisans typically operate behind enemy lines, using guerrilla tactics such as ambushes, sabotage of railways and communications, and raids on supply depots, rather than confronting regular forces in open battle.

Etymology and early use

The word derives from the Italian partigiano, meaning an adherent of a party or cause. In early modern warfare, "parties" of light troops were detached from main armies to harass the enemy. During the Peninsular War (1808–1814), Spanish guerrillas fighting Napoleon's occupying forces gave the wider vocabulary of irregular warfare one of its most enduring terms, guerrilla, or "little war".

Second World War

Partisan movements reached their greatest scale during the Second World War. In Yugoslavia, the Partisans led by Josip Broz Tito grew into a force of several hundred thousand that liberated much of the country largely by its own efforts. In the Soviet Union, partisan detachments operating in the forests of Belarus and Ukraine attacked German supply lines supporting the Eastern Front. In Italy, the partigiani of the Resistance fought German occupation forces and the Italian Social Republic after 1943, while in France the Resistance carried out sabotage in support of the Allied landings. Large partisan forces also operated in Poland, Greece, Albania, China and the Philippines.

Legal status

Under the laws of armed conflict, partisans may qualify as lawful combatants entitled to prisoner-of-war status if they belong to an organised resistance movement, are commanded by a responsible person, wear a fixed distinctive sign recognisable at a distance, carry arms openly and observe the laws of war — criteria reflected in the Geneva Conventions of 1949. In practice, occupying powers frequently treated captured partisans as bandits or francs-tireurs and executed them, and partisan wars were often accompanied by brutal reprisals against civilian populations.

Later conflicts

Partisan-style warfare continued after 1945 in anti-colonial struggles, in the resistance to Soviet control in the Baltic states (the "Forest Brothers"), in Ukraine, and in numerous insurgencies of the Cold War and after. The term continues to be used for irregular fighters resisting an occupation, and has broader political meanings in some languages.
