Paradise Regained

Paradise Regained is a poem by the English poet John Milton, first published in 1671 in the same volume as his tragedy Samson Agonistes. It is a sequel to his earlier epic Paradise Lost. Whereas Paradise Lost is a long epic of more than ten thousand lines, Paradise Regained is a much briefer work of 2,070 lines in four books.

Background

According to Milton's friend Thomas Ellwood, the poem originated when Ellwood, having read the manuscript of Paradise Lost, remarked to Milton: "Thou hast said much here of Paradise lost, but what hast thou to say of Paradise found?" Milton later showed him the new poem, replying, "This is owing to you, for you put it into my head." Milton wrote most of his later works while blind, dictating to amanuenses.

Synopsis

The poem recounts the temptation of Christ in the wilderness, drawing on the Gospels of Matthew and Luke. After Christ's baptism, Satan convenes his council and resolves to tempt him. Over the course of the poem Satan offers Jesus food to break his fast, wealth and glory, the kingdoms of the world, and finally the learning and culture of Athens — each refused through patient obedience and scriptural reasoning. Satan, defeated, returns in a storm by night, and in the final temptation sets Jesus on the pinnacle of the Temple in Jerusalem, daring him to cast himself down; Jesus answers, "Tempt not the Lord thy God," stands unshaken, and Satan falls. Angels then minister to the Son of God and sing his triumph, and Jesus returns to his mother's house.

Themes

Whereas Paradise Lost depicts humanity's fall through Adam's disobedience, Paradise Regained presents the second Adam who reverses the fall through obedience and endurance. Critics have noted its emphasis on the kingdom of God as inward and spiritual rather than political — a pointed theme for Milton after the failure of the English Commonwealth — and its quiet, dialectical style, in contrast with the grandeur of the earlier epic.

Reception

Though long overshadowed by Paradise Lost, the poem has been admired for its compressed power; Samuel Johnson and later critics praised its language even while finding it less engaging than its predecessor. Modern scholarship, including the work of Barbara Lewalski and Stanley Fish, treats it as a major poem in its own right and the capstone of Milton's career alongside Samson Agonistes.
