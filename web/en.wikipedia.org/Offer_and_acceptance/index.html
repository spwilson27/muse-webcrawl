Offer and acceptance

Offer and acceptance are the traditional building blocks of a binding contract in common-law systems. An agreement is formed when one party (the offeror) makes an offer — a clear statement of terms by which it is prepared to be bound — and the other party (the offeree) accepts those terms unequivocally.

Offer

An offer must be definite, communicated to the offeree, and distinguishable from an "invitation to treat" — such as goods displayed in a shop window or an advertisement — which is merely an invitation to negotiate (Fisher v Bell; Pharmaceutical Society of Great Britain v Boots Cash Chemists). Advertisements can occasionally amount to offers, as in Carlill v Carbolic Smoke Ball Company (1893), where a unilateral offer to the whole world was accepted by performance. An offer ends by revocation, rejection, lapse of time, the death of a party, or a counter-offer, which destroys the original offer (Hyde v Wrench).

Acceptance

Acceptance must be an unqualified assent to the terms of the offer, normally communicated to the offeror; silence generally cannot constitute acceptance. Where parties negotiate on their own standard terms, the "battle of the forms" may be resolved by the "last shot" rule. Under the postal rule of Adams v Lindsell (1818), an acceptance sent by post takes effect when posted, not when received, whereas instantaneous communications such as email generally follow the receipt rule (Entores v Miles Far East).

Related doctrines

Offer and acceptance analysis is supplemented by the requirements of consideration and intention to create legal relations, and in practice courts increasingly look to the parties' conduct as a whole rather than a rigid moment of agreement. Statute and international instruments such as the CISG modify the classical rules in commercial contexts.
