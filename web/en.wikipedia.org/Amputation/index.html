Amputation

Modern prostheses can restore much mobility after a lower-limb amputation.

Amputation is the removal of a limb by trauma, medical illness, or surgery. As a surgical measure, it is used to control pain or a disease process in the affected limb, such as malignancy or gangrene. In some cases, it is carried out on individuals as a preventive surgery for such problems. Traumatic amputation, by contrast, results from an accident in which a limb is fully or partially severed.

Causes

In developed countries the most common cause of amputation is poor circulation — typically from diabetes and peripheral artery disease — followed by trauma, cancer, and infection. Diabetes alone accounts for the majority of lower-limb amputations worldwide, and the World Health Organization estimates that tens of millions of people live with limb amputations. Congenital limb deficiency, present at birth, is technically distinguished from surgical amputation.

Procedure and rehabilitation

The goal of surgical amputation is to remove all diseased tissue while preserving as much healthy limb as possible, fashioning a residual limb ("stump") suited to a prosthesis. Common levels include below-knee (transtibial) and above-knee (transfemoral) amputations. Rehabilitation involves wound care, desensitization, strengthening, and prosthetic fitting; many patients experience phantom limb sensations or phantom pain, which can be treated with medication, mirror therapy, and other techniques.
