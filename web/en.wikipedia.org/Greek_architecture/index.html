Greek architecture

Greek architecture refers to the architecture of the Greek-speaking peoples who inhabited the Greek mainland, the Aegean islands, and colonies in Asia Minor and Italy during the period from the emergence of a recognisably Greek civilisation around 900 BC until the 1st century AD, with the earliest remaining works dating from the 7th century BC. It is best known for its temples, which are found throughout the Greek world, and for the formal vocabulary of the classical orders that profoundly influenced later Roman and Western architecture.

The Parthenon in Athens, the most famous surviving Greek temple

The orders

Greek architecture is structured by three classical orders. The Doric order, the earliest and plainest, has sturdy fluted columns with simple capitals and no bases. The Ionic order, developed in Ionia, is slimmer, with scrolled volutes on its capitals and a moulded base. The Corinthian order, the most ornate, is crowned by capitals carved with acanthus leaves; it was favoured more by the Romans than the Greeks. Temples were laid out on precise proportional systems, with subtle refinements such as entasis (a slight swelling of the column shaft) and an upward curvature of horizontal lines to correct optical illusions.

Temples and public buildings

The temple, usually housing a cult statue and set on a stepped platform (crepidoma), was the central building type. Around the agora, Greeks built stoas (covered colonnades), bouleuteria (council houses), theatres exploiting natural hillsides, stadia, and gymnasia. Most construction was of local stone, especially marble on the mainland and islands, roofed with timber and terracotta tiles.

Legacy

Copied and adapted by Rome, Greek architecture supplied the grammar of classical design revived in the Renaissance and in the Greek Revival of the 18th and 19th centuries, visible in civic buildings across Europe and North America.
