Diazepam

Diazepam, first marketed as Valium, is a medicine of the benzodiazepine family that acts as an anxiolytic. It is used to treat anxiety, alcohol withdrawal syndrome, muscle spasms, seizures, insomnia, and restless legs syndrome. It may also be given before certain medical procedures to reduce tension and anxiety.

Pharmacology

Diazepam works by enhancing the effect of the neurotransmitter gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) at the GABAA receptor, resulting in sedative, hypnotic, anxiolytic, anticonvulsant, and skeletal muscle relaxant properties. It is long-acting, with a half-life of 20–100 hours, and produces active metabolites including nordiazepam and oxazepam.

History

Diazepam was patented in 1959 by Hoffmann-La Roche and developed by chemist Leo Sternbach. It was released in 1963 and became one of the most frequently prescribed medications in the United States from 1969 to 1982. It appears on the World Health Organization's List of Essential Medicines.

Adverse effects

Common side effects include drowsiness and trouble with coordination. Long-term use can lead to tolerance, physical dependence, and withdrawal symptoms on abrupt discontinuation. Because of these risks, diazepam is generally recommended only for short-term treatment.
