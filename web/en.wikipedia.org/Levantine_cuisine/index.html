Levantine cuisine is the traditional cuisine of the Levant, the historical region of the Eastern Mediterranean encompassing modern Lebanon, Syria, Jordan, Palestine, Israel, Cyprus and the Hatay region of Turkey. It is one of the oldest continuously developed cuisines in the world, shaped by the agricultural heartland of the Fertile Crescent and by centuries of trade and cultural exchange.

The cuisine is characterised by an abundance of fresh vegetables, olive oil, grains, legumes and herbs, with meat — especially lamb — used more sparingly than in many Western cuisines. Meals are often served as mezze, a selection of small dishes shared communally, accompanied by flatbreads such as pita used to scoop dips and salads.

History

The Levant was among the first regions where wheat, barley, lentils and chickpeas were domesticated, and its cooking reflects this ancient agricultural heritage. Phoenician and later Arab, Ottoman and French influences layered new ingredients and techniques onto the regional base: the Ottomans spread dishes such as kebabs and stuffed vegetables across the region, while trade brought spices, coffee and later New World ingredients like tomatoes and peppers.

Common ingredients and dishes

  • Hummus — a dip of mashed chickpeas blended with tahini, lemon juice and garlic.
  • Falafel — deep-fried balls of ground chickpeas or fava beans with herbs and spices.
  • Tabbouleh — a salad of finely chopped parsley, bulgur, tomato, mint and lemon.
  • Kibbeh — dishes based on spiced ground meat and bulgur, including the national dish of Lebanon, kibbeh nayyeh (raw kibbeh).
  • Shawarma — marinated meat roasted on a vertical spit and shaved into wraps.
  • Baklava — layered filo pastry with nuts and syrup, shared with neighbouring cuisines.

Regional variations

While the core repertoire is shared, each country adds its own specialities: Aleppo in Syria is famed for its use of chili and sour cherries; Lebanese cooking emphasises garlic and lemon; Palestinian cuisine is known for musakhan (sumac chicken with onions and flatbread); Jordanian mansaf — lamb cooked in fermented dried yogurt — is a national dish served at celebrations.
