The Convention on International Civil Aviation, commonly called the Chicago Convention, is the foundational treaty of international air law. Signed on 7 December 1944 by 52 states meeting in Chicago during the Second World War, it established the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) and set the rules governing airspace, aircraft registration and safety, and the rights of signatory countries in international air transport.

Key provisions

The Convention affirms that every state has complete and exclusive sovereignty over the airspace above its territory. It requires aircraft to carry the nationality of their state of registration, sets rules for scheduled and non-scheduled flights, prohibits the use of civil aviation for purposes inconsistent with the Convention, and provides for the adoption of international standards and recommended practices through ICAO's Annexes. Article 33 provides for mutual recognition of certificates of airworthiness and personnel licences.

The freedoms of the air

Delegates could not agree in Chicago on exchanging commercial traffic rights multilaterally; instead, two side agreements — the Transit Agreement and the Transport Agreement — codified the first "freedoms of the air". Commercial rights have since been exchanged mainly through thousands of bilateral air services agreements.

Legacy

Amended several times (including Article 83 bis on leased aircraft), the Convention now has 193 parties and remains the constitutional document of world civil aviation, the legal basis for ICAO's safety, security, and environmental standard-setting.
