Vivisection
DefinitionSurgery or experimentation on living organisms
Modern usageOften applied to all animal experimentation
Key frameworkThe Three Rs (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement)

Vivisection (from Latin vivus "alive" + sectio "cutting") is surgery conducted for experimental purposes on a living organism, typically animals with a central nervous system, to observe living internal structure and function. The term is often used more broadly — especially by opponents of the practice — to refer to any experimentation on live animals.

History

Dissection and experimentation on living animals date to antiquity (Galen's work on pigs and apes), and possibly to alleged human vivisections in Hellenistic Alexandria. The practice expanded with experimental physiology in the 17th–19th centuries — William Harvey's circulation studies, and later Claude Bernard's influential Introduction to the Study of Experimental Medicine (1865). Public opposition grew in parallel: Britain's Cruelty to Animals Act 1876 was the first national law regulating animal experiments, and the 1903 Brown Dog affair in London became a flashpoint of the controversy.

Regulation and debate

Modern animal research is regulated by ethical review, licensing, and the "Three Rs" principles articulated by Russell and Burch (1959): Replacement of animals where possible, Reduction in numbers, and Refinement to minimise suffering. Defenders argue such research has yielded major medical advances (insulin, vaccines, surgical techniques); critics question its necessity and predictive value and press for alternatives such as cell culture, organoids, and computer modelling. The related term "vivisection" historically never applied to routine modern toxicity testing, but it remains the campaigning word of the anti-vivisection movement.
