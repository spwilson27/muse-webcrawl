In mathematics, ring theory is the study of rings — algebraic structures consisting of a set equipped with two binary operations, addition and multiplication, generalising the arithmetic of the integers. It is a central branch of abstract algebra.

Definitions

A ring is an abelian group under addition, a monoid under multiplication, with multiplication distributing over addition. Basic examples include the integers, polynomial rings, matrix rings, and residue-class rings Z/nZ. Within a ring one studies subrings, ideals (kernels of homomorphisms), quotient rings, and modules — the ring-theoretic analogues of vector spaces.

History

The subject grew out of 19th-century work on algebraic number theory: attempts to prove Fermat's Last Theorem led Ernst Kummer to introduce "ideal numbers", which Dedekind recast as ideals of rings of algebraic integers. David Hilbert systematised invariant theory, and in the 1920s Emmy Noether's axiomatic approach — recognising the power of chain conditions (Noetherian rings) — transformed the field. Emil Artin developed the structure theory of semisimple rings, culminating in the Artin–Wedderburn theorem.

Commutative ring theory

The commutative case, called commutative algebra, underlies algebraic geometry: points of a variety correspond to prime ideals of a coordinate ring, a correspondence anchored by Hilbert's Nullstellensatz. Principal ideal domains, unique factorisation domains, Dedekind domains and local rings are the classical well-behaved classes.

Noncommutative ring theory

When multiplication is not commutative — as for matrices or operators — ideals become left/right asymmetric and the theory connects to representation theory and functional analysis. Simple rings, division rings and the Jacobson radical are key concepts.
