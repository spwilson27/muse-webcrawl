Egyptian hieroglyphs (from Greek ἱερογλυφικά, "sacred carvings") were the formal writing system used in Ancient Egypt for writing the Egyptian language. Hieroglyphs combined logographic, syllabic and alphabetic elements, with some 1,000 distinct characters in use at their most expansive. Cursive hieroglyphs were used for religious literature on papyrus and wood, while the related hieratic and later demotic scripts served everyday purposes.

Knowledge of how to read hieroglyphs was lost as temple priesthoods declined in late antiquity. The script was deciphered in the early 19th century following the discovery of the Rosetta Stone in 1799, with Jean-François Champollion's 1822 breakthrough — building on the work of Thomas Young — establishing that the signs recorded sound as well as meaning.

Writing system

Hieroglyphic signs fall into three functional classes: phonograms, representing one, two, or three consonants; logograms, representing a whole word; and determinatives, silent signs placed after a word to clarify its meaning. Vowels were generally not written. Texts could run left-to-right, right-to-left, or in columns, with human and animal signs facing the beginning of the line. Egyptian scribes arranged signs into aesthetically balanced square blocks (quadrats) rather than simple lines.

History

The earliest hieroglyphic inscriptions date to about 3200 BC, making the script one of the world's oldest writing systems, roughly contemporary with Mesopotamian cuneiform. It remained in use for more than three millennia. The last dated hieroglyphic inscription, at the Temple of Philae, was carved in AD 394. Gardiner's sign list, published in 1927, remains the standard catalogue of the common signs of the Middle Egyptian language.

Decipherment

The Rosetta Stone (196 BC) bears a priestly decree in hieroglyphic, demotic, and Greek. By comparing royal names in cartouches — Ptolemy and Cleopatra — Young and Champollion established the phonetic values of the signs, and Champollion, drawing on his knowledge of Coptic, announced a full decipherment in his Lettre à M. Dacier (1822).
