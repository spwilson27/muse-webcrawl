Neocortex

The neocortex, also called the neopallium, is the part of the mammalian brain involved in higher-order functions such as sensory perception, cognition, motor commands, spatial reasoning, and language. It forms the bulk of the cerebral cortex — in humans about 90% of it — and is arranged as a sheet of six distinct layers of neurons.

Structure

The six layers, numbered I (outermost) to VI, differ in cell types and connection patterns: layer IV receives most sensory thalamic input, while layers V and VI send major outputs to subcortical structures and the spinal cord. Neurons are organized into vertical cortical columns. In larger brains the sheet folds into gyri and sulci to increase surface area; human neocortex has an area of roughly 2,500 cm² and contains on the order of 16 billion neurons.

Evolution and function

The neocortex is unique to mammals and is thought to have expanded dramatically in primates. Regional specializations correspond to sensory areas (visual, auditory, somatosensory), motor cortex, and association areas supporting memory, planning, and — in humans — language.
