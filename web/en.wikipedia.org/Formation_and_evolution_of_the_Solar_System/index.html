Formation and evolution of the Solar System

Solar System formation
Age: 4.568 billion years
Model: Nebular hypothesis
Key stages: Collapse of molecular cloud, protoplanetary disk, planetesimal accretion
Future: Sun becomes red giant in ~5 billion years

The formation and evolution of the Solar System began about 4.6 billion years ago with the gravitational collapse of a small part of a giant molecular cloud. Most of the collapsing mass collected in the center, forming the Sun, while the rest flattened into a protoplanetary disk out of which the planets, moons, asteroids, and other small Solar System bodies formed.

Nebular hypothesis

The widely accepted nebular hypothesis, first developed by Emanuel Swedenborg, Immanuel Kant, and Pierre-Simon Laplace, proposes that the Solar System formed from a rotating cloud of gas and dust. Radiometric dating of meteorites gives an age of 4.568–4.571 billion years.

Formation of the planets

Dust grains accreted into planetesimals, which collided and merged into planetary embryos. Inside the frost line, rocky planets Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars formed; beyond it, the cores of the giant planets Jupiter and Saturn captured vast envelopes of hydrogen and helium. The giant-planet impact hypothesis explains the formation of Earth's Moon.

Later evolution

The Nice model describes migration of the giant planets that reshaped the outer Solar System and may have triggered the Late Heavy Bombardment about 4 billion years ago.

Future

In about 5 billion years the Sun will expand into a red giant, engulfing the inner planets, and later shed its outer layers as a planetary nebula, leaving a white dwarf.
