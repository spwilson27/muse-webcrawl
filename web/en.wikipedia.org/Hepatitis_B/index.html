Hepatitis B is an infectious disease caused by the hepatitis B virus (HBV) that affects the liver; it is a type of viral hepatitis. It can cause both acute and chronic disease. Many people have no symptoms during an initial infection. In acute infection, some may develop a rapid illness with vomiting, yellowish skin, tiredness, dark urine, and abdominal pain. Chronic infection may eventually cause cirrhosis and liver cancer.

Transmission

HBV is transmitted by exposure to infectious blood or body fluids, including through sexual contact, sharing of needles, and from mother to child during birth (perinatal transmission). In highly endemic regions, spread from mother to infant and between young children is most common. The virus can survive outside the body for at least seven days. The incubation period is 30 to 180 days.

The virus

HBV is a partially double-stranded DNA virus of the family Hepadnaviridae. The complete infectious virion (the Dane particle) is about 42 nm in diameter. Its genome encodes the surface antigen (HBsAg) — whose detection in blood is the principal screening marker — the core antigen (HBcAg), and the e antigen (HBeAg), a marker of high infectivity and viral replication.

Course of disease

The likelihood of chronic infection depends strongly on age: about 90% of infants infected at birth become chronically infected, compared with fewer than 5% of immunocompetent adults. Chronic hepatitis B can lead, over decades, to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma; HBV is estimated to cause several hundred thousand deaths annually worldwide.

Prevention and treatment

A safe and effective vaccine has been available since 1982; the World Health Organization recommends a birth dose within 24 hours followed by two or three further doses, and hepatitis B vaccination is part of infant immunisation schedules in most countries. Hepatitis B immunoglobulin and vaccination are given to infants of infected mothers. There is no cure for chronic infection, but antiviral drugs such as tenofovir and entecavir suppress viral replication and reduce the risk of cirrhosis and liver cancer. Acute hepatitis B usually requires only supportive care.

History

The virus was discovered in the 1960s through the work of Baruch Blumberg, who identified the "Australia antigen" (HBsAg) — work for which he received the Nobel Prize in 1976 — and went on to help develop the first hepatitis B vaccine.
