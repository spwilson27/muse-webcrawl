The N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor (NMDA receptor or NMDAR) is a glutamate receptor and ion channel found in neurons. The NMDA receptor is one of three types of ionotropic glutamate receptors, the other two being AMPA and kainate receptors. It is activated when glutamate and the co-agonist glycine (or D-serine) bind to it, and it is unusual among receptors in also being voltage-dependent: at resting membrane potentials its pore is blocked by Mg2+ ions, a block relieved only upon depolarisation of the postsynaptic membrane.

Structure

NMDA receptors are heterotetramers composed of two obligatory GluN1 subunits and typically two GluN2 subunits (GluN2A–D), sometimes incorporating GluN3 subunits. Each subunit has an extracellular amino-terminal domain, a ligand-binding domain, a transmembrane domain forming the ion pore, and an intracellular carboxyl-terminal domain that interacts with postsynaptic density proteins such as PSD-95.

Function

The opening of the NMDA receptor channel allows Ca2+ influx that triggers intracellular signalling cascades central to synaptic plasticity. The receptor's dual requirement — presynaptic glutamate release plus postsynaptic depolarisation — makes it a molecular coincidence detector underlying Hebbian learning, long-term potentiation (LTP), and long-term depression (LTD), and it is considered essential for learning and memory.

Clinical significance

Excessive NMDA receptor activation contributes to excitotoxicity in stroke and neurodegenerative disease. Antagonists include the Alzheimer's drug memantine, the anaesthetic and antidepressant ketamine, and phencyclidine. Autoantibodies against the GluN1 subunit cause anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis, a treatable autoimmune disorder featuring psychosis, seizures, and dyskinesias.
