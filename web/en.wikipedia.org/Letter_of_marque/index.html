Letter of marque

A letter of marque and reprisal (French: lettre de marque) was a government licence authorising a private person, known as a privateer, to attack and capture vessels of a foreign state at war with the issuing state. The holder of a letter of marque was entitled to keep captured ships and cargo as prizes, subject to adjudication by a prize court; without one, the same acts constituted piracy.

History

Letters of marque originated in the medieval practice of reprisal, by which a wronged merchant could obtain redress by seizing the goods of the wrongdoer's countrymen. From the 16th to the 19th centuries they were a standard instrument of naval warfare, allowing states to expand their effective fleets cheaply. Privateers operating under letters of marque — such as those of Elizabethan England against Spain, or American privateers during the Revolutionary War and the War of 1812 — captured thousands of merchant ships. The Paris Declaration of 1856 abolished privateering among its signatories, though the United States never formally joined; the U.S. Constitution still empowers Congress "to grant Letters of Marque and Reprisal" (Article I, Section 8).
