Continental crust

Continental crust is the layer of igneous, sedimentary, and metamorphic rocks that forms the geological continents and the areas of shallow seabed close to their shores, known as continental shelves. This layer is sometimes called sial because its bulk composition is richer in aluminium silicate minerals than that of the oceanic crust, which is called sima because it is richer in magnesium silicate minerals. Changes in seismic wave speed show that at a certain depth the continental crust changes from a more brittle upper crust to a stronger lower crust, a boundary known as the Conrad discontinuity.

Thickness and density

Continental crust consists of a lower basaltic layer and an upper granitic layer. It is less dense than the material of the Earth's mantle and thus "floats" on top of it. Continental crust is also less dense than oceanic crust, whose density is about 2.9 g/cm³; continental crust has a density of about 2.7 g/cm³. The thickness of continental crust ranges from about 25 to 70 km: it averages about 35–40 km, but is thickened to more than 70 km beneath great mountain ranges such as the Himalayas and the Andes, and thinned to less than 25 km where it has been stretched by rifting.

Composition

The bulk composition of the continental crust is intermediate (andesitic to granodioritic), in contrast to the basaltic composition of oceanic crust. Most continental crust is dry land above sea level, although continental blocks also include submerged margins. The upper crust, accessible to sampling, is on average granodioritic, while the lower crust is more mafic. Weathering, erosion and sediment transport continually rework the surface of the continents.

Age and formation

Unlike oceanic crust, which is nowhere older than about 200 million years, parts of the continental crust — the cratons — are up to about 4 billion years old; the oldest known terrestrial material, zircon crystals from Jack Hills, Australia, is about 4.4 billion years old. Continental crust grows over geological time principally at convergent plate margins, where subduction-related magmatism adds new material, although the details of crustal growth remain debated. Because it is buoyant, continental crust resists subduction and so survives for billions of years, accumulating a long geological record.
