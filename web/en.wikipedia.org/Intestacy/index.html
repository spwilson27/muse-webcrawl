Intestacy

Intestacy is the condition of the estate of a person who dies without having in force a valid will or other binding declaration. Alternatively this may also apply where a will or declaration has been made, but only applies to part of the estate; the remaining estate forms the "Intestate Estate".

Intestacy law, also referred to as the law of descent and distribution, is the body of law (statutory and case law) that determines who is entitled to the property from the estate under the rules of inheritance.

Rules of intestate succession

Most jurisdictions prescribe a fixed order of inheritance. Typically, a surviving spouse or civil partner inherits first, often the whole estate or a statutory share, followed by children (usually per stirpes), then parents, siblings, and more remote kin. If no eligible relatives can be found, the estate escheats to the state or crown — as bona vacantia in English law.

In England and Wales, the rules are governed primarily by the Administration of Estates Act 1925 as amended. In the United States, intestacy is governed by state law, often drawing on the Uniform Probate Code.

Partial intestacy and related concepts

A person may die partly testate and partly intestate where a will disposes of only some assets, or where a gift in a will fails. An administrator, usually a close relative, is appointed by the court to distribute the intestate estate, in contrast to the executor named in a will.

Historical note

Rules favouring the eldest son (primogeniture) historically governed land inheritance in England until reforms of 1925. Roman law developed early systems of intestate succession in the Twelve Tables.
