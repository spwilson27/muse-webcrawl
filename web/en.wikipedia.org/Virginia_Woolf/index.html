Adeline Virginia Woolf (née Stephen; 25 January 1882 – 28 March 1941) was an English writer and one of the most important modernist authors of the 20th century. A pioneer of the stream-of-consciousness technique, she wrote novels including Mrs Dalloway (1925), To the Lighthouse (1927) and The Waves (1931), and the feminist essay A Room of One's Own (1929). She was a central figure of the Bloomsbury Group.

Early life

Woolf was born into a literary family in South Kensington, London; her father, Sir Leslie Stephen, was a noted historian and editor of the Dictionary of National Biography. Educated at home, she suffered the first of several mental breakdowns after her mother's death in 1895, and began writing professionally in 1900. In 1912 she married Leonard Woolf; together they founded the Hogarth Press in 1917, which published her works and those of T. S. Eliot, Katherine Mansfield and Sigmund Freud in English.

Major works

After conventional early novels, Woolf developed her mature style in Jacob's Room (1922), Mrs Dalloway — which follows Clarissa Dalloway through a single day in post-war London — and To the Lighthouse, a meditation on family, time and perception built around the Ramsay family on Skye. Orlando (1928), a fantastical biography spanning centuries and a change of sex, was dedicated to her lover Vita Sackville-West. Her essays, diaries and letters fill many volumes.

Later life and legacy

Woolf struggled with recurrent depression throughout her life, worsened during the Second World War by the destruction of her London home in the Blitz. In March 1941 she drowned herself in the River Ouse near her home, Monk's House in Rodmell, Sussex. Long treated as a minor modernist beside Joyce and Eliot, she was recovered by feminist scholarship in the 1970s and is now regarded as one of the greatest English novelists, her work continually adapted for stage and screen.
