Octans

Octans (the Octant)
Abbreviation: Oct
Genitive: Octantis
Area: 291 sq. deg.
Brightest star: Nu Octantis
Notable: contains the south celestial pole

Octans is a faint constellation located in the deep southern sky. Its name is Latin for the eighth part of a circle, an allusion to the octant, a navigational instrument. The constellation was devised in the early 1750s by French astronomer Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille, who charted the southern stars from the Cape of Good Hope, and was originally called l'Octant de Réflexion.

South celestial pole

Octans contains the south celestial pole. Unlike the northern sky, it has no bright pole star: Sigma Octantis, the star nearest the pole, is only of apparent magnitude 5.4 and barely visible to the naked eye, which makes finding south by the stars less convenient in the southern hemisphere. Navigators instead commonly use the constellation Crux (the Southern Cross) to locate the pole.

Stars

Octans is one of the faintest and least conspicuous constellations. Its brightest star, Nu Octantis, is an orange giant of magnitude 3.7. Beta and Delta Octantis are also relatively prominent, and Delta Octantis is notable as Saturn's south polar star.

Deep-sky objects

The constellation contains few deep-sky objects; it includes the peculiar galaxy NGC 2573 ("Polarissima Australis") lying close to the south celestial pole, and the open cluster Melotte 227.
