Direct oral anticoagulant

DOACs are taken orally and do not require routine blood monitoring.

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), also called non-vitamin K antagonist oral anticoagulants (NOACs), are a class of anticoagulant medications that act by directly inhibiting a single clotting factor. Unlike warfarin, which reduces several vitamin-K-dependent factors, DOACs specifically target thrombin (factor IIa) or activated factor X (factor Xa).

Agents

Dabigatran is a direct thrombin inhibitor, approved in 2010 for stroke prevention in atrial fibrillation. The factor Xa inhibitors include rivaroxaban, apixaban, and edoxaban, approved in the following years, and betrixaban. Ximelagatran, an early thrombin inhibitor, was withdrawn in 2006 because of liver toxicity.

Uses

DOACs are used for the prevention of stroke and systemic embolism in non-valvular atrial fibrillation, the treatment and secondary prevention of deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism, and thromboprophylaxis after hip or knee replacement surgery. They are generally not recommended in patients with mechanical heart valves or moderate-to-severe mitral stenosis, for whom warfarin remains standard.

Advantages and risks

Compared with warfarin, DOACs have rapid onset, fixed dosing, few food interactions, fewer drug interactions, and do not require routine INR monitoring; trials showed rates of intracranial hemorrhage are substantially lower. Their main risks are bleeding — particularly gastrointestinal bleeding with some agents — and the consequences of short half-lives when doses are missed. Specific reversal agents exist: idarucizumab for dabigatran and andexanet alfa for factor Xa inhibitors, in addition to supportive prohemostatic treatments.
