Lecithin

Lecithin
Type: Mixture of phospholipids
Main component: Phosphatidylcholine
Sources: Egg yolk, soybeans, sunflower
Food additive: E322 (emulsifier)

Lecithin is a generic term for mixtures of phospholipids — principally phosphatidylcholine — together with triglycerides and other substances, occurring naturally in animal and plant tissues. It was first isolated from egg yolk in 1845 by the French chemist Théodore Gobley, who named it after the Greek lekithos (egg yolk).

Uses

Because its molecules have both water-loving and fat-loving parts, lecithin is an effective emulsifier and is widely used in foods such as chocolate, margarine, baked goods and infant formula, and as a release agent and wetting agent in industry. Commercial lecithin is obtained mainly from soybean oil processing, with sunflower lecithin as an alternative for soy-free products.

Biology

Phosphatidylcholine is a major component of cell membranes and a dietary source of choline; the body also uses it to make the neurotransmitter acetylcholine.
