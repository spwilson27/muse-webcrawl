Biofuel

Biofuel is a fuel produced from biomass — recently living plant or animal material — as opposed to fossil fuels formed over geological time. Common biofuels include ethanol, made by fermenting sugars and starches, and biodiesel, made from vegetable oils or animal fats. Biofuels are used mainly for transport, often blended with petrol or diesel.

Types

First-generation biofuels are made from food crops: ethanol from corn (United States) and sugarcane (Brazil), and biodiesel from rapeseed, soy, or palm oil. Second-generation biofuels use non-food lignocellulosic feedstocks such as agricultural residues, wood, and dedicated energy grasses, while third-generation fuels are derived from algae. Biogas, a mixture of methane and carbon dioxide from anaerobic digestion, is used for heat, power, and upgraded biomethane.

Advantages and criticism

Supporters argue biofuels can reduce net carbon dioxide emissions because the carbon released in combustion was recently captured by growing plants, and can improve energy security. Critics point to competition with food production (the "food versus fuel" debate), land-use change and deforestation — for example palm-oil expansion in Southeast Asia — which can erase or reverse the carbon benefit, plus effects on food prices and biodiversity. Advanced fuels from wastes and residues are promoted as avoiding most of these problems.
