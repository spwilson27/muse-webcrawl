Amplifier

An amplifier, electronic amplifier or (informally) amp is an electronic device that can increase the magnitude of a signal (a time-varying voltage or current). It is a two-port electronic circuit that uses electric power from a power supply to increase the amplitude or power of a signal applied to its input terminals, producing a proportionally greater signal at its output. The amount of amplification provided is measured by gain: the ratio of output voltage, current, or power to input.

Properties

Key properties of amplifiers include gain, bandwidth, efficiency, linearity (and the resulting distortion), noise, output dynamic range, slew rate, and stability. Amplifiers are designed for many different functions: weak signals from sensors or antennas may be amplified with minimal added noise (low-noise amplifiers), while power amplifiers deliver large amounts of power to loads such as loudspeakers or transmitters.

History

The first practical amplifying device was the Audion (triode) vacuum tube, invented by Lee de Forest in 1906, which made radio and long-distance telephony practical. Vacuum-tube amplifiers dominated electronics until the transistor, invented at Bell Labs in 1947, enabled smaller, more efficient solid-state designs. Later, the operational amplifier became a standard building block of analog electronics.

Types

  • Audio power amplifiers for driving loudspeakers
  • RF power amplifiers in transmitters
  • Instrumentation amplifiers for precision measurement
  • Distributed amplifiers for wideband signals
  • Optical amplifiers in fiber-optic communication
