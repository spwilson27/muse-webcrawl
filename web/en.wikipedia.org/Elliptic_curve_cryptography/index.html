Elliptic curve cryptography

Elliptic curve cryptography (ECC) is an approach to public-key cryptography based on the algebraic structure of elliptic curves over finite fields. It was proposed independently by Neal Koblitz and Victor S. Miller in 1985. ECC allows smaller keys to provide security equivalent to non-elliptic systems: a 256-bit elliptic-curve key is generally considered comparable in strength to a 3072-bit RSA key.

Mathematical basis

An elliptic curve over a finite field consists of points satisfying an equation of the form y² = x³ + ax + b, together with a point at infinity. The points form an abelian group under a chord-and-tangent addition rule. Scalar multiplication — adding a point to itself many times — is easy to compute but practically impossible to reverse; this is the elliptic curve discrete logarithm problem (ECDLP), on which the security of ECC rests.

Applications

ECC underlies the ECDSA and EdDSA signature algorithms, the ECDH key agreement protocol, and implementations in TLS, SSH, messaging applications and cryptocurrencies such as Bitcoin and Ethereum. Standardised curves include the NIST P-curves, Curve25519 and secp256k1.

Implementation and security

Implementations must guard against side-channel attacks, invalid-curve attacks and faults such as the use of weak random numbers when generating keys or ECDSA nonces. A large-scale quantum computer running Shor's algorithm would break ECC, motivating the transition to post-quantum cryptography; in 2024 NIST standardised lattice-based replacements for key establishment and signatures.
