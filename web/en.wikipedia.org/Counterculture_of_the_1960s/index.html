Counterculture of the 1960s

The counterculture of the 1960s was an anti-establishment cultural phenomenon and political movement that developed in the Western world during the mid-1960s as a specific reaction to both the United States' involvement in the Vietnam War and the perceived conformity of postwar society. The movement encompassed the hippie subculture, the New Left, anti-war protests, the sexual revolution, experimentation with psychedelic drugs, and new forms of popular music and art.

Origins

The counterculture drew on the Beat Generation of the 1950s, the civil rights movement, free speech activism on campuses such as Berkeley in 1964, and mounting opposition to the draft and the Vietnam War. The generation born after the Second World War reached adulthood questioning the materialism, racial segregation, and Cold War politics of their parents' era.

Culture and lifestyle

San Francisco's Haight-Ashbury district became a symbolic centre during the "Summer of Love" in 1967, when tens of thousands of young people converged on the city. Psychedelic rock — by the Grateful Dead, Jefferson Airplane, Janis Joplin, and others — provided a soundtrack, while figures such as Timothy Leary popularized LSD. Communes, alternative newspapers, head shops, and festivals such as Woodstock (1969) expressed a desire for communal living, personal liberation, and peace. The movement spread internationally, intersecting with the May 1968 protests in France and youth movements in Britain, Germany, and beyond.

Decline and legacy

By the early 1970s the counterculture fragmented under the pressures of hard drugs, commercialization, the end of the draft, and disillusionment after events such as the Altamont festival. Its legacy endures in environmentalism, feminism, gay liberation, attitudes toward sexuality and dress, and the mainstreaming of rock music and Eastern spirituality in the West.
