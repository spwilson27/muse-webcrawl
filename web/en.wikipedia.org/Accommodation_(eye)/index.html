Accommodation (eye)

Accommodation
Organ systemVisual system
StructuresCrystalline lens, ciliary muscle, zonules of Zinn
EffectChange in optical power of the eye to focus at different distances
Typical amplitudeAbout 15 dioptres in a young adult; declines with age

Accommodation is the process by which the vertebrate eye changes optical power to maintain a clear image, or focus, on an object as its distance varies. In this process the ciliary muscle contracts or relaxes, altering the tension on the zonular fibres (zonules of Zinn), which in turn changes the shape of the crystalline lens and thus its refractive power.

Mechanism

According to the Helmholtz theory, when the eye focuses on a near object, the ciliary muscle contracts, reducing the diameter of the ciliary ring. This slackens the zonules, allowing the elastic lens capsule to round the lens into a more convex shape, increasing its power. For distant vision the muscle relaxes, zonular tension increases, and the lens flattens. In humans the response is a reflex (the accommodation reflex) coupled with convergence of the eyes and constriction of the pupil — the near triad.

Amplitude and age

The amplitude of accommodation is the difference in dioptric power between the far point and near point. It is greatest in childhood (around 14–16 dioptres at age 10) and declines steadily with age, a loss known as presbyopia, which typically becomes noticeable in the mid-40s when the amplitude falls below about 4 dioptres. The decline results mainly from stiffening of the lens substance and capsule.

Disorders

Disorders of accommodation include accommodative insufficiency, accommodative spasm (pseudomyopia), accommodative lag, and paralysis of accommodation following pharmacological cycloplegia or neurological disease. Excessive near work is associated by some researchers with accommodative fatigue, though its role in myopia progression remains debated.
