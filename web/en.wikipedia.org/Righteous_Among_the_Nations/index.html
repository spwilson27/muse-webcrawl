Righteous Among the Nations

Righteous Among the Nations (Hebrew: חֲסִידֵי אֻמּוֹת הָעוֹלָם) is an honorific used by the State of Israel to describe non-Jews who risked their lives during the Holocaust to save Jews from extermination by the Nazis. The term originates with the concept of "righteous gentiles", a term used in rabbinic Judaism to refer to non-Jews who abide by the Seven Laws of Noah and thus are assured a place in the World to Come.

When Yad Vashem, the Shoah Martyrs' and Heroes' Remembrance Authority, was established in 1953 by the Knesset, one of its tasks was to commemorate the "Righteous Among the Nations". The Righteous were defined as non-Jews who risked their lives to save Jews during the Holocaust.

Recognition

Recipients are awarded a medal and a certificate of honour, and their names are inscribed on the Wall of Honour in the Garden of the Righteous at Yad Vashem in Jerusalem. A commission headed by a retired Supreme Court justice examines evidence and testimony before awarding the title. Criteria include that the rescue involved risk to the rescuer's life, liberty or position; that the motivation was humanitarian rather than for payment or other gain; and that there is testimony from those who were helped or reliable documentary evidence.

Well-known recipients include Oskar Schindler, Raoul Wallenberg, Irena Sendler, Chiune Sugihara and Corrie ten Boom. As of recent decades, more than 28,000 individuals from over 50 countries have been recognised.
