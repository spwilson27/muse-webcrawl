Light rail

Light rail is a form of passenger urban rail transit that uses rolling stock similar to a tram but operates mainly on reserved rights-of-way with higher capacity and speed than street-running trams, while being lighter and cheaper to build than a full heavy-rail metro. Vehicles are typically electrically powered from overhead lines, run singly or in short trains, and board passengers at low platforms.

Development

The term was coined in North America in the 1970s (from the German Stadtbahn concept) as cities such as Edmonton (1978) and San Diego (1981) built modern systems reviving the electric railway. Germany's Stadtbahn networks, blending tramways with tunnel sections, were a major model. Light rail spread widely from the 1980s onward, in cities including Manchester, Los Angeles, Portland, and Sydney.

Characteristics

Systems vary from near-tramways with some street running to fully segregated lines approaching metro standards (sometimes called "light metro"). Typical features include articulated low-floor vehicles, headways of 3–15 minutes, and capacities of roughly 2,000–20,000 passengers per hour per direction. Compared with buses, light rail offers higher capacity and permanence that can anchor transit-oriented development; compared with metros, it offers lower construction cost at the price of speed and capacity.
