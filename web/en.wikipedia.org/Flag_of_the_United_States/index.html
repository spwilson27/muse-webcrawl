Flag of the United States

The flag of the United States of America, often called the American flag or the Stars and Stripes, consists of thirteen horizontal stripes — seven red alternating with six white — and a blue canton ("union") bearing fifty white, five-pointed stars arranged in nine offset horizontal rows.

Symbolism

The thirteen stripes represent the original thirteen colonies that declared independence from Great Britain; the fifty stars represent the fifty states of the Union. The Flag Act of 1818 fixed the stripe count at thirteen and provided that a star be added for each new state on the July 4 following its admission. The current fifty-star design, proposed by high-school student Robert G. Heft, was adopted by President Dwight D. Eisenhower and first flown on 4 July 1960, following Hawaii's admission — making it the longest-serving design.

History

The Continental Congress adopted the first flag on 14 June 1777 — a date now celebrated as Flag Day — resolving that it have thirteen stripes and thirteen stars "representing a new constellation." Tradition credits Betsy Ross with sewing an early version, though historians regard the story as legend. The 15-star, 15-stripe "Star-Spangled Banner" that flew over Fort McHenry in 1814 inspired the national anthem.

Etiquette

The United States Flag Code prescribes respectful treatment: the flag should not touch the ground, should be illuminated if flown at night, and worn-out flags should be destroyed in a dignified manner, preferably by burning. In 1989 the Supreme Court ruled in Texas v. Johnson that desecration of the flag as political protest is protected speech under the First Amendment.
