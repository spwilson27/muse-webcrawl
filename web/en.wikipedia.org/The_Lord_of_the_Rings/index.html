The Lord of the Rings

The Lord of the Rings
Author: J. R. R. Tolkien
Language: English
Genre: High fantasy
Publisher: Allen & Unwin
Publication date: 1954–1955 (three volumes)

The Lord of the Rings is an epic high-fantasy novel by the English author and scholar J. R. R. Tolkien. Set in Middle-earth, the story began as a sequel to Tolkien's 1937 children's book The Hobbit but eventually developed into a much larger work. Written in stages between 1937 and 1949, The Lord of the Rings is one of the best-selling books ever written, with over 150 million copies sold.

Plot summary

The Dark Lord Sauron seeks the One Ring, forged to dominate the other Rings of Power, which has come into the possession of the hobbit Frodo Baggins. Guided by the wizard Gandalf, Frodo and eight companions — the Fellowship of the Ring — set out to destroy the Ring in the fires of Mount Doom in Mordor, the only place it can be unmade. The Fellowship breaks apart; Frodo and his servant Sam Gamgee continue toward Mordor guided by the treacherous Gollum, while the others join the wars against Sauron and the traitorous wizard Saruman. After immense sacrifice, the Ring is destroyed, Sauron falls, and Aragorn is crowned king, but Frodo, wounded in body and spirit, sails from Middle-earth with the Elves.

Publication

For economic reasons the work was published in three volumes over the course of a year, from 29 July 1954 to 20 October 1955: The Fellowship of the Ring, The Two Towers, and The Return of the King. Tolkien intended it as a single volume with The Silmarillion. The book includes extensive appendices on the history, languages, and peoples of Middle-earth.

Themes and influences

The work draws on Tolkien's interests in Germanic and Norse mythology, Anglo-Saxon literature such as Beowulf, Catholic theology, and his experiences in the First World War. Major themes include the corrupting nature of power, providence, pity, death and immortality, and the defence of a rural, pre-industrial way of life against industrialization.

Legacy

The novel reshaped modern fantasy literature and popular culture. It has been adapted into radio, theatre, and film, most famously Peter Jackson's film trilogy (2001–2003). It was named Britain's best-loved novel in the BBC's 2003 Big Read poll.
