Public good (economics)

Public good

Defining propertiesNon-excludable and non-rivalrous
ExamplesNational defense, lighthouses, clean air, street lighting
Key problemFree-rider problem

In economics, a public good (also referred to as a social good or collective good) is a good that is both non-excludable and non-rivalrous. For a good to be non-excludable means that it is costly or impossible for one user to exclude others from using it. For a good to be non-rivalrous means that when one person uses the good, it does not prevent others from using it.

Classic examples include national defense, public fireworks displays, clean air, and lighthouses. Public goods contrast with private goods, which are both excludable and rivalrous; with club goods, which are excludable but non-rivalrous; and with common-pool resources, which are rivalrous but non-excludable.

The free-rider problem

Because people cannot be excluded from enjoying a public good, each individual has an incentive to let others pay for it while enjoying it free of charge — the free-rider problem. As a result, markets acting alone tend to undersupply public goods, which is one standard economic justification for government provision funded by taxation.

History of the concept

Paul Samuelson formalized the theory of public goods in his 1954 paper "The Pure Theory of Public Expenditure." Richard Musgrave further developed the classification of goods. Garrett Hardin's "tragedy of the commons" (1968) highlighted the related problem of overuse of rivalrous common resources, while Elinor Ostrom's work showed how communities can sometimes govern shared resources without either privatization or central control.

Provision

Public goods may be provided by governments, by private firms under contract, by charitable organizations, or through assurance contracts and other mechanisms designed to overcome free riding. Global public goods — such as disease eradication and climate stability — raise additional challenges of international coordination.
