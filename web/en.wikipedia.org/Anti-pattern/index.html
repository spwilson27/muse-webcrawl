Anti-pattern

An anti-pattern in software engineering is a commonly used but ineffective or counterproductive solution to a recurring problem. The term, popularised by the 1998 book AntiPatterns by William Brown and colleagues, plays on the idea of a design pattern: where a pattern is a reusable good solution, an anti-pattern is a reusable bad one, entered into for understandable reasons but producing more problems than it solves.

Characteristics

Anti-patterns are documented with the forces that lead to them, their symptoms and consequences, and a "refactored solution". Classic examples include the god object (one class that does everything), spaghetti code, golden hammer (using one familiar tool for every problem), cargo cult programming, analysis paralysis and reinventing the wheel. Organisational anti-patterns, such as micromanagement or design by committee, are also catalogued.

Purpose

Naming anti-patterns gives teams a shared vocabulary for recognising dysfunctional practices early, understanding why they arise — schedule pressure, inexperience, or misapplied patterns — and replacing them with proven alternatives. The concept has spread beyond software to architecture, project management and social processes generally.
