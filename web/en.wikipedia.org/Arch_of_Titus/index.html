The Arch of Titus (Italian: Arco di Tito) is a first-century AD honorary triumphal arch on the Via Sacra in Rome, at the foot of the Palatine Hill just south-east of the Roman Forum. It was constructed in about 81 AD by the Emperor Domitian shortly after the death of his elder brother Titus, to commemorate Titus's official deification and his victory with their father Vespasian in the First Jewish–Roman War, including the sack of Jerusalem in 70 AD.

Sculptural programme

The arch's interior panels are among the most famous survivals of Roman relief sculpture. The south panel shows the triumphal procession carrying spoils from the Temple in Jerusalem: the great seven-branched menorah, silver trumpets, and the table of showbread. The north panel depicts Titus riding in a four-horse chariot, crowned by Victory. A frieze around the architrave shows the wider procession. In 2012, a digital restoration project detected traces suggesting the menorah had originally been painted in yellow ochre.

Later history

The arch served as a model for many later triumphal arches, including the Arc de Triomphe in Paris. In the Middle Ages it was incorporated into the fortifications of the Frangipane family; Pope Paul IV made it the site of an annual oath. For centuries Rome's Jewish community traditionally avoided walking beneath the arch; in 1948, when the State of Israel was founded, members of the community processed through it in the opposite direction of the ancient triumph, a gesture repeated in later Hanukkah celebrations.
