Vena cava

SystemCirculatory system
Drains toRight atrium of the heart
PartsSuperior vena cava; Inferior vena cava

The venae cavae are the two large veins that return deoxygenated blood from the body into the right atrium of the heart. The superior vena cava drains the head, neck, upper limbs, and thorax, while the inferior vena cava, the largest vein in the human body, drains the abdomen, pelvis, and lower limbs. The name is Latin for "hollow vein".

Superior vena cava

The superior vena cava is formed by the union of the left and right brachiocephalic veins behind the first costal cartilage. It descends about 7 cm before entering the right atrium, receiving the azygos vein along the way. Obstruction of the vessel produces superior vena cava syndrome, a medical emergency often caused by malignancy.

Inferior vena cava

The inferior vena cava begins where the two common iliac veins join, at about the level of the fifth lumbar vertebra, and ascends to the right of the aorta. It receives the hepatic, renal, and lumbar veins, passes through the diaphragm at the eighth thoracic vertebra, and empties into the right atrium. Filters may be placed in the inferior vena cava to trap blood clots and prevent pulmonary embolism.

Clinical significance

Because the venae cavae are thin-walled and low-pressure, they can be compressed by tumours or an enlarged uterus in pregnancy. Cannulation of the superior vena cava via central venous catheters is a routine hospital procedure for delivering fluids, drugs, and parenteral nutrition.
