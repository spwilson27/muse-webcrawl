Achilles tendinitis

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Achilles tendinitis, also known as Achilles tendinopathy, is a condition in which the Achilles tendon—the large tendon connecting the calf muscles to the heel bone—becomes painful and stiff, typically from overuse. It is especially common in runners and middle-aged recreational athletes. Although the name implies inflammation, chronic cases often involve degeneration of the tendon fibres (tendinosis) rather than active inflammation, and the broader term tendinopathy is increasingly preferred.

Signs and symptoms

The main symptom is pain along the back of the lower leg just above the heel, often worst with the first steps in the morning or at the start of exercise, easing as the tendon warms up and returning afterwards. The tendon may be thickened and tender to the touch. Two forms are distinguished: insertional tendinopathy, at the point where the tendon attaches to the heel bone, and non-insertional (mid-portion) tendinopathy, typically 2–6 cm above the insertion.

Causes and risk factors

Sudden increases in training load, tight calf muscles, poor footwear, and running on hills or hard surfaces are common contributors. Certain antibiotics—particularly fluoroquinolones—are associated with tendinopathy and tendon rupture. A severe complication is Achilles tendon rupture, a complete tear that usually requires surgical or intensive conservative management.

Treatment

Management is usually non-surgical: relative rest or modification of activity, ice, analgesia, heel lifts, and structured rehabilitation. Eccentric strengthening exercises (the Alfredson protocol of heavy-load heel drops) have the strongest evidence for mid-portion tendinopathy; heavy slow resistance training is an alternative. Most cases improve over several months. Surgery is reserved for cases that fail prolonged conservative care.
