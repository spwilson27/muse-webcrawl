United States free speech exceptions

The First Amendment to the United States Constitution provides that Congress shall make no law abridging the freedom of speech, but the Supreme Court has long recognized that the guarantee is not absolute. United States free speech exceptions are categories of expression that receive lesser or no protection, together with doctrines — such as the prohibition on most prior restraints and content-based restrictions — that structure how the Court analyzes speech regulation. Justice Hugo Black's "no law means no law" absolutism never commanded a majority.

Unprotected categories

  • Incitement — speech directed to inciting imminent lawless action and likely to produce it (Brandenburg v. Ohio, 1969).
  • Obscenity — material satisfying the three-part Miller test: prurient appeal, patently offensive sexual content, and lack of serious literary, artistic, political, or scientific value.
  • Defamation — false statements of fact damaging a reputation, with "actual malice" required for public officials and figures (New York Times v. Sullivan, 1964).
  • True threats — serious expressions of intent to commit unlawful violence; the Court in Counterman v. Colorado (2023) required at least a reckless mental state.
  • Fighting words — face-to-face insults likely to provoke immediate violence (Chaplinsky v. New Hampshire, 1942), narrowly applied since.
  • Fraud, perjury, and speech integral to criminal conduct, including solicitation and conspiracy.
  • Child sexual abuse material (New York v. Ferber, 1982).

Lesser protection and structural doctrines

Commercial speech receives intermediate protection under the Central Hudson test. Content-neutral time, place, and manner restrictions must be narrowly tailored to a significant government interest. Content-based restrictions outside the unprotected categories are presumptively unconstitutional and reviewed under strict scrutiny (Reed v. Town of Gilbert, 2015). In United States v. Alvarez (2012) and United States v. Stevens (2010), the Court refused to create new unprotected categories by balancing alone, tying the existing exceptions to long historical traditions.
