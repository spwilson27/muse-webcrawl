Precision and recall

In pattern recognition, information retrieval, and classification (machine learning), precision and recall are performance metrics that apply to data retrieved from a collection, corpus, or sample space.

Precision (also called positive predictive value) is the fraction of relevant instances among the retrieved instances, while recall (also known as sensitivity) is the fraction of relevant instances that were retrieved. Both precision and recall are therefore based on relevance.

Definitions

In terms of true positives (TP), false positives (FP), and false negatives (FN): precision = TP / (TP + FP), and recall = TP / (TP + FN). A system that retrieves everything achieves perfect recall but low precision; a conservative system that retrieves only items it is sure about achieves high precision but may have low recall.

F-measure

The two quantities are often combined into the F1 score, the harmonic mean of precision and recall, and plotted against each other in precision–recall curves when a classifier's decision threshold varies. In information retrieval, precision and recall originated with Cyril Cleverdon's Cranfield experiments in the 1950s and 1960s.
