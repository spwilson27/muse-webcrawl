Round (music)

Notation of a simple vocal round

A round is a musical composition in which two or more voices sing the same melody, each beginning at a different time, so that the parts harmonise with one another. It is the simplest type of canon and is sometimes called a perpetual canon because, once all voices have entered, the piece can in principle repeat indefinitely: when one singer reaches the end of the melody, they start again at the beginning.

Characteristics

The melody of a round must be composed so that each of its segments sounds consonant against the segments sung simultaneously by the other voices. Entries are usually spaced one or two bars apart and at the unison or octave. Familiar examples in English include "Row, Row, Row Your Boat," "Three Blind Mice," "Frère Jacques" (a French round), "London's Burning," and "Kookaburra" in Australia. Because they require no accompaniment or notation, rounds are staples of music education, campfire singing and communal music-making.

History

The oldest known secular song in English, "Sumer is icumen in" (c. 1250), is a rota—a medieval perpetual canon for up to six voices. Rounds flourished in 16th- and 17th-century England, where collections of rounds, catches and canons by composers such as Thomas Ravenscroft were published for convivial singing; the "catch" was a humorous, often bawdy variant in which the staggered entries create comic juxtapositions of words. Many drinking songs and glee-club pieces from the period are rounds.

Although usually simple, the round principle underlies elaborate learned canons in art music, and modern works such as the "perpetual canons" of the minimalist era continue to exploit it. Folk traditions worldwide contain analogous staggered-entry songs.
