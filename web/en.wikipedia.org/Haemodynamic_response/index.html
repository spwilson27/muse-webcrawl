Haemodynamic response

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

In haemodynamics, the haemodynamic response (also spelled hemodynamic response or HR) describes the rapid delivery of blood to active neuronal tissue in the brain. Because neurons lack internal reserves of glucose and oxygen, increased neural activity triggers a locally increased cerebral blood flow that supplies more oxygen and glucose than the tissue consumes, producing a characteristic change in the ratio of oxygenated to deoxygenated haemoglobin.

Mechanism

Synaptic activity, particularly glutamate release, activates neurons and astrocytes, which release vasodilatory signals such as nitric oxide, prostaglandins and adenosine. Local arterioles dilate, and blood flow to the active region rises within about one to two seconds, peaks roughly five to six seconds after stimulus onset, and returns to baseline over the following seconds, often with a small post-stimulus undershoot. This vascular coupling is termed neurovascular coupling.

Use in neuroimaging

The haemodynamic response underlies blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) functional magnetic resonance imaging: because active regions receive an oversupply of oxygenated blood, the local concentration of paramagnetic deoxyhaemoglobin falls, changing the MR signal. The sluggish, delayed shape of the response — modelled as the haemodynamic response function (HRF) — limits the temporal resolution of fMRI and must be deconvolved when analysing neural timing. The response can be blunted or altered by vascular disease, ageing, caffeine and many drugs, complicating interpretation.
