Military slang
Nature: Informal vocabulary used by service personnel
Features: Acronyms, abbreviations, borrowings, gallows humour

Military slang is the informal language used by members of armed forces. It blends official jargon and acronyms with borrowed foreign words, abbreviations and darkly comic coinages, and it varies by country, service and era. Much of it passes into everyday civilian speech.

Examples and origins

British troops of the First World War borrowed from Hindi and French, producing words like "Blighty" (home), "cushy" and "shell shock." The Second World War gave English snafu ("situation normal: all fouled up"), fubar and jeep. American forces in Korea and Vietnam popularised "boonies," "grunt" and "short-timer," while more recent conflicts added "FOB," "sandbox," and the phonetic-alphabet brevity of radio procedure. Official acronyms — AWOL, ASAP, MRE ("meals, ready-to-eat") — form a parallel layer of slang with formal status.

Functions

Slang reinforces group identity, speeds communication under stress, and lets personnel joke about hardship and danger. Dictionaries of military slang have been compiled since the nineteenth century, reflecting its constant reinvention by each generation of recruits.
