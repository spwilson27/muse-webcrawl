Armenian Highlands

The Armenian Highlands (Armenian: Հայկական լեռնաշխարհ, Haykakan leṙnašxarh) is a mountainous plateau region in eastern Anatolia and the South Caucasus, centred on present-day eastern Turkey and Armenia, with parts in northwestern Iran, Georgia, and Azerbaijan. It is one of the most elevated and rugged regions of Western Asia, with average elevations around 1,500–2,000 metres, and is sometimes described as the "roof" of the Near East.

Geography

The highlands are dominated by volcanic mountains and lava plateaus, including Mount Ararat (5,137 m), the highest peak of the region and the traditional resting place of Noah's Ark; Mount Aragats in Armenia; and Süphan Dağı above Lake Van, the largest lake in Turkey. The region is the source of great rivers of the Near East, including the Euphrates, the Tigris, the Aras, and the Kura. Lake Sevan in Armenia is one of the largest high-altitude lakes in Eurasia. The climate is continental, with cold winters and hot, dry summers.

History

The highlands were home to the Hurrians and the kingdom of Urartu in the Iron Age, and formed the heartland of ancient, medieval, and early modern Armenian kingdoms, giving the region its name. After centuries divided among Roman, Persian, Arab, Byzantine, Seljuk, and Ottoman powers, the plateau was split between the Ottoman and Russian empires and, following World War I and the Armenian genocide, between Turkey and the Soviet Union; today the Republic of Armenia occupies its eastern portion.
