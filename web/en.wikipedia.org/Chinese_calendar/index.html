The Chinese calendar is a lunisolar calendar that organises years, months, and days according to astronomical phenomena: months begin with the new moon and last 29 or 30 days, while the year is periodically lengthened with an intercalary (leap) month so that it stays aligned with the solar year and the seasons. It determines the dates of traditional festivals such as Chinese New Year (Spring Festival) and the Mid-Autumn Festival, and it remains in everyday use in China alongside the Gregorian calendar for cultural, agricultural, and astrological purposes.

Traditionally attributed in legend to the Yellow Emperor (Huangdi), the calendar is often counted in a continuous cycle of years; by the commonly used reckoning, the mid-2020s fall in the 41st year of the current 60-year cycle, whose years are named by combining one of ten Heavenly Stems with one of twelve Earthly Branches, the latter also giving the twelve-animal zodiac (shengxiao).

Structure

An ordinary year has 12 months (353–355 days); a leap year has 13 months (383–385 days), with the leap month taking the number of the preceding month. Seven leap years occur in each 19-year period, approximately following the Metonic cycle. The placement of leap months is governed by the 24 solar terms (jieqi), which divide the ecliptic into 24 segments of 15 degrees and track the agricultural seasons; a month lacking a major solar term (zhongqi) is designated the leap month. Chinese New Year falls on the second new moon after the winter solstice in the most common formulation and can occur between 21 January and 20 February.

History

Evidence of lunisolar reckoning appears on oracle bones of the Shang dynasty (c. 14th century BCE). Over the centuries numerous official calendars were promulgated, including the Taichu calendar of 104 BCE, which fixed the 24 solar terms and the 19-year leap cycle, and the Shoushi calendar of Guo Shoujing (1281), used into the Ming dynasty. Jesuit astronomers such as Adam Schall von Bell assisted the late Ming and early Qing courts, and the last imperial calendar, the Shixian calendar (1645), incorporated European astronomical methods. The Republic of China adopted the Gregorian calendar for official use in 1912, but the traditional calendar continues to govern festivals and almanac (huangli) day-selection practices.

Days and hours

Days begin at midnight in the modern calendar, but traditional timekeeping divided the day into twelve double-hours (shichen), each named by an Earthly Branch. A continuous day-count using stem-branch pairs (the sexagenary day cycle) has been maintained for well over two thousand years and is still printed in almanacs.

Festivals

Major festivals fixed by the calendar include Spring Festival (1st day of the 1st month), Lantern Festival (15th of the 1st month), Dragon Boat Festival (5th of the 5th month), Qixi (7th of the 7th month), Ghost Festival (15th of the 7th month), Mid-Autumn Festival (15th of the 8th month), and Double Ninth Festival (9th of the 9th month).
