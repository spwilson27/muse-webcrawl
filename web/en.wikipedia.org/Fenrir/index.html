Fenrir (Old Norse: "fen-dweller"; also called Fenrisúlfr, "Fenrir's wolf") is a monstrous wolf in Norse mythology, the son of the god Loki and the jötunn Angrboða, and brother of the world-serpent Jörmungandr and the death-goddess Hel. Forewarned by prophecy that Fenrir would cause them great harm, the gods raised the wolf among them until he grew too large and dangerous, then bound him with the magical fetter Gleipnir — at the cost of the god Týr's hand. At Ragnarök, Fenrir will break free and swallow Odin, before being killed by Odin's son Víðarr.

The binding of Fenrir

As told in the Prose Edda book Gylfaginning, the gods first tried to bind Fenrir with great iron chains, which he broke easily under the guise of a test of strength. They then commissioned the dwarves to make Gleipnir, a silken ribbon woven from six impossible things: the sound of a cat's footfall, the beard of a woman, the roots of a mountain, the sinews of a bear, the breath of a fish, and the spittle of a bird. Suspicious, Fenrir agreed to be bound only if a god placed a hand in his mouth as a pledge; Týr volunteered. When Fenrir could not break Gleipnir and the gods refused to release him, he bit off Týr's hand at the wrist.

Ragnarök

Fenrir's sons, the wolves Sköll and Hati, chase the sun and moon and will catch them at Ragnarök. Fenrir will then run with his jaws agape, the upper jaw reaching the heavens and the lower the earth, and devour Odin. Víðarr, wearing a special shoe, will step on Fenrir's lower jaw and tear his mouth apart, avenging his father.

Legacy

Fenrir appears on Scandinavian picture stones and in modern literature, games and film, and is among the most recognisable figures of Norse myth. Scholars have compared his binding and escape with Indo-European myths of a bound monster released at the end of the world.
