Phidias or Pheidias (Ancient Greek: Φειδίας; c. 480 – 430 BC) was an Athenian sculptor, painter and architect, generally considered the greatest of all classical sculptors. His Statue of Zeus at Olympia was one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World. Phidias also designed the statues of the goddess Athena on the Athenian Acropolis, namely the Athena Parthenos inside the Parthenon and the Athena Promachos, a colossal bronze statue that stood between the Parthenon and the Propylaea.

Life

Phidias was the son of Charmides and a close associate of the statesman Pericles, who entrusted him with artistic supervision of the great rebuilding of the Acropolis after the Persian Wars. Ancient sources report that he was prosecuted for embezzling gold intended for the Athena Parthenos — whose gold plates were made removable so they could be weighed to prove his innocence — and later accused of impiety for including portraits of himself and Pericles on Athena's shield. The accounts of his death differ: some say he died in prison in Athens, others that he withdrew to Olympia, where he made the Statue of Zeus.

Works

None of Phidias's original major works survive; they are known from ancient descriptions, Roman copies, and coins. The Athena Parthenos (438 BC) was a chryselephantine (gold and ivory) statue about 11.5 metres (38 ft) tall. The Statue of Zeus at Olympia (c. 435 BC), also chryselephantine and about 12.4 metres (41 ft) tall, stood in the Temple of Zeus for some eight centuries. Excavation of Phidias's workshop at Olympia uncovered tools, moulds and a cup inscribed "I belong to Phidias." Phidias is also credited with overall artistic direction of the Parthenon's sculptural program, including the frieze, metopes and pediments.

Legacy

Phidias's name became synonymous with classical Greek art. The golden ratio is sometimes denoted by the Greek letter phi (φ) in an association (popularized in the 20th century) with his name, though the historical connection is doubtful.
