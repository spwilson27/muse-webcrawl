Economy of Puerto Rico

The economy of Puerto Rico is classified as a high income economy by the World Bank and as the most competitive economy in Latin America by the World Economic Forum. The main drivers of the economy are manufacturing, primarily pharmaceuticals, textiles, petrochemicals, and electronics; followed by the service industry, notably finance, insurance, real estate, and tourism. Puerto Rico is a territory of the United States, uses the U.S. dollar, and its residents are U.S. citizens, though the island is not incorporated into the U.S. customs territory in the same way as the states and its tax system operates partly independently.

History

Under Spanish rule the economy rested on agriculture, especially sugarcane, coffee, and tobacco. After 1898, U.S. investment expanded sugar production. In the mid-twentieth century, Operation Bootstrap industrialized the island, shifting employment from agriculture to manufacturing through tax incentives. Section 936 of the U.S. Internal Revenue Code, which allowed U.S. corporations to repatriate profits from Puerto Rican subsidiaries tax-free, attracted pharmaceutical and electronics plants from 1976 until Congress phased it out between 1996 and 2006, a change many economists link to the long recession that followed.

Sectors

Manufacturing accounts for roughly half of gross domestic product, with pharmaceuticals the largest component; Puerto Rico is one of the world's largest pharmaceutical production centers, and a large share of the top-selling drugs in the United States are made on the island. Agriculture now contributes a small share of GDP, with coffee, plantains, and dairy among the main products. Tourism centered on San Juan, and rum production (the island supplies most of the rum consumed in the United States), are significant earners.

Debt crisis

After a decade of economic contraction beginning in 2006, Puerto Rico accumulated roughly $70 billion in public debt plus large unfunded pension liabilities. In 2015 the governor declared the debt unpayable. The U.S. Congress responded with the PROMESA Act in 2016, creating a federal financial oversight board and a bankruptcy-like process under which much of the debt was restructured, with the central government's plan of adjustment confirmed in 2022. Hurricanes Irma and Maria in 2017 devastated infrastructure and accelerated outmigration to the mainland United States, deepening the island's long demographic decline.

Labor and demographics

Labor-force participation is lower than in the U.S. mainland, and poverty rates are markedly higher, with a large share of the population receiving federal assistance. Sustained emigration has reduced the population from a peak of about 3.8 million in 2004 to roughly 3.2 million.
