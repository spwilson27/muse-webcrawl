Generative grammar

Generative grammar is a linguistic theory that regards linguistics as the study of a hypothesised innate grammatical structure. It is a biological or biologistic modification of earlier structuralist theories of linguistics, deriving ultimately from glossematics. Generative grammar considers grammar as a system of rules that generates exactly those combinations of words that form grammatical sentences in a given language, and it defines the general theory partly through the notion that expressions are formed by the repeated application of a limited set of rules.

Origins

The framework was introduced by Noam Chomsky, beginning with his 1955 work The Logical Structure of Linguistic Theory and his widely read 1957 monograph Syntactic Structures. Chomsky argued that human beings possess an innate faculty for language, often called universal grammar, which constrains the form that grammars of particular languages can take. His famous example sentence "Colorless green ideas sleep furiously" illustrated that grammatical well-formedness is independent of meaning.

Development

Successive versions of the theory include transformational grammar, the standard theory, the extended standard theory, government and binding theory, and the minimalist program, introduced in the 1990s. Central concepts include phrase structure rules, transformations, deep structure and surface structure (in earlier versions), and, in minimalism, the operation Merge, which combines syntactic objects into larger structures.

Criticism and alternatives

Generative grammar has been highly influential in linguistics, psychology, and cognitive science, but it has also attracted criticism from proponents of functionalist, usage-based, and construction grammar approaches, who question the innateness hypothesis and the autonomy of syntax. The "linguistics wars" of the 1960s and 1970s saw rival generative semanticists break away from Chomsky's interpretive semantics.
