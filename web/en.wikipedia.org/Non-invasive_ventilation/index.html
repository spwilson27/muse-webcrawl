Non-invasive ventilation

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Non-invasive ventilation (NIV) is the delivery of mechanical ventilation to the lungs without an endotracheal or tracheostomy tube, usually through a tightly fitting mask covering the nose, the nose and mouth, or the whole face. By supporting breathing while avoiding intubation and its complications — sedation, ventilator-associated pneumonia, and airway injury — NIV has become a standard treatment for acute respiratory failure due to exacerbations of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema, and for chronic hypoventilation in neuromuscular and chest-wall disease.

Modes

  • Continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP): a constant pressure throughout the breathing cycle; splints open the upper airway and recruits alveoli. CPAP is also the principal treatment for obstructive sleep apnoea.
  • Bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP): different pressures for inspiration (IPAP) and expiration (EPAP), actively assisting each breath and increasing tidal volume; the usual mode for hypercapnic (type 2) respiratory failure.

Uses

Randomised trials show that NIV started early in acidotic COPD exacerbations reduces mortality and the need for intubation, and it is recommended as first-line therapy in international guidelines. In acute cardiogenic pulmonary edema, CPAP and BiPAP rapidly improve oxygenation and dyspnoea. Long-term home NIV improves survival and quality of life in conditions such as motor neurone disease, Duchenne muscular dystrophy, kyphoscoliosis, and obesity hypoventilation syndrome. NIV is also used to support patients for whom intubation is not appropriate, and in weaning from invasive ventilation.

History

Negative-pressure ventilators — the "iron lung" and cuirass ventilators used during the polio epidemics — were the first non-invasive ventilators, acting by expanding the chest from outside. Positive-pressure mask ventilation developed from anaesthesia and intensive-care practice in the 1980s, when it was shown that patients with acute exacerbations of COPD could be supported without intubation; home bilevel devices for sleep apnoea and neuromuscular disease spread widely in the 1990s.

Limitations

NIV requires a conscious, cooperative patient who can protect the airway; it is unsuitable in vomiting, severe agitation, facial trauma, or copious secretions, and failure to improve within one to two hours should prompt escalation to intubation where appropriate.
