Parabolic reflector

A parabolic reflector (or paraboloid reflector) is a reflective surface used to collect or distribute energy such as light, sound, or radio waves. Its shape is part of a circular paraboloid — the surface generated by a parabola rotated around its axis. The reflector has the property that energy entering parallel to its axis is reflected to a single point, the focus; conversely, energy emanating from the focus is reflected into a collimated beam parallel to the axis.

Principle

For a parabola, any ray parallel to the axis of symmetry reflects off the curve through the focal point, regardless of where it strikes. Rotating the parabola produces a paraboloid with the same property in three dimensions. Because the total path length from a planar wavefront to the focus is constant for all rays, the reflected waves arrive in phase, producing constructive interference and a strong signal at the focus.

History

The focusing property of parabolas was known in antiquity — legend attributes to Archimedes a burning mirror used against Roman ships at Syracuse, though this is probably apocryphal. Parabolic mirrors were used in lighthouses and searchlights from the 18th century, and in the 20th century became the standard shape for radio telescope dishes and satellite communications antennas following the pioneering work of Grote Reber and others.

Applications

  • Reflecting telescopes — paraboloidal primary mirrors gather starlight to a focus.
  • Radio telescopes and satellite dishes — a feed antenna at the focus receives or transmits signals.
  • Solar concentrators and solar cookers — sunlight is focused to heat a receiver.
  • Headlamps, flashlights and searchlights — a source at the focus produces a directed beam.
  • Parabolic microphones — sound is focused onto a microphone for long-distance recording.

Variations

Large telescope mirrors are often slightly aspheric or segmented. Off-axis parabolic reflectors use only a section of the paraboloid, avoiding the shadow of a centrally mounted feed. The Cassegrain design folds the optical path with a secondary mirror to shorten the instrument. At radio wavelengths, mesh or perforated surfaces can replace solid dishes.
