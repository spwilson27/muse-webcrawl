Mozarab

The Mozarabs (Spanish: mozárabes [moˈθaɾaβes]; Portuguese: moçárabes; Catalan: mossàrabs) were the Iberian Christians who lived under Muslim rule in Al-Andalus after the Ummayad conquest of Hispania in 711. Their descendants remained unconverted to Islam, but did however adopt elements of the Arabic language and culture. They were mostly Roman Catholic and initially followed the late Roman, Hispanic liturgical tradition, known as the Mozarabic rite.

Church of San Román in Toledo, built in the Mozarabic tradition

History

After the Muslim conquest of the Visigothic Kingdom in the early 8th century, many Christians continued to live under Muslim rule, paying the jizya tax as dhimmis. Over time, they became increasingly Arabized in language and culture while retaining their Christian faith and liturgy. Mozarabic communities flourished especially in Toledo, Córdoba, Mérida, Seville and Granada.

During periods of intolerance, notably under the Almoravids and Almohads in the 11th and 12th centuries, many Mozarabs emigrated north to the Christian kingdoms of León, Castile and Aragon, bringing with them their distinctive art, architecture and liturgical practice.

Language

The Mozarabs spoke a variety of Ibero-Romance heavily influenced by Arabic, often written in the Arabic script (aljamiado) or in Latin script. The language, referred to as Mozarabic, is preserved mainly in refrains (kharjas) closing Andalusi Arabic poems.

Mozarabic rite

The Mozarabic rite is the ancient liturgy of the Iberian Church, dating to the Visigothic period. It was suppressed in most of Spain in favor of the Roman rite during the 11th century, but survived in Toledo, where it is still celebrated in a chapel of Toledo Cathedral.
