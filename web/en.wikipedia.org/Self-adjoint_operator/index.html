Self-adjoint operator

In mathematics, a self-adjoint operator on an infinite-dimensional complex vector space V with inner product is a linear operator A (a map from V to itself) that is equal to its own adjoint: A = A*. In finite dimensions, these are simply the Hermitian matrices with respect to an orthonormal basis. Self-adjoint operators are fundamental in functional analysis and in the mathematical formulation of quantum mechanics.

Definition and domain subtleties

For bounded operators on a Hilbert space, self-adjointness is the simple equality of an operator with its adjoint. For unbounded operators — which include most differential operators of physics — the notion is more delicate: a symmetric operator satisfies ⟨Ax, y⟩ = ⟨x, Ay⟩ on its domain, but it is self-adjoint only if its domain equals the domain of its adjoint. This distinction matters: only self-adjoint operators have a full spectral theory and generate unitary time evolution.

Spectral theory

The spectrum of a self-adjoint operator is real. The spectral theorem states that every self-adjoint operator can be represented as multiplication by a real-valued function on some L² space, or equivalently diagonalized via a projection-valued measure. This generalizes the finite-dimensional fact that Hermitian matrices are diagonalizable by unitary transformations with real eigenvalues.

Role in quantum mechanics

In quantum mechanics, observable quantities such as position, momentum, and energy are represented by self-adjoint operators on a Hilbert space; their real spectra correspond to possible measurement outcomes, and the Hamiltonian, as a self-adjoint operator, generates the unitary dynamics of the Schrödinger equation by Stone's theorem.
