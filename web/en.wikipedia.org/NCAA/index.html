The National Collegiate Athletic Association (NCAA) is a nonprofit organisation that regulates athletics at about 1,100 colleges and universities in the United States. It organises national championships in dozens of sports, most famously the Division I men's and women's basketball tournaments — "March Madness" — and administers eligibility, recruiting and playing rules for roughly half a million student-athletes.

History

The NCAA was founded in 1906 as the Intercollegiate Athletic Association of the United States, largely in response to injuries and deaths in college football, taking its present name in 1910. It held its first championship, in track and field, in 1921 and its first basketball tournament in 1939. Under executive director Walter Byers (1951–1988) it became a powerful governing and commercial body, centralised in Indianapolis since 1999. Control of women's championships came in the early 1980s following the passage of Title IX.

Structure and recent change

Member schools compete in three divisions: Division I (the largest programmes, with athletic scholarships and major media revenues), Division II and Division III (which offers no athletic scholarships). The association has faced sustained legal and public pressure over athlete compensation: following court rulings including the Supreme Court's 2021 decision in NCAA v. Alston, athletes gained rights to profit from their name, image and likeness (NIL), and a major settlement in the mid-2020s opened the door to direct revenue sharing between schools and athletes.
