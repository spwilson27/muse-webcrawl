Cycloalkane

In organic chemistry, the cycloalkanes (also called naphthenes, but distinct from naphthalene) are monocyclic saturated hydrocarbons. A cycloalkane consists of only carbon and hydrogen atoms, arranged in a ring with all carbon-carbon bonds single. Unsubstituted cycloalkanes have the general formula CnH2n.

Nomenclature and examples

Cycloalkanes are named by adding the prefix "cyclo-" to the corresponding alkane: cyclopropane (3 carbons), cyclobutane (4), cyclopentane (5), and cyclohexane (6). Cyclohexane is the most important member commercially, used in making nylon precursors. Cycloalkanes occur naturally in petroleum, where naphthenes form a significant fraction of crude oil.

Ring strain and conformation

Small rings are strained because their bond angles deviate from the tetrahedral ideal of 109.5°. Cyclopropane, with 60° angles, is highly strained and correspondingly reactive. Cyclopentane puckers into an envelope shape to relieve strain, while cyclohexane adopts the nearly strain-free chair conformation, whose axial and equatorial positions are central to stereochemistry. Adolf von Baeyer first proposed ring strain theory in 1885.

Reactions

Cycloalkanes resemble alkanes in their reactions, undergoing combustion and free-radical substitution. The strained small rings additionally undergo ring-opening addition reactions: cyclopropane adds hydrogen and bromine across a carbon-carbon bond, behaving in this respect somewhat like an alkene.
