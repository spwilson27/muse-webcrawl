Rhea Silvia (also called Ilia) was, in Roman mythology, the mother of the twins Romulus and Remus, who founded the city of Rome. Her story is told most fully by Livy in the first book of Ab Urbe Condita, and also by Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Plutarch, and Ovid.

Mythology

Rhea Silvia was the daughter of Numitor, king of Alba Longa, who was deposed by his younger brother Amulius. To prevent her from producing heirs who might reclaim the throne, Amulius forced her to become a Vestal Virgin, sworn to thirty years of celibacy. Nevertheless, she bore twin sons. In the most common version, the father was Mars, the god of war, who came to her in the sacred grove (or, in some accounts, in a dream); other versions name Hercules or a mysterious phantom.

When Amulius learned of the twins, he imprisoned Rhea Silvia — some accounts say he had her buried alive, the standard punishment for an unchaste Vestal, or drowned in the Tiber — and ordered the infants exposed in the river. The Tiber's waters, in flood, left the twins safely ashore at the foot of the Palatine Hill, where a she-wolf suckled them and a woodpecker helped feed them until the shepherd Faustulus adopted them. In one strand of the tradition, Rhea Silvia was rescued by, or married to, the river god Tiberinus.

Grown to manhood, Romulus and Remus killed Amulius and restored their grandfather Numitor to the throne, then founded a new city — Rome. Rhea Silvia's violated vow and divine parentage gave Rome's founders both royal Alban descent and the blood of Mars, anchoring Roman identity in myth.

In art and literature

The meeting of Mars and Rhea Silvia and the discovery of the twins were popular subjects in Roman and later European art, appearing on coins, sarcophagi, and Renaissance and Baroque paintings.
