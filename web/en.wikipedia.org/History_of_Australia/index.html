History of Australia

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The history of Australia is the history of the land and peoples which comprise the Commonwealth of Australia. The modern nation is formed from the federation in 1901 of six British self-governing colonies founded from 1788 onward on a continent inhabited by Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples for at least 65,000 years.

Indigenous Australia

Aboriginal Australians arrived by sea from Southeast Asia during the last glacial period, at least 65,000 years ago (as evidenced by artefacts at Madjedbebe rock shelter in Arnhem Land). They developed one of the world's oldest continuous cultures, comprising hundreds of distinct language groups, complex kinship systems, land management practices including fire-stick farming, and extensive trade networks. Torres Strait Islanders, of Melanesian origin, settled the islands between Australia and New Guinea. At the time of European colonisation the Indigenous population is estimated at between 300,000 and over one million.

European exploration and colonisation

Dutch navigators charted much of the west and south coasts in the 17th century (Willem Janszoon's Duyfken reached Cape York in 1606; Abel Tasman mapped Tasmania and New Zealand in 1642–44), but did not settle. In 1770 Lieutenant James Cook charted the east coast in HMS Endeavour and claimed it for Britain as New South Wales. Following the loss of the American colonies, Britain established a penal colony: the First Fleet under Captain Arthur Phillip arrived at Sydney Cove in January 1788 with more than a thousand convicts and marines. Transportation of convicts continued until 1868 in Western Australia, bringing some 162,000 convicts in total. Further colonies were founded at Van Diemen's Land (Tasmania, 1803), Western Australia (1829), South Australia (1836, a planned free settlement), Victoria (1851) and Queensland (1859).

Colonisation was catastrophic for Indigenous peoples: introduced diseases, dispossession of land—justified legally by the fiction of terra nullius—and frontier violence reduced populations drastically. Resistance by figures such as Pemulwuy and Jandamarra is now recognised as part of a long frontier war. The gold rushes of the 1850s transformed the colonies, trebling the population and fuelling democratic reforms including manhood suffrage and the secret ballot (the "Australian ballot").

Federation and the new Commonwealth

On 1 January 1901 the six colonies federated as the Commonwealth of Australia, with Edmund Barton as first prime minister. The new nation was among the world's most progressive in labour and welfare law—early old-age pensions, a basic wage (the 1907 Harvester judgment), and votes for women at federal level from 1902—while the Immigration Restriction Act 1901 founded the racially discriminatory "White Australia policy". Australia fought as part of the British Empire in the First World War; the landing at Gallipoli on 25 April 1915 became a founding national legend (Anzac Day), and some 60,000 Australians died in the war overall.

Depression, war, and post-war transformation

The Great Depression hit Australia hard under the Labor government of James Scullin. In the Second World War Australia fought first alongside Britain and, after Japan's entry into the war in 1941–42—including the bombing of Darwin and the threat of invasion—increasingly under United States protection in the Pacific. Post-war governments pursued mass immigration ("populate or perish"), initially from Britain and Europe, and a vast nation-building agenda: the Snowy Mountains Scheme, expanding universities, and suburban home ownership. The long prime ministership of Robert Menzies (1949–1966) presided over prosperity and Cold War alignment with the United States, including commitment of troops to Korea and Vietnam.

Toward a multicultural republic debate

From the 1960s the White Australia policy was dismantled (completed by the Whitlam government, 1972–75), immigration broadened to Asia and the world, and Australia reoriented economically toward the Asia-Pacific. Key landmarks include the 1967 referendum extending federal power over Aboriginal affairs, the Mabo decision of 1992 rejecting terra nullius and recognising native title, the economic reforms of the Hawke–Keating governments (floating the dollar, deregulation, tariff cuts), and the 2008 national apology to the Stolen Generations under Kevin Rudd. Australia remained a constitutional monarchy after the 1999 republic referendum failed. In the 21st century, debates continue over climate policy, Indigenous recognition—including the failed 2023 Voice referendum—immigration, and Australia's strategic position between the United States and China.
