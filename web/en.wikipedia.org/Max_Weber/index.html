Max Weber

Maximilian Karl Emil Weber (21 April 1864 – 14 June 1920) was a German sociologist, historian, jurist and political economist who is regarded as one of the founding figures of modern sociology. His ideas profoundly influenced social theory, the study of religion, bureaucracy, and the methodology of the social sciences. Weber's most famous work, The Protestant Ethic and the Spirit of Capitalism (1904–05), argued that ascetic Protestantism — especially Calvinism — helped create the disciplined work ethic and rational accumulation characteristic of modern capitalism.

Life

Weber was born in Erfurt, the eldest son of a prosperous lawyer and politician. He studied law, history, economics and philosophy at Heidelberg, Berlin and Göttingen, completing a doctorate on medieval trading companies. He became professor of economics at Freiburg (1894) and Heidelberg (1897), but a nervous breakdown after his father's death forced him to withdraw from teaching for several years. He returned to scholarship as a private scholar, co-founding the journal Archiv für Sozialwissenschaft und Sozialpolitik and the German Sociological Association. Active in politics, Weber advised the German delegation at Versailles and helped draft the Weimar Constitution. He died of pneumonia in Munich in 1920, leaving his vast study Economy and Society unfinished; it was published posthumously.

Key ideas

  • Verstehen — interpretive understanding: sociology should grasp the subjective meanings actors attach to their conduct.
  • Ideal types — analytical constructs (e.g. bureaucracy, charisma) that accentuate features of reality to aid comparison.
  • Authority — legitimate domination takes three pure forms: traditional, charismatic, and legal-rational.
  • Bureaucracy and rationalisation — modern life is increasingly governed by calculable rules, a process Weber feared would trap humanity in an "iron cage" of disenchanted rationality.
  • The Protestant ethic — religious ideas can have unintended, world-transforming economic consequences, an argument Weber extended in comparative studies of China, India and ancient Judaism.
  • Politics as a Vocation and Science as a Vocation — his celebrated 1919 lectures defining the ethic of responsibility and the state's monopoly on legitimate violence: "a state is a human community that (successfully) claims the monopoly of the legitimate use of physical force within a given territory."

Influence

Rediscovered internationally after the Second World War — through Talcott Parsons's translations — Weber became central to sociology, political science, organisation theory and religious studies, and his concepts of charisma, bureaucracy and disenchantment remain everyday scholarly vocabulary.
