Macula

The macula (also called the macula lutea, Latin for "yellow spot") is a pigmented area near the centre of the retina of the human eye, about 5.5 mm in diameter, responsible for high-acuity, detailed central vision. It is rich in cone photoreceptors and in the xanthophyll pigments lutein and zeaxanthin, which give it a yellow colour and help filter blue light.

Structure

Within the macula lies the fovea centralis, a small pit about 1.5 mm across packed almost exclusively with cones at the highest density in the retina, providing the sharpest vision. At the very centre of the fovea is the foveola, which contains only red and green cones. The area immediately surrounding the macula is the parafovea and perifovea. The macula lies temporal to the optic disc and is supplied by branches of the central retinal vessels, though the fovea itself is avascular and nourished by the choroid.

Function

The macula mediates the central ~10 degrees of the visual field used for reading, recognising faces, and perceiving fine detail and colour. Damage to it leaves peripheral vision relatively intact but severely impairs these tasks.

Clinical significance

Age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is the leading cause of vision loss in older adults in developed countries, occurring in "dry" (atrophic) and "wet" (neovascular) forms. Other conditions affecting the macula include macular holes, epiretinal membranes ("macular pucker"), diabetic macular oedema, and inherited macular dystrophies such as Stargardt disease.
