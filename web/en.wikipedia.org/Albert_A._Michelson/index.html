Albert Abraham Michelson (19 December 1852 – 9 May 1931) was an American physicist known for his work on measuring the speed of light and especially for the Michelson–Morley experiment. In 1907 he received the Nobel Prize in Physics, becoming the first American to win a Nobel Prize in a science.

Life and career

Born in Prussia to Polish-Jewish parents, Michelson emigrated with his family to the United States as a small child and grew up in mining towns in California and Nevada. He graduated from the United States Naval Academy in 1873 and, after service at sea, began precision optical experiments while an instructor there. He later held chairs at Case School of Applied Science, Clark University, and, from 1892, headed the physics department at the new University of Chicago.

The Michelson–Morley experiment

In 1887, with chemist Edward Morley, Michelson performed the famous experiment designed to detect the motion of the Earth through the supposed luminiferous aether. Using his interferometer, which split a beam of light into perpendicular paths and recombined it to compare speeds, the experiment found no significant difference — the most famous "failed" result in physics. The null result undermined aether theory and helped pave the way for Einstein's special theory of relativity.

Other work

Michelson devoted much of his career to ever more precise determinations of the speed of light, culminating in measurements over a 22-mile path between Mount Wilson and Mount San Antonio in California. He also pioneered stellar interferometry, measuring the diameter of the star Betelgeuse in 1920 with a 20-foot interferometer mounted on the Hooker telescope.
