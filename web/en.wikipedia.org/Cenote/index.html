Cenote

A cenote (English: /sɪˈnoʊti/ or /sɛˈnoʊteɪ/; Spanish: [seˈnote]) is a natural pit, or sinkhole, resulting from the collapse of limestone bedrock that exposes groundwater. The term originated on the Yucatán Peninsula of Mexico, where cenotes were commonly used for water supplies by the ancient Maya, and occasionally for sacrificial offerings.

The term derives from a word used by the lowland Yucatec Maya — tsʼonoʼot — to refer to any location with accessible groundwater. There are thousands of cenotes in the state of Yucatán alone; their distribution traces the ring of the buried Chicxulub crater.

Formation and types

Cenotes form as rainwater slowly dissolves limestone, creating underground caverns whose roofs eventually collapse. They are classified as young (open pit), mature (semi-open with partial roof), ancient (open basin) and dry cenotes. Many connect to extensive flooded cave systems such as Ox Bel Ha and Sac Actun, among the longest underwater caves in the world.

Ecology

Cenotes support unique ecosystems with endemic species, including blind fish and remipede crustaceans, and anchor trees whose roots reach the water table. A halocline separates fresh water from intruding seawater in coastal systems.

Maya significance

The Maya regarded cenotes as portals to Xibalba, the underworld. The Sacred Cenote at Chichén Itzá received offerings of gold, jade, ceramics and human sacrifices. Because the northern Yucatán has almost no surface rivers, cenotes determined settlement locations.
