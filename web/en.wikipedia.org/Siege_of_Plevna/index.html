Siege of Plevna

Date19 July – 10 December 1877
LocationPlevna (Pleven), Bulgaria, Ottoman Empire
ResultRussian–Romanian victory
CommandersOsman Nuri Pasha (Ottoman); Grand Duke Nicholas Nikolaevich, Carol I of Romania, Eduard Totleben (Russian–Romanian)

The Siege of Plevna, also known as the Siege of Pleven, was a major land battle of the Russo-Turkish War of 1877–1878, fought between the combined armies of the Russian Empire and the Kingdom of Romania, and the Ottoman Army. After the Russian army crossed the Danube at Svishtov, it began advancing towards the centre of modern Bulgaria, with the aim of crossing the Balkan Mountains to reach Constantinople. The Ottoman commander Osman Nuri Pasha, on his way from Vidin, arrived at Plevna just hours before the first Russian troops and hastily prepared defensive positions.

Course of the siege

The Russian and Romanian forces launched three major assaults against the fortified town. The first battle, on 20 July 1877, saw a small Russian force repulsed. In the second battle, on 30–31 July, General Mikhail Skobelev attacked the Grivitsa redoubts but the assault failed with heavy losses. The third battle, beginning on 11 September — the Tsar's name day — was the largest, with over 100,000 Allied troops attacking; the Russians captured several redoubts but could not take the town, and Osman Pasha counter-attacked successfully in most sectors.

After the third assault failed, the Allies, advised by the engineer general Eduard Totleben, settled into a formal siege, completing the encirclement of Plevna by late October and cutting off all supplies. Starvation eventually forced Osman Pasha to attempt a breakout on 10 December 1877. The attempt failed; Osman was wounded and surrendered with his army of some 43,000 men.

Significance

The five-month delay at Plevna gave the Ottoman Empire time to reinforce its defences and allowed the war to become a matter of European diplomacy. The siege is regarded as a landmark in military history for demonstrating the power of entrenched infantry armed with modern breech-loading rifles — including the Turkish Peabody-Martini and Winchester repeaters — against frontal assault, foreshadowing the trench warfare of the First World War. The fall of Plevna opened the road across the Balkans and led directly to the Treaty of San Stefano and, subsequently, the Congress of Berlin.
