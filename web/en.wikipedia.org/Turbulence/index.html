In fluid dynamics, turbulence or turbulent flow is fluid motion characterized by chaotic changes in pressure and flow velocity. It is in contrast to laminar flow, which occurs when a fluid flows in parallel layers, with no disruption between those layers. Turbulence is commonly observed in everyday phenomena such as surf, fast flowing rivers, billowing storm clouds, and smoke from a chimney, and most fluid flows occurring in nature and created in engineering applications are turbulent.

Characteristics

Turbulent flows are always highly irregular, three-dimensional, rotational, and dissipative, exhibiting eddies over a wide range of length and time scales. They greatly enhance mixing, heat transfer, and drag. The onset of turbulence in a flow is predicted by the Reynolds number — the ratio of inertial to viscous forces; pipe flow typically becomes turbulent above Re ≈ 2,300–4,000, a transition Osborne Reynolds studied with dye experiments in 1883. Leonardo da Vinci sketched turbulent eddies in water around 1500 and coined observations on the phenomenon.

The energy cascade

Lewis Fry Richardson pictured turbulence as a hierarchy of eddies: energy is injected at the largest scales, transferred by vortex stretching to ever smaller eddies with little loss, and finally dissipated as heat by viscosity at the smallest (Kolmogorov) scales — summarized in his rhyme "Big whirls have little whirls that feed on their velocity..." Andrey Kolmogorov's 1941 theory (K41) predicted statistical universality in the inertial range, most famously the −5/3 power law for the energy spectrum, E(k) ∝ ε2/3 k−5/3, confirmed by many experiments. G. I. Taylor and Theodore von Kármán developed the statistical theory of homogeneous turbulence.

Modeling and significance

Despite the completeness of the Navier–Stokes equations, no general analytical solution for turbulence exists; the equations' regularity is itself a Millennium Prize Problem. Engineering relies on computational fluid dynamics with turbulence models — Reynolds-averaged Navier–Stokes (RANS) closures such as k–ε and k–ω, large eddy simulation (LES), and, where affordable, direct numerical simulation (DNS). Turbulence governs weather and ocean mixing, aircraft drag and noise, combustion efficiency, blood flow, and the interstellar medium, making it often described as the most important unsolved problem of classical physics.
