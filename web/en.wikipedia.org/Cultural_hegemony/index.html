Cultural hegemony

Cultural hegemony is a concept developed by Italian Marxist philosopher Antonio Gramsci describing how a ruling class maintains dominance not only through political and economic coercion but by shaping cultural norms, values, and beliefs so that its worldview becomes accepted as common sense by society as a whole.

Key thinker: Antonio Gramsci
Source: Prison Notebooks (1929–1935)
Related: Ideology, hegemony, civil society

Gramsci's theory

Writing in prison under Mussolini's fascist regime, Gramsci argued in his Prison Notebooks that the state maintains power through a combination of coercion (political society) and consent (civil society). Cultural institutions — schools, churches, media, and popular culture — disseminate the values of the dominant group, making existing social arrangements appear natural and inevitable.

Hegemony and counter-hegemony

Gramsci proposed that subordinate groups could challenge hegemony through a "war of position" — a long cultural and intellectual struggle to build alternative institutions and a counter-hegemonic worldview, led by what he called organic intellectuals emerging from the working class itself.

Influence

The concept has profoundly influenced cultural studies, media studies, education theory, and post-colonial theory. Scholars such as Stuart Hall applied Gramscian hegemony to analyse mass media, race, and popular culture in contemporary capitalist societies.
