The Mosque–Cathedral of Córdoba (Spanish: Mezquita-Catedral de Córdoba), officially known by its ecclesiastical name of Cathedral of Our Lady of the Assumption (Spanish: Catedral de Nuestra Señora de la Asunción), is the cathedral of the Roman Catholic Diocese of Córdoba, dedicated to Our Lady of the Assumption, and located in the Spanish region of Andalusia. Built originally as the Great Mosque of Córdoba, it is regarded as one of the most accomplished monuments of Moorish architecture.

History

The site originally housed a Visigothic church, the Catholic Basilica of Saint Vincent of Saragossa. In 785, Abd al-Rahman I, emir of Córdoba, began building a great mosque on the site. It was successively enlarged by his successors, notably Abd al-Rahman II, al-Hakam II — who added the richly decorated mihrab and maqsurah — and the chamberlain al-Mansur, reaching its present vast extent of 856 columns of jasper, onyx, marble, and granite supporting double-tiered horseshoe arches. After King Ferdinand III captured Córdoba in 1236, the mosque was consecrated as a cathedral. In the 16th century a Renaissance cathedral nave was inserted into the centre of the prayer hall, a change Charles V is said to have regretted on seeing it.

Architecture

The building covers some 24,000 square metres. Its famous hypostyle hall of red-and-white voussoir arches, the Patio de los Naranjos (Courtyard of the Orange Trees), and the former minaret — encased in the Renaissance bell tower — make it the emblem of Córdoba, whose historic centre is a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
