Node of Ranvier

Nodes of Ranvier, also known as myelin-sheath gaps, occur along a myelinated axon where the axolemma is exposed to the extracellular space. Nodes of Ranvier are uninsulated, highly enriched in ion channels, and participate in the rapid and efficient propagation of action potentials, which "jump" from node to node in a process called saltatory conduction.

Structure

Nodes are typically about one micrometre long. They are densely packed with voltage-gated sodium and potassium channels, anchored by cell-adhesion molecules, and flanked by paranodal junctions where glial loops attach to the axon. In the peripheral nervous system the sheaths are formed by Schwann cells; in the central nervous system by oligodendrocytes.

Function

Because the myelin sheath insulates the internode segments, action potentials are regenerated only at nodes, greatly increasing conduction velocity and reducing metabolic cost compared with unmyelinated fibres. The nodes are named after the French anatomist Louis-Antoine Ranvier, who described them in the 1870s. Demyelinating diseases such as multiple sclerosis disrupt nodal architecture and slow or block conduction.
