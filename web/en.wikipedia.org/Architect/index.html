Architect

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

An architect is a person who plans, designs, and oversees the construction of buildings. To practise architecture means to provide services in connection with the design of buildings and the space within the site surrounding the buildings that have human occupancy or use as their principal purpose. Etymologically, the term derives from the Latin architectus, from the Greek arkhitéktōn (ἀρχιτέκτων, "chief builder", from ἀρχι- "chief" and τέκτων "builder").

The profession

In most jurisdictions the title "architect" and the practice of architecture for regulated building work are reserved by law to licensed professionals. Licensing typically requires an accredited professional degree, a period of supervised practical experience (internship), and passing an examination — in the United States the Architect Registration Examination administered under NCARB, in the United Kingdom registration with the Architects Registration Board following RIBA-validated qualifications. Architects carry professional liability for their designs and often a duty to administer the building contract impartially between client and builder.

Practice

Throughout a project, an architect coordinates a design team that may include structural, mechanical, and electrical engineers, quantity surveyors or cost consultants, landscape architects, and specialist consultants. Work proceeds through stages — feasibility, concept and schematic design, design development, construction documents, tendering, and construction supervision — increasingly delivered through building information modelling (BIM). Architects' designs must satisfy the client's brief and budget as well as building codes, planning rules, accessibility standards, and fire and life-safety requirements. The architect's central role in reconciling aesthetics, function, structure, and regulation has made the profession a paradigm case of applied design: Vitruvius summarised its aims as firmitas, utilitas, venustas — firmness, commodity, and delight.

History

Named designer-builders appear early: Imhotep in Egypt, Ictinus (architect of the Parthenon) in Greece, and Vitruvius, whose De architectura is the only major architectural treatise to survive from antiquity. Medieval master masons directed cathedral construction; the Renaissance — Brunelleschi and Alberti, whose De re aedificatoria founded architectural theory — recast the architect as a learned artist distinct from the craftsman. The modern profession crystallised in the 19th century with institutes (the RIBA in 1834, the AIA in 1857), formal education (the École des Beaux-Arts; later the Bauhaus), and statutory registration in the 20th century.
