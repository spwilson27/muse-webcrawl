The Lakota (Lakota: Očhéthi Šakówiŋ, "Seven Council Fires") are a Native American people entitled to live in the Great Plains of North America. They are the westernmost of the three major Sioux groups, the others being the Dakota and Nakota, and speak Lakota, one of the large Siouan languages. Historically nomadic hunters of the American bison, the Lakota became the dominant power on the northern Plains in the nineteenth century.

History

Sioux-speaking peoples originally lived in the woodlands around the western Great Lakes and moved westward onto the Plains in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, a migration accelerated by the adoption of the horse. By the early 1800s the Lakota controlled a vast territory from the Missouri River to the Bighorn Mountains, including Paha Sapa, the Black Hills, sacred in Lakota spirituality.

Conflict with the United States intensified after the Fort Laramie Treaty of 1868 guaranteed Lakota possession of the Black Hills. The discovery of gold there led to the Great Sioux War of 1876–77, in which Lakota and Cheyenne forces under leaders including Sitting Bull, Crazy Horse and Red Cloud defeated Custer's Seventh Cavalry at the Little Bighorn in 1876 but were ultimately forced onto reservations. The massacre at Wounded Knee in December 1890, in which U.S. troops killed some 250–300 Lakota, most of them unarmed followers of the Ghost Dance, effectively ended armed resistance.

Contemporary Lakota

Today the Lakota are organised in several federally recognised tribes, including the Oglala Sioux Tribe of the Pine Ridge Reservation, the Rosebud Sioux Tribe, the Cheyenne River Sioux Tribe and the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe. In 1980 the U.S. Supreme Court ruled in United States v. Sioux Nation that the Black Hills had been taken illegally and awarded compensation, which the Sioux nations have refused, insisting on the return of the land. The 2016–17 protests against the Dakota Access Pipeline at Standing Rock drew renewed international attention to Lakota treaty rights. Language revitalisation programmes seek to preserve Lakota, now spoken fluently by a shrinking number of elders and a growing number of immersion-school students.
