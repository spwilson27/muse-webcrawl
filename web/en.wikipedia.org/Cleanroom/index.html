A cleanroom is an engineered environment in which the concentration of airborne particles is controlled to specified limits, along with temperature, humidity and pressure. Cleanrooms are essential wherever microscopic contamination can ruin a product: in semiconductor fabrication a single dust particle can destroy a microchip, and in pharmaceutical manufacture airborne microbes can contaminate sterile drugs.

Classification

Cleanrooms are classified by the number and size of particles permitted per volume of air. The international standard ISO 14644 defines classes from ISO 1 (cleanest) to ISO 9. The older US Federal Standard 209E used counts of 0.5 μm particles per cubic foot — a "class 100" room allows at most 100 such particles per cubic foot, roughly ISO 5. Semiconductor fabs operate at the cleanest classes; hospital operating theatres and food plants at less stringent ones.

Design and operation

Air is continuously supplied through high-efficiency particulate air (HEPA) or ultra-low penetration air (ULPA) filters, often covering the entire ceiling so that air flows in a smooth laminar stream downward and out through the floor or low walls. Rooms are held at positive pressure so unfiltered air cannot leak in. Surfaces are smooth and non-shedding. Personnel — the largest source of contamination — enter through airlocks and gown in special garments ("bunny suits"), gloves, masks and hoods, and follow strict protocols.

History

Precursors arose in hospital operating theatres in the nineteenth century, but the modern cleanroom was developed in the 1960s, notably by Willis Whitfield at Sandia National Laboratories, whose laminar-flow design reduced particle counts a thousand-fold. The space race and the semiconductor industry drove rapid adoption, and cleanroom technology underpins the production of every modern integrated circuit.
