The Saturday Evening Post

The Saturday Evening Post is an American magazine, published six times a year. It was issued weekly from 1821 until 1969 and regained a modest following as a quarterly and later bimonthly publication. The magazine, rooted in The Pennsylvania Gazette founded by Benjamin Franklin in 1728, is best remembered for its covers illustrated by Norman Rockwell, whose 321 cover paintings between 1916 and 1963 became emblematic of American life.

History

The Post was founded in 1821 in Philadelphia by Samuel D. Patterson and was published weekly for nearly 150 years. Its most transformative era began when Cyrus Curtis purchased the struggling magazine in 1897 for $1,000 and installed editor George Horace Lorimer, who built it into one of the largest-circulation magazines in the world. Under Lorimer, the Post published fiction and essays by leading writers of the day, including F. Scott Fitzgerald, Ring Lardner, Sinclair Lewis, and Jack London.

By the 1920s weekly circulation exceeded two million. The magazine's covers, painted by Rockwell and other illustrators such as J. C. Leyendecker, N. C. Wyeth, and John Clymer, made it a fixture of American popular culture. Rising television competition eroded its advertising base in the 1950s and 1960s, and weekly publication ceased in 1969. The magazine was revived in 1971 by Indianapolis industrialist Beurt SerVaas and his wife, medical columnist Cory SerVaas, and has since been published by the nonprofit Saturday Evening Post Society, emphasizing health, fiction, and nostalgia.

Norman Rockwell covers

Rockwell's 321 covers, produced over 47 years, chronicled everyday American scenes—small-town barbershops, wartime homecomings, Thanksgiving dinners—and remain the magazine's most enduring legacy. His final cover appeared in 1963, the year he moved to Look magazine. Original Rockwell Post paintings are held by the Norman Rockwell Museum in Stockbridge, Massachusetts, and regularly command millions of dollars at auction.
