Starting blocks

Starting blocks are a device used in the sport of track and field by sprint athletes to brace their feet against at the start of a race so they do not slip as they stride forward at the sound of the starter's pistol. The blocks consist of two adjustable pedals attached to a rigid frame, or "rail", which is anchored to the track. They are used in races up to and including 400 metres, as well as the first leg of some relays.

History

Before starting blocks, sprinters dug small holes in cinder tracks to gain purchase. The first starting blocks were patented in 1929 by George T. Bresnahan and William J. Byrd. They were used at the 1948 Summer Olympics in London, the first Games at which the device was permitted internationally, and became universal on synthetic tracks.

Use in competition

In the "on your marks" phase of the start, the athlete places their feet on the pedals; on "set", the hips rise; the athlete then drives forward explosively when the gun fires. Under World Athletics rules, a reaction time of less than 0.1 seconds is treated as a false start. Modern blocks are fitted with electronic pressure sensors connected to the timing system to detect false starts.

Construction

Blocks are typically made of aluminium with rubber-faced pedals whose angle and position along the centre rail can be adjusted to suit the athlete's stance preferences—commonly described as bullet, medium, or elongated spacing.
