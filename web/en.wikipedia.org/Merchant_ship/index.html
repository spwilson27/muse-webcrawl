Merchant ship

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A merchant ship, merchant vessel, or trading vessel is a watercraft that transports cargo or carries passengers for hire. This excludes pleasure craft, naval vessels, and ships used for fishing or research. The fleets of merchant ships registered by a country constitute its merchant navy or merchant marine, crewed by merchant seafarers.

Types

Modern merchant ships include container ships, bulk carriers for ore, grain, and coal, oil and chemical tankers, liquefied gas carriers, roll-on/roll-off ships for vehicles, refrigerated cargo ships, general cargo ships, and passenger vessels such as ferries and cruise ships. The largest ships — ultra-large container vessels and crude carriers — exceed 300 metres in length and 200,000 tonnes deadweight.

Regulation

Merchant shipping is regulated internationally by the International Maritime Organization through conventions on safety of life at sea (SOLAS), pollution prevention (MARPOL), and seafarer training (STCW). Ships are registered under flags of their owners' choosing, with much of the world fleet sailing under open registries such as Panama, Liberia, and the Marshall Islands.

Trade

Well over 80 percent of world trade by volume moves by sea, making merchant shipping the backbone of the global economy.
