Euphrates

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

The Euphrates is the longest and one of the most historically important rivers of West Asia. Together with the Tigris, it is one of the two defining rivers of Mesopotamia ("the land between the rivers"). The river originates in the Armenian Highlands of eastern Turkey, flows southeast through Syria and Iraq, and joins the Tigris in southern Iraq to form the Shatt al-Arab, which empties into the Persian Gulf.

Course

The Euphrates is formed by the confluence of the Karasu and Murat rivers in eastern Turkey. From there it flows south through deep gorges, enters Syria near Jarabulus, is joined by the Balikh and Khabur tributaries, and continues into Iraq. Its total length is about 2,800 kilometres (1,740 miles), making it the longest river in West Asia. Major cities on its banks include Raqqa and Deir ez-Zor in Syria, and Fallujah, Ramadi, Karbala (nearby), Najaf, Nasiriyah, and Samawah in Iraq.

History

The Euphrates valley formed part of the Fertile Crescent, where agriculture and some of the earliest cities arose. Its banks saw the rise of Sumer, Akkad, Babylon, and Assyria. The river is mentioned in the Hebrew Bible as one of the four rivers of the Garden of Eden and as the promised boundary of Israel. In the Book of Revelation it is prophesied to dry up in preparation for the end times.

Water use and disputes

The river's flow is heavily managed by dams, including Turkey's Keban, Karakaya, and Atatürk dams (part of the Southeastern Anatolia Project), Syria's Tabqa Dam (forming Lake Assad), and Iraq's Haditha Dam. Disagreements among Turkey, Syria, and Iraq over water allocation have been a persistent source of regional tension, and flows have declined significantly since the 1970s, contributing to water scarcity in Iraq.
