Envelope

Material: Usually paper

Purpose: Enclosing flat objects, especially letters, for mailing

An envelope is a common packaging item, usually made of thin, flat material. It is designed to contain a flat object, such as a letter or card. Traditional envelopes are made from sheets of paper cut to one of three shapes: a rhombus, a short-arm cross, or a kite, which allows the envelope structure — front, back, and four folded flaps — to be formed from a single sheet.

History

Enclosures for documents are ancient: Babylonian messages were encased in clay envelopes, and Chinese paper envelopes (hongbao) were used for monetary gifts. Machine-made paper envelopes became common in the nineteenth century; Edwin Hill and Warren De La Rue patented an envelope-folding machine in Britain in 1845, and Rowland Hill's postal reforms, including the Penny Post and prepayment, greatly expanded their use.

Sizes and styles

Envelopes are produced in standard sizes, such as the ISO C-series (C4, C5, C6) designed to hold A-series paper, and North American sizes such as No. 10. Window envelopes reveal the address printed on the enclosed document; other styles include padded mailers, security-tinted envelopes, and self-seal designs closed with latex or peel-and-seal adhesive.

Closure and materials

Flaps may be sealed with moisture-activated gum, pressure-sensitive adhesive, or metal clasps. Beyond paper, envelopes are made from plastic films and fibreboard for durability, and plantable and recycled variants serve niche uses.
