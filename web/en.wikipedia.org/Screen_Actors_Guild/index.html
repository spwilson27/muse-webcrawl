The Screen Actors Guild (SAG) was an American labor union which represented over 100,000 film and television principal and background performers worldwide. On March 30, 2012, the union leadership announced that the membership had voted to merge with the American Federation of Television and Radio Artists (AFTRA), creating SAG-AFTRA.

History

SAG was founded in 1933 at a time when film actors worked brutal schedules — routinely seven days a week, with no paid overtime, rest periods, or limits on hours — under long-term studio contracts that gave them no choice of roles. Early meetings were held in secret, disguised as parties, because actors feared blacklisting; the guild's first members included Berton Churchill, who pushed hardest for its organisation, and its first president was Ralph Morgan. In 1935, President Franklin D. Roosevelt granted the guild recognition through the National Labor Relations Act, and in 1937 SAG won its first contract with the film studios, securing minimum pay, reasonable working hours and meal breaks.

Under president Ronald Reagan (1947–1952, 1959–1960), SAG negotiated the first residuals for actors when their films were shown on television, following the 1960 strike. Subsequent disputes included the 2000 commercials strike and the contested 2008–2009 contract round, in which disagreement over new-media compensation delayed a settlement.

Awards

The guild presented the Screen Actors Guild Awards from 1995, honouring outstanding performances in film and television as voted by union members; the statuette, "The Actor", depicts a performer holding masks of comedy and tragedy. The awards continued under SAG-AFTRA after the merger, and are considered an important precursor to the Academy Awards.

Global Rule One

SAG maintained "Global Rule One", requiring members not to work for producers who had not signed a union agreement, and operated a financial-core system permitting non-member actors to work union productions for restricted fees. The union also maintained the famous rule that no two members may share the same professional name, which is why many actors adopt middle initials or stage names.
