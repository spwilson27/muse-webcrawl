World literature

World literature encompasses the classics of many national traditions.

World literature (German: Weltliteratur) refers both to the totality of the world's national literatures and to the body of literary works that circulate beyond their culture of origin, chiefly through translation. The term was popularized by Johann Wolfgang von Goethe, who wrote in 1827 that "national literature is now a rather unmeaning term; the epoch of world literature is at hand."

Concepts and debates

Scholars disagree on what qualifies a work as world literature. For Goethe and Karl Marx — who invoked Weltliteratur in The Communist Manifesto — it described the emerging global literary marketplace. Modern theorist David Damrosch defines world literature not as a canon but as a mode of circulation and reading: works that gain in translation and are read outside their home culture. Others, such as Pascale Casanova, emphasize the unequal "world republic of letters," in which prestige concentrates in literary capitals like Paris and London, while scholars from the Global South criticize canons dominated by European texts.

Scope

World literature courses and anthologies typically range from ancient epics — the Epic of Gilgamesh, Homer, Mahabharata, and Journey to the West — through classical Chinese, Sanskrit, Arabic, and Persian poetry, medieval romances, and the modern novel, to contemporary writers such as Gabriel García Márquez, Chinua Achebe, Haruki Murakami, and Olga Tokarczuk. The growth of translation prizes and international publishing has steadily widened what circulates as world literature.
