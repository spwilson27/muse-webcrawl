Circle of fifths

In music theory, the circle of fifths (sometimes also called the circle of fourths) is the relationship among the 12 tones of the chromatic scale, their corresponding key signatures, and the associated major and minor keys. More specifically, it is a geometrical representation of relationships among the 12 pitch classes of the chromatic scale in pitch class space.

Structure

Beginning with any tone, ascending by the interval of a perfect fifth generates all twelve pitch classes before returning to the starting tone an octave-equivalent later. Arranged in a circle, adjacent keys differ by a single sharp or flat in their key signatures: moving clockwise from C major adds sharps (G, D, A, E, B, F♯, C♯), while moving counterclockwise adds flats (F, B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭, G♭). Each major key is paired with its relative minor, shown on an inner ring. Enharmonically equivalent keys meet at the bottom of the circle.

Because twelve perfect fifths exceed seven octaves by the Pythagorean comma, the circle closes exactly only in equal temperament, where each fifth is slightly narrowed.

Uses

The circle of fifths is a foundational tool for understanding key signatures, chord progressions, and modulation. Closely related keys—those adjacent on the circle—share most of their notes and are natural targets for modulation. Chord progressions in tonal and popular music frequently move around the circle, as in the descending-fifth sequences of Baroque music and the ii–V–I progressions of jazz. The circle also organizes the order of sharps (F♯, C♯, G♯, D♯, A♯, E♯, B♯) and flats (B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭, G♭, C♭, F♭).

History

The circle was described in the late 17th and early 18th centuries by theorists including the Ukrainian composer Mykola Diletsky and the German theorist Johann David Heinichen, whose 1728 treatise presented a version close to the modern diagram. It has since become one of the most widely used visual aids in music education.
