Heinrich Aloysius Maria Elisabeth Brüning (26 November 1885 – 30 March 1970) was a German politician and economist who served as Chancellor of Germany from 1930 to 1932, during the final years of the Weimar Republic and the worst of the Great Depression.

Chancellorship

A conservative member of the Catholic Centre Party and a veteran of the First World War, Brüning was appointed chancellor by President Paul von Hindenburg in March 1930. Lacking a parliamentary majority, he governed largely through presidential emergency decrees under Article 48 of the constitution. His government pursued drastic austerity — cuts to wages, salaries and unemployment benefits, and tax rises — intended to balance the budget, stabilise the currency and demonstrate that Germany could not pay war reparations. The reparations question was largely settled in his favour: payments were suspended by the Hoover Moratorium in 1931 and effectively ended at Lausanne in 1932, shortly after his fall.

Fall and exile

Brüning's deflationary policies deepened mass unemployment, which fuelled the rise of the Nazi and Communist parties; he became known as the "Hunger Chancellor". Hindenburg dismissed him in May 1932. After the Nazi seizure of power he fled Germany in 1934, eventually settling in the United States, where he taught at Harvard University. He returned to Germany after the war, teaching at the University of Cologne, before retiring again to Vermont.

Assessment

Historians debate whether Brüning's austerity was unavoidable orthodoxy or a catastrophic error that destroyed the republic's remaining support, and whether his decree-based rule fatally weakened German democracy before Hitler.
