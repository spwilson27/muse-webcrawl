Carbon tax

A carbon tax is a tax levied on the carbon content of goods and services, generally on fossil fuels at a rate proportional to the carbon dioxide released when they are burned. It is a form of carbon pricing intended to reduce greenhouse gas emissions by incorporating the social cost of carbon into market prices.

Design

Carbon taxes are usually applied upstream, on fuel producers and importers, per tonne of CO2. Revenue may be returned to citizens as dividends ("fee and dividend"), used to cut other taxes, or spent on climate programmes. Economists widely regard a carbon tax as a cost-effective mitigation instrument because it gives a uniform incentive to reduce emissions wherever cheapest; it is often contrasted with emissions trading systems (cap and trade), which fix the quantity of emissions and let the price vary.

Implementation

Finland introduced the first carbon tax in 1990, followed by other Nordic countries. Sweden's tax, begun in 1991, is among the world's highest and is credited with substantial emission reductions alongside economic growth. British Columbia introduced a revenue-neutral carbon tax in 2008, and Canada adopted a federal backstop price in 2019. As of the mid-2020s, several dozen national and subnational jurisdictions price carbon in some form.

Debate

Supporters cite simplicity, price stability, and efficiency. Critics raise concerns about regressivity for low-income households, competitiveness and carbon leakage to untaxed jurisdictions, and political acceptability; design features such as rebates and border carbon adjustments are proposed to address these issues.
