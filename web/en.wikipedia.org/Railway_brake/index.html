Railway brake

Main typesAir brake, vacuum brake, dynamic brake, hand brake
Key inventorGeorge Westinghouse (air brake, 1869/1872)
Safety featureFail-safe: loss of air pressure applies the brakes

A railway brake is a type of brake used on the cars of railway trains to enable deceleration, control acceleration downhill, or keep vehicles stationary when parked. Because a train's cars are coupled together, brakes must act simultaneously and reliably along the whole train.

Air brakes

The dominant system is the compressed-air brake developed by George Westinghouse. In its automatic form (patented 1872), a brake pipe runs the length of the train; a drop in pipe pressure — whether commanded by the driver or caused by the train parting — causes each car's triple valve to admit air from a local reservoir into its brake cylinder, applying the brakes. This makes the system fail-safe.

Other systems

Vacuum brakes, which use ejectors to create a vacuum instead of compressed air, were historically common in Britain and its colonies. Locomotives also use dynamic braking (rheostatic or regenerative), in which traction motors act as generators, and modern passenger trains rely heavily on disc brakes and blended electro-pneumatic control. Freight operations add retainers, load-proportional braking and parking (hand) brakes.

History

Early trains depended on brakemen turning hand brakes in each vehicle; runaway accidents drove the adoption of continuous brakes, mandated for passenger trains in Britain after the Armagh disaster of 1889.
