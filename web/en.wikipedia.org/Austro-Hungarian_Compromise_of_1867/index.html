Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867

Franz Joseph I crowned King of Hungary in Budapest, 8 June 1867.

The Austro-Hungarian Compromise of 1867 (German: Ausgleich; Hungarian: kiegyezés) was the constitutional settlement that transformed the Austrian Empire into the dual monarchy of Austria-Hungary. It divided the Habsburg state into two autonomous halves — the Austrian (Cisleithanian) lands and the Kingdom of Hungary (the Lands of the Crown of Saint Stephen) — united in the person of the monarch and by common ministries for foreign affairs, war and joint finance.

Background

After the Hungarian Revolution of 1848–49 was crushed, Hungary was governed from Vienna under neo-absolutism. Austria's defeats in Italy (1859) and Germany (1866), the latter at Königgrätz, left the monarchy financially strained and diplomatically isolated, and persuaded Emperor Franz Joseph that a settlement with the Hungarian political nation was unavoidable. Negotiations were led on the Hungarian side by Ferenc Deák, the "Sage of the Fatherland", whose moderate programme — formulated in the Easter Article of 1865 — demanded restoration of Hungary's constitutional rights within a shared monarchy.

Terms

The Hungarian Diet enacted the Compromise as Law XII of 1867; in Austria it was embodied in the December Constitution. Hungary regained its parliament, its own responsible government (under Prime Minister Gyula Andrássy), and internal self-government including control of its own administration, justice and education. Croatia-Slavonia received partial autonomy under the Croatian–Hungarian Settlement of 1868. The two states shared the monarch (Emperor in Austria, King in Hungary), the common army, foreign policy and the finances needed for them; joint expenses were apportioned by quota — initially 70% Austria to 30% Hungary — renegotiated every ten years, as was the common customs and commercial union. Franz Joseph was crowned King of Hungary in Budapest on 8 June 1867.

Consequences

The Compromise stabilised the monarchy for half a century, produced rapid economic growth and made Budapest one of Europe's fastest-growing capitals. Its critics noted that it entrenched German and Magyar predominance over the empire's Slav and Romanian peoples, and that the decennial renegotiations repeatedly produced constitutional crises. The dualist structure lasted until the dissolution of Austria-Hungary in 1918.
