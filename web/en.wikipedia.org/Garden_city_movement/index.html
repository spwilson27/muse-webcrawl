Garden city movement

Image placeholder: Ebenezer Howard's "Three Magnets" diagram

The garden city movement is a method of urban planning initiated in 1898 by Sir Ebenezer Howard in the United Kingdom. Garden cities were intended to be planned, self-contained communities surrounded by "greenbelts", containing proportionate areas of residences, industry, and agriculture.

Howard's vision

In his book To-morrow: A Peaceful Path to Real Reform (1898), reissued in 1902 as Garden Cities of To-morrow, Howard proposed combining the advantages of town and country while eliminating their disadvantages — his famous "Three Magnets" diagram. A garden city would be built on inexpensive agricultural land held in common, would be limited in size (about 32,000 people on 6,000 acres, with the surrounding greenbelt permanently preserved), and when full, would be succeeded by another similar city nearby, forming a cluster of "social cities" linked by rail.

Implementation

The Garden City Association (founded 1899) promoted the idea, and the first garden city, Letchworth in Hertfordshire (begun 1903, planned by Raymond Unwin and Barry Parker), was followed by Welwyn Garden City (1919), in which Howard himself was involved. Garden suburbs such as Hampstead Garden Suburb adapted the ideas at a smaller scale. The movement influenced town planning internationally, including in Germany, the United States (Radburn, New Jersey; the Greenbelt towns of the New Deal), Australia (Canberra's landscape plan), and Japan. In Britain it contributed to the New Towns Act 1946 and the post-war New Towns programme.

Legacy and criticism

Howard's ideas shaped modern planning concepts including the greenbelt, zoning, and the neighbourhood unit. Critics note that many supposed garden cities became dormitory suburbs lacking the self-sufficiency and social mix Howard intended, and that the concept's low densities can encourage car dependence. Nevertheless, the garden city remains an influential ideal in twenty-first-century planning, echoed in eco-towns and garden-community programmes.
