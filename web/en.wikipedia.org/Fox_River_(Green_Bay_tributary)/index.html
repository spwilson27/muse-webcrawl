Fox River (Green Bay tributary)

The Fox River is a 182-mile-long (293 km) river in eastern and central Wisconsin, United States, flowing into Green Bay of Lake Michigan at the city of Green Bay. It is divided by Lake Winnebago into the Upper Fox, which rises in south-central Wisconsin and flows northeast to the lake, and the Lower Fox, which leaves Lake Winnebago at Menasha and descends about 39 miles to Green Bay, dropping roughly 164 feet through a series of rapids. This article concerns the Fox River tributary of Green Bay; a separate Fox River flows south through Illinois to join the Illinois River.

Course and history

The Fox–Wisconsin waterway, joined by a short portage at present-day Portage, was one of North America's great Indigenous and fur-trade routes, linking the Great Lakes to the Mississippi River. In 1673 Jacques Marquette and Louis Jolliet traversed it on their voyage to the Mississippi. The Lower Fox's rapids powered sawmills and then the paper industry: cities such as Appleton, Neenah, Menasha, Kaukauna, and De Pere grew around water power, and the valley became one of the world's densest concentrations of paper mills. A system of locks — 17 on the Lower Fox — once allowed navigation from Green Bay to Lake Winnebago; most were closed in the 1980s, though several have since been restored for recreational use.

PCB contamination and cleanup

From the 1950s to 1971, paper mills discharged polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) from the manufacture and recycling of carbonless copy paper into the Lower Fox, creating one of the largest PCB sediment contamination sites in the world. The river and Green Bay were designated a Superfund site, and an extensive remediation — dredging and capping of contaminated sediments, costing roughly a billion dollars — was carried out from 2009 to 2020 by the responsible paper companies under government oversight. Fish consumption advisories remain in effect while monitoring continues.
