Dystopia

A dystopia (from Ancient Greek dys- "bad" and topos "place"; also called a cacotopia or anti-utopia) is a fictional or real community or society that is undesirable or frightening. It is often characterized by an authoritarian or totalitarian form of government, dehumanization, tyranny, societal collapse, environmental disaster or other characteristics associated with a cataclysmic decline in society.

In literature

Dystopian fiction is a major genre of speculative fiction, often serving as a warning against current trends extrapolated to extremes. Canonical works include Yevgeny Zamyatin's We (1924), Aldous Huxley's Brave New World (1932), George Orwell's Nineteen Eighty-Four (1949), Ray Bradbury's Fahrenheit 451 (1953) and Margaret Atwood's The Handmaid's Tale (1985). Dystopian societies are often controlled through propaganda, surveillance, suppression of independent thought and rigid conformity.

Themes

Common themes include the loss of individual freedom, the dangers of unchecked state power or corporate power, technological domination, scarcity, and the dehumanizing effects of bureaucracy. Dystopias are frequently the mirror image of utopias: societies that aspired to perfection but produced oppression instead. The word was first used by John Stuart Mill in a parliamentary speech of 1868, contrasting "dystopians" with "utopians".
