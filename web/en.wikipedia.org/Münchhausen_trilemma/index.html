Münchhausen trilemma

The Münchhausen trilemma is a thought experiment from the philosophy of knowledge (epistemology) intended to show that it is impossible to prove any truth with absolute certainty. It was formulated by the German philosopher Hans Albert, building on earlier work associated with Karl Popper and the problem of justification first explored in antiquity, notably by Agrippa the Sceptic, whose similar argument is known as Agrippa's trilemma.

The name alludes to Baron Munchausen, the literary character who claimed to have pulled himself out of a swamp by his own hair — an image of a justification that supports itself without any external foundation.

The three horns

According to the trilemma, any attempt to justify a proposition must end in one of three unsatisfying outcomes:

  1. Infinite regress: each justification requires a further justification, which requires another, and so on without end.
  2. Circular reasoning: the chain of justification eventually loops back on itself, so the proposition is ultimately used to support itself.
  3. Dogmatic termination: the chain is simply broken off at some point that is declared self-evident or certain without further justification — an axiomatic stopping point.

Responses

Philosophers have responded in several ways. Foundationalists accept a version of the third horn, holding that some basic beliefs are self-justifying. Coherentists embrace a structured form of circularity, arguing that beliefs justify one another as a web rather than a chain. Fallibilists, following Popper, abandon the demand for certainty altogether: knowledge can be rational and well supported without being proven beyond all possible doubt.

Hans Albert used the trilemma within his programme of critical rationalism to argue that no ultimate justification (Letztbegründung) is possible, whether in science, ethics, or theology.
