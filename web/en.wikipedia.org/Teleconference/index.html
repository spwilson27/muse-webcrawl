Teleconference

A teleconference is a live exchange of information among several people located at a distance from one another, conducted through a telecommunications system. The term most commonly covers audio conferencing (conference calls), videoconferencing, and web-based meetings that add screen sharing, chat, and collaborative documents. Teleconferencing lets participants hold meetings, classes, and presentations without travelling to a common location.

History

Telephone conference calls became practical with multi-line switching in the mid-twentieth century, and the first large-scale service, AT&T's conference calling, spread through business from the 1960s. Video systems were demonstrated as early as AT&T's Picturephone at the 1964 New York World's Fair but remained costly and niche for decades. Dedicated videoconferencing systems from companies such as Polycom and Tandberg became common in corporate boardrooms in the 1990s, before software services — Skype, GoToMeeting, Webex, Zoom, and Microsoft Teams — brought teleconferencing to personal computers and phones over the internet.

Forms and technology

Modern systems use Voice over IP, codecs for compressing audio and video, echo cancellation, and cloud "bridges" that mix many participants. Features include dial-in numbers, scheduling integration, recording and transcription, virtual backgrounds, breakout rooms, and security controls. Teleconferencing expanded enormously during the COVID-19 pandemic, becoming the default medium for remote work, telemedicine, distance education, and court proceedings.

Advantages and drawbacks

Benefits include reduced travel cost and time and easier inclusion of remote participants. Drawbacks include audio delays, reduced non-verbal cues, technical failures, meeting fatigue ("Zoom fatigue"), and privacy and security concerns.
