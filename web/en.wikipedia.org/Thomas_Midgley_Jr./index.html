Thomas Midgley Jr.

Thomas Midgley Jr.
Born: 18 May 1889, Beaver Falls, Pennsylvania, U.S.
Died: 2 November 1944 (aged 55), Worthington, Ohio, U.S.
Known for: Leaded petrol (tetraethyllead); chlorofluorocarbons (Freon)
Fields: Mechanical engineering, chemistry

Thomas Midgley Jr. (18 May 1889 – 2 November 1944) was an American mechanical engineer and chemist. He developed both tetraethyllead (TEL), the lead additive to petrol that dramatically improved engine performance, and chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs), the first widely used refrigerants — two inventions later found to cause severe environmental and public-health harm. The historian J. R. McNeill once remarked that Midgley "had more adverse impact on the atmosphere than any other single organism in Earth's history."

Leaded petrol

Midgley graduated from Cornell University in mechanical engineering in 1911 and joined Charles F. Kettering's research operation, which became part of General Motors. Tasked with eliminating engine knock, Midgley systematically tested thousands of compounds and in December 1921 found that tetraethyllead was a highly effective anti-knock agent. Leaded petrol, marketed as "Ethyl", went on sale in 1923. Although the toxicity of lead was already well known — and Midgley himself suffered lead poisoning and took a leave of absence in 1923 — the additive was promoted for decades. Leaded petrol was eventually phased out worldwide, with the last country banning it in 2021, after studies linked it to widespread neurological damage, particularly in children.

Chlorofluorocarbons

In the late 1920s Midgley turned to refrigeration, seeking a non-toxic, non-flammable refrigerant to replace ammonia, methyl chloride, and sulfur dioxide. In 1928 he and his colleagues synthesised dichlorodifluoromethane, the first CFC, marketed by DuPont as Freon. At a 1930 demonstration, Midgley inhaled the gas and used it to blow out a candle to dramatise its safety. CFCs were adopted globally in refrigeration, air conditioning, and aerosol propellants. Only decades later was it discovered that they destroy stratospheric ozone; they were phased out under the Montreal Protocol of 1987.

Death

Stricken with poliomyelitis in 1940, Midgley devised a system of pulleys and ropes to lift himself from bed. On 2 November 1944 he became entangled in the ropes of this device and died of strangulation, aged 55. He held over 170 patents and received numerous honours during his lifetime, including the Perkin Medal and the presidency of the American Chemical Society.
