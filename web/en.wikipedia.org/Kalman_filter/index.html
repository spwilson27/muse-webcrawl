The Kalman filter is an algorithm that uses a series of measurements observed over time, which contain statistical noise and other inaccuracies, to produce estimates of unknown variables that tend to be more accurate than those based on a single measurement alone. Named after Rudolf E. Kálmán, who published the method in 1960, it is a cornerstone of modern control theory and signal processing.

Overview

The filter operates recursively in two steps. In the prediction step, it uses a model of the system's dynamics to project the current state estimate and its uncertainty forward in time. In the update step, it combines that prediction with a new measurement, weighting the two according to their respective uncertainties via the Kalman gain; measurements with less noise influence the estimate more. For linear systems with Gaussian noise, the result is the optimal (minimum mean-square error) estimator.

History

Similar recursive least-squares ideas go back to Gauss, and related methods were developed independently by Thorvald Thiele and Peter Swerling. Kálmán's formulation in terms of state-space models made the method suitable for digital computers. Stanley Schmidt at NASA Ames recognised its value, and Kalman filters guided the Apollo programme's navigation computers; the algorithm has since been used in spacecraft navigation, radar tracking, GPS receivers, weather forecasting, and financial time-series analysis.

Extensions

Real systems are often nonlinear. The extended Kalman filter (EKF) linearises the model about the current estimate, while the unscented Kalman filter (UKF) propagates a set of sigma points through the true nonlinearity and generally performs better. Related techniques include particle filters (sequential Monte Carlo), which represent the state distribution by samples and handle non-Gaussian cases.
