Edward Jenner (17 May 1749 – 26 January 1823) was an English physician and scientist who pioneered the smallpox vaccine, the world's first vaccine. He is often called "the father of immunology", and his work is said to have saved more lives than that of any other human.

The smallpox experiment

Jenner, a country doctor in Berkeley, Gloucestershire, noticed that milkmaids who had contracted cowpox, a mild disease, did not subsequently catch smallpox. On 14 May 1796 he inoculated an eight-year-old boy, James Phipps, with pus from a cowpox lesion on a milkmaid's hand; when Jenner later exposed Phipps to smallpox matter, the boy did not develop the disease. He published his findings in 1798 in An Inquiry into the Causes and Effects of the Variolae Vaccinae, coining the term "vaccination" from vacca, the Latin for cow.

Spread and legacy

Vaccination spread rapidly across Europe and beyond, replacing the riskier practice of variolation. Jenner received honours from Parliament and monarchs, and the procedure he devised became the model for immunology. Smallpox was eventually declared eradicated by the World Health Organization in 1980 — the first disease eliminated by human effort. Jenner was also a keen naturalist, publishing observations on the cuckoo that won him fellowship of the Royal Society.
