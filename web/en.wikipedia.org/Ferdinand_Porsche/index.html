Ferdinand Porsche

Ferdinand Porsche (3 September 1875 – 30 January 1951) was a Bohemian-born Austrian-German automotive engineer and the founder of the Porsche engineering consultancy and, with his family, the Porsche automobile company. He designed the Lohner–Porsche Mixte Hybrid, among the first hybrid vehicles; the Mercedes-Benz SSK; the Auto Union Grand Prix racing cars; and the Volkswagen Beetle, one of the most successful car designs of all time. During the Second World War his firm designed German armoured vehicles including the Tiger tank prototypes and the Elefant tank destroyer.

Early career

Porsche was born in Maffersdorf (now Vratislavice nad Nisou, Czech Republic) into a German-speaking family. Largely self-taught in electrical engineering, he joined the Vienna carriage firm of Jacob Lohner in 1898 and designed the Lohner–Porsche, an electric car with wheel-hub motors, followed by a petrol-electric hybrid version. He later worked for Austro-Daimler, designing the successful Prinz Heinrich model and, during the First World War, aircraft engines and heavy artillery tractors, and then for Daimler in Stuttgart, where his supercharged Mercedes-Benz S, SS and SSK sports cars dominated racing.

Consultancy and the Volkswagen

In 1931 Porsche founded his own design office in Stuttgart. For Auto Union he created the revolutionary mid-engined Grand Prix cars that battled Mercedes-Benz in the 1930s. In 1934 Adolf Hitler commissioned him to design a "people's car" (Volkswagen); the resulting KdF-Wagen, developed with his team including Erwin Komenda, became after the war the Volkswagen Type 1 (Beetle), whose rear-mounted air-cooled flat-four layout — refined by Porsche's office into the Porsche 356 and 911 — defined his company's cars for generations.

War and imprisonment

During the Second World War, Porsche's firm designed the VK 4501 (P) Tiger prototype (whose chassis became the Elefant/Ferdinand tank destroyer), the super-heavy Maus tank, and the Schwimmwagen and Kübelwagen for the Wehrmacht, using forced labour at the Wolfsburg plant. After the war he was arrested by the French and imprisoned for about 20 months in connection with war-crimes allegations and a disputed industrial mission, before being released without trial in 1947. His health broken, he died in Stuttgart in 1951, having seen the first Porsche 356 sports cars built by his son Ferry under the family name.
