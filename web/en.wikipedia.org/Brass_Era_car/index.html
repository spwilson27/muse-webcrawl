The Brass Era is a period in early automotive history, roughly from 1905 to 1915, named for the extensive use of polished brass on radiators, lamps, horns, and fittings. It follows the Veteran Era and precedes the Vintage Era in the periodization used by antique-car clubs such as the Horseless Carriage Club of America.

Characteristics

Brass Era cars moved decisively away from the "horseless carriage" look toward recognizable automobile form: front-mounted engines under a bonnet, radiators at the front, and steering wheels replacing tillers. Lighting was by acetylene or oil lamps with brass housings, and starting was by hand crank until Cadillac introduced the electric self-starter in 1912. Construction remained craft-based, with wooden bodywork over ash frames, though mass production was emerging — the Ford Model T, introduced in 1908, spans the era.

Significance

During these years the automobile became faster, more reliable, and socially established: road infrastructure, motor clubs, and events such as the Glidden Tours flourished. Brass Era machines, from the Ford Model T to luxury marques like Pierce-Arrow and Rolls-Royce's Silver Ghost, are now prized collector cars, and their gleaming brasswork remains the era's visual signature.
