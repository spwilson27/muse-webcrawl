Petroleum reservoir

Rock typesSandstone, limestone, dolomite
Key propertiesPorosity, permeability
ContentsOil, natural gas, formation water

A petroleum reservoir or oil and gas reservoir is a subsurface accumulation of hydrocarbons contained in porous or fractured rock formations, sealed by an impermeable cap rock that traps the buoyant oil and gas. The hydrocarbons, formed from buried organic matter in a source rock, migrate upward until they are held in a structural or stratigraphic trap.

An anticlinal trap: gas caps the oil, which sits above formation water

Reservoir quality depends chiefly on porosity — the fraction of the rock volume available to fluids — and permeability, the ease with which fluids flow through connected pore spaces. Common reservoir rocks are sandstones and carbonate rocks such as limestone and dolomite. Within a typical reservoir the fluids segregate by density: gas at the top forming a gas cap, oil below, and saline formation water beneath the oil–water contact.

Classification and recovery

Reservoirs are classified as conventional or unconventional. Conventional reservoirs produce through natural drive mechanisms — water drive, gas-cap drive and solution-gas drive — supplemented by waterflooding and other enhanced oil recovery methods. Unconventional reservoirs such as tight formations and oil shales require hydraulic fracturing or horizontal wells. Reservoir engineering estimates the original hydrocarbons in place and the recoverable reserves, which for conventional oil fields typically range from about 20 to 50 percent of the oil in place.
