Signal transduction

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Signal transduction is the process by which a chemical or physical signal is transmitted through a cell as a series of molecular events. Proteins responsible for detecting stimuli are generally termed receptors, although in some cases the term sensor is used. A signal molecule (ligand) binds to a receptor, which changes shape or activity, generating intracellular signals — often involving second messengers such as cyclic AMP — that are amplified through cascades of enzymes, ultimately altering gene expression, metabolism, or cell behaviour.

Mechanism

In a typical pathway, an extracellular signal — a hormone, neurotransmitter, growth factor, or photon — binds a receptor in the plasma membrane or inside the cell. Receptor types include G protein-coupled receptors, receptor tyrosine kinases, ion channels, and nuclear receptors. Activation triggers cascades of phosphorylation and other modifications that amplify and distribute the signal, often modulated by feedback loops, scaffolding proteins, and cross-talk between pathways.

Biological roles and disease

Signal transduction underlies vision, smell, immunity, hormone action, development, and learning. Its disruption causes many diseases: constitutively active receptors and kinases drive cancers (and are targets of drugs such as imatinib), while defective insulin signalling contributes to type 2 diabetes. The field's history includes Earl Sutherland's discovery of cyclic AMP and the award of multiple Nobel Prizes for work on G proteins and phosphorylation.
