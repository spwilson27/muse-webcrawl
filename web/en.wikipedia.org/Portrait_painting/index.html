Portrait painting

Portrait painting is a genre in painting, where the intent is to depict a human subject. The term "portrait painting" also describes the actual painted portrait. Portraitists may create their work by commission, for public and private persons, or they may be inspired by admiration or affection for the subject. Portraits often serve as important state and family records, as well as remembrances.

History

Some of the earliest surviving portraits are the Fayum mummy portraits from Roman Egypt. Portraiture flourished in ancient Greek and Roman sculpture and coinage. In the European tradition, portrait painting was revitalized in the Renaissance, with artists such as Jan van Eyck, Leonardo da Vinci and Raphael producing psychologically rich depictions of their sitters.

Technique and practice

Traditionally, a portrait painting depicts the sitter in a pose that suggests their status, personality or occupation, using costume, setting and accessories as visual cues. Portraits may be bust-length, half-length, three-quarter or full-length, and may show one person or groups, as in the Dutch group portraits of Frans Hals and Rembrandt.

Decline and revival

The invention of photography in the 19th century reduced demand for painted likenesses, but portraiture continued through artists such as John Singer Sargent, and modern and contemporary painters from Picasso to Lucian Freud and Kehinde Wiley have kept the genre central to art.
