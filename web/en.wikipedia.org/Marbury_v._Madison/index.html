Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. (1 Cranch) 137 (1803), was a landmark decision of the U.S. Supreme Court that established the principle of judicial review in the United States, meaning that American courts have the power to strike down laws and statutes they find to violate the Constitution. Decided in 1803, Marbury is regarded as the single most important decision in American constitutional law.

Chief Justice John Marshall wrote the unanimous opinion.

Background

In the final days of his presidency, John Adams appointed dozens of Federalist "midnight judges". Secretary of State John Marshall failed to deliver some of the commissions before Thomas Jefferson took office, and Jefferson's secretary, James Madison, refused to deliver them. One appointee, William Marbury, petitioned the Supreme Court under Section 13 of the Judiciary Act of 1789 for a writ of mandamus ordering delivery.

Decision

Chief Justice Marshall held that Marbury was entitled to his commission and that the law provided a remedy, but that Section 13's grant of original jurisdiction to issue mandamus conflicted with Article III of the Constitution. Because a law repugnant to the Constitution is void, and "it is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is," the Court must disregard the statute. In denying itself the jurisdiction Congress had granted, the Court simultaneously claimed the far greater power to invalidate acts of Congress.

Significance

The decision entrenched judicial review as a defining feature of the American system and a model studied worldwide, though the Court did not strike down another federal statute until Dred Scott in 1857.
