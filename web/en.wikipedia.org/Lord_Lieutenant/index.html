A lord-lieutenant (or lord lieutenant) is the British monarch's personal representative in each lieutenancy area of the United Kingdom. In England the areas correspond broadly to the ceremonial counties; there are also lord-lieutenants in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland, while Ireland retained the office of Lord Lieutenant of Ireland as viceroy until 1922.

History

The office originated in the reign of Henry VIII, when lieutenants were commissioned to take charge of the local militia in place of the sheriffs. By the seventeenth century the lord-lieutenant was the principal figure in county government, responsible for raising and commanding the militia and for recommending magistrates. Military duties were gradually removed in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries.

Modern role

Today the role is largely ceremonial and non-political: lord-lieutenants arrange and accompany royal visits, present honours and awards on the monarch's behalf, support the armed forces and volunteer organisations in the county, and act as a link between the county and the Crown. They are assisted by a vice lord-lieutenant and deputy lieutenants, and retire at 75.
