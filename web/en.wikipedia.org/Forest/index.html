Major typesTropical rainforest, temperate forest, boreal forest (taiga)

A forest is an ecosystem characterized by dense community of trees. Hundreds of definitions of forest are used throughout the world, incorporating factors such as tree density, tree height, land use, legal standing, and ecological function. The United Nations' Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) defines a forest as land spanning more than 0.5 hectares with trees higher than 5 meters and a canopy cover of more than 10 percent.

Classification

Forests are classified by latitude and climate. Tropical forests near the equator hold the greatest biodiversity. Temperate forests of the mid-latitudes include deciduous and evergreen types. Boreal forests (taiga) of conifers ring the subarctic and store enormous quantities of carbon in their soils. Forests are further described by canopy structure — emergent layer, canopy, understory and forest floor — and by whether they are primary (old-growth) or secondary.

Ecological importance

Forests are the predominant terrestrial ecosystem of Earth and are the habitat of the majority of the world's terrestrial species. They regulate the water cycle, prevent soil erosion, produce oxygen and sequester carbon; their destruction releases stored carbon as fossil fuels do when burned. Forest ecosystems form the land-based half of countless food webs.

Human use and threats

People depend on forests for timber, fuel, food, medicine and recreation. Deforestation — driven mainly by agriculture, logging and mining — removed roughly 10 million hectares per year in recent decades, though rates have slowed in some regions. Climate change, wildfire and disease pose growing threats, while reforestation and afforestation are used to restore cover.
