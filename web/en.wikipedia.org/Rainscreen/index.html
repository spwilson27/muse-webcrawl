Rainscreen
Exterior wall detail: outer cladding layer, air gap, and water-resistant inner wall
Manages wind-driven rain and moisture

A rainscreen is an exterior wall detail in which the siding (the "rainscreen") stands off from the moisture-resistant surface of an air and water barrier applied to the sheathing to create a capillary break and a channel for drainage and evaporative drying. The rainscreen is the first interruption between conditions that might allow water to reach the interior of the wall assembly and the material that actually prevents water penetration.

How it works

Wind-driven rain that passes the open joints of the cladding enters a ventilated cavity; because the air in the cavity is pressure-equalized with the exterior, the driving force pushing water inward is greatly reduced, and any moisture drains down and out at flashings or dries by air movement. The inner leaf carries a continuous drainage plane or weather-resistant barrier.

Development

The principle emerged from Scandinavian research in the 1940s–1960s and building failures studied in Norway and Canada; "pressure-equalized rainscreen" design was formalized in building-science literature in the 1960s–1980s. Modern rainscreen cladding uses panels of metal, fibre cement, terracotta, stone or high-pressure laminate on rail systems, and systems must also manage fire spread within the cavity — a major regulatory focus after cladding-related fires.
