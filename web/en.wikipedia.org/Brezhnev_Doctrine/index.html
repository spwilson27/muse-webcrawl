The Brezhnev Doctrine was a Soviet foreign policy first and most clearly articulated by S. Kovalyov in a 26 September 1968 article in Pravda entitled "Sovereignty and the International Obligations of Socialist Countries." Leonid Brezhnev reiterated it in a speech at the Fifth Congress of the Polish United Workers' Party on 13 November 1968, which stated:

"When forces that are hostile to socialism try to turn the development of some socialist country towards capitalism, it becomes not only a problem of the country concerned, but a common problem and concern of all socialist countries."

The doctrine justified the Warsaw Pact invasion of Czechoslovakia in August 1968, which crushed the reformist Prague Spring led by Alexander Dubček. It asserted that the Soviet Union had the right to intervene in any socialist state whose government deviated from orthodox Marxism-Leninism, on the grounds that the gains of socialism were irreversible and that no country could leave the socialist commonwealth.

Implications

The doctrine effectively limited the sovereignty of the Soviet Union's satellite states in Eastern Europe. It was invoked as a threat during subsequent crises, including the rise of the Solidarity movement in Poland in 1980–81, although the USSR ultimately chose not to invade directly and martial law was imposed by the Polish government itself. The doctrine also underpinned Soviet reasoning during the invasion of Afghanistan in 1979, though Afghanistan was not a Warsaw Pact member.

Abandonment

Under Mikhail Gorbachev, the doctrine was repudiated. In 1989 Gorbachev's spokesman Gennadi Gerasimov jokingly dubbed the new policy of non-intervention the "Sinatra Doctrine," after the song "My Way," signalling that Eastern European states were free to go their own way. The repudiation of the Brezhnev Doctrine removed the threat of Soviet military intervention and enabled the peaceful revolutions of 1989 and the collapse of communist governments across Eastern Europe.
