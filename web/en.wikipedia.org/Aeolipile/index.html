An aeolipile, also known as a Hero's (or Heron's) engine, is a simple, bladeless radial steam turbine which spins when the central water container is heated. Steam jets exhausted from nozzles arranged tangentially on the container make it spin by reaction torque, in accordance with Newton's third law. It is generally regarded as the first recorded steam engine or reaction steam turbine, described by Hero of Alexandria in 1st-century Roman Egypt in his treatise Pneumatica.

In Hero's design, a closed cauldron of water sits over a fire; steam rises through a pair of pipes that also serve as the axle for a hollow sphere. Two bent nozzles on opposite sides of the sphere discharge the steam, and the reaction thrust spins the sphere at considerable speed. The device demonstrates the power of steam but appears to have been treated in antiquity as a philosophical curiosity or temple wonder — part of a repertoire of pneumatic automata — rather than as a practical prime mover; it has very low efficiency and no mechanism for doing useful work.

History and significance

The name derives from Aeolus, the Greek god of the winds. Vitruvius, writing a century earlier, described hollow bronze spheres filled with water that produced steam when heated, used to demonstrate properties of the weather. Later writers and engineers repeatedly cited the aeolipile as evidence that the principle of the steam turbine was known in antiquity, contrasting it with the working steam engines of the 17th–18th centuries, beginning with those of Savery and Newcomen.
