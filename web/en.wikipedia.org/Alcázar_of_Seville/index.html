Alcázar of Seville

The Alcázar of Seville (Spanish: Reales Alcázares de Sevilla) is a royal palace in Seville, Spain, originally developed by Muslim rulers and renowned as one of the most outstanding examples of Mudéjar architecture. It is the oldest royal palace still in use in Europe; the upper storeys remain a residence of the Spanish royal family when in Seville.

History

The site began as a fortified residence for the Muslim rulers of Seville from the 10th century, expanded under the Abbadid and Almohad dynasties. After Ferdinand III conquered Seville in 1248, the palace passed to the Christian kings of Castile. In the 1360s Pedro I ("the Cruel") rebuilt the palace with the help of Muslim craftsmen from Granada and Toledo, creating its famous Mudéjar halls, including the Hall of Ambassadors with its gilded cedar dome and the Courtyard of the Maidens. Later monarchs added Gothic, Renaissance, and Baroque elements, and extensive gardens.

Architecture and gardens

The palace complex is celebrated for its intricate tilework (azulejos), carved stucco, horseshoe arches, and courtyards, surrounded by gardens laid out over several centuries. Together with Seville Cathedral and the Archivo de Indias, it was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 1987.
