Solid-state drive

A solid-state drive (SSD) is a data-storage device that stores persistent data on integrated-circuit assemblies, typically NAND flash memory, with no moving parts. SSDs replaced hard disk drives (HDDs) in most computers during the 2010s because of their far higher speed, lower latency, shock resistance, and silent operation.

Architecture

An SSD consists of flash-memory chips and a controller that manages wear levelling, garbage collection, error correction, and the TRIM command, mapping logical blocks to physical pages. Flash cells may be single-level (SLC) or multi-level (MLC, TLC, QLC), trading endurance and speed for density.

Interfaces and form factors

Early SSDs used SATA in 2.5-inch housings shaped like HDDs. Modern drives use the NVMe protocol over PCIe in the M.2 form factor, reaching several gigabytes per second, or U.2 and EDSFF formats in servers.

Comparison with HDDs

SSDs offer much faster random access but historically higher cost per gigabyte and finite write endurance; HDDs remain in use for bulk archival storage.
