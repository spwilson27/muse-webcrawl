Singular value decomposition

Geometrically, the SVD expresses a linear map as a rotation, a scaling along orthogonal axes, and another rotation.

In linear algebra, the singular value decomposition (SVD) is a factorization of a real or complex matrix. It generalises the eigendecomposition of a square normal matrix with an orthonormal eigenbasis to any matrix. Specifically, an m × n matrix M can be written as M = UΣV*, where U is an m × m unitary matrix, Σ is a rectangular diagonal matrix with non-negative real numbers (the singular values) on the diagonal, and V is an n × n unitary matrix whose conjugate transpose is V*.

Properties

The diagonal entries of Σ are the singular values of M, conventionally ordered in decreasing order. The columns of U and V are the left and right singular vectors. The rank of M equals the number of non-zero singular values; the product of the singular values equals the absolute value of the determinant for a square matrix; and the two-norm of M equals its largest singular value, while the Frobenius norm is the square root of the sum of squared singular values.

Applications

The SVD underlies the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse, least-squares fitting, and the solution of homogeneous linear systems via the null space. Truncating the decomposition to the largest singular values gives the best low-rank approximation to a matrix (the Eckart–Young theorem), a fact exploited in image compression, signal processing, latent semantic analysis of text, recommender systems and principal component analysis, where the SVD of the centred data matrix yields the principal components.

History

The decomposition was developed independently by Eugenio Beltrami (1873), Camille Jordan (1874), James Joseph Sylvester, Erhard Schmidt and Hermann Weyl, among others; efficient numerical algorithms, notably those of Gene Golub and co-workers in the 1960s, made it a cornerstone of computational linear algebra.
