Atomic force microscopy

Atomic force microscopy (AFM) is a type of scanning probe microscopy with demonstrated resolution on the order of fractions of a nanometre — more than 1,000 times better than the optical diffraction limit. The AFM was invented in 1986 by Gerd Binnig, Calvin Quate and Christoph Gerber as an offspring of the scanning tunnelling microscope. It gathers information by "feeling" a surface with a mechanical probe: a sharp tip (a few micrometres long, with an end radius of a few nanometres) mounted on a flexible cantilever raster-scans the specimen, and the deflection of the cantilever, measured usually by a laser beam reflected onto a photodiode array, is converted into a topographic map of the surface.

Operating modes

In contact mode, the tip is dragged across the surface while a feedback loop holds the cantilever deflection constant; it gives high resolution but can damage soft samples. In the widely used tapping mode (intermittent-contact or amplitude-modulation mode), the cantilever is oscillated near its resonance frequency and only touches the surface briefly each cycle, greatly reducing lateral forces and enabling routine imaging of polymers and biomolecules. Non-contact mode, usually performed in vacuum with frequency modulation, senses attractive force gradients above the surface and can achieve true atomic resolution, resolving individual atoms and bonds. Piezoelectric scanners position the sample or tip with sub-angstrom precision.

Applications

Unlike electron microscopes, AFM works in air and in liquids and requires little or no sample preparation, making it valuable in materials science (surface roughness, thin films, semiconductor metrology), chemistry, and biology — imaging DNA, proteins, membranes and living cells, and measuring single-molecule forces, stiffness and adhesion. Force spectroscopy with a functionalised tip can probe molecular recognition and the unfolding of individual proteins. Limitations include a small scan area (typically under ~100 µm), slow scanning speed, and image artefacts arising from finite tip size ("tip convolution"). High-speed AFM, developed especially by Toshio Ando's group, can record movies of dynamic molecular processes at several frames per second.
