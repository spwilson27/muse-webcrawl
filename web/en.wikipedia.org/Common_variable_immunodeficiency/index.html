Common variable immunodeficiency

Common variable immunodeficiency (CVID) is an immune disorder characterised by low levels of serum immunoglobulins (antibodies) and an increased susceptibility to infection. It is one of the most common primary immunodeficiencies, with an estimated prevalence of about 1 in 25,000, affecting males and females equally. Symptoms usually appear in the teens to thirties, although the onset can be earlier or later, and diagnosis is often delayed for years.

Signs and symptoms

Patients typically suffer recurrent bacterial infections of the respiratory tract, such as sinusitis, bronchitis, and pneumonia, often with encapsulated organisms. Many develop non-infectious complications, including autoimmune diseases, granulomatous inflammation (especially of the lungs), gastrointestinal disorders with malabsorption, splenomegaly, and an increased risk of lymphoma and other malignancies. Diagnosis requires markedly reduced IgG together with low IgA and/or IgM and a poor antibody response to vaccines, after excluding other causes of hypogammaglobulinaemia.

Cause and treatment

Most cases are sporadic; in a minority, genetic variants in genes such as TNFRSF13B (TACI), ICOS, CD19, and CTLA4 have been identified. There is no cure; the mainstay of treatment is immunoglobulin replacement therapy, given intravenously or subcutaneously, plus antibiotics for infections and management of autoimmune and inflammatory complications.
