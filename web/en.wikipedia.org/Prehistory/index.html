Prehistory

Prehistory is the period of human history before the existence of written records. It spans from the first use of stone tools by hominins roughly 3.3 million years ago to the invention of writing systems some 5,000 years ago — meaning the overwhelming majority of the human past is prehistoric. Because no contemporary documents exist, prehistory is studied through archaeology, paleoanthropology, genetics, and related sciences.

Terminology

The term was popularized in English by Daniel Wilson (1851) and John Lubbock's Pre-historic Times (1865). The boundary between prehistory and history is gradual and differs by region: writing appeared around 3400–3200 BC in Mesopotamia and Egypt, later in China and Mesoamerica, and in some regions only with outside contact in recent centuries. The transitional phase in which a society is described in others' writings but has none of its own is sometimes called protohistory.

The three-age system

Danish antiquarian Christian Jürgensen Thomsen (1836) ordered prehistory into the Stone Age, Bronze Age, and Iron Age, according to the dominant material of cutting tools. The Stone Age is subdivided into the Paleolithic (Old Stone Age), Mesolithic (Middle), and Neolithic (New). The scheme works best for Europe and the Near East and is applied cautiously elsewhere.

Human evolution

Prehistory encompasses the evolution of the genus Homo in Africa, the mastery of fire, the spread of hominins across Eurasia, and the emergence of anatomically modern humans (Homo sapiens) by about 300,000 years ago (Jebel Irhoud, Morocco). Behavioral modernity — art, ornament, elaborate burial, and complex tools — is richly documented from the Upper Paleolithic onward, including cave art at Chauvet, Lascaux, and Sulawesi.

The Neolithic transition

After the last Ice Age, beginning around 10,000 BC in the Fertile Crescent, some societies independently invented agriculture and animal domestication — in the Near East, China, Mesoamerica, the Andes, New Guinea, and elsewhere. Farming enabled permanent settlements, population growth, pottery, monumental building (as at Göbekli Tepe and Çatalhöyük), social hierarchy, and eventually cities and states.

End of prehistory

Prehistory ends locally when writing arrives: with cuneiform and hieroglyphs in the late 4th millennium BC, the Indus script around 2600 BC (still undeciphered), Chinese oracle bones by c. 1250 BC, and Mesoamerican scripts in the 1st millennium BC. The discipline studying prehistory draws on excavation, radiocarbon dating, ancient DNA, and paleoclimatology to reconstruct lives that left no texts.
