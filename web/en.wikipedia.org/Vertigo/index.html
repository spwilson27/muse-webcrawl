Vertigo

Vertigo is a symptom, rather than a condition itself, in which a person feels as though they or the objects around them are moving, spinning, or swaying when they are not. It is a specific type of dizziness that reflects dysfunction of the vestibular system of the inner ear or, less commonly, of the brain. Vertigo is often accompanied by nausea, vomiting, sweating, and difficulty walking.

Causes

Causes are divided into peripheral (inner ear) and central (brainstem or cerebellum). The most common peripheral cause is benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV), in which displaced calcium crystals (otoconia) in the semicircular canals trigger brief attacks of spinning when the head moves in certain ways. Other peripheral causes include Ménière's disease (episodes with tinnitus, aural fullness, and hearing loss), vestibular neuritis, and labyrinthitis. Central causes include stroke, multiple sclerosis, vestibular migraine, and tumours of the cerebellopontine angle; these are suggested by additional neurological signs.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis rests on the history — the duration and triggers of episodes are particularly informative — and examination including the Dix–Hallpike manoeuvre for BPPV and observation of nystagmus. Treatment depends on the cause: BPPV is treated with repositioning manoeuvres such as the Epley manoeuvre, which usually resolve it rapidly; Ménière's disease is managed with lifestyle measures and medication; and vestibular suppressant drugs such as meclizine are used for short-term relief of acute attacks. Vestibular rehabilitation exercises help many chronic cases.
