Codification (law)

In law, codification is the process of collecting and restating the law of a jurisdiction in certain areas, usually by subject, forming a legal code, i.e. a codex (book) of law. Codification is one of the defining features of civil law jurisdictions, in contrast with common law, under which judge-made law and precedent play a larger role. Codified law may be comprehensively enacted by a legislature, rather than being built up from case law over time.

History

Ancient examples of codified law include the Code of Ur-Nammu, the Code of Hammurabi in Babylon, and the Roman Twelve Tables. The most influential modern codifications emerged from Roman law scholarship and Enlightenment rationalism. The French Civil Code of 1804, known as the Napoleonic Code, synthesized Roman law, customary law, and revolutionary principles and was imposed or imitated across much of Europe, Latin America, and beyond. Germany's Bürgerliches Gesetzbuch (BGB), which took effect in 1900, is another highly influential civil code.

Types of codification

Codification may simply compile existing statutes and case law into an organized code without changing their substance, or it may reform the law, resolving contradictions and modernizing rules. In the United States, the United States Code collects federal statutes by subject; model laws such as the Uniform Commercial Code codify commercial law across states. In England and Wales, codification has historically been resisted, though consolidation statutes gather existing law, and criminal law commissions have repeatedly proposed a full criminal code.

Criticism

Critics argue that codes can be rigid and slow to adapt, and that attempts at exhaustive codification inevitably leave gaps that courts must fill. Supporters respond that codification makes law accessible, certain, and democratically enacted rather than judge-made.
