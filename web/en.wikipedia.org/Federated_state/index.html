Federated state

Federations of the world: each is composed of federated states with constitutionally protected self-government.

A federated state (also called a state, province, region, canton, land, governorate, or oblast) is a territorial and constitutional community forming part of a federation. A federated state holds administrative jurisdiction over a defined geographic territory and is a form of regional government; crucially, its powers and existence are protected by the federation's constitution and cannot be abolished unilaterally by the central government, which distinguishes it from the provinces or regions of a unitary state.

Characteristics

In a federation, sovereignty is constitutionally divided between the federal government and the federated states. Each level of government has its own legislature, executive, and (usually) judiciary, and each acts directly on the citizens rather than one level ruling through the other. Federated states typically control such matters as education, police, local infrastructure, and civil law, while the federation handles defence, foreign policy, and currency — though the exact division varies widely.

Examples

The oldest modern federation, the United States, comprises 50 states, of which California and Texas are the most populous. Germany is divided into 16 Länder, India into 28 states and 8 union territories, Brazil into 26 states and a federal district, Canada into 10 provinces (alongside 3 territories that lack the same constitutional status), Australia into 6 states, and Switzerland into 26 cantons. Some federations are highly asymmetric: in Canada and India, for instance, certain states hold special arrangements not shared by others.

Status in international law

Federated states are generally not sovereign in international law and cannot conduct their own foreign relations, although some — such as the German Länder and Swiss cantons — may conclude limited agreements with foreign states in areas of their competence, and a few, historically, have even held seats in international bodies (the Ukrainian and Byelorussian SSRs were founding members of the United Nations as constituent republics of the Soviet Union).
