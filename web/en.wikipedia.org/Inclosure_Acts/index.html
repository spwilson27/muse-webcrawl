Inclosure Acts

The Inclosure Acts (also spelled Enclosure Acts) were a series of Acts of the Parliament of the United Kingdom, passed mainly between 1750 and 1850, that enclosed open fields and common land in England, converting them into privately owned, hedged or fenced parcels. More than 5,000 individual acts enclosed roughly 6.8 million acres — about a fifth of the area of England.

Background

Under the open-field system, villagers farmed scattered strips in large common fields and enjoyed rights of common grazing and gathering on waste land. Enclosure by agreement had proceeded for centuries, but the parliamentary acts provided a compulsory legal mechanism: on petition of a majority of landowners (by value), commissioners surveyed the parish, extinguished common rights, and reallotted the land in compact blocks.

General acts

To streamline the process Parliament passed general Inclosure Acts in 1801, 1836 and 1845, the last establishing permanent Inclosure Commissioners and standard procedures.

Social effects

Enclosure raised agricultural productivity and consolidated farms, but it also deprived many cottagers and smallholders of common rights that supplemented their livelihoods, contributing to rural depopulation and migration to industrial towns. Historians continue to debate its balance of economic gains against social costs.
