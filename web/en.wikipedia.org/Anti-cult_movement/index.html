Anti-cult movement

The movement emerged largely through parents' groups in the 1970s

The anti-cult movement (ACM) is a loosely organised collection of individuals and groups — often families of members, former members, and concerned professionals — that oppose some new religious movements (NRMs) which they characterise as "cults". The movement emerged in the early 1970s in the United States, partly in response to high-profile groups such as the Children of God, the Unification Church, and the People's Temple.

History and activities

Early organisations included FREECOG and later the Cult Awareness Network (CAN) and the American Family Foundation. The ACM promoted the theory that cults recruit through "brainwashing" or coercive persuasion, and some adherents supported "deprogramming" — forcibly removing members and attempting to reverse their beliefs — a practice that led to kidnapping convictions and civil suits. After litigation bankrupted CAN in 1996, its name and assets were purchased by a Scientology-affiliated group.

Debates

Scholars of religion have often criticised the ACM's brainwashing model as unscientific and its framing as a threat to religious freedom, giving rise to a contrasting "cult information" and religious-studies perspective. Anti-cult activists counter that some groups genuinely engage in abuse, fraud and exploitation, pointing to tragedies such as Jonestown (1978) and the Aum Shinrikyo sarin attack (1995). Government responses have varied: France established agencies such as MIVILUDES, while other states favour monitoring only illegal conduct.
