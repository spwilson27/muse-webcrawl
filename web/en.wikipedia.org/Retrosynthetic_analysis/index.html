Retrosynthetic analysis is a technique for planning the synthesis of complex organic molecules. The chemist works backwards from the target molecule, repeatedly breaking bonds (in concept) to generate progressively simpler precursor structures until commercially available starting materials are reached.

Concepts

The method was systematised by E. J. Corey, who introduced its vocabulary: a disconnection is an imaginary bond cleavage corresponding to the reverse of a real reaction; the fragments it generates are synthons, idealised species that are then matched to real synthetic equivalents (actual reagents). A retron is the structural pattern that signals a particular transform is applicable, and a functional group interconversion changes one group into another without altering the carbon skeleton. Retrosynthetic routes are drawn as trees, evaluated for step count, yield, selectivity, cost, safety, and availability of starting materials.

Strategy

Good disconnections simplify the target maximally, cut at strategic bonds (often in the middle of the molecule or at ring junctions), exploit symmetry, and rely on reliable, high-yielding reactions. Convergent routes, in which fragments are built independently and joined late, are generally favoured over long linear sequences. Corey's own syntheses, and total syntheses of molecules such as prostaglandins, ginkgolide B, and (by the wider community) taxol and strychnine, demonstrated the power of the approach.

Computer-assisted synthesis planning

Corey's group built LHASA, one of the first programs for computer-assisted synthesis design. Modern tools apply reaction databases and machine learning to propose and rank retrosynthetic routes, reviving the field as automated synthesis planning.
