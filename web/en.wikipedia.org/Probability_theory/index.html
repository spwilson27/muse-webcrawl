Probability theory

Probability theory is the branch of mathematics concerned with probability — the analysis of random phenomena. Its central objects are random variables, stochastic processes, and events: mathematical abstractions of non-deterministic events or measured quantities.

History

The theory began in the 16th and 17th centuries with Gerolamo Cardano and the correspondence of Blaise Pascal and Pierre de Fermat in 1654 on games of chance. Andrey Kolmogorov's 1933 axiomatization, Grundbegriffe der Wahrscheinlichkeitsrechnung, placed probability on a rigorous measure-theoretic foundation.

Foundations

A probability space is a triple (Ω, F, P): a sample space Ω, a σ-algebra F of events, and a probability measure P assigning each event a number in [0,1] with P(Ω)=1. The axioms of Kolmogorov govern all subsequent theory.

Random variables and distributions

A random variable is a measurable function from Ω to the real numbers (or other spaces). Its distribution is described by a cumulative distribution function, and for discrete variables by a probability mass function, for continuous variables by a probability density function. Expectation, variance, and higher moments summarize distributions.

Key results

The law of large numbers, central limit theorem, Bayes' theorem, conditional probability, and martingale theory are cornerstones. Applications span statistics, finance, physics, computer science, and philosophy.
