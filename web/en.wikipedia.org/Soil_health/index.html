Soil health

Healthy, biologically active soil

Soil health is the continued capacity of soil to function as a vital living ecosystem that sustains plants, animals, and humans. The concept emerged in the late twentieth century as soil scientists broadened the older notion of "soil quality" to emphasise the soil's biological dimension — its living community of bacteria, fungi, earthworms, and other organisms — alongside its physical and chemical properties.

Healthy soil performs five essential functions: regulating water, sustaining plant and animal life, filtering and buffering potential pollutants, cycling nutrients, and providing physical stability and support for roots and structures.

Indicators

Soil health is assessed through a set of measurable indicators. Physical indicators include texture, structure, aggregate stability, and water infiltration rate. Chemical indicators include pH, salinity, cation exchange capacity, and nutrient levels. Biological indicators include soil organic matter, microbial biomass, respiration rate, earthworm populations, and potentially mineralisable nitrogen. Frameworks such as the Comprehensive Assessment of Soil Health (CASH), developed at Cornell University, combine these measures into a single score.

Principles of management

Five widely promoted principles guide soil-health management: keep the soil covered, minimise disturbance (such as tillage), keep living roots in the ground as much as possible, diversify plantings and livestock, and integrate livestock grazing where appropriate. Practices implementing these principles include cover crops, crop rotation, reduced tillage, compost application, and managed grazing. Proponents argue they improve water retention, reduce erosion, and can sequester atmospheric carbon in soil organic matter.
