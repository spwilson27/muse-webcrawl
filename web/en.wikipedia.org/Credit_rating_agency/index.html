A credit rating agency (CRA) is a company that assigns credit ratings, which rate a debtor's ability to pay back debt by making timely principal and interest payments and the likelihood of default. An agency may rate the creditworthiness of issuers of debt obligations, of the debt instruments themselves, and in some cases of the servicers of the underlying debt. Debts rated include government bonds, corporate bonds, certificates of deposit, municipal bonds, preferred stock, and collateralised securities such as mortgage-backed securities and collateralised debt obligations.

The Big Three

The industry is dominated by three firms — Moody's, S&P Global Ratings, and Fitch Ratings — which together account for roughly 95% of the global market. All three are designated Nationally Recognized Statistical Rating Organizations (NRSROs) by the US Securities and Exchange Commission, and their ratings are embedded in financial regulation, investment mandates, and capital requirements worldwide.

Business model

Historically, agencies charged investors for subscription access to ratings. From the 1970s, the industry shifted to the "issuer pays" model, in which the entity issuing the debt pays for its rating. Critics argue this creates conflicts of interest, since agencies compete for issuers' business and may hesitate to downgrade paying clients; agencies respond that reputational risk disciplines their accuracy and that the model funds broader coverage.

Role in the 2008 financial crisis

Credit rating agencies were widely criticised for assigning high investment-grade ratings to structured finance products backed by subprime mortgages that later defaulted in large numbers. The US Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission called the agencies "key enablers" of the crisis. In 2015, S&P agreed to pay about US$1.5 billion to settle US government claims over its pre-crisis ratings; Moody's settled similar claims for about US$864 million in 2017. The Dodd–Frank Act of 2010 created an SEC Office of Credit Ratings and removed some statutory references to ratings in US regulation.

Sovereign ratings

Agencies also rate sovereign states; downgrades can raise a government's borrowing costs and trigger political controversy, as occurred during the eurozone debt crisis and when S&P downgraded the United States from AAA in 2011 — the first such downgrade in modern history.
