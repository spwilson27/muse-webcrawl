Pathology is the study of disease — its causes (etiology), mechanisms of development (pathogenesis), structural changes in cells and organs, and functional consequences. The word derives from the Greek pathos ("suffering, disease") and logos ("study"). As a medical specialty, pathology underpins diagnosis: pathologists examine tissues, cells and body fluids to identify disease and guide treatment, and investigate deaths through autopsy.

Branches

Anatomical pathology concerns the examination of organs and tissues removed in surgery or biopsy (surgical pathology), cells obtained by smears and aspiration (cytopathology), and deceased persons (autopsy pathology). Clinical pathology (laboratory medicine) covers the analysis of blood, urine and other fluids in disciplines such as clinical chemistry, haematology, microbiology, immunology and transfusion medicine. Forensic pathology applies pathology to legal investigations of death. Academic pathology divides the subject into general pathology — the basic reactions of cells to injury, including inflammation, necrosis, neoplasia and healing — and systemic pathology, the study of diseases organ system by organ system.

History

Systematic study of disease began with anatomical dissection in Renaissance Italy; Giovanni Battista Morgagni's De Sedibus et Causis Morborum (1761) correlated symptoms with organ lesions found at autopsy. In the 19th century Rudolf Virchow established cellular pathology ("every cell from a cell"), and microscopy, staining and bacteriology transformed diagnosis. The 20th century added immunohistochemistry, electron microscopy, cytogenetics and molecular diagnostics; 21st-century pathology increasingly integrates genomics and digital imaging.

Role in medicine

Pathology findings determine most cancer diagnoses and staging, identify infectious agents, and monitor disease through laboratory testing. Because so much medical decision-making depends on it, pathology is sometimes described as the "science behind the cure", even though pathologists often have little direct patient contact.
