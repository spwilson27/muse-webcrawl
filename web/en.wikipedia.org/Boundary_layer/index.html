Boundary layer

[Image placeholder: boundary layer velocity profile]

In fluid dynamics, a boundary layer is the thin layer of fluid in the immediate vicinity of a bounding surface where the effects of viscosity are significant. Within the boundary layer, the fluid velocity transitions from zero at the surface (the no-slip condition) to the free-stream velocity at the layer's outer edge. The concept was introduced in 1904 by the German engineer Ludwig Prandtl, in a paper widely regarded as founding modern fluid mechanics: it explained how viscosity, though small in most flows, dominates behavior near surfaces, producing skin-friction drag and the phenomenon of flow separation.

Types

A boundary layer may be laminar, with smooth, orderly flow, or turbulent, with chaotic mixing that greatly increases skin friction but also transfers momentum toward the surface, delaying separation. Transition between the two depends chiefly on the Reynolds number, surface roughness, and pressure gradient. Prandtl's student Paul Blasius derived the first exact solution for the laminar layer on a flat plate in 1908, showing its thickness grows roughly as the distance from the leading edge divided by the square root of the local Reynolds number.

Separation and drag

When a boundary layer encounters a rising (adverse) pressure gradient, as over the rear of a wing or a sphere, it may separate from the surface, forming a wake and greatly increasing pressure drag; on an airfoil, separation produces stall. Engineers manage the boundary layer with streamlined shapes, vortex generators, suction, and — famously — the dimples of a golf ball, which trip the layer to turbulence and reduce overall drag.

Related concepts

Analogous thermal and concentration boundary layers describe temperature and species transport near surfaces. In meteorology, the planetary boundary layer is the lowest part of the atmosphere, directly influenced by contact with the Earth's surface, typically a few hundred metres to a few kilometres deep.
