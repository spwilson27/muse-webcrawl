Optimal control theory is a branch of control theory and mathematical optimisation that deals with finding a control law for a dynamical system over a period of time such that an objective function (cost functional) is optimised. Given a system described by differential equations ẋ = f(x, u, t), the problem is to choose the control u(t) — subject to constraints — so as to minimise (or maximise) a cost such as total energy, time, or accumulated error, while steering the state from an initial to a desired condition.

Formulation

A standard problem minimises J = Φ(x(tf)) + ∫ L(x, u, t) dt over trajectories satisfying the system dynamics and boundary conditions. Solutions may be open-loop functions of time or feedback (closed-loop) policies u = π(x, t) that respond to the measured state; the latter are essential under uncertainty and disturbance.

Maximum principle and dynamic programming

Two great frameworks were developed in the 1950s. Lev Pontryagin and his collaborators in the Soviet Union proved the maximum principle: an optimal control must, at each instant, maximise (in their convention) a Hamiltonian formed with costate (adjoint) variables satisfying their own differential equations — necessary conditions that generalise the calculus of variations. In the United States, Richard Bellman developed dynamic programming, whose Hamilton–Jacobi–Bellman partial differential equation characterises the value function, the optimal cost from any state, and yields optimal feedback laws. Bellman's "principle of optimality" also underlies reinforcement learning.

Linear-quadratic control

For linear dynamics with quadratic costs (the LQ problem), the solution is a linear state feedback obtained from the Riccati equation; adding Gaussian noise gives the LQG problem, solved by combining the linear-quadratic regulator with a Kalman filter under the separation principle. These results, from around 1960, remain workhorses of engineering design.

Methods and applications

Computational approaches include indirect methods (solving the two-point boundary-value problem from the necessary conditions), direct transcription and collocation (discretising the problem into a large nonlinear programme), and model predictive control, which repeatedly re-solves a finite-horizon problem online. Applications range from minimum-fuel spacecraft trajectories and missile guidance to robot motion planning, chemical process optimisation, macroeconomic policy and epidemic management.
