Ice–albedo feedback

Ice–albedo feedback is a climate change feedback in which a change in the area of ice caps, glaciers and sea ice alters the albedo — the reflectivity — of a planet's surface, amplifying the original change in temperature. Ice and snow are bright and reflect most incoming sunlight back to space; open ocean and bare land are dark and absorb most of it. When warming melts ice, the surface darkens, more solar energy is absorbed, and warming increases further: a positive feedback loop. Conversely, cooling that expands ice cover raises albedo and drives further cooling.

Melting ice exposes dark ocean, which absorbs more sunlight and accelerates warming.

Mechanism

Fresh snow has an albedo of roughly 0.8–0.9, sea ice about 0.5–0.7, open ocean only about 0.06, and tundra or forest roughly 0.1–0.2. Because the contrast is greatest at high latitudes — where ice cover is also most variable — the feedback is a principal cause of polar amplification, the faster warming of the Arctic compared with the global average. The same mechanism operated in reverse during glacial periods, when expanding ice sheets helped lock Earth into ice-age conditions, a process formalised in the energy-balance models of Budyko and Sellers in the late 1960s.

Evidence and significance

Arctic sea ice has declined sharply since satellite records began in 1979, with September extent falling at roughly 13 percent per decade relative to the 1981–2010 average. Antarctica's sea ice behaved differently for decades, growing slightly before dropping to record lows in the 2020s. Satellite measurements of Earth's radiation budget confirm the darkening of the Arctic surface and an associated increase in absorbed solar radiation. In climate models, surface-albedo feedback is one of the largest contributors to the spread of projected warming, and its runaway form — in which a planet freezes entirely — is invoked in explanations of the "Snowball Earth" episodes of the Neoproterozoic era.
