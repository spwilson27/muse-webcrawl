The Great Purge, also known as the Great Terror (Russian: Большой террор) or Yezhovshchina, was Joseph Stalin's campaign of political repression in the Soviet Union that occurred from 1936 to 1938. It marked the climax of political repression in the Soviet Union, with the Soviet regime targeting Communist Party members, government officials, Red Army leadership, peasants, people suspected of sabotage, and ordinary citizens accused of being "enemies of the people".

Course of events

The assassination of Leningrad party chief Sergei Kirov in December 1934 provided a pretext for repression. Between 1936 and 1938, three staged Moscow Trials publicly tried prominent Old Bolsheviks — including Zinoviev, Kamenev, Bukharin and Radek — on fabricated charges of treason and espionage; nearly all were executed after coerced confessions. In 1937–38, NKVD Order No. 1 (00447) launched mass operations against "former kulaks" and other categories, with troikas handing down sentences against quotas. The purge of the military struck three of five marshals, including Mikhail Tukhachevsky, and tens of thousands of officers. Foreign communists, ethnic minorities (notably in the Polish operation), and large numbers of ordinary people were swept up.

Scale and toll

Archival research after 1991 indicates roughly 1.5–1.6 million arrests in 1937–38 and at least 680,000 documented executions, with many historians estimating total deaths above one million when deaths in the Gulag are included. NKVD chief Nikolai Yezhov was himself arrested and executed in 1940, and the Terror was officially blamed on his excesses; Lavrentiy Beria succeeded him.

Interpretation and legacy

Historians debate the motives behind the Terror — Stalin's paranoia and drive to eliminate any potential opposition, bureaucratic radicalization, and anticipation of war — following the opening of Soviet archives, building on earlier accounts by Robert Conquest (The Great Terror, 1968) and debates with revisionist historians. The purges gravely weakened the Red Army on the eve of the Second World War and remain a defining symbol of Stalinist totalitarianism. Many victims were posthumously rehabilitated during Khrushchev's de-Stalinization and again under Gorbachev and in post-Soviet Russia.
