Venta Icenorum

Venta Icenorum, the "town of the Iceni", was the civitas capital of the Iceni tribe in Roman Britain. Its remains lie beneath and around the village of Caistor St Edmund in Norfolk, England, a few miles south of modern Norwich. It is one of only a handful of Roman towns in Britain that were not later rebuilt upon, so its street plan can be traced in remarkable detail from aerial photography and geophysical survey.

History

The town was established after the revolt of Boudica in AD 60/61, when the Iceni, under their queen, had destroyed several Roman towns. As part of the subsequent reorganisation of the region, a planned Romano-British town was laid out on a grid of streets near the River Tas around the AD 70s. The name Venta is a Latinised Brittonic word for a market or administrative centre.

The town acquired public buildings typical of a civitas capital, including a forum and basilica, public baths, temples and an amphitheatre outside the walls. Defensive walls were added in the third century. Like many Romano-British towns, it declined in the late fourth and early fifth centuries and was eventually abandoned, with settlement shifting to Norwich.

Archaeology

Because the site was never built over, cropmarks revealing the street grid were famously photographed from the air in 1928, producing some of the most iconic images in British archaeology. Excavations in 1929–35 uncovered the forum and baths, and renewed excavations by the University of Nottingham beginning in 2009 have investigated the town's defences, buildings and suburbs. The site is managed by the Norfolk Archaeological Trust and is open to the public.
