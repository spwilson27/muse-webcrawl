History of mathematics


Plimpton 322, a Babylonian tablet of Pythagorean triples

The history of mathematics extends from prehistoric counting through the abstract, proof-based discipline of today. Mathematical knowledge developed in Mesopotamia, Egypt, Greece, China, India and the Islamic world before flourishing in early modern Europe and becoming a global enterprise in the 20th century.

Ancient mathematics

Babylonian scribes (from c. 2000 BCE) used a sexagesimal (base-60) positional system to solve quadratic problems, compute compound interest and tabulate Pythagorean triples (Plimpton 322). Egyptian papyri such as the Rhind Papyrus (c. 1550 BCE) show methods for arithmetic, unit fractions and areas. Greek mathematics transformed the subject into a deductive science: Thales and Pythagoras are credited with early theorems; Euclid's Elements (c. 300 BCE) organised geometry axiomatically; Archimedes computed areas and volumes by exhaustion and approximated pi; Apollonius studied conic sections; Diophantus pioneered algebraic methods.

Chinese and Indian mathematics

Chinese mathematicians compiled the Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art (by the 1st century CE), using rod numerals, negative numbers and methods equivalent to Gaussian elimination; Zu Chongzhi computed pi to seven digits in the 5th century. Indian mathematicians developed the decimal place-value system with zero, transmitted westward by Brahmagupta (7th century), who gave rules for zero and negative numbers. Bhāskara II (12th century) advanced algebra and calculus-like ideas; the Kerala school of Mādhava (14th–15th centuries) derived infinite series for pi and trigonometric functions.

Islamic golden age

Scholars in Baghdad's House of Wisdom preserved and extended Greek and Indian work. Al-Khwārizmī's treatise on al-jabr (c. 820) founded algebra as a discipline and gave us the word "algorithm". Omar Khayyam solved cubic equations geometrically; al-Karajī and others developed algebraic notation and induction-like arguments; Ibn al-Haytham advanced optics and number theory; Nasir al-Din al-Tusi systematised trigonometry as a subject in its own right.

Early modern Europe

Fibonacci's Liber Abaci (1202) popularised Hindu–Arabic numerals in Europe. Renaissance algebraists (del Ferro, Tartaglia, Cardano, Bombelli) solved cubic and quartic equations and admitted complex numbers. Viète and Descartes created analytic geometry; Fermat and Pascal founded probability theory (1654). Newton and Leibniz independently invented calculus in the late 17th century, sparking a priority dispute. The Bernoulli family and Euler dominated 18th-century analysis; Euler introduced much modern notation and made fundamental contributions across nearly every field.

19th century: rigour and abstraction

The 19th century professionalised mathematics. Gauss proved the fundamental theorem of algebra and created modular arithmetic (Disquisitiones Arithmeticae, 1801). Non-Euclidean geometry (Lobachevsky, Bolyai, Riemann) overturned the uniqueness of Euclidean space. Galois theory explained the insolvability of the quintic; Abel and others rigorised analysis (Cauchy, Weierstrass); Cantor founded set theory and the study of infinite cardinalities; Riemann's geometry later underpinned general relativity. Hamilton's quaternions, Boole's logic and Cayley's matrices expanded algebra.

20th century and beyond

Hilbert's 23 problems (1900) set much of the century's agenda. Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) limited formal systems; Turing's work founded computability and computer science. Mathematics proliferated into hundreds of subfields — topology, functional analysis, category theory, algebraic geometry — and landmark results included the classification of finite simple groups, Wiles's proof of Fermat's Last Theorem (1994), and Perelman's proof of the Poincaré conjecture (2002–03). Computers became tools of proof and experiment, while mathematical methods permeated physics, biology, economics and machine learning.
