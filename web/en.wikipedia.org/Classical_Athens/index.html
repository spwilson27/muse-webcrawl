Classical Athens

The Parthenon, symbol of classical Athens, completed in 432 BC.

Classical Athens refers to the city-state of Athens during the classical period of ancient Greek history, roughly from 508 BC, when Cleisthenes established Athenian democracy, to 322 BC, when Macedonian supremacy ended the city's independent democracy after the death of Alexander the Great. In this period Athens became the leading power of the Greek world, the head of the Delian League, and an unmatched centre of art, drama, philosophy and architecture.

Democracy and society

Athenian democracy was direct: male citizens voted in the Assembly (Ekklesia) on war, law and policy, served by lot on the Council of 500 and in the popular courts, and could ostracise leading figures for ten years. Reforms by Cleisthenes (508/7 BC), Ephialtes (462 BC) and Pericles progressively widened participation, including pay for jury service. Citizens numbered perhaps 30,000–50,000 out of a total population of Attica of 250,000 or more; women, slaves and resident foreigners (metics) were excluded from political rights, though metics were vital to trade and crafts and slavery underpinned much of the economy, including the silver mines at Laurion that financed the fleet.

War and empire

Athens led the Greek resistance in the Persian Wars, winning at Marathon (490 BC) and Salamis (480 BC), then converted the Delian League into an empire whose treasury funded the building programme of Pericles. Rivalry with Sparta led to the Peloponnesian War (431–404 BC), in which plague, the disastrous Sicilian Expedition and Persian support for Sparta brought Athens to surrender. Democracy was restored in 403 BC, and in the 4th century Athens rebuilt its influence, producing Plato, Aristotle (who taught there), Demosthenes and the great orators, before defeat by Philip II of Macedon at Chaeronea (338 BC) and the imposition of an oligarchy after the Lamian War (322 BC).

Culture

Classical Athens produced the tragedies of Aeschylus, Sophocles and Euripides, the comedies of Aristophanes, the histories of Herodotus and Thucydides, the philosophy of Socrates and Plato, and the Parthenon and other monuments of the Acropolis. The city's festivals, above all the City Dionysia and the Panathenaea, integrated religion, theatre and civic identity. See also Athenian democracy, Pericles and Peloponnesian War.
