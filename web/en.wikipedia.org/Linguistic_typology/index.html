Linguistic typology

Field: Linguistics
Key figures: Joseph Greenberg, Edward Sapir, Bernard Comrie
Major resource: World Atlas of Language Structures (WALS)

Linguistic typology (or language typology) is a field of linguistics that studies and classifies languages according to their structural features, in order to allow their comparison. Its aim is to describe and explain the common properties and the structural diversity of the world's languages, distinguishing what is possible, what is common, and what is rare or unattested in human language.

Approaches

Classical typology, developed in the 19th century by the Schlegel brothers, Humboldt, and later Sapir, grouped languages morphologically as isolating, agglutinative, fusional, or polysynthetic. Modern typology, reshaped by Joseph Greenberg from the 1960s, is empirical and cross-linguistic: it samples many unrelated languages and seeks statistical universals and implicational hierarchies — for example, Greenberg's universals relating subject–object–verb word order to the placement of adpositions and adjectives.

Major domains

Typologists study word order (SOV, SVO, VSO and rarer orders), alignment of case marking (nominative–accusative vs. ergative–absolutive), phoneme inventories and tone, evidentiality and numeral classifiers, and how languages encode possession, negation, and questions. Functional typologists such as Talmy Givón and Bernard Comrie explain recurring patterns through pressures of communication, processing, and grammaticalisation. The World Atlas of Language Structures documents hundreds of features across more than 2,500 languages.

Relation to other fields

Typology informs language documentation and revitalisation, historical linguistics, language acquisition, and natural language processing, and it tests theories of universal grammar against real cross-linguistic diversity.
