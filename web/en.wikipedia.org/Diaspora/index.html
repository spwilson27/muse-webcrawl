Diaspora

A diaspora is a population that is scattered across regions separate from its geographic place of origin. The word comes from the Greek diasporá, "scattering", and was first used in reference to the Jewish dispersion following the Babylonian captivity and later the destruction of the Second Temple. In contemporary usage it applies to many populations dispersed by migration, exile, trade, colonization, or forced displacement.

Characteristics

Scholars commonly identify several features of diasporas: dispersal from an original homeland to two or more foreign regions; a collective memory and myth about the homeland; an idealization of return; a strong ethnic group consciousness maintained over time; and a sense of empathy and solidarity with co-ethnic members in other countries. Modern diaspora studies also emphasize hybridity and the hostland homelands created by later generations.

Historical examples

Historical examples include the Jewish, Armenian, and Greek diasporas; the African diaspora created by the transatlantic slave trade; the Chinese and Indian diasporas formed through trade and indentured labor; and the Irish diaspora driven by famine and emigration. In the 20th and 21st centuries, refugee movements have created large diaspora communities worldwide.
