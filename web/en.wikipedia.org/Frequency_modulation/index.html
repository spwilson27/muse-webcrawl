Frequency modulation

Frequency modulation (FM) is the encoding of information in a carrier wave by varying the instantaneous frequency of the wave. This contrasts with amplitude modulation (AM), in which the amplitude of the carrier varies while its frequency remains constant.

History

Wideband FM was invented by Edwin Armstrong in the 1930s to overcome the static and interference that plagued AM radio. FM broadcasting began in the United States around 1940 and now occupies the VHF band from 87.5 to 108 MHz in most of the world, offering higher fidelity and noise resistance than AM.

Theory

In FM the instantaneous frequency of the carrier deviates from its centre value in proportion to the amplitude of the modulating signal. The maximum deviation and the highest modulating frequency determine the required bandwidth, approximated by Carson's rule. Because most noise affects amplitude rather than frequency, FM receivers can reject much interference, giving FM an intrinsic signal-to-noise advantage; FM also exhibits a capture effect, in which a receiver locks onto the stronger of two signals on the same frequency.

Applications

Beyond FM radio broadcasting, frequency modulation is used in two-way radio, television sound, radar, telemetry, and synthesis of musical tones (FM synthesis, popularised by the Yamaha DX7 synthesiser). Its digital analogue is frequency-shift keying.
