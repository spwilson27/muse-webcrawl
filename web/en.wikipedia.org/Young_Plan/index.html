Young Plan
Agreed1929; finalised at The Hague, January 1930
Named afterOwen D. Young
PurposeRevision of German reparations after World War I
SupersededDawes Plan (1924)

The Young Plan was a 1929 programme for settling Germany's reparations debts after World War I. Drawn up by an international committee chaired by the American businessman Owen D. Young, it replaced the Dawes Plan and fixed Germany's total liability at 121 billion Reichsmarks, payable in annual instalments over 59 years, until 1988.

Terms

The plan reduced the annual payments required under the Dawes Plan, split each annuity into an unconditional portion and a postponable portion, and abolished the foreign controls over the German economy imposed in 1924. It also provided for the creation of the Bank for International Settlements in Basel to handle the transfers, and was linked to the early evacuation of Allied troops from the Rhineland, completed in 1930.

Reaction in Germany

Although the plan lightened Germany's burden, German nationalists led by Alfred Hugenberg, in alliance with the Nazi Party, campaigned against it in the failed "Liberty Law" referendum of 1929, which nonetheless gave Hitler a national platform. Foreign minister Gustav Stresemann, who had negotiated German acceptance, died in October 1929, shortly after the plan was agreed in principle.

Collapse

The Wall Street crash of 1929 and the onset of the Great Depression undermined the plan almost immediately. The Hoover moratorium of 1931 suspended reparations payments, and the Lausanne Conference of 1932 effectively ended reparations altogether, long before the Young Plan's schedule could run its course.
