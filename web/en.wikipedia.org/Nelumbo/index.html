Nelumbo

Scientific classification
Kingdom:Plantae
Order:Proteales
Family:Nelumbonaceae
Genus:Nelumbo Adans.

Nelumbo is a genus of aquatic plants with large, showy flowers. Members are commonly called lotus, though "lotus" is a name also applied to various other plants and plant groups, including the unrelated genus Lotus. Members of Nelumbo resemble species in the family Nymphaeaceae ("water lilies"), but Nelumbo is actually very distant from Nymphaeaceae, being in the order Proteales. There are only two known living species of Nelumbo: the sacred lotus, Nelumbo nucifera, native to Asia, and the American lotus, Nelumbo lutea, native to North and Central America.


Nelumbo nucifera, the sacred lotus, in flower.

Description

Nelumbo species are rooted in the mud of ponds and slow rivers, with thick rhizomes and long-stalked leaves. The leaves are peltate — the stalk attaches at the centre of the leaf blade — and in N. nucifera may rise well above the water surface on stalks up to two metres tall; the leaf blades can reach 60 cm across and are famously water-repellent (superhydrophobic), so that water beads and rolls off, carrying dirt with it — a phenomenon known as the "lotus effect", widely studied for biomimetic self-cleaning surfaces.

The flowers are large and fragrant, with numerous petals and a distinctive central receptacle shaped like an inverted cone or shower head, in which the seeds develop. Unusually among flowering plants, Nelumbo flowers are thermogenic: they can maintain a temperature of about 30–36 °C during flowering, even when ambient air is much cooler, apparently stabilizing conditions for pollinating beetles. The seeds are exceptionally long-lived; seeds of N. nucifera recovered from a dry lake bed in China have been germinated after more than 1,000 years.

Species

Nelumbo nucifera

The sacred lotus is native to Asia, from Iran eastward to Japan and south to northern Australia. It is the national flower of India and Vietnam and holds deep religious significance in Hinduism and Buddhism, symbolizing purity, beauty, and spiritual unfolding. Nearly all parts of the plant are used: the seeds are eaten raw, roasted, or in pastes; the rhizomes ("lotus root") are a common vegetable in Asian cuisines; the leaves serve as food wrappers; and the stamens flavour tea. It is extensively cultivated in China — the largest producer — both for food and ornament, with thousands of named cultivars.

Nelumbo lutea

The American lotus has pale yellow flowers and ranges across eastern and central North America and into Central America and the Caribbean. Native American peoples used its seeds and rhizomes as food. It is grown ornamentally in water gardens and has become invasive in some localities outside its native range.

Taxonomy and evolution

Although superficially similar to water lilies, Nelumbo is placed in its own family, Nelumbonaceae, within the eudicot order Proteales — making it a relative of proteas and plane trees rather than of Nymphaeaceae, which lie near the base of the flowering plants. The genus has a rich fossil record: Nelumbo-like leaves and fruits are known from Cretaceous and Cenozoic deposits across the Northern Hemisphere, indicating that the genus was formerly much more widespread, including in Europe, before contracting to its present disjunct distribution.

Culture

The lotus appears in Egyptian, Hindu, Buddhist, Jain, and Chinese art and scripture. In Buddhism, the Buddha is often depicted seated on a lotus, and the Lotus Sutra is among the most influential Mahayana texts. The lotus position in yoga and meditation takes its name from the flower. The plant's combination of emergence from mud with unsullied blossoms made it a widespread symbol of purity and rebirth.
