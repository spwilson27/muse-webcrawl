Pumped-storage hydroelectricity (PSH), or pumped hydroelectric energy storage (PHES), is a type of hydroelectric energy storage used by electric power systems for load balancing. Water is pumped from a lower-elevation reservoir to a higher one when electricity is cheap or abundant, storing energy in the form of gravitational potential energy; during periods of high demand the water is released back through turbines to generate electricity.

Principle and efficiency

Although the process consumes more energy than it returns — typical round-trip efficiency is 70 to 85 per cent — it is economically valuable because it stores energy generated at low-value times for release at high-value times, and provides grid services such as frequency regulation and black-start capability. PSH accounts for the overwhelming majority of the world's installed energy-storage capacity.

History and notable plants

The first pumped-storage plants were built in Switzerland and Italy in the 1890s, and the technology expanded greatly from the 1960s to the 1980s alongside nuclear power. The largest facility is the Bath County plant in Virginia, United States, with a capacity of about 3,003 MW; other major plants include Dinorwig in Wales — nicknamed "Electric Mountain" — and Fengning in China.

Development

Interest grew again in the twenty-first century as wind and solar generation created new demand for large-scale storage. New designs include seawater plants, underground reservoirs in former mines, and refurbishment of existing schemes. Environmental concerns include land use, effects on aquatic ecosystems and the long construction times typical of the technology.
