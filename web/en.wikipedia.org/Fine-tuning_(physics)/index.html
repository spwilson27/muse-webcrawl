Fine-tuning (physics)

In theoretical physics, fine-tuning refers to the apparent need for the parameters of a theory to be adjusted to extremely precise values, or for enormous cancellations between separate contributions, in order to agree with observation — a situation many physicists regard as unnatural and as a sign of missing physics.

Examples

The best-known example is the hierarchy problem: the Higgs boson's mass receives quantum corrections that would naturally push it toward the Planck scale, some 17 orders of magnitude above its measured value of about 125 GeV, unless bare parameters and corrections cancel with extreme precision. Another is the cosmological constant problem, in which the observed vacuum energy density is far smaller than estimates from quantum field theory. Fine-tuning also arises in discussions of the strong CP problem and the flatness of the early universe, the latter commonly addressed by cosmic inflation.

Naturalness

The guiding intuition, called naturalness, was articulated by Gerard 't Hooft: a small parameter is natural if setting it to zero increases the symmetry of the theory. Supersymmetry, extra dimensions, and other proposed extensions of the Standard Model were motivated largely by the desire to solve the hierarchy problem without fine-tuning; the absence so far of evidence for such physics at the Large Hadron Collider has led some physicists to question whether naturalness is a reliable guide, and others to invoke anthropic selection within a multiverse.
