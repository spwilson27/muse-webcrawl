Obedience training

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Obedience training is the training of an animal — most commonly a dog — to respond reliably to commands such as sit, stay, come, down and heel. It ranges from basic manners for companion animals to the precision work tested in competitive obedience trials run by kennel clubs, and it forms a foundation for working roles including assistance dogs, police dogs and search-and-rescue dogs.

Methods

Modern training draws on learning theory, especially operant conditioning: behaviours are shaped by their consequences, using rewards (food, play, praise) to reinforce desired responses and, in some approaches, corrections for undesired ones. Reward-based ("positive reinforcement") methods are recommended by most veterinary and animal-welfare bodies. Tools include treats, toys, clickers used as conditioned markers, and long lines for distance work.

Competitions

In obedience trials, dog-and-handler teams perform set exercises — heeling patterns, recalls, retrieves, stays and scent discrimination — judged for accuracy and willingness. Titles progress from novice to advanced utility and obedience championship levels.

Benefits

Beyond commands, training provides mental stimulation, builds communication between animal and handler, and improves safety and socialisation in everyday situations.
