North Island

North Island
Māori name: Te Ika-a-Māui
Location: New Zealand
Area: 113,729 km² (43,911 sq mi)
Highest point: Mount Ruapehu, 2,797 m
Population: about 3.9 million (majority of New Zealand's population)

The North Island (Māori: Te Ika-a-Māui, "the fish of Māui") is one of the two main islands of New Zealand, separated from the South Island by Cook Strait. Covering 113,729 square kilometres, it is the world's 14th-largest island. It contains New Zealand's capital, Wellington, and its largest city, Auckland, and is home to roughly three-quarters of the country's population.

Geography

The North Island is generally less mountainous than the South Island but is marked by active volcanism, especially in the Taupō Volcanic Zone, which runs through the centre of the island and includes Mount Ruapehu, Mount Tongariro, and Mount Ngauruhoe. Lake Taupō, New Zealand's largest lake, occupies a caldera formed by one of the world's largest known eruptions. The island's longest river, the Waikato River, flows north from Lake Taupō to the Tasman Sea.

Regions and cities

The island is divided into nine regions, including Northland, Auckland, Waikato, Bay of Plenty, Taranaki, Manawatū-Whanganui, Hawke's Bay, Gisborne, and Wellington. Besides Auckland and Wellington, major urban areas include Hamilton, Tauranga, Palmerston North, Napier-Hastings, and Whangārei.

Name

In Māori tradition the island is the fish hauled up by the demigod Māui, while the South Island is his canoe. The official name "North Island" was supplemented in 2013 by the alternative official Māori name Te Ika-a-Māui.
