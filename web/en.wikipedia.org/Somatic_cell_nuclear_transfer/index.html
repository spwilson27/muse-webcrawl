Somatic cell nuclear transfer

In genetics and developmental biology, somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT) is a laboratory strategy for creating a viable embryo from a body cell and an egg cell. The technique consists of taking a denucleated oocyte (egg cell) and implanting a donor nucleus from a somatic (body) cell. It is used in both therapeutic and reproductive cloning.

Procedure

The nucleus of a somatic cell is removed — or the whole cell is fused with an enucleated egg — and inserted into an oocyte whose own nucleus has been removed. Chemical or electrical stimuli then prompt the reconstructed egg to begin dividing as if fertilized. If development proceeds, the resulting embryo is genetically nearly identical to the donor of the somatic nucleus (mitochondrial DNA comes from the egg donor).

History

SCNT first succeeded in amphibians in the 1950s in the work of Robert Briggs, Thomas King, and John Gurdon. Its most famous application produced Dolly the sheep in 1996 at the Roslin Institute in Scotland, the first mammal cloned from an adult somatic cell. Since then the technique has been used to clone many species, and in 2013 it was used to derive human embryonic stem cells. In 2018, Chinese scientists reported the first cloned primates, the crab-eating macaques Zhong Zhong and Hua Hua.

Applications and ethics

Proposed applications include producing patient-matched stem cells (therapeutic cloning), conserving endangered species, and livestock breeding. Reproductive cloning of humans is widely opposed and banned in many jurisdictions.
