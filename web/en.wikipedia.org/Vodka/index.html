Vodka

Vodka (Polish: wódka; Russian: водка) is a clear distilled alcoholic beverage. It originates in Poland and Russia and is composed primarily of water and ethanol (grain spirit), sometimes with traces of impurities and flavourings. Traditionally, it is made by distilling liquid from fermented cereal grains and potatoes, though some modern brands use fruits, honey, or sugarcane as the base. It typically has an alcoholic content of about 40% by volume.

Production

The fermented mash is distilled — modern producers often use continuous column stills — and the spirit is repeatedly rectified and filtered, frequently through charcoal, to achieve a neutral character. Water is added to bottling strength; soft, pure water is prized. In the European Union, vodka made from ingredients other than grain or potato must declare its raw material; in the United States, vodka is defined as a neutral spirit "without distinctive character, aroma, taste, or color", though this strict neutrality has eased in practice with the growth of craft and flavoured vodkas.

History

The word is a diminutive of the Slavic voda ("water"). Distilled grain spirits were produced in Poland and Russia by the late medieval period, spreading through the "vodka belt" of northern and eastern Europe. Poland and Russia long disputed priority of invention. State monopolies made vodka a major source of revenue in Imperial and Soviet Russia. In the mid-20th century vodka conquered the American market through cocktails such as the Moscow mule, Bloody Mary, and vodka martini, becoming the best-selling spirit category in the United States.

Consumption

In the vodka belt, vodka is traditionally drunk neat, chilled, and often accompanied by food (zakuski). Elsewhere it is chiefly a cocktail base valued for its neutrality.
