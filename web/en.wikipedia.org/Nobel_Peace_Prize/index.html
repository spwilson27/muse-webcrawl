The Nobel Peace Prize is one of the five Nobel Prizes established by the will of Swedish industrialist and inventor Alfred Nobel (1895). It is awarded annually in Oslo — uniquely among the Nobel Prizes, which are otherwise presented in Stockholm — to the person or organization that has "done the most or the best work for fraternity between nations, for the abolition or reduction of standing armies and for the holding and promotion of peace congresses."

Selection and ceremony

The laureate is chosen by the Norwegian Nobel Committee, five members appointed by the Norwegian parliament (Storting); nominations close on 31 January each year and the result is announced in October. The award ceremony takes place on 10 December at Oslo City Hall, where the laureate receives a gold medal, a diploma and a monetary award (11 million Swedish kronor in recent years).

History and notable awards

The first prizes in 1901 went jointly to Henry Dunant, founder of the Red Cross, and Frédéric Passy. The International Committee of the Red Cross has won three times (1917, 1944, 1963). Memorable laureates include Theodore Roosevelt (1906), Martin Luther King Jr. (1964, then the youngest recipient), Lech Wałęsa (1983), Nelson Mandela and F. W. de Klerk (1993), Malala Yousafzai (2014, the youngest laureate at 17) and organizations such as the United Nations, the European Union (2012) and the World Food Programme (2020).

Criticism

The prize has also attracted controversy — including awards to Henry Kissinger (1973, prompting two committee resignations), Yasser Arafat (1994), Barack Obama (2009, early in his presidency) and the European Union (2012) — and for notable omissions, above all Mahatma Gandhi, who never received it despite several nominations.
