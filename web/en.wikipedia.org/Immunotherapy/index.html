Immunotherapy

Immunotherapy or biological therapy is the treatment of disease by activating or suppressing the immune system. Immunotherapies designed to elicit or amplify an immune response are classified as activation immunotherapies, while those that reduce or suppress are classified as suppression immunotherapies. In recent years immunotherapy has transformed the treatment of cancer, allergic disease and some autoimmune conditions.

Cancer immunotherapy

Cancer immunotherapy seeks to stimulate the immune system to destroy tumors. Major approaches include:

  • Immune checkpoint inhibitors — antibodies blocking inhibitory receptors such as PD-1, PD-L1 and CTLA-4, which tumors exploit to evade T cells. Drugs like pembrolizumab, nivolumab and ipilimumab have produced durable responses in melanoma, lung cancer and many other malignancies. The 2018 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine was awarded to James Allison and Tasuku Honjo for this work.
  • Cellular therapy — T cells are harvested from the patient, engineered (for example with chimeric antigen receptors, CAR-T) or expanded, and reinfused. CAR-T therapies such as tisagenlecleucel are used in certain leukemias and lymphomas.
  • Monoclonal antibodies — targeted antibodies (e.g., rituximab against CD20, trastuzumab against HER2) that mark cancer cells for destruction or block growth signals.
  • Cancer vaccines and oncolytic viruses — treatments that present tumor antigens or selectively infect tumor cells, e.g., sipuleucel-T and T-VEC.
  • Cytokines — interleukin-2 and interferons, among the earliest immunotherapies.

Other applications

Allergen immunotherapy (allergy shots or sublingual tablets) gradually desensitizes patients to pollen, dust mite, venom and other allergens, and is the only disease-modifying treatment for allergic rhinitis and venom allergy. Immunosuppressive therapy — the mirror image — prevents organ transplant rejection and treats autoimmune diseases using corticosteroids, calcineurin inhibitors and biologics such as TNF inhibitors. Passive immunotherapy with antibodies is also used against infections and toxins, from rabies immunoglobulin to monoclonal antibodies for viral diseases.

Adverse effects

Activation immunotherapies can cause immune-related adverse events — inflammation of the colon, lungs, liver, skin or endocrine glands — as the unleashed immune system attacks healthy tissue. Cellular therapies may cause cytokine release syndrome and neurotoxicity. Suppressive immunotherapies increase susceptibility to infection.
