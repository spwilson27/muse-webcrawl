Raster scan

A raster scan is the rectangular pattern of image capture and reconstruction used in television, computer displays and imaging systems. A raster is a grid of parallel lines (rows); a scanning system traces each line in sequence, usually left to right and top to bottom, converting brightness or colour at each point into an electrical or digital signal, and a display retraces the same pattern to reconstruct the picture.

Scanning process

In a cathode-ray tube (CRT), magnetic deflection sweeps an electron beam across the screen in horizontal lines; after each line the beam is blanked and returned (horizontal retrace), and after the full frame it returns to the top (vertical retrace). Timing signals — horizontal and vertical sync — coordinate the process. Analogue television standards used interlaced scanning, drawing alternate lines in two fields to reduce flicker within limited bandwidth; computer displays generally use progressive scan, drawing every line in order.

Raster graphics

Digital imaging inherited the raster pattern: a raster image (bitmap) stores the value of each pixel in row order in a framebuffer, and display controllers read it out line by line in the same sequence. Raster scanning also applies to sensors — many image scanners and some camera sensors read out rows sequentially — and to fax machines, photocopiers, radar displays, electron microscopes and laser printers, where a rasterised beam writes each line of the page.

Comparison with vector systems

Vector displays, used in some early computer graphics and radar, steer the beam directly along the lines of the drawing rather than covering the whole rectangle. Raster systems won out for television and general-purpose computing because they handle full-colour filled images and their cost fell with cheap memory for framebuffers in the 1970s and 1980s.
