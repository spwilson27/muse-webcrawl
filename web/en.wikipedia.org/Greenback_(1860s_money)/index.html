Greenback (1860s money)

A one-dollar United States Note of 1862, printed with green ink on the back.

Greenbacks were emergency paper currency issued by the United States during the American Civil War. The name comes from the green ink used on the back of the notes. Two forms were issued: Demand Notes (1861) and, principally, United States Notes authorised by the Legal Tender Acts beginning with the act of 25 February 1862. Together they put roughly $450 million of fiat money into circulation to finance the Union war effort after the suspension of specie payments in December 1861.

History

Championed by Treasury Secretary Salmon P. Chase and congressman Elbridge G. Spaulding, legal-tender notes were controversial because the Constitution did not expressly authorise paper money as legal tender; the Supreme Court first struck them down in Hepburn v. Griswold (1870) and then upheld them in the Legal Tender Cases (1871). Greenbacks depreciated against gold during the war, trading at barely 35 cents on the dollar at their low in 1864, and their value and eventual redemption in coin became a central political issue of the post-war decades: the Greenback Party of the 1870s–1880s campaigned to keep them in circulation, while the Resumption Act of 1875 led to specie payments resuming in 1879, restoring the notes to par.

Legacy

United States Notes remained in circulation into the 20th century — the last were issued in 1971 — and "greenback" survives as a common nickname for the U.S. dollar generally. The greenbacks were the first lasting American experiment with fiat legal-tender currency and set precedents for modern monetary policy.
