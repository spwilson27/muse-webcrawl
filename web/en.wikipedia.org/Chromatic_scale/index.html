Chromatic scale

The chromatic scale is a musical scale of twelve pitches, each a semitone (half step) apart, spanning an octave. It contains every pitch available in the Western twelve-tone equal-tempered system: where a diatonic scale selects seven of the twelve, the chromatic scale uses all of them. The word derives from the Greek chroma, "color," because chromatic notes were understood to color the diatonic framework.

Notation

Ascending chromatic scales are conventionally written with sharps (C, C♯, D, D♯, E, F, F♯, G, G♯, A, A♯, B) and descending scales with flats (C, B, B♭, A, A♭, G, G♭, F, E, E♭, D, D♭). In practice, spelling follows the harmonic context, so that chromatic passing notes in a key are written to show their function — for instance as raised or lowered diatonic degrees.

Use in music

Chromatic notes have served as ornament and passing tones since the Renaissance; the madrigalists and composers such as Carlo Gesualdo pushed chromaticism for expressive effect. In the nineteenth century, Wagner and Liszt expanded chromatic harmony until the sense of a single tonic weakened, a path that led to the atonality and twelve-tone technique of Schoenberg, in which all twelve notes of the scale are treated as equal. Chromatic runs are also standard technical exercises for singers and instrumentalists.

Non-Western and theoretical contexts

Other musical cultures divide the octave differently — Indian classical theory recognizes 22 śrutis, for example — but the twelve-semitone chromatic framework underlies most modern Western instruments, from the piano keyboard to the guitar fretboard and valved brass.
