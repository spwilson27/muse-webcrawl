Snowball Earth

EventsSturtian (c. 717–659 Ma) and Marinoan (c. 650–635 Ma) glaciations
EraCryogenian Period, Neoproterozoic Era
Hypothesis proposedDeveloped by Joseph Kirschvink; named and elaborated by Paul Hoffman and colleagues (1998)

The Snowball Earth hypothesis proposes that during one or more glacial episodes of the Cryogenian period, the surface of the Earth froze over entirely, or nearly entirely — with sea ice extending to the equator — forming a planet sheathed in ice. The hypothesis explains glacial deposits found within tropical paleolatitudes and the distinctive "cap carbonate" rocks laid down as the glaciations ended.

Evidence

Tillites and dropstone-bearing sediments of Cryogenian age occur on nearly every continent, and paleomagnetic measurements show that some accumulated close to the equator. Directly above many glacial deposits sit cap carbonates — unusual limestone and dolostone layers interpreted as the product of intense weathering and ocean alkalinity changes during a super-greenhouse aftermath. Excursions in carbon isotope ratios accompany the events.

Mechanism and escape

The runaway ice-albedo feedback can carry the Earth into a globally frozen state once ice sheets pass a critical latitude. Escape from a hard snowball requires enormous greenhouse warming: with silicate weathering shut down under ice, volcanic outgassing of carbon dioxide could build up over millions of years until CO2 levels melted the ice, producing a brief, extremely hot climate. Life survived in refugia such as cracks in the ice, thin equatorial ice, and hydrothermal systems.

Significance

The snowball episodes immediately precede the Ediacaran period and the rise of complex multicellular life, and some researchers propose that the extreme environmental stress helped spur biological innovation.
