The Albertine Rift is the western branch of the East African Rift, running from the northern end of Lake Albert to the southern end of Lake Tanganyika through Uganda, the Democratic Republic of the Congo, Rwanda, Burundi and Tanzania. Named after Lake Albert, it is one of the most biologically rich regions on the African continent.

Geology

Like the rest of the East African Rift, the Albertine Rift formed as the African Plate began splitting into the Somali and Nubian plates. Its flanks include some of Africa's highest mountains — the Rwenzori Mountains (Mountains of the Moon) and the Virunga volcanoes — while its floor holds a chain of deep rift lakes: Albert, George, Edward, Kivu and Tanganyika, the second-deepest lake in the world.

Biodiversity

The rift's montane forests are an Afromontane biodiversity hotspot with exceptional endemism: more endemic mammals, birds, reptiles and amphibians occur here than anywhere else in mainland Africa. It is home to mountain gorillas, chimpanzees, L'Hoest's monkeys, and more than half of Africa's bird species. Key protected areas include Bwindi Impenetrable, Virunga, Kibale and Queen Elizabeth national parks.

Conservation and conflict

The region combines dense human populations with civil conflict, poaching and habitat loss, and oil exploration around Lake Albert has raised further environmental concerns. Conservation organisations work across borders to protect the remaining forests and their great apes.
