A geyser is a hot spring characterized by intermittent, turbulent discharge of water ejected as steam and liquid — a rare phenomenon requiring a specific combination of heat, water and underground plumbing. The word derives from Geysir in Iceland, one of the earliest geysers described in European literature. About 1,000 geysers exist worldwide, roughly half of them in Yellowstone National Park in the United States.

Formation and mechanism

Geysers form where magma lies close to the surface, heating groundwater that circulates through fractured rock. Surface water percolates down into a system of chambers and narrow constrictions. Because of the pressure of the overlying water column, water deep in the system can be heated well above its surface boiling point without boiling. Eventually steam bubbles form and expand, pushing water out of the vent; the sudden drop in pressure causes the superheated water below to flash into steam, driving an eruption. Afterward the system refills and reheats, and the cycle repeats — in some geysers with striking regularity, as with Yellowstone's Old Faithful, which erupts roughly every 90 minutes to heights of 32–56 metres.

Silica dissolved from hot rock precipitates around geyser vents as a porous deposit called geyserite (siliceous sinter), which helps seal the plumbing and maintain pressure.

Distribution

Major geyser fields include Yellowstone; the Valley of Geysers on Russia's Kamchatka Peninsula; El Tatio in the Chilean Andes, the largest field in the southern hemisphere; the Taupō Volcanic Zone in New Zealand; and Iceland, where the Great Geysir now erupts infrequently but nearby Strokkur erupts every few minutes. Geyser activity is fragile: earthquakes can alter plumbing, and geothermal power extraction has extinguished many fields, notably in New Zealand and Nevada.

Extraterrestrial geysers

Cryovolcanic plumes resembling geysers have been observed elsewhere in the Solar System, including water-vapour plumes at the south pole of Saturn's moon Enceladus and dark streak deposits attributed to nitrogen geysers on Neptune's moon Triton.

Biology

The near-boiling waters around geysers host heat-loving (thermophilic) bacteria and archaea that form vividly coloured microbial mats. Enzymes from Yellowstone's Thermus aquaticus — Taq polymerase — became the workhorse of the polymerase chain reaction (PCR) in molecular biology.
