Six Acts

The Six Acts were a series of laws passed by the Parliament of the United Kingdom in December 1819, in the aftermath of the Peterloo Massacre, aimed at suppressing radical reform movements and public unrest in post-Napoleonic Britain.

Background

On 16 August 1819, cavalry charged a large peaceful meeting at St Peter's Fields, Manchester, called to demand parliamentary reform, killing and injuring many. The government of Lord Liverpool, fearing revolution, introduced emergency legislation despite opposition criticism that it curtailed civil liberties.

The Acts

The six measures were the Training Prevention Act (banning military-style drilling), the Seizure of Arms Act, the Misdemeanours Act (speeding up trials), the Seditious Meetings Prevention Act (restricting public meetings), the Blasphemous and Seditious Libels Act (harsher penalties and powers of banishment), and the Newspaper and Stamp Duties Act (extending stamp tax to cheap radical publications).

Legacy

The Six Acts became a byword for repression and were invoked in later debates over free speech and assembly. Most restrictions lapsed or were repealed through the nineteenth century, but the episode shaped radical and Chartist politics for a generation.
