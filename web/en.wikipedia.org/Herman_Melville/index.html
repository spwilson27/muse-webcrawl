Herman Melville (August 1, 1819 – September 28, 1891) was an American novelist, short story writer, and poet of the American Renaissance period. Among his most notable works are Moby-Dick (1851); Typee (1846), a romanticised account of his experiences of Polynesian life; and Billy Budd, Sailor, a posthumously published novella. At the time of his death Melville was almost completely forgotten; the "Melville Revival" of the 1920s established Moby-Dick as one of the greatest American novels.

Life

Born in New York City to a prosperous merchant family that fell on hard times, Melville worked as a bank clerk, teacher, and farmhand before shipping out as a common sailor. In 1841 he sailed on the whaler Acushnet to the South Pacific; he deserted at Nuku Hiva in the Marquesas, lived briefly among the Typee people, and later took part in a mutiny on an Australian whaler before reaching Hawaii and returning home in 1844 with the US Navy frigate United States.

His first books, Typee (1846) and Omoo (1847), were popular adventure narratives. Mardi (1849) began a turn toward ambitious philosophical allegory that culminated in Moby-Dick — dedicated to his friend Nathaniel Hawthorne — which sold poorly and bewildered reviewers. Later fiction included Pierre (1852), the short stories of The Piazza Tales (1856), among them "Bartleby, the Scrivener" and "Benito Cereno", and the satirical The Confidence-Man (1857), his last prose work published in his lifetime.

Later years

From 1866 to 1885 Melville worked as a customs inspector in New York, publishing poetry including Battle-Pieces (1866) and the long poem Clarel (1876). In retirement he returned to prose and completed Billy Budd, left unpublished at his death in 1891 and first published in 1924.

Legacy

The centenary of Melville's birth in 1919 began his revival; Raymond Weaver's 1921 biography and new editions brought Moby-Dick to prominence. It is now regarded as a central work of world literature, and Melville is considered one of the greatest American writers.
