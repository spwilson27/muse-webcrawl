Wigner's friend

Schematic of the Wigner's friend scenario

Wigner's friend is a thought experiment in theoretical quantum physics, first published by Hungarian-American physicist Eugene Wigner in 1961, and further developed by David Deutsch in 1985. The scenario involves an indirect observation of a quantum measurement: an observer W observes another observer F who performs a quantum measurement on a physical system. The two observers then formulate statements about the system's state after measurement, based on the laws of quantum theory.

The paradox

In the scenario, Wigner's friend measures a quantum system (such as a spin or a photon) inside a sealed laboratory and obtains a definite outcome. From the friend's perspective, the wavefunction has collapsed. From Wigner's perspective outside the lab, however, the friend and the system together remain in an entangled superposition until Wigner learns the result. The thought experiment thus raises the question of when, and for whom, a measurement outcome becomes definite.

Interpretations and developments

Wigner originally used the scenario to argue that consciousness plays a special role in completing a measurement. Most modern discussions instead use it to contrast interpretations of quantum mechanics: the many-worlds interpretation denies collapse altogether; relational quantum mechanics and QBism treat states as relative to observers; objective-collapse theories posit a physical collapse mechanism. Extended versions of the experiment (such as the Frauchiger–Renner argument of 2016) purport to show that single-world interpretations cannot consistently combine several seemingly natural assumptions, and local-friendliness experiments have tested related inequalities in the laboratory.
