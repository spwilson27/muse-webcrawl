The French campaign in Egypt and Syria (1798–1801) was Napoleon Bonaparte's expedition to occupy Ottoman Egypt, threaten British routes to India, and spread revolutionary influence. Although it ended in French withdrawal, it had lasting scientific and cultural consequences, notably launching modern Egyptology.

Invasion

Sailing from Toulon in May 1798 with some 40,000 troops and 167 scholars (the savants), Napoleon seized Malta, landed at Alexandria on 1 July 1798, and defeated the Mamluks at the Battle of the Pyramids (21 July). But on 1 August Nelson destroyed the French fleet at the Battle of the Nile, stranding the army in Egypt.

Syria and collapse

In 1799 Napoleon invaded Syria, failed at the siege of Acre against Ottoman forces aided by the British under Sir Sidney Smith, and retreated. He won a last victory at Aboukir on land in July 1799, then secretly sailed for France in August, leaving General Kléber in command. Kléber was assassinated in 1800; his successor Menou capitulated to a British-Ottoman force in 1801, and the French were repatriated on British ships.

Scientific legacy

The accompanying savants produced the monumental Description de l'Égypte and founded the Institut d'Égypte. In 1799 French soldiers found the Rosetta Stone near Rashid, whose trilingual inscription enabled Champollion to decipher Egyptian hieroglyphs in 1822.
