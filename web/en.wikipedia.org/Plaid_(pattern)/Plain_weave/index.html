Plain weave

Plain weave (also called tabby weave, linen weave, or taffeta weave) is the most basic of the three fundamental types of textile weaves, alongside twill and satin. In plain weave, the weft thread crosses the warp threads alternately — over one, then under the next, and so on across the row — and the following row reverses the pattern, producing a simple criss-cross structure resembling a checkerboard.

Characteristics

Plain weave fabric is strong for its weight because of the many interlacings between warp and weft, which maximise friction and stability. It has no distinct right or wrong side unless printed or finished differently, and it tends to wrinkle and absorb more moisture than the other basic weaves. Because the structure is simple, it can be produced on simple looms and is the cheapest weave to manufacture.

Uses

A great many common fabrics are plain weaves, including chiffon, organza, taffeta, muslin, percale for bedsheets, canvas, and much quilting cotton. Balanced plain weaves, in which warp and weft threads are of similar size and count, are used for embroidery grounds and needlework canvas as well as apparel.

Derivative weaves

Variations include rib weaves, in which grouped or heavier threads create ridges, and basket weave (also called hopsack), in which two or more threads are treated as one in both directions, giving a chequered texture used in monk's cloth and some upholstery.
