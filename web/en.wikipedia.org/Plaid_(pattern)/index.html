Plaid (pattern)

A plaid is a cloth made with a tartan pattern woven from coloured yarns in both the warp and weft, producing a pattern of criss-crossed horizontal and vertical bands in multiple colours. The word "plaid" in North American English refers to the checked pattern itself, whereas in Scotland a plaid (from Gaelic plaide, "blanket") is a large piece of tartan cloth worn over the shoulder, also historically called the belted plaid, the forerunner of the modern kilt.

Terminology

In Scotland, "tartan" names the pattern and "plaid" the garment; in North America the meanings are reversed, with "plaid" used for any checked pattern, including simpler designs that are not strictly tartans. Where the warp and weft patterns are identical, the cloth shows even, symmetric checks; translucent effects occur where bands of colour overlap.

Patterns and use

Plaid patterns range from regulated clan tartans registered in Scotland, to district and fashion tartans, to generic checks such as glen plaid (Prince of Wales check), houndstooth, gingham, and windowpane. Plaid flannel shirts became emblematic workwear and, later, fashion items — associated in turn with lumberjacks, grunge music culture in the 1990s, and hipster style. Brands such as Burberry made their proprietary plaid (the Burberry check) a luxury trademark.

History

Checked woollen cloth has been woven in Scotland since at least the 3rd or 4th century AD, as shown by the Falkirk tartan fragment. Tartan became strongly identified with the Highlands, was proscribed after the Jacobite rising of 1745 under the Dress Act, and was romanticised in the 19th century, when Queen Victoria and Prince Albert adopted Highland dress at Balmoral and many clan tartans were codified.
