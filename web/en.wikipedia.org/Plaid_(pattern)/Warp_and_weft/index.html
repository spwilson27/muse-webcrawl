Warp and weft

The warp and the weft are the two basic components of weaving that turn thread or yarn into fabric. The warp consists of the set of lengthwise yarns held in tension on a loom; the weft (also called the filling) is the yarn drawn through, over, and under the warp threads to create the cloth. The way warp and weft interlace defines the weave — plain weave, twill, or satin — and, with coloured yarns, patterns such as gingham, houndstooth, and tartan.

Warp

Warp yarns must withstand high tension and abrasion on the loom, so they are usually stronger, more tightly twisted, and often sized (coated with starch) before weaving. Each warp thread (an end) passes through a heddle and a reed; raising and lowering groups of warp threads forms the shed through which the weft passes.

Weft

The weft yarn crosses the fabric width in a single row called a pick. Historically it was carried through the shed by a shuttle; modern looms use air jets, water jets, rapiers, or projectiles. Weft yarns can be softer and more textured than warp yarns, contributing to the fabric's surface and drape.

Terminology

The terms derive from Old English: "warp" from weorpan, to throw (the threads are thrown across the loom), and "weft" from wefan, to weave. The finished edges parallel to the warp are the selvedges.
