Placenta

The placenta is a temporary organ that develops in the uterus during pregnancy in most mammals. It connects the developing fetus to the uterine wall via the umbilical cord, allowing nutrient uptake, gas exchange, waste elimination, and hormone production, while keeping the maternal and fetal blood supplies largely separate. The placenta is expelled from the body shortly after childbirth, when it is called the afterbirth.

Structure

The human placenta is a discoid organ about 22 cm in diameter and 2–2.5 cm thick at term, weighing roughly 500 g. It has a fetal side, smooth and covered by the amnion from which the umbilical cord arises, and a maternal side divided into lobes called cotyledons. Chorionic villi containing fetal capillaries project into pools of maternal blood in the intervillous space, where exchange occurs.

Functions

The placenta transfers oxygen, glucose, amino acids, and antibodies (IgG) from mother to fetus, and carbon dioxide and waste products in the reverse direction. It also secretes hormones — including human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG), progesterone, and estrogens — that maintain pregnancy, and acts as a partial barrier to infections and drugs.

Clinical significance

Disorders of the placenta include placenta praevia, in which it covers the cervix; placental abruption, premature separation from the uterine wall; and placenta accreta spectrum, in which it attaches too deeply. Preeclampsia is also thought to originate in abnormal placental development.
