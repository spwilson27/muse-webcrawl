Placenta praevia

Placenta praevia (also spelled placenta previa) is a condition of pregnancy in which the placenta attaches inside the uterus in a position that covers the cervix, partially or completely. It can cause severe, typically painless vaginal bleeding in the second half of pregnancy and is a leading cause of antepartum haemorrhage. Affected women often need to deliver by caesarean section because a placenta blocking the cervix prevents a safe vaginal delivery.

Classification

Placenta praevia is traditionally classified by the relationship of the placental edge to the internal cervical os: a complete praevia covers the os entirely, while partial and marginal types cover it in part or reach its edge. Modern ultrasound-based classification commonly distinguishes placenta praevia (placenta covering the os or lying within about 2 cm of it) from a low-lying placenta.

Causes and risk factors

The exact cause is unknown, but risk factors include previous placenta praevia, previous caesarean delivery or uterine surgery, multiparity, multiple gestation, smoking, and advanced maternal age. The condition occurs in roughly 1 in 200 pregnancies.

Diagnosis and management

The condition is usually detected on routine ultrasound in mid-pregnancy; many low-lying placentas identified early move away from the cervix as the uterus grows ("placental migration"). Digital vaginal examination is avoided when praevia is suspected because it can provoke bleeding. Management may include pelvic rest, hospital observation after bleeding episodes, corticosteroids to mature the fetal lungs if preterm delivery is likely, and planned caesarean delivery, typically at 36–37 weeks in uncomplicated cases.

Complications

Complications include severe maternal haemorrhage, preterm birth, and an association with placenta accreta spectrum, in which the placenta grows too deeply into the uterine wall — a risk that rises sharply in women with both a praevia and a prior caesarean scar.
