Caesarean section

Other names: C-section, caesarean delivery
Specialty: Obstetrics
Indications: Obstructed labour, fetal distress, placenta praevia, breech presentation

A caesarean section, often called a C-section, is the surgical procedure by which a baby is delivered through an incision in the mother's abdomen and uterus. It is performed when vaginal delivery would put the mother or baby at risk — for example in obstructed labour, fetal distress, abnormal presentation, or placenta praevia, in which the placenta covers the cervix. Globally, roughly one in five births is by caesarean section, with rates varying widely between countries.

Procedure

The operation is usually performed under regional anaesthesia (spinal or epidural), allowing the mother to remain awake. The surgeon makes a horizontal incision just above the pubic hairline, opens the uterus, delivers the baby, and then removes the placenta before closing the incisions in layers. The procedure typically takes 45 minutes to an hour.

History

Caesarean delivery on deceased or dying mothers is ancient — Roman law, the Lex Caesarea, required it — but successful operations with maternal survival were rare before the 19th century. Antisepsis, anaesthesia, and the lower-segment uterine incision introduced in the early 20th century dramatically improved safety.

Risks and recovery

Compared with vaginal birth, caesarean delivery carries higher risks of infection, haemorrhage, and complications in future pregnancies, including placenta accreta, in which the placenta grows too deeply into the uterine wall. Recovery typically takes longer than after vaginal delivery. The World Health Organization notes that caesarean sections are most beneficial at population rates around 10–15%, while higher rates do not further reduce mortality.
