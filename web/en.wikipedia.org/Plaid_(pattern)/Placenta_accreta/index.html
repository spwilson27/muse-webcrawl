Placenta accreta

Specialty: Obstetrics
Risk factors: Prior caesarean section, placenta praevia, uterine surgery
Complications: Massive postpartum haemorrhage

Placenta accreta is a serious pregnancy condition in which the placenta grows too deeply into the wall of the uterus, so that part or all of it remains attached after childbirth instead of separating normally. It is the mildest form of the placenta accreta spectrum; deeper invasion is termed placenta increta (into the uterine muscle) and placenta percreta (through the wall, sometimes into the bladder). The condition can cause life-threatening haemorrhage at delivery.

Causes and risk factors

Placenta accreta is believed to result from a defect in the decidua, the uterine lining, often where the uterus has been scarred. The strongest risk factors are a previous caesarean section combined with placenta praevia in the current pregnancy; risk rises steeply with the number of prior caesareans. Rising caesarean rates worldwide have made accreta steadily more common, from roughly 1 in 30,000 pregnancies in the 1950s to about 1 in 300–500 in recent decades.

Diagnosis

The condition is usually detected before birth by ultrasound, looking for loss of the normal clear zone behind the placenta, placental lacunae, and abnormal blood flow; MRI is used when the diagnosis is uncertain. Antenatal diagnosis greatly improves outcomes by allowing planned delivery at a specialist centre.

Management

Standard management is planned preterm caesarean delivery, frequently followed by hysterectomy with the placenta left in place, since attempting removal can trigger catastrophic bleeding. Blood products and interventional radiology support are arranged in advance; in selected cases conservative management leaving the placenta to resorb is attempted.
