Weaving

Weaving is a method of textile production in which two distinct sets of yarns or threads are interlaced at right angles to form a fabric or cloth. The longitudinal threads are called the warp and the lateral threads the weft. The way the warp and filling threads interlace with each other is called the weave: the three basic types are plain weave, satin weave, and twill, from which patterns such as plaid, tartan, gingham, and houndstooth are built by combining weave structure with sequences of coloured yarns.

Process

Weaving is done on a loom, which holds the warp threads in tension. The principal motions are shedding (separating warp threads to form a shed), picking (passing the weft through the shed), and beating-up (pushing the weft into place with the reed). Handlooms have been used for thousands of years; power looms, developed during the Industrial Revolution, mechanised the process and transformed textile manufacture.

History

Woven textiles date to the Neolithic era, and woven flax fragments over 30,000 years old have been found in Georgia's Dzudzuana Cave. Weaving was central to economies from ancient Egypt and China (silk) to medieval Europe's wool trade and the tartan cloth of the Scottish Highlands. The Jacquard loom of 1804, controlled by punched cards, allowed intricate patterned weaving and influenced early computing.

Patterns and cloth types

Combining coloured warp and weft sequences with the basic weaves produces checked and patterned cloths: plain-weave gingham and madras; 2/2 twill tartans and houndstooth; and satin-weave fabrics with their glossy surfaces.
