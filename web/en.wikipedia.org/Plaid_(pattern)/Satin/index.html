Satin

Satin is a weave that produces a characteristically glossy, smooth fabric. In a satin weave, warp yarns float over several weft yarns (typically four or more) before interlacing, so the surface is dominated by long floats that reflect light evenly. Satin is one of the three fundamental textile weaves, alongside plain weave and twill.

History

Satin originated in medieval China, woven in silk in the port city of Quanzhou (known in Arabic as Zayton, from which the word "satin" derives). Silk satins reached Europe via the Silk Road in the 12th and 13th centuries and were prized for garments, hangings, and upholstery.

Types and uses

Traditional satin is woven from silk filament; modern versions use polyester or nylon filament yarns. Variants include duchess satin, charmeuse, sateen (a cotton fabric with a satin-like weave in the weft direction), and antique satin. Satin is used for evening gowns, wedding dresses, lingerie, linings, and ribbons. Its floats make it lustrous but also prone to snagging.
