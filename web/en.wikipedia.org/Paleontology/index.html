Paleontology

Palaeontologists at work exposing a fossil skeleton in the field.

Paleontology (also spelled palaeontology) is the scientific study of life that existed prior to the start of the Holocene epoch about 11,700 years ago. It includes the study of fossils to classify organisms and study their interactions with each other and their environments. Paleontology lies on the border between biology and geology, and uses techniques drawn from biochemistry, mathematics and engineering.

History

Fossils were noticed and debated since antiquity — Xenophanes observed fossil shells inland in the sixth century BC — but the field took shape in the late 18th and 19th centuries with Georges Cuvier's work on comparative anatomy and extinction, William Smith's use of fossils to order rock strata, and Mary Anning's discoveries of marine reptiles on the Dorset coast. Charles Darwin's theory of evolution gave the fossil record its modern interpretive framework. The field's name was coined in the 1820s–30s (from Greek palaios, "old", onta, "beings", and logos, "study").

Branches and methods

Vertebrate paleontology studies fossil fish, amphibians, reptiles, birds and mammals; invertebrate paleontology covers molluscs, arthropods and other animals without backbones; paleobotany studies fossil plants; and micropaleontology works with microscopic fossils. Taphonomy examines how organisms decay and become fossilised, while biostratigraphy uses index fossils to correlate rock layers. Modern methods include radiometric dating, CT scanning of specimens, and phylogenetic analysis.

Fossils and the geologic timescale

By documenting the appearance, diversification and extinction of lineages — including the five great mass extinctions — paleontology underpins the geologic time scale and reconstructs ancient ecosystems and climates. Famous fossil assemblages include the Cambrian Burgess Shale, the feathered dinosaurs of Liaoning, and the La Brea Tar Pits.
