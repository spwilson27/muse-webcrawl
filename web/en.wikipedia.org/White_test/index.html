White test

The White test is a statistical test that establishes whether the variance of the errors in a regression model is constant: that is, whether homoskedasticity holds or heteroskedasticity is present. It was proposed by Halbert White in 1980 and is one of the most commonly used tests for heteroskedasticity in econometrics.

Procedure

To test for constant variance, one first estimates the model by ordinary least squares and saves the residuals. An auxiliary regression of the squared residuals on the original regressors, their squares and their pairwise cross-products is then run. Under the null hypothesis of homoskedasticity, the sample size multiplied by the R² of the auxiliary regression follows a chi-squared distribution with degrees of freedom equal to the number of auxiliary regressors (excluding the constant).

Advantages and alternatives

The White test does not require specifying the form of the heteroskedasticity and, unlike the Goldfeld–Quandt test, does not depend on normality of the errors. Closely related diagnostics include the Breusch–Pagan test. White also developed heteroskedasticity-consistent ("robust") standard errors, which are widely used when the test rejects homoskedasticity.
