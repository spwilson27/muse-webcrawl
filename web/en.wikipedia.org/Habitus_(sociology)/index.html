Habitus (sociology)

Habitus is a key concept in the sociology of Pierre Bourdieu, referring to the set of durable, transposable dispositions — habits, tastes, bodily postures, perceptions, and expectations — that individuals acquire through their life experiences, especially their social class background and upbringing. The habitus generates practices and representations that are patterned by social structures without being the product of conscious obedience to rules.

Origins

The term has a long philosophical history, from Aristotle's notion of hexis through medieval scholasticism and the work of Norbert Elias and Marcel Mauss. Bourdieu developed it most fully in Outline of a Theory of Practice (1972) and The Logic of Practice (1980), defining the habitus as a system of structured and structuring dispositions.

Concepts

The habitus operates within a field — a social arena with its own stakes and rules — and interacts with the various forms of capital (economic, cultural, social, and symbolic) that agents possess. Because it is formed by past conditions, the habitus can lag behind changes in the field, an effect Bourdieu called hysteresis, producing mismatches between expectations and opportunities. The concept has been enormously influential in sociology, anthropology, and education research, though critics argue it can be deterministic and difficult to operationalise.
