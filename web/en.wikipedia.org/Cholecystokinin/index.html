Cholecystokinin

Cholecystokinin (CCK or CCK-PZ; from Greek chole, "bile"; cysto, "sac"; kinin, "move"; hence, move the bile-sac (gallbladder)) is a peptide hormone of the gastrointestinal system responsible for stimulating the digestion of fat and protein. Cholecystokinin, officially called pancreozymin when first discovered, is synthesized and secreted by enteroendocrine cells in the duodenum, the first segment of the small intestine. Its presence causes the release of digestive enzymes and bile from the pancreas and gallbladder, respectively, and also acts as a hunger suppressant.

History

Evidence that the small intestine controls gallbladder contraction emerged in the late 1920s. In 1928, Ivy and Oldberg proposed a humoral agent, which they named "cholecystokinin", released from the intestinal mucosa that caused gallbladder contraction in dogs. Separately, Harper and Raper described "pancreozymin" in 1943 as a factor stimulating pancreatic enzyme secretion. The two activities were later shown to reside in a single 33-amino-acid peptide, purified by Jorpes and Mutt in the 1960s.

Structure

CCK is a member of the gastrin/cholecystokinin family of peptide hormones and is structurally related to gastrin, with which it shares the same five C-terminal amino acids. CCK exists in several molecular forms differing in length — principally CCK-58, CCK-33, CCK-22 and CCK-8 — all derived from a 115-amino-acid precursor encoded by the CCK gene. A sulfated tyrosine residue near the C-terminus is essential for full biological activity at CCKA receptors.

Functions

CCK is released into the blood after a meal, particularly in response to fatty acids and certain amino acids in the chyme entering the duodenum. Its principal actions include:

  • Gallbladder contraction and relaxation of the sphincter of Oddi, expelling bile into the duodenum.
  • Pancreatic secretion of enzyme-rich fluid from acinar cells, acting largely via vagal afferents.
  • Delayed gastric emptying and reduced gastric acid secretion.
  • Satiety: CCK acts as a short-term hunger suppressant, signalling fullness via the vagus nerve and brainstem; within the central nervous system it also functions as a neurotransmitter implicated in anxiety, memory and analgesia.
  • Intestinal motility modulation and stimulation of insulin, glucagon and pancreatic polypeptide release.

Clinical significance

The CCK provocation test (using the analogue sincalide) is used in cholescintigraphy to assess gallbladder ejection fraction in suspected biliary dyskinesia. Excess CCK release can provoke pain in gallstone disease, and CCK receptor antagonists have been investigated for pancreatitis, irritable bowel syndrome and anxiety disorders, though none has become a mainstay therapy.
