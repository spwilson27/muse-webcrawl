Neonatal intensive care unit

A neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), also known as an intensive care nursery (ICN), is an intensive care unit (ICU) specialising in the care of ill or premature newborn infants. The first American newborn intensive care unit, designed by Louis Gluck, opened in October 1960 at Yale New Haven Hospital.

Care levels

Hospitals classify newborn care into levels. Level I nurseries provide care for healthy term newborns; Level II units care for moderately ill infants; Level III NICUs provide continuous life support including mechanical ventilation for infants born at very low birth weight; and Level IV units, usually at regional centres, offer surgical repair of complex congenital conditions in addition to full intensive care.

Common conditions

Infants are admitted to the NICU most commonly for prematurity and its complications, including respiratory distress syndrome, intraventricular haemorrhage, necrotising enterocolitis, sepsis, and hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy. Care is provided by a multidisciplinary team of neonatologists, nurses, respiratory therapists, pharmacists, and dietitians, in an environment that controls temperature, oxygen, noise, and infection exposure.

History

Special care for premature infants began with incubator exhibitions in the late 19th century and the premature infant stations pioneered by Martin Couney. Modern neonatology developed rapidly after the 1960s with mechanical ventilation, surfactant therapy, and pulse oximetry dramatically improving survival of extremely preterm infants.
