Sixth Amendment to the United States Constitution

Part ofUnited States Bill of Rights
Ratified15 December 1791
GuaranteesSpeedy and public trial, impartial jury, notice of accusation, confrontation of witnesses, compulsory process, assistance of counsel

The Sixth Amendment to the United States Constitution sets out the rights of defendants in criminal prosecutions. Ratified in 1791 as part of the Bill of Rights, it guarantees a speedy and public trial by an impartial jury, the right to be informed of the accusation, to confront adverse witnesses, to obtain witnesses in one's favour, and to have the assistance of counsel.

Text

"In all criminal prosecutions, the accused shall enjoy the right to a speedy and public trial, by an impartial jury of the State and district wherein the crime shall have been committed... and to have the Assistance of Counsel for his defence."

Right to a speedy and public trial

The speedy-trial guarantee guards against indefinite pretrial detention and stale evidence; in Barker v. Wingo (1972) the Supreme Court set out a balancing test for violations. The public-trial right, shared with the public and press, was affirmed in cases such as Richmond Newspapers v. Virginia (1980).

Jury trial and confrontation

The Amendment requires juries to be impartial and drawn from the state and district of the crime; Duncan v. Louisiana (1968) applied the jury-trial right to the states. The Confrontation Clause was given renewed force in Crawford v. Washington (2004), which barred testimonial hearsay from absent witnesses unless the defendant had a prior chance to cross-examine.

Assistance of counsel

In Gideon v. Wainwright (1963), the Court held that states must provide counsel to indigent defendants in felony cases, a right later extended to misdemeanours carrying imprisonment. Strickland v. Washington (1984) established the standard for claims of ineffective assistance.
