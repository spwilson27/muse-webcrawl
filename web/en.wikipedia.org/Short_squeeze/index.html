Short squeeze


A rapid, self-reinforcing price spike is the hallmark of a short squeeze.

A short squeeze is a rapid increase in the price of a stock or other security that occurs largely because of an excess of short sellers being forced to cover their positions. When the price of a heavily shorted asset begins to rise, short sellers face mounting losses and margin calls; their buying to close positions adds demand, which drives the price up further and forces still more short sellers to cover. The feedback loop can push the price to levels far above anything justified by fundamentals, though the elevation is usually temporary.

Conditions

Squeezes require a large short interest relative to the free float, a catalyst that lifts the price (good news, a takeover approach, or coordinated buying), and difficulty in borrowing additional shares. Stocks with short interest exceeding 20–30% of the float are often considered squeeze candidates; short interest above 100% of the float is possible when borrowed shares are re-lent and sold again.

Notable examples

  • Piggly Wiggly (1923): Clarence Saunders attempted to corner the stock of his grocery chain; the ensuing squeeze and exchange intervention bankrupted him.
  • Volkswagen (2008): Porsche's disclosure that it controlled about 74% of Volkswagen shares, combined with the State of Lower Saxony's stake, left almost no free float for shorts to buy. In October 2008 Volkswagen briefly became the world's most valuable company, its shares exceeding €1,000 intraday.
  • GameStop (2021): Retail traders organised through the Reddit forum r/wallstreetbets bought shares and call options in GameStop, whose short interest exceeded its float. The GameStop short squeeze drove the stock from about $20 to an intraday high of $483 in January 2021 and inflicted heavy losses on funds such as Melvin Capital.
  • Tesla (2019–2020): a prolonged squeeze against one of the market's most-shorted large caps, as the shares rose roughly tenfold.

Related phenomena

A short squeeze differs from a market corner, in which a single buyer deliberately accumulates control of supply, though the two can interact. The mirror image for long holders is the long squeeze, in which falling prices force leveraged buyers to sell. Brokers may respond to extreme squeezes by restricting purchases of new positions, a practice controversially applied during the GameStop episode.
