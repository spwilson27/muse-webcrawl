The independence of Brazil was the process by which Brazil ceased to be a colony and constituent kingdom of Portugal and became the independent Empire of Brazil in 1822. It culminated on 7 September 1822, when the Portuguese prince regent Dom Pedro, defying orders to return to Lisbon, proclaimed Brazilian independence in the celebrated Cry of Ipiranga: "Independência ou Morte!" ("Independence or Death!").

Background

In 1808, fleeing Napoleon's invasion, the Portuguese court transferred to Rio de Janeiro, which became the seat of the Portuguese Empire. Brazil's ports were opened to friendly nations, and in 1815 Brazil was raised to the status of a kingdom united with Portugal. After the Liberal Revolution of 1820 in Portugal, however, the Lisbon Cortes sought to restore Brazil's colonial subordination and ordered Dom Pedro, who had stayed behind as regent when his father John VI returned to Lisbon in 1821, to come back to Portugal as well.

The road to separation

Brazilian elites, fearing the loss of their hard-won status and trade freedoms, pressed Pedro to stay. On 9 January 1822 — the Dia do Fico ("I Shall Stay Day") — he announced he would remain. Through 1822 tensions escalated: Pedro convoked a constituent assembly, dismissed Portuguese troops, and took the title of Perpetual Defender of Brazil. In September, travelling in São Paulo, he received fresh decrees from Lisbon annulling his acts and threatening him. On 7 September, by the Ipiranga brook, he tore the Portuguese insignia from his uniform and declared independence.

War and recognition

Unlike Spanish America's fragmentation, Brazil held together as a single monarchy — but not without fighting. Loyalist forces in Bahia, Maranhão, Pará and the Cisplatina resisted, and a war of independence ran into 1823–24, with the Brazilian cause aided by a new navy commanded by the British admiral Lord Cochrane. Pedro was crowned Emperor Pedro I on 1 December 1822; the United States recognised Brazil in 1824, and Portugal followed in the Treaty of Rio de Janeiro of 1825, in exchange for a Brazilian indemnity of £2 million.

Legacy

Brazilian independence was comparatively conservative: it preserved the monarchy, the territorial unity of Portuguese America, and the institution of slavery, which lasted until 1888. The 7th of September is Brazil's national day, celebrated with military parades; Pedro Américo's monumental painting of the Cry of Ipiranga (1888) fixed the event's heroic image in national memory.
