Human vestigiality

Human vestigiality is the retention, during the process of evolution, of genetically determined structures or attributes that have lost some or all of their ancestral function in humans. Vestigial features are widely cited as evidence for evolution, since they are most readily explained as remnants of functional structures in ancestors.

Anatomical examples

Commonly cited examples include the vermiform appendix, the coccyx (tailbone, the remnant of an ancestral tail), wisdom teeth, the palmaris longus and plantaris muscles, the muscles that move the external ear, the plica semilunaris of the eye, goose bumps produced by the arrector pili muscles, and Darwin's tubercle on the ear. Some structures described as vestigial have since been found to retain secondary functions — the appendix, for instance, appears to serve as a reservoir for gut bacteria and has immune tissue — illustrating that "vestigial" means reduced from an original function, not necessarily useless.

Molecular and behavioural vestiges

At the genetic level, humans carry pseudogenes such as the broken copy of the GULO gene, which prevents synthesis of vitamin C as in most other mammals. Behavioural remnants include the palmar grasp reflex of infants and hiccups, which have been proposed as remnants of amphibian breathing patterns.

History of the concept

Charles Darwin discussed rudimentary organs in On the Origin of Species and listed several in The Descent of Man. In 1893 the anatomist Robert Wiedersheim catalogued 86 supposedly vestigial human structures, a list often reproduced — and criticized — in later debates between evolutionists and creationists.
