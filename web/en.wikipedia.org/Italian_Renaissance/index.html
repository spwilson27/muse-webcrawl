The Italian Renaissance (Italian: Rinascimento) was a period in Italian history between the 14th and 16th centuries. The period is known for the initial development of the broader Renaissance culture that spread across Western and Central Europe and marked the transition from the Middle Ages to modernity. Proponents of a "long Renaissance" argue that it started around the year 1300 and lasted until about 1600.

Origins

The Renaissance began in Tuscany, centred on the city of Florence. Florence, one of the several city-states of the peninsula, rose to economic prominence through trade and banking, and the wealthy Medici family became leading patrons of the arts. The city's guilds, its wool trade, and its civic humanist culture all contributed. The rediscovery of classical Greek and Latin texts, some preserved by Byzantine scholars who fled west after the fall of Constantinople in 1453, fed the humanist movement.

Characteristics

Italian Renaissance culture emphasised humanism — the study of classical antiquity and the belief in human potential — and a revival of classical learning. In art it produced linear perspective, foreshortening, chiaroscuro, and a renewed interest in naturalism and anatomy. In architecture it revived classical orders, symmetry, and proportion.

Key figures

Filippo Brunelleschi engineered the dome of Florence Cathedral (completed 1436) and formalised linear perspective. Leon Battista Alberti wrote foundational treatises on painting and architecture. The High Renaissance (c. 1495–1527) produced Leonardo da Vinci, Michelangelo, and Raphael — the period's defining masters — under patrons including the Medici popes in Rome. In literature, Dante, Petrarch, and Boccaccio were proto-Renaissance pioneers, while Machiavelli reshaped political thought with The Prince (written 1513).

Spread and decline

From Italy, Renaissance ideas spread through Europe, carried by trade, diplomacy, print, and travelling artists, giving rise to regional Renaissances in France, England, the Low Countries, Germany, Spain, and elsewhere. The Sack of Rome in 1527 and the wars of foreign invasion in Italy hastened the transition to Mannerism.
