Romanov dynasty

The Romanov dynasty was the ruling royal house of Russia from 1613 to 1917. It was the second imperial dynasty to rule Russia, following the Rurik dynasty, and came to power after the Time of Troubles, a period of civil war and foreign intervention. The Romanovs ruled Russia for more than three centuries, transforming it from a medium-sized tsardom into one of the largest empires in world history, stretching from Poland in the west to the Pacific Ocean in the east.

Origins

The Romanov family were boyars of Muscovy whose ancestry can be traced to the 14th century. The family rose to prominence through the marriage of Anastasia Romanovna to Ivan the Terrible, the first Tsar of All Russia, in 1547. Anastasia was the great-aunt of Michael I, the first Romanov tsar. Following the death of Feodor I, the last Rurikid tsar, in 1598, Russia descended into the Time of Troubles. In 1613, a Zemsky Sobor (national assembly) elected the sixteen-year-old Michael Romanov as tsar, beginning the dynasty's rule.

The early Romanovs

Michael I (reigned 1613–1645) restored stability after the Time of Troubles. His son Alexis (1645–1676) codified Russian law in the Sobornoye Ulozheniye of 1649, which formalised serfdom. Alexis's son Feodor III was followed, after a period of regency by Sophia Alekseyevna, by the co-rule of Ivan V and Peter I.

Peter the Great and the Empire

Peter I, known as Peter the Great, radically reformed Russia along Western European lines, founding St. Petersburg in 1703 and defeating Sweden in the Great Northern War. In 1721 he proclaimed the Russian Empire and took the title of Emperor. His reforms modernised the army, navy, government administration, and aspects of Russian culture and dress.

The eighteenth century

After Peter's death in 1725, Russia experienced an era of palace coups. The reign of Catherine II (1762–1796), known as Catherine the Great, is often regarded as the Golden Age of the Russian Empire: she expanded its territory through wars against the Ottoman Empire and the partitions of Poland, patronised the arts, and corresponded with Enlightenment philosophers. Her son Paul I was assassinated in 1801.

The nineteenth century

Alexander I defeated Napoleon in the Patriotic War of 1812 and played a leading role at the Congress of Vienna. Nicholas I maintained a conservative, autocratic regime. Alexander II, known as the Liberator, emancipated the serfs in 1861 but was assassinated in 1881. Alexander III pursued Russification and repression, while his son Nicholas II became the last emperor.

Fall of the dynasty

Russia's involvement in the First World War, combined with economic hardship and political unrest, led to the February Revolution of 1917, in which Nicholas II abdicated, ending over 300 years of Romanov rule. Nicholas, his wife Alexandra, and their five children were executed by Bolshevik forces in Yekaterinburg on 17 July 1918. Many other members of the family were also killed, though some escaped into exile.

Legacy

The Romanovs left a lasting legacy in Russian architecture, including the Winter Palace and the palaces of St. Petersburg. In 1998 the remains of Nicholas II and his family were reinterred in the Peter and Paul Cathedral in St. Petersburg, and in 2000 the Russian Orthodox Church canonised them as passion-bearers.
