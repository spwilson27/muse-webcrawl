Elementary charge

The elementary charge, usually denoted by e, is a fundamental physical constant: the electric charge carried by a single proton, or, equivalently, the magnitude of the negative charge carried by a single electron. Its value is exactly 1.602176634×10−19 coulombs, fixed by definition since the 2019 revision of the SI.

Millikan's oil drop experiment (1909) first measured the elementary charge with precision.

Quantization of charge

Free particles carry charges that are integer multiples of e: the proton has charge +e and the electron −e. Quarks carry fractional charges of ±1/3 e or ±2/3 e, but they are always confined within composite hadrons of integer charge. The quantization of charge is one of the deep unexplained regularities of physics, though it follows naturally in theories containing magnetic monopoles or grand unification.

History

The term "electron" for a unit of charge was proposed by G. Johnstone Stoney in 1891. J. J. Thomson measured the electron's charge-to-mass ratio in 1897, and Robert Millikan's oil drop experiment determined e itself. Since May 2019, e has been an exact defining constant of the SI, used in the definition of the ampere.
