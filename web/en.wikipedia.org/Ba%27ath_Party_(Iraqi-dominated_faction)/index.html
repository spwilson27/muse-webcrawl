Ba'ath Party (Iraqi-dominated faction)

The Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party – Iraq Region, commonly known as the Ba'ath Party (Iraqi-dominated faction), was the Iraqi branch of the original Ba'ath Party. It split from the Syrian-dominated faction in 1966, following the schism within the unified Ba'ath movement, and thereafter looked to Baghdad rather than Damascus as the movement's rightful center.

Arab Socialist Ba'ath Party – Iraq Region
Founded: early 1950s (as part of the unified Ba'ath)
Ideology: Ba'athism, Arab nationalism, Arab socialism
Headquarters: Baghdad, Iraq
Historical leaders: Fuad al-Rikabi, Ahmed Hassan al-Bakr, Saddam Hussein

History

The Ba'ath was founded in Syria in 1947 by Michel Aflaq and Salah al-Din al-Bitar, and an Iraqi regional branch was established in the early 1950s, initially led by Fuad al-Rikabi. Iraqi Ba'athists participated in the turbulent politics of the republic after 1958 and briefly shared power after the February 1963 coup against Abd al-Karim Qasim.

The 1966 split

In February 1966 a military coup in Syria brought a radical faction to power in Damascus and ousted the party's historic leadership. The Iraqi branch refused to recognize the new Syrian command, and the movement split permanently into rival Iraqi- and Syrian-dominated factions, each claiming to be the legitimate Ba'ath Party and denouncing the other as deviating from its principles.

Rule in Iraq

The Iraqi Ba'ath seized power in the coup of 17 July 1968. Ahmed Hassan al-Bakr became president, with Saddam Hussein rising as his deputy and taking the presidency himself in 1979. Under Saddam, the party became the backbone of an authoritarian state, penetrating government, the military, education, and mass organizations, while promoting Arab nationalism, state-led development, and the Ba'athist slogan "Unity, Freedom, Socialism."

Downfall

The party was dissolved and banned after the 2003 invasion of Iraq, when the Coalition Provisional Authority issued its de-Ba'athification order in May 2003, removing senior party members from public employment. Remnants of the party later participated in insurgent activity, and its legacy remains deeply controversial in Iraq.
