Absolute space and time

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Absolute space and time is a theory of the foundation of physics holding that space and time are independent, fixed structures that exist whether or not anything occupies them. In Isaac Newton's formulation in the Principia Mathematica (1687), absolute space "remains always similar and immovable" and absolute time "flows equably without relation to anything external." Newton's framework treated space as a three-dimensional Euclidean stage and time as a universal clock, and it underlies classical mechanics.

Newton's view

Newton distinguished absolute space and time from their relative, measured counterparts in the Scholium to the Definitions of the Principia. To show that absolute motion is meaningful, he proposed the rotating bucket experiment: the surface of water in a spinning bucket becomes concave even when the water is at rest relative to the bucket, which Newton took as evidence of rotation relative to absolute space itself.

Opposition

Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz argued against Newton in the Leibniz–Clarke correspondence (1715–16), maintaining that space is merely the order of relations among objects, so that shifting the whole universe three feet to the left would make no observable difference. George Berkeley and later Ernst Mach similarly criticised absolute space; Mach proposed that inertia arises from relation to the mass of the universe as a whole. Einstein credited Mach's critique as an influence on relativity.

Relativity

Special relativity (1905) abolished absolute simultaneity and unified space and time into spacetime, while general relativity (1915) made spacetime itself dynamic and influenced by matter. Nevertheless, in the weak-field, low-velocity limits of everyday experience, Newton's absolutes remain an excellent approximation, and the notion of an absolute reference survives in modified form in topics such as the cosmic microwave background rest frame.
