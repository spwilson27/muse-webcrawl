Nicotinic acetylcholine receptor

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) are ligand-gated ion channels in the plasma membrane of neurons and other cells that respond to the neurotransmitter acetylcholine and to the agonist nicotine, from which they take their name. They belong to the Cys-loop superfamily of ligand-gated ion channels, which also includes GABAA, glycine and 5-HT3 receptors. Unlike the G protein-coupled muscarinic acetylcholine receptors, nicotinic receptors directly open a cation channel when activated.

Structure

Nicotinic receptors are pentamers assembled from combinations of subunits. In vertebrates, subunits are encoded by 17 genes (α1–α10, β1–β4, γ, δ, ε). The muscle-type receptor found at the neuromuscular junction has the subunit composition (α1)2β1δγ in fetal muscle and (α1)2β1δε in adults. Neuronal receptors are either homopentamers (such as α7) or heteropentamers of α and β subunits. Each subunit has a large extracellular N-terminal domain, four transmembrane helices (M1–M4), and the M2 helices line the central ion pore. Two acetylcholine-binding sites lie at the interfaces between α subunits and their neighbours.

Function

Binding of two acetylcholine molecules triggers a conformational change that opens the channel, allowing Na+ and K+ (and, for some subtypes, Ca2+) to flow down their electrochemical gradients. At the neuromuscular junction this produces the end-plate potential that initiates muscle contraction. In the central nervous system, nicotinic receptors modulate the release of other neurotransmitters and are implicated in attention, learning, memory and reward; α4β2 receptors are the principal high-affinity nicotine-binding sites in the brain and are central to nicotine addiction.

Pharmacology and clinical significance

Nicotinic receptors are targets of many drugs and toxins. Antagonists include the plant alkaloid tubocurarine, historically used as a muscle relaxant, α-bungarotoxin from krait venom, and the ganglion blocker mecamylamine. Agonists and partial agonists include nicotine and varenicline, used in smoking cessation. Autoantibodies against muscle nicotinic receptors cause the autoimmune disease myasthenia gravis, and mutations in receptor subunits cause forms of congenital myasthenic syndrome and autosomal dominant nocturnal frontal lobe epilepsy.
