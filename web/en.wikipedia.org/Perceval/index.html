Perceval

Perceval (also spelled Percival, Parsifal or Parzival) is a figure of Arthurian legend, one of the Knights of the Round Table and the original hero of the quest for the Holy Grail. He first appears in Chrétien de Troyes's unfinished romance Perceval, the Story of the Grail (c. 1180s), the earliest known Grail narrative.

Story

Raised in ignorant isolation in the Welsh forest by his widowed mother, who wished to keep him from the life that killed his father and brothers, the young Perceval encounters knights, mistakes them for angels or gods, and rides to King Arthur's court to become a knight himself. In the course of his adventures he is trained by the knight Gornemant and falls in love with Blancheflor. He then visits the castle of the wounded Fisher King, where he witnesses a mysterious procession — a bleeding lance and a grail carried by a maiden — but, following advice not to talk too much, fails to ask the question that would have healed the king and restored his land. Learning of his failure, and that his mother has died of grief, Perceval vows to find the Grail castle again; Chrétien's poem breaks off unfinished amid Perceval's and Gawain's adventures.

Later versions

Numerous continuations completed Chrétien's story. Wolfram von Eschenbach's Middle High German epic Parzival (c. 1210) makes Perceval the Grail king's heir and gives the legend a profound spiritual dimension; it inspired Richard Wagner's opera Parsifal (1882). In later prose romances and in Malory's Le Morte d'Arthur, Perceval remains one of the three Grail achievers alongside Galahad (who increasingly supplants him as chief Grail hero) and Bors. The character's innocence and gradual education made him a symbol of the "pure fool" who succeeds through simplicity of heart.
