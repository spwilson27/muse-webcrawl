Gambler's fallacy

The gambler's fallacy, also known as the Monte Carlo fallacy, is the mistaken belief that if a particular event occurs more frequently than normal during the past, it is less likely to happen in the future (or vice versa), when it has otherwise been established that the probability of such events does not depend on what has happened in the past. Such events are statistically independent, and the history of outcomes has no bearing on future ones.

The Monte Carlo incident

The fallacy is named for a famous episode at the Monte Carlo Casino on 18 August 1913, when the roulette ball landed on black 26 times in a row. Gamblers lost millions betting on red, reasoning that the long streak of black made red "due." In fact each spin is independent, and the probability of red remains the same on every spin.

Psychology

The fallacy arises from the representativeness heuristic and a misapplication of the law of large numbers to small samples: people expect even short sequences to be "balanced." Its inverse — the hot-hand fallacy — is the belief that a person on a winning streak is more likely to keep winning. Both distort decisions in gambling, investing, and everyday judgment, such as believing that after several daughters a family is "due" a son.
