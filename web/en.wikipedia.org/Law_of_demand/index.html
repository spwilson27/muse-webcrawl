Law of demand

A demand curve slopes downward: as price falls, quantity demanded rises
Law of demand
FieldMicroeconomics
StatementOther things equal, as the price of a good increases, the quantity demanded decreases, and vice versa
Formalised byAlfred Marshall, Principles of Economics (1890); anticipated by Augustin Cournot (1838) and others
MechanismsSubstitution effect; income effect
ExceptionsGiffen goods; Veblen (conspicuous) goods; speculative bubbles

The law of demand is a fundamental principle of economics which states that there is an inverse relationship between price and quantity demanded: consumers will buy more of a good when its price falls and less when its price rises, ceteris paribus — all other factors being equal. Graphically it is represented by the downward-sloping demand curve, one half of the supply-and-demand model.

Explanation

Two effects drive the law. The substitution effect: when a good's price rises, buyers switch towards relatively cheaper alternatives. The income effect: a higher price reduces the buyer's real purchasing power. For normal goods the two effects reinforce each other; for inferior goods they oppose, and in the rare extreme where the income effect dominates, the result is a Giffen good, whose demand rises with price — the classic (and historically contested) examples involve staple foods consumed by the very poor. Veblen goods, by contrast, are desired partly because they are expensive, as status signals.

Demand versus quantity demanded

Economists distinguish a movement along the demand curve (a change in quantity demanded caused by the good's own price) from a shift of the whole curve (a change in demand), caused by changes in income, tastes, expectations, or the prices of substitutes and complements. Together with the law of supply, the law of demand determines market equilibrium; the responsiveness of quantity demanded to price is measured by the price elasticity of demand, central to tax incidence, pricing strategy and competition policy.

History

Antoine Augustin Cournot gave the first mathematical statement in 1838; Jules Dupuit, Hans von Mangoldt and Fleeming Jenkin developed the idea before Alfred Marshall's 1890 synthesis made the demand curve a standard tool. Marshall noted that the law could be derived from diminishing marginal utility — each additional unit of a good yields less satisfaction, so buyers will take more only at a lower price.
