Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem

Sampling theorem
FieldSignal processing
Key figuresHarry Nyquist, Claude Shannon

The Nyquist–Shannon sampling theorem is a fundamental result in signal processing which states that a continuous signal that contains no frequencies higher than B hertz can be completely determined by samples taken at a rate greater than 2B samples per second. This minimum rate, 2B, is called the Nyquist rate.

Statement

If a function x(t) contains no frequencies higher than B hertz, it can be completely determined by giving its values at a series of points spaced 1/(2B) seconds apart. Reconstruction is performed by summing a series of sinc functions scaled by the sample values, a process known as Whittaker–Shannon interpolation.

Aliasing

If a signal is sampled below the Nyquist rate, higher frequencies are misrepresented as lower ones, a distortion called aliasing. Practical systems therefore use anti-aliasing filters and sample somewhat above the theoretical minimum.

History

Harry Nyquist derived related results in 1928 in the context of telegraphy, and Claude Shannon proved the theorem rigorously in his 1949 paper "Communication in the Presence of Noise". Related work was done independently by E. T. Whittaker and Vladimir Kotelnikov.

Applications

The theorem underpins all digital audio and communications: compact discs, for example, sample audio at 44,100 Hz, slightly more than twice the roughly 20 kHz limit of human hearing.
