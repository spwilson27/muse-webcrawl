The Treaty of Ghent (8 Stat. 218) was the peace treaty that ended the War of 1812 between the United States and the United Kingdom. It took effect in February 1815. Both sides signed it on 24 December 1814, in the city of Ghent in the United Netherlands (now in Belgium). The treaty restored relations between the two nations to status quo ante bellum (the state of things before the war), restoring the pre-war borders of June 1812.

Background

By 1814, peace was increasingly desired by both parties. The British, preoccupied with the end of the Napoleonic Wars, and the American commissioners — who included John Quincy Adams, Henry Clay, Albert Gallatin, James A. Bayard, and Jonathan Russell — negotiated in Ghent through the summer and autumn of 1814. The British delegation included William Adams, Henry Goulburn, and Admiral Lord Gambier.

Terms

The treaty released all prisoners, restored all war lands and possessions to their status before the war, and required the United States to return captured Canadian territory while Britain returned the Louisiana territory it had seized. The treaty made no major changes to the pre-war situation, but did make a few promises. Britain promised to return captured slaves, but instead paid the United States £250,000 for them. Both sides also agreed to work toward the abolition of the international slave trade.

Ratification and aftermath

The treaty was unanimously approved by the United States Senate on 16 February 1815 and proclaimed by President James Madison the next day. Because communications were slow, news of the treaty did not reach the armies around New Orleans until after the famous Battle of New Orleans was fought on 8 January 1815. The treaty effectively ended the war, and the last fighting occurred in mid-February. Boundaries with Canada were later settled by subsequent agreements, including the Rush–Bagot Agreement of 1817 and the Convention of 1818.
