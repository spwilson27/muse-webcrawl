Disaster risk reduction

Disaster risk reduction (DRR) is a systematic approach to identifying, assessing and reducing the risks of disaster. It aims to reduce socio-economic vulnerability to disaster as well as dealing with the environmental and other hazards that trigger them. DRR is commonly used by governments, international organisations, non-governmental organisations (NGOs), communities and individuals, to reduce the loss of life, personal injury and economic damage from natural hazards such as floods, earthquakes, cyclones and droughts, as well as human-made hazards.

History

The field grew out of civil defence and emergency management traditions of the mid-20th century. The United Nations designated the 1990s the International Decade for Natural Disaster Reduction. The Hyogo Framework for Action (2005–2015) set global priorities, and its successor, the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction, was adopted in 2015 at the Third UN World Conference on Disaster Risk Reduction in Sendai, Japan. The Sendai Framework sets seven global targets, including substantially reducing global disaster mortality and economic losses by 2030.

Key concepts

Risk is conventionally expressed as a function of hazard, exposure, vulnerability and capacity. DRR measures include structural interventions such as levees and earthquake-resistant construction; non-structural measures such as land-use planning, building codes and public awareness; and early warning systems. Climate change adaptation is increasingly integrated with DRR, since climate change alters the frequency and intensity of weather-related hazards.

Approaches

Community-based disaster risk management emphasises local knowledge and participation. Ecosystem-based approaches use wetlands, mangroves and forests as natural buffers. Risk transfer mechanisms such as insurance and catastrophe bonds spread financial losses. Critics note that DRR funding remains small compared with spending on post-disaster relief and reconstruction.
