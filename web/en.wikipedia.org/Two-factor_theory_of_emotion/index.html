Two-factor theory of emotion

Two-factor theory of emotion
Proposed by: Stanley Schachter and Jerome Singer (1962)
Factors: physiological arousal + cognitive label

The two-factor theory of emotion, proposed by Stanley Schachter and Jerome E. Singer in 1962, holds that emotion results from two components: physiological arousal and a cognitive interpretation, or label, of that arousal based on the immediate situation.

The Schachter–Singer experiment

Participants injected with epinephrine (which produces arousal) were placed with a confederate acting either euphoric or angry. Those not informed of the injection's side effects tended to "catch" the confederate's apparent emotion, while informed participants attributed their arousal to the drug and reported weaker emotions — interpreted as evidence that unexplained arousal is labelled from context.

Related research

The theory underlies the misattribution of arousal phenomenon, famously illustrated by the Dutton and Aron "shaky bridge" study of 1974, and influenced later appraisal theories of emotion, though replications and interpretations have been debated.
