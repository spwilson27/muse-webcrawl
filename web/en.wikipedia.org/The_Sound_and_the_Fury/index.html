The Sound and the Fury is a novel by the American author William Faulkner, published in 1929. Set in Faulkner's fictional Yoknapatawpha County, Mississippi, it traces the decline of the Compsons, a once-aristocratic Southern family, through four sections narrated from different perspectives. Employing stream of consciousness and radical shifts in time, it is regarded as Faulkner's masterpiece and one of the greatest novels of the twentieth century. Its title alludes to Macbeth's soliloquy: life is "a tale / Told by an idiot, full of sound and fury, / Signifying nothing."

Structure

The first section (April 7, 1928) is narrated by Benjy, the intellectually disabled youngest Compson son, whose impressions jump unmarked across thirty years and who mourns the loss of his sister Caddy. The second (June 2, 1910) follows Quentin, the eldest son, on the day of his suicide at Harvard, obsessed with Caddy's promiscuity and the family's honor. The third (April 6, 1928) is narrated by the bitter, bigoted Jason, who schemes against Caddy's daughter, also named Quentin. The fourth section (April 8, 1928, Easter Sunday) shifts to third person centered on Dilsey, the family's Black servant, whose endurance and faith close the novel on a note of qualified redemption as the younger Quentin escapes with Jason's hoarded money.

Themes and publication

Faulkner said the novel grew from an image of a little girl's muddy drawers as she climbed a pear tree to peer into a window — the scene of the children's grandmother's funeral. The book sold poorly at first but gained reputation through the 1940s, aided by Malcolm Cowley's The Portable Faulkner (1946), for which Faulkner wrote an appendix clarifying the Compson genealogy. His Nobel Prize in Literature (1949) cemented the novel's standing.
