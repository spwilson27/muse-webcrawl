Silage

Silage is a type of fodder made from green foliage crops which have been preserved by fermentation to the point of souring. It is fed to domestic livestock, including cattle, sheep and goats. Silage is made either by placing cut green vegetation in a silo or pit, by piling it in a large heap and compressing it down so as to purify the air, or by wrapping bales in plastic film.

Production

The most common silage crop is maize (corn), harvested whole while still green; grasses, sorghum, alfalfa and other legumes are also widely ensiled. The crop is typically chopped finely to aid compaction, which expels oxygen. In the resulting anaerobic conditions, naturally occurring lactic acid bacteria ferment plant sugars to lactic acid, dropping the pH to around 4 and preserving the material against spoilage. Well-made silage keeps for years and loses little nutritional value.

Use and variants

Silage allows farmers to store summer-grown forage for winter feeding and is a cornerstone of intensive dairy and beef production. When wrapped round bales are fermented the product is often called baleage or haylage (the latter also used for higher-dry-matter grass silage). Poorly sealed silage can develop moulds and listeria, and the effluent produced is a potent pollutant of watercourses.
