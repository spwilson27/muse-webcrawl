Jargon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Jargon is the specialized terminology associated with a particular field or area of activity. Jargon is normally employed in a particular communicative context and may not be well understood outside that context. The context is usually a particular occupation (that is, in a certain trade, profession, vernacular or academic field), but any ingroup can have jargon. The key characteristic that distinguishes jargon from the rest of a language is its specialized vocabulary, which includes terms and definitions of words that are uncommon or have been given a specific meaning, and also some cases of more widespread words being assigned a different meaning. Also, words that are commonly used in everyday language but have specific technical meanings in certain fields are characteristic of jargon, while slang is informal language that is used by a particular social group.

Etymology

The term jargon may have its roots in the Old French word gargon, meaning "Twittering of birds", "warbling", "chattering", or "murmuring". The word appeared in English in the 14th century, originally in the sense of "unintelligible speech", and was later applied to the language of particular groups or professions.

Use

Jargon is used by specialists to communicate efficiently and precisely among themselves. Technical terms can pack a great deal of meaning into a single word or phrase, allowing experts to discuss complex ideas quickly. However, jargon can also be a barrier to understanding for outsiders, and its excessive use may obscure meaning, exclude non-specialists, or make the speaker appear pretentious.

Many professions and fields have developed extensive jargon, including medicine, law, computing, business, science, and the military. Medical jargon, for example, includes Latin and Greek-derived terms for anatomy and disease; legal jargon includes terms such as habeas corpus and pro bono; computer jargon includes words like bandwidth, firewall, and debugging.

Criticism

Critics of jargon argue that it can be used deliberately to confuse, deceive, or impress rather than to inform. Plain-language movements in government, law, and medicine have sought to reduce jargon in documents intended for the general public, replacing obscure terms with clearer alternatives where doing so does not sacrifice precision.
