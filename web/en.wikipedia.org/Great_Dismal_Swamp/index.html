The Great Dismal Swamp is a large swamp on the coastal plain of southeastern Virginia and northeastern North Carolina, between Norfolk, Virginia, and Edenton, North Carolina. Once extending over as much as a million acres, the forested wetland now covers roughly 750 square kilometres in its remnant core. At its centre lies Lake Drummond, one of only two natural freshwater lakes in Virginia. The swamp's dense stands of Atlantic white cedar, bald cypress, tupelo and maple shelter black bears, bobcats and more than 200 bird species.

History

Indigenous peoples lived in and around the swamp for millennia. In the colonial era, William Byrd II surveyed the Virginia–North Carolina boundary through the swamp in 1728 and popularised the name "Dismal". George Washington visited in 1763 and promoted a scheme — through the Dismal Swamp Land Company — to drain the swamp and dig a canal; enslaved labourers dug the Washington Ditch and other canals, and the Dismal Swamp Canal, completed in the early 19th century, became an important commercial waterway linking Chesapeake Bay with Albemarle Sound. In the 19th and 20th centuries, logging companies heavily timbered the swamp, building railroads deep into it.

Maroons and the Underground Railroad

The swamp's inaccessibility made it a refuge: from the late 17th century until the Civil War, thousands of self-emancipated African Americans — the Great Dismal Swamp maroons — lived in hidden communities on its interior islands, one of the largest maroon societies in North America. The swamp also served as a station on the Underground Railroad and inspired Harriet Beecher Stowe's novel Dred: A Tale of the Great Dismal Swamp (1856) and Henry Wadsworth Longfellow's poem "The Slave in the Dismal Swamp".

Conservation

In 1973 the Union Camp Corporation donated 49,100 acres to The Nature Conservancy, which transferred the land to the federal government; the Great Dismal Swamp National Wildlife Refuge was established in 1974. Management today focuses on restoring the swamp's hydrology and Atlantic white cedar forests, both damaged by ditching, logging and severe peat fires such as the 2011 Lateral West fire.
