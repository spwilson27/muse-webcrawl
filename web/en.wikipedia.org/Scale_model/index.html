Scale model

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A scale model is a physical model that represents an object, usually at a smaller size, while maintaining accurate proportions between all of its parts. The ratio between the model and its subject is the scale: in a 1:72 scale model aircraft, one unit of length on the model represents 72 units on the real aircraft. Scale models are used in engineering, architecture, film-making, military planning, and as a widespread hobby.

Uses

Engineering and science

Engineers test scale models of aircraft in wind tunnels, ships in towing tanks, and buildings and bridges under simulated loads. Because physical behaviour does not always scale linearly—fluid dynamics, for example, depends on the Reynolds number—model testing requires scaling laws such as those derived from the Buckingham π theorem.

Architecture and planning

Architects build presentation and study models of buildings and urban developments to communicate designs to clients and the public. Common architectural scales include 1:50, 1:100, and 1:500.

Hobby modelling

Plastic model kits of aircraft, vehicles, ships, and figures are mass-produced by companies such as Airfix, Revell, and Tamiya in standard scales (1:35, 1:48, 1:72, 1:144 for aircraft; 1:700 and 1:350 for ships). Model railroading uses its own scale/gauge combinations such as HO scale (1:87) and N scale (1:160). Hobbyists value kits for historical accuracy, painting, and weathering techniques.

Standard scales

Certain scales became standardized within industries: 1:24 and 1:25 for model cars, 1:43 for die-cast cars, and 1:6 ("Playscale") for action figures. Die-cast collectible models are typically sold finished, whereas plastic kits are assembled and painted by the modeller.
