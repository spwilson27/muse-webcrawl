Twelve-step program

Twelve-step fellowships hold peer-support meetings in which members share their experience of addiction and recovery.

A twelve-step program is a set of guiding principles for recovery from addiction, compulsion and other behavioural problems, first set out by Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) in 1939 in the book Alcoholics Anonymous (the "Big Book"). The program combines peer support, sponsorship, and a spiritual (though not specifically religious) process summarised in twelve steps. It has been adopted, with the steps reworded to suit the problem addressed, by dozens of other fellowships, including Narcotics Anonymous, Gamblers Anonymous, Overeaters Anonymous and Al-Anon/Alateen (for families of alcoholics).

The Twelve Steps (summary)

The steps of AA, in summary, ask members to:

  1. Admit powerlessness over alcohol and that their lives had become unmanageable.
  2. Believe that a Power greater than themselves could restore them to sanity.
  3. Make a decision to turn their will and lives over to the care of God as they understood Him.
  4. Make a searching and fearless moral inventory of themselves.
  5. Admit to God, to themselves and to another human being the exact nature of their wrongs.
  6. Be entirely ready to have God remove these defects of character.
  7. Humbly ask Him to remove their shortcomings.
  8. Make a list of all persons they had harmed, and become willing to make amends to them all.
  9. Make direct amends to such people wherever possible, except when to do so would injure them or others.
  10. Continue to take personal inventory and promptly admit when they were wrong.
  11. Seek through prayer and meditation to improve their conscious contact with God, praying for knowledge of His will and the power to carry it out.
  12. Having had a spiritual awakening, carry the message to other alcoholics and practise these principles in all their affairs.

Organisation and practice

Twelve-step fellowships are non-professional, self-supporting and free to attend; they are governed by the Twelve Traditions, which emphasise anonymity, group autonomy and a single primary purpose. Newcomers are encouraged to attend meetings, find a sponsor, and work the steps. Alongside the fellowships, many professional treatment programmes incorporate twelve-step principles ("twelve-step facilitation" and the Minnesota Model).

Evidence and criticism

A 2020 Cochrane review found that AA and twelve-step facilitation interventions were at least as effective as other established treatments for alcohol use disorder, and produced higher rates of continuous abstinence, though research on other fellowships is thinner. Criticisms include the program's spiritual language (an obstacle for some non-religious participants, giving rise to secular alternatives), variable retention rates, and the limited evidence base for some adapted fellowships. Alternatives include SMART Recovery and LifeRing Secular Recovery.
