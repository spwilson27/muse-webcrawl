Tailings dam

Purpose: Permanent storage of mine tailings
Construction methods: Upstream, downstream, centreline
Examples: Syncrude Tailings Dam (Canada) among the largest embankments
Hazards: Static liquefaction, overtopping, seismic failure

A tailings dam is an earth-fill embankment dam used to store the waste products of mining operations (tailings). Unlike conventional water-retention dams, tailings dams are typically raised in stages over the life of a mine as more waste accumulates, and must remain stable permanently after closure.

Construction methods

Three principal designs are used. In upstream construction, each new raise is built on top of previously deposited tailings — cheapest but most vulnerable to liquefaction, and now banned in some jurisdictions. In downstream construction the dam grows outward onto firm ground, the safest but most material-hungry method. Centreline construction raises the crest vertically and is intermediate in cost and stability.

Failures

Tailings dam failures occur at a much higher rate than failures of water dams and can be catastrophic, releasing millions of cubic metres of slurry that flow for kilometres. Notable disasters include the Stava dam collapse in Italy (1985, 268 deaths), the Mount Polley failure in Canada (2014), the Mariana (Fundão) disaster in Brazil (2015) and the Brumadinho disaster (2019, 270 deaths), the latter caused by static liquefaction of an upstream dam and leading Brazil to ban upstream construction entirely.
