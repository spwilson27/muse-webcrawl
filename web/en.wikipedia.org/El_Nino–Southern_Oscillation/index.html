El Nino–Southern Oscillation

Sea surface temperature anomalies in the Pacific during a strong El Niño event

The El Niño–Southern Oscillation (ENSO) is an irregular periodic variation in winds and sea surface temperatures over the tropical eastern Pacific Ocean, affecting the climate of much of the tropics and subtropics. The warming phase of the sea temperature is known as El Niño and the cooling phase as La Niña. The Southern Oscillation is the accompanying atmospheric component, the change in air pressure between the western and eastern tropical Pacific.

Mechanism

Under normal conditions, trade winds push warm surface water westward across the Pacific, piling it up near Indonesia while cold water upwells off South America. During an El Niño, the trade winds weaken, warm water spreads eastward, and upwelling is suppressed. The Walker circulation weakens correspondingly, shifting rainfall patterns: drought typically affects Indonesia and Australia, while unusually heavy rain falls on the normally arid coasts of Peru and Ecuador.

Impacts

ENSO events, which recur every two to seven years and last about nine to twelve months, influence weather worldwide through teleconnections, affecting monsoons, hurricane seasons, agriculture, and fisheries — including the collapse of the Peruvian anchoveta fishery during strong events. Particularly strong El Niño episodes occurred in 1982–83, 1997–98, and 2015–16.
