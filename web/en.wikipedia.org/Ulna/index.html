The ulna (PL: ulnae) is a long bone found in the forearm that stretches from the elbow to the wrist, and when in anatomical position, is found on the medial side of the forearm. It is the larger of the two bones of the forearm, the other being the radius, and it defines the elbow joint together with the humerus.

Structure

The ulna is a long bone found in the forearm. At its proximal end it bears the olecranon process — the bony point of the elbow — and the trochlear notch, which grips the trochlea of the humerus to form the hinge of the elbow joint. The coronoid process projects forward below the notch. The shaft of the ulna is triangular in cross-section along much of its length and connects to the radius through the interosseous membrane. At the distal end, the head of the ulna articulates with the wrist, and the small styloid process provides attachment for ligaments of the wrist joint.

Function

Together with the radius, the ulna allows the forearm to rotate in pronation and supination. Unlike the radius, which carries most of the load at the wrist, the ulna is the principal bone of the elbow joint, and it provides attachment for many muscles that flex and extend the forearm and fingers, including the triceps brachii at the olecranon.

Clinical significance

The ulna is frequently fractured, often together with the radius, in falls onto an outstretched hand. A fracture of the ulnar shaft with dislocation of the radial head is known as a Monteggia fracture. The ulnar nerve passes behind the medial epicondyle of the humerus near the proximal ulna; a blow here produces the tingling sensation commonly called hitting one's "funny bone".
