Inverse condemnation

FieldUnited States constitutional and property law
BasisTakings Clause, Fifth Amendment
ClaimantProperty owner (as plaintiff)
RemedyJust compensation
ContrastEminent domain (direct condemnation)

Inverse condemnation is a legal cause of action in United States law in which a property owner seeks compensation for a taking or damaging of private property by the government that occurred without the formal exercise of eminent domain. It is called "inverse" because the roles are reversed: instead of the government suing to condemn the property, the owner brings the action.

Background

The Takings Clause of the Fifth Amendment to the United States Constitution provides that private property shall not "be taken for public use, without just compensation". Where government action — such as flooding caused by a public project, severe aircraft noise from a government airport, or a regulation that goes "too far" — effectively takes property without formal condemnation proceedings, the owner may sue in inverse condemnation to recover the compensation the Constitution guarantees.

Types of claims

Claims may allege a physical taking, as in United States v. Causby (1946), where frequent military overflights were held to take an easement over a chicken farm, or a regulatory taking, governed by the ad hoc balancing test of Penn Central Transportation Co. v. New York City (1978) and the categorical rules of cases such as Lucas v. South Carolina Coastal Council (1992).

Procedure

Claims against the federal government are generally brought in the Court of Federal Claims under the Tucker Act; claims against states proceed under state constitutions and statutes. Depending on jurisdiction, a jury or a judge determines the amount of just compensation, generally the fair market value of the property interest taken.

See also

Related doctrines include the per se rule for permanent physical occupations from Loretto v. Teleprompter Manhattan CATV Corp. (1982) and the exactions doctrine of Nollan v. California Coastal Commission and Dolan v. City of Tigard.
