Deadbeat escapement

The deadbeat escapement is a type of escapement used in pendulum clocks, invented around 1675 by Richard Towneley and first widely applied by the clockmaker George Graham from about 1715. It is named for its key property: the second hand moves with a "dead" motion — stepping forward once per second with no recoil — and the escape wheel does not push the pendulum backward during its swing.

How it works

In the earlier anchor escapement, the escape wheel recoils slightly as each tooth lands, disturbing the pendulum and limiting accuracy. In the deadbeat design, the pallets of the anchor have two distinct faces: curved locking faces, shaped as arcs centred on the anchor's pivot so that a resting tooth slides neither forward nor back, and flat impulse faces that give the pendulum its push. Because the wheel remains perfectly still while a tooth rests on a locking face, the pendulum swings freely and errors from the wheel train are greatly reduced.

Use

The deadbeat escapement became the standard for precision regulator clocks in observatories and for most quality longcase (grandfather) clocks from the eighteenth century until pendulum clocks were superseded. Its seconds hand advancing in discrete one-second steps is also the visual origin of the "stepping" seconds motion associated with precision timepieces.
