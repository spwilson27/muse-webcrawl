Tariff in American history

Tariffs have played a central role in American economic and political history, serving for the republic's first 130 years both as the federal government's chief source of revenue and as its main instrument of industrial policy. Disputes over protective tariffs — between an industrializing North and an export-dependent, slaveholding South — were among the defining sectional conflicts of the 19th century.

Early republic and sectional conflict

The Tariff of 1789, the second act of the First Congress, levied modest duties mainly for revenue. Alexander Hamilton's Report on Manufactures (1791) argued for protection of infant industries. Duties rose with the Tariff of 1816, the "Tariff of Abominations" of 1828, which provoked South Carolina's Nullification Crisis of 1832–33, and fell under the Compromise Tariff of 1833 and the Walker Tariff of 1846.

Civil War to the Great Depression

The Morrill Tariff (1861) began a half-century of high Republican protection, continued by the McKinley Tariff (1890) and Dingley Tariff (1897). The Underwood Tariff (1913) cut rates substantially and was paired with the new federal income tax, ending tariffs' fiscal dominance. After the First World War, the Fordney–McCumber Tariff (1922) raised rates again, and the Smoot–Hawley Tariff Act (1930) pushed duties to historic highs amid the Great Depression, provoking foreign retaliation — economists debate how much it deepened the downturn.

Reciprocity and the modern era

The Reciprocal Trade Agreements Act (1934) shifted policy toward negotiated liberalization, carried forward through the GATT and the WTO. Average US tariffs fell to low single digits, punctuated by episodes of protection such as the 2018–2019 tariff rounds on steel, aluminium, and Chinese goods, which revived tariffs as an instrument of trade and industrial strategy in the 2020s.
