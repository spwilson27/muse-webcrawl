Legionary

The legionary (Latin: legionarius) was a professional heavy infantryman of the Roman army. Legionaries were Roman citizens who formed the backbone of the legions, distinct from the non-citizen auxiliary troops who provided cavalry, archers, and light infantry. A legion of the imperial period contained roughly 5,000 legionaries organised into ten cohorts, each subdivided into centuries commanded by centurions.

Recruitment and service

After the reforms traditionally attributed to Gaius Marius, legionaries enlisted voluntarily for long terms of service—eventually standardised at 20 years plus five in reserve—receiving regular pay, a share of booty, and on retirement a cash bonus or grant of land. Recruits swore an oath of loyalty (the sacramentum), underwent harsh training in marching, entrenchment, and weapons drill, and carried their equipment on famous long route marches, earning the nickname "Marius's mules".

Equipment

The classical legionary wore a helmet (galea), body armour that over time included mail (lorica hamata), the articulated plate armour known as lorica segmentata, and later scale armour. He carried a large curved rectangular shield (scutum), two heavy javelins (pila) designed to bend on impact and disable enemy shields, a short thrusting sword (gladius), and a dagger (pugio). On the march he also carried tools, rations, and personal kit weighing perhaps 30–45 kg.

Battle tactics

Legionaries fought in a disciplined formation, typically in several lines, throwing their pila in a volley before closing with shield and sword. The cohort system gave Roman commanders flexibility to relieve front-line troops, manoeuvre on broken ground, and combine with auxiliaries. Legionaries were also engineers: they built roads, bridges, fortifications, and a fortified camp every night on campaign.
