Greek alphabet

The 24 letters of the Greek alphabet.

The Greek alphabet has been used to write the Greek language since the late 9th or early 8th century BC. It is derived from the earlier Phoenician alphabet, and was the first alphabetic script to have distinct letters for vowels as well as consonants. Its 24 letters are still in use today, and the script is the ancestor of the Latin and Cyrillic alphabets.

History

The alphabet was adopted from Phoenician around the 9th–8th century BC, with letters for Semitic sounds not needed in Greek reassigned to vowels. Local ("epichoric") variants existed — the western variant, carried to Italy, gave rise to the Latin alphabet, while the eastern Ionic variant became standard. Athens officially adopted the Ionic alphabet in 403/402 BC, and it spread throughout the Greek world under Macedonian hegemony. Archaic letters such as digamma, qoppa and sampi fell out of alphabetic use but survived in the Greek numeral system.

Uses beyond Greek

Greek letters are widely used as symbols in mathematics and science: π for the circle constant, Δ for change, Σ for summation, α and β in many contexts, and ω for angular frequency. They are also used to name fraternities and sororities in North America, variants of concern of SARS-CoV-2, and stars in Bayer designations.
