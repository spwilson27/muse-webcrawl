Moon Treaty

The Moon Treaty, formally the Agreement Governing the Activities of States on the Moon and Other Celestial Bodies, is a multilateral treaty adopted by the United Nations General Assembly in 1979 and opened for signature that year. It entered into force in 1984, but has been ratified by only a small number of states — none of them a major spacefaring power — and is widely regarded as a failed or dormant instrument of space law.

Provisions

The treaty extends the principles of the 1967 Outer Space Treaty to the Moon and other celestial bodies in the solar system. Its most controversial provision, Article 11, declares that the Moon and its natural resources are "the common heritage of mankind," that no part of the Moon may become the property of any state, organisation, or person, and that an international regime should be established to govern the exploitation of lunar resources "as such exploitation is about to become feasible."

Failure to gain acceptance

The common-heritage and future-regime provisions alarmed the United States, the Soviet Union, and other spacefaring states, which declined to sign or ratify; organised opposition in the US, notably by the L5 Society, helped block Senate ratification. As of the 2020s, parties number fewer than twenty — including Australia, Mexico, the Philippines, Turkey, and Saudi Arabia (which later withdrew) — while signatories such as France and India never ratified.

Legacy

The treaty's weakness contrasts with the near-universal acceptance of the Outer Space Treaty. Later initiatives, including the United States' Commercial Space Launch Competitiveness Act of 2015 and the Artemis Accords of 2020, assert that extraction of space resources does not inherently constitute national appropriation — a position difficult to reconcile with the Moon Treaty's regime, and one reason its future remains uncertain.
