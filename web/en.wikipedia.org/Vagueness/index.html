Vagueness
Field: philosophy of language, logic, linguistics
Contrasted with: precision, exactness
Key theories: fuzzy logic, supervaluationism, epistemicism

Vagueness is the property of a term or concept whose extension is not sharply defined — there are borderline cases to which it is unclear whether the term applies. A classic example is the predicate "is bald": removing hairs one by one from a full head, there is no precise hair count at which a person stops being "not bald" and becomes "bald".

The sorites paradox illustrated: at no single step does "hairy" become "bald".

The sorites paradox

The sorites paradox (from Greek sōros, "heap") dramatizes vagueness: if one grain of sand does not make a heap, and adding one grain to a non-heap never makes a heap, then no number of grains makes a heap — an absurd conclusion. Vagueness thus challenges classical classical logic's principle of bivalence.

Theories of vagueness

  • Epistemicism (Timothy Williamson): there are sharp boundaries; we are just necessarily ignorant of them.
  • Supervaluationism: a vague sentence is true if true under all admissible precisifications.
  • Fuzzy logic: predicates have degrees of truth between 0 and 1.
  • Contextualism: extensions shift with conversational context.

Vagueness in law and language

Vagueness is distinct from ambiguity (multiple precise meanings). In law, overly vague statutes may be struck down under the void-for-vagueness doctrine. Linguists distinguish vague terms from hedges like "sort of" that signal approximation.
