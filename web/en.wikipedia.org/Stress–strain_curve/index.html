Stress–strain curve

Stress–strain curve
Obtained by: Tensile test
Key points: Yield strength, UTS, fracture

A stress–strain curve is a graph of the relationship between stress and strain exhibited by a material, typically obtained from a tensile test in which a specimen is pulled until fracture. Its shape reveals elastic modulus, yield strength, ultimate tensile strength, ductility and toughness.

Regions

Initially stress is proportional to strain — the elastic region governed by Hooke's law, whose slope is Young's modulus. Beyond the yield point the material deforms plastically. Strain hardening raises the curve to the ultimate tensile strength, after which necking localises deformation until fracture.

Ductile and brittle behaviour

Ductile materials such as mild steel show extensive plastic deformation; brittle materials such as cast iron or ceramics fracture with little yielding. Engineering curves use original cross-sectional area, while true stress–strain curves account for instantaneous area.

Use

Designers use the curve to select materials and predict forming, fatigue and failure behaviour.
