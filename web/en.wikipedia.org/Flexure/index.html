Flexure

Flexure is the deformation of a material under bending loads. In geology and geophysics, the term is particularly associated with the bending of the lithosphere — the rigid outer shell of the Earth — under vertical and horizontal loads, such as the weight of mountain ranges, volcanoes, ice sheets, or sedimentary basins. In engineering it also describes the bending of beams and other structural elements.

Geological flexure

When a load is placed on the lithosphere, the plate bends downward beneath the load and is often accompanied by a slight upward bulge, the forebulge, at some distance away. The degree of bending depends on the elastic thickness of the lithosphere: a stronger plate spreads the deflection over a wider region, while a weaker plate deforms more locally. Flexural isostasy models this process and is used to explain the geometry of foreland basins that form in front of mountain belts such as the Alps, the Himalayas and the Andes, where sediments accumulate in the moat-like depression created by the bending plate.

The classic model treats the lithosphere as a thin elastic plate overlying a fluid asthenosphere, an approach developed from studies of ocean islands such as Hawaii, where the volcanic load depresses the oceanic lithosphere and creates a surrounding archipelago of subsided seamounts and a moat filled with sediment.

In engineering

In structural engineering, flexure refers to bending in beams, slabs and other members, governed by the flexure formula relating bending moment, stress and the geometry of the cross-section. Materials under bending experience compression on one side of the neutral axis and tension on the other. Flexural strength — the maximum stress a material can withstand before yielding in bending — is a standard test property for concrete, timber, plastics and ceramics.
