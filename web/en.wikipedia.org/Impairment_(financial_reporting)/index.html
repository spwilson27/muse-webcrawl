Impairment (financial reporting)

Impairment (financial reporting)
[Image: Balance sheet extract showing a goodwill impairment write-down]
FieldAccounting / financial reporting
Key standardsIAS 36; US GAAP ASC 330, 350, 360

In financial reporting, an asset is impaired when its carrying amount (book value) exceeds the amount that can be recovered from it, whether through use or sale. Impairment accounting recognises the resulting loss in the income statement and writes the asset down to its recoverable amount, ensuring the balance sheet does not overstate economic value.

Under IFRS

International standard IAS 36 requires an entity, at each reporting date, to assess whether there is any indication of impairment — such as obsolescence, physical damage, adverse market changes, or poor performance. If so, recoverable amount is estimated as the higher of fair value less costs of disposal and value in use (the present value of future cash flows from the asset). Goodwill and indefinite-lived intangible assets must be tested for impairment annually, allocated to cash-generating units for the test. Except for goodwill, impairment losses may be reversed in later periods if conditions improve.

Under US GAAP

US GAAP applies different models by asset type. Long-lived assets held and used are tested with a two-step approach comparing undiscounted cash flows with carrying amount before measuring any loss at fair value (ASC 360); inventories and financial assets follow their own rules, and since the CECL standard (ASC 326) credit losses on financial assets are recognised on an expected-loss basis. Goodwill impairment (ASC 350) uses a simplified qualitative-then-quantitative test at the reporting-unit level, and reversals are prohibited.
