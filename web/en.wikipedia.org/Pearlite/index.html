Pearlite

TypeTwo-phase microstructure of steel
ConstituentsFerrite (α-iron) and cementite (Fe3C)
Carbon content0.76 wt% (eutectoid composition)
Eutectoid temperature727 °C

Pearlite is a two-phase, lamellar (layered) microstructure of alternating layers of ferrite (88 wt%) and cementite (12 wt%) that forms in steels and cast irons during slow cooling. It occurs at the eutectoid composition of about 0.76% carbon in the iron–carbon system. Its name derives from its pearly, mother-of-pearl-like appearance under the microscope when etched.

Formation

When a steel of eutectoid composition is cooled slowly below the eutectoid temperature of 727 °C, the parent phase austenite (a solid solution of carbon in γ-iron) decomposes simultaneously into ferrite, which can dissolve very little carbon, and cementite (iron carbide, Fe3C), which is rich in carbon. Because the two products must grow side by side and exchange carbon by diffusion, they form interpenetrating plates or lamellae.

Properties

The spacing of the lamellae depends on the cooling rate: faster cooling produces finer pearlite with smaller interlamellar spacing, which is stronger and harder than coarse pearlite formed by slow cooling. Pearlite offers a balance of strength and ductility intermediate between soft ferrite and brittle cementite, and pearlitic steels are widely used for rails, wires and structural applications. Fine pearlitic wire, cold-drawn to high strength, is among the strongest bulk materials in commercial use.

Related microstructures

Steels with less carbon than the eutectoid composition (hypoeutectoid steels) form proeutectoid ferrite plus pearlite, while carbon-rich (hypereutectoid) steels form proeutectoid cementite plus pearlite. Rapid cooling suppresses pearlite formation in favour of bainite or martensite.
