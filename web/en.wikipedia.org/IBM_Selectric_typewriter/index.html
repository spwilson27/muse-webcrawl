The IBM Selectric (a portmanteau of "selective" and "electric") was a highly successful line of electric typewriters introduced by IBM on 31 July 1961. Instead of the "basket" of individual typebars that swung up to strike the ribbon and platen in conventional typewriters, the Selectric had a type element (frequently called a "typeball", or less formally, a "golf ball") that rotated and pivoted to the correct position before striking.

Design

The typeball moved along the paper-holding carriage while the paper itself remained stationary horizontally — a reversal of conventional designs. Because the element was interchangeable, different typefaces could be fitted in seconds, and elements were made for many fonts, symbols, and scripts. The mechanism, coordinated by a system of latches, cams and a "whiffletree" selector, was a mechanical binary logic system: key presses encoded the selected character as motions along two axes (rotate and tilt) of the ball.

Models

The original Selectric (1961) was followed by the Selectric II (1971), which offered dual-pitch (10/12 characters per inch) and optional correction features, and the Selectric III (1980). Self-correcting models used a lift-off correction tape that could remove typed characters — a feature widely prized by typists. A related line, the IBM 2741 terminal, put the Selectric mechanism into computer terminals, making the Selectric a defining interface of early interactive computing.

Impact

The Selectric dominated the office typewriter market — IBM held an estimated three-quarters of the U.S. electric typewriter market at its peak — and became a design icon. Its speed (up to 15.5 characters per second), interchangeable elements, and later correction features displaced manual typewriters in offices throughout the 1960s and 1970s, before electronic typewriters and word processors succeeded it in the 1980s.
