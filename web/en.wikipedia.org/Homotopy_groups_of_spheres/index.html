Homotopy groups of spheres

In the mathematical field of algebraic topology, the homotopy groups of spheres describe how spheres of various dimensions can be wrapped around each other. They are examples of topological invariants, answering the question of whether two maps from an m-sphere to an n-sphere can be continuously deformed into each other. Despite the elementary statement of the problem, the homotopy groups of spheres are among the most difficult computations in algebraic topology and remain only partially understood.

Some results

The groups πn(Sn) are infinite cyclic, generated by the identity map. The first "unexpected" group is π3(S2), which is also infinite cyclic, generated by the Hopf map discovered by Heinz Hopf in 1931. Jean-Pierre Serre proved in the early 1950s that almost all homotopy groups of spheres are finite, and developed spectral-sequence methods that transformed the subject. In the stable range, the groups stabilize into the stable homotopy groups of spheres, which are related to cobordism theory and are a central object in stable homotopy theory.

Methods and significance

Computations use techniques including the Freudenthal suspension theorem, fibrations, the Serre and Adams spectral sequences, and, in recent decades, motivic homotopy theory and computational methods that extended known tables to higher stems. The groups matter beyond pure topology, appearing in the theory of exotic spheres and smooth structures, and in physics through anomalies and topological phases.
