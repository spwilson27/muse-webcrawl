The siege of Baghdad took place in 1258, when Mongol forces under Hulagu (Hulegu) Khan, accompanied by Christian and other allied contingents, besieged and sacked Baghdad, then the capital of the Abbasid Caliphate. The city's fall is widely regarded as ending the Islamic Golden Age and is remembered as one of the most destructive sieges in history.

Background

Hulagu, grandson of Genghis Khan, had been charged by his brother Möngke Khan with subduing the Muslim lands of the Middle East. After destroying the Nizari Ismaili (Assassin) strongholds in Persia, Hulagu demanded the Abbasid caliph Al-Musta'sim's submission. The caliph refused and threatened the Mongols with divine retribution, while failing to prepare the city's defences or pay his troops.

Siege and sack

Mongol armies, including Chinese siege engineers, Armenian and Georgian auxiliaries, and Frankish troops from Antioch, encircled Baghdad in January 1258, building palisades and ditches and bombarding the walls with catapults and gunpowder-era siege weapons. The caliph's field army was defeated at the Tigris and the city was invested on 29 January. Baghdad surrendered on 10 February 1258.

A week of pillage and massacre followed. Estimates of the dead range from tens of thousands to a million or more. The Grand Library of Baghdad — the House of Wisdom — was destroyed, and legend holds that the Tigris ran black with ink from books thrown into it, and red with blood. The caliph was executed, reportedly rolled in a carpet and trampled by horses so his royal blood would not touch the ground. Irrigation canals and agricultural infrastructure were wrecked, dealing Iraq's agriculture a blow from which, some historians argue, it never fully recovered.

Aftermath

Baghdad was left a depopulated ruin and ceased to be the political centre of the Islamic world. Hulagu founded the Ilkhanate, ruling Persia and Iraq; an Abbasid caliphate-in-exile was later re-established in Mamluk Cairo, but without real power. Mosul and other cities submitted, and in 1260 the Mongols were checked by the Mamluks at the Battle of Ain Jalut.
