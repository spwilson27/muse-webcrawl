Personal identity

Personal identity is the philosophical and psychological question of what makes a person the same person over time — what persistence conditions a person has, and by virtue of what someone at one time is identical with someone at another time. In everyday usage, "identity" also refers to the qualities, beliefs and expressions that characterise a person or group.

The persistence question

A person's body and psychology both change continually, yet we ordinarily judge that the same person survives. Philosophers ask what the criterion of this survival is. Thought experiments — such as teletransportation, brain transplantation and fission, in which a person's psychology is duplicated — test candidate criteria.

Main theories

Psychological continuity

John Locke proposed that personal identity consists in continuity of consciousness, in particular memory. Joseph Butler and Thomas Reid objected that memory presupposes identity rather than constituting it. Modern psychological-continuity theories, notably those of Derek Parfit and Sydney Shoemaker, use overlapping chains of psychological connectedness. Parfit argued in Reasons and Persons (1984) that identity is "not what matters" in survival; what matters is psychological continuity and connectedness (Relation R).

Bodily and animalist views

Bodily theories tie identity to the persistence of the same body or brain. Animalism, defended by Eric Olson, holds that each person is numerically identical with a human animal, and that persistence consists in the animal's continued life.

Narrative and social identity

Other accounts emphasise the self as constructed through narrative — the story a person tells about their life — and through social recognition, roles and group memberships, a theme developed in the psychology of Erik Erikson and in social identity theory.

Related issues

The problem connects with questions about consciousness, free will, moral responsibility, and practical puzzles such as identity in dementia, brain death and personal identity in law.
