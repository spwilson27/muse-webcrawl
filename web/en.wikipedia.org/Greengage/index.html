Greengage

GenusPrunus
SpeciesPrunus domestica
SubspeciesP. domestica subsp. italica
OriginAsia Minor / Europe

The greengages are a group of cultivars of the common plum, Prunus domestica. They are classified in the subspecies Prunus domestica subsp. italica, which also includes the related mirabelles and damsons in some classifications. Greengages are small, round-oval stone fruits noted for their rich, honey-like flavour and their green to golden-yellow skin, which distinguishes them from most other dessert plums.

History

Greengage-type plums were cultivated in France, where they are known as Reine Claude, after Claude, Queen consort of Francis I of France in the 16th century. They were introduced to England in the early 18th century, traditionally said to be in 1724 by Sir William Gage of Hengrave Hall in Suffolk; the English name "greengage" combines "green" with the surname Gage.

Cultivation

Greengages are grown across temperate Europe and in parts of North America, Australia and New Zealand. The trees are generally less hardy and more exacting than other plums, preferring sheltered, sunny sites and well-drained soil. Fruit ripens in late summer; yields can be irregular, and the fruits bruise easily and keep poorly, so greengages are mostly eaten fresh, cooked into jams and desserts, or grown in home gardens rather than shipped commercially over long distances.

Well-known cultivars include 'Reine Claude Verte', 'Cambridge Gage', 'Laxton's Gage', 'Coe's Golden Drop' (a yellow form) and the very sweet 'Reine Claude de Bavay'.

Culinary use

Greengages are considered among the finest of all dessert plums, eaten fresh when fully ripe and soft. They are also used in tarts, preserves, and compotes, and in France are distilled or macerated into liqueurs. The French town of Bar-le-Duc is famous for its greengage preserves.
