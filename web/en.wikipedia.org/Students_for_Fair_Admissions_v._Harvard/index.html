Students for Fair Admissions v. Harvard

Students for Fair Admissions, Inc. v. President and Fellows of Harvard College, 600 U.S. 181 (2023), is a landmark decision of the Supreme Court of the United States in which the Court held that race-conscious admissions programs at Harvard University and the University of North Carolina violated the Equal Protection Clause of the Fourteenth Amendment. The ruling effectively ended affirmative action in college admissions in the United States.

Background

Students for Fair Admissions (SFFA), an organisation founded by legal activist Edward Blum, sued Harvard in 2014 alleging that its undergraduate admissions process discriminated against Asian American applicants through the use of racial quotas and subjective "personal ratings". A parallel suit challenged UNC's admissions. Federal trial courts upheld both universities' programs in 2019 and 2021, and the First Circuit affirmed in the Harvard case.

Decision

On June 29, 2023, the Supreme Court ruled 6–3 in the Harvard case (and 6–2 in the UNC case, Justice Jackson having recused herself from the Harvard matter) that the programs lacked sufficiently focused and measurable objectives, employed race in a negative manner, and lacked meaningful end points. Chief Justice John Roberts wrote the majority opinion. The decision overturned the precedent of Grutter v. Bollinger (2003), which had permitted narrowly tailored consideration of race in admissions.

Reactions and aftermath

The decision was praised by opponents of affirmative action and criticised by its supporters and by many universities. In the admissions cycles following the ruling, several highly selective institutions reported significant declines in enrolment of Black and Hispanic students. The opinion expressly preserved universities' ability to consider an applicant's discussion of how race affected their life, and did not address admissions at military academies.
