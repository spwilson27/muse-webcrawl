Robert Capa

BornEndre Ernő Friedmann; 22 October 1913, Budapest, Austria-Hungary
Died25 May 1954 (aged 40), Thái Bình, French Indochina
OccupationPhotojournalist, war photographer

Robert Capa (born Endre Ernő Friedmann; 22 October 1913 – 25 May 1954) was a Hungarian-American war photographer and photojournalist, regarded as one of the greatest combat photographers in history. His maxim was, "If your pictures aren't good enough, you're not close enough."

Career

Working first with his partner Gerda Taro, Capa covered the Spanish Civil War, producing his most famous image, The Falling Soldier (1936), whose authenticity has long been debated. Taro was killed in the war in 1937. Capa went on to photograph the Second Sino-Japanese War, and then the Second World War across North Africa and Europe: he landed with the first wave on Omaha Beach on D-Day, 6 June 1944 — most of his rolls were ruined in a London darkroom accident, leaving eleven surviving frames known as the "Magnificent Eleven." He also covered the 1948 Arab–Israeli War.

Magnum and death

In 1947 Capa co-founded the Magnum Photos cooperative with Henri Cartier-Bresson, David "Chim" Seymour, George Rodger and William Vandivert. In 1954, while covering the First Indochina War for Life magazine, he stepped on a landmine near Thái Bình and was killed, aged 40.

Legacy

The Robert Capa Gold Medal for courageous war photography is named for him, and his rediscovered archive of Spanish Civil War negatives — the "Mexican Suitcase," recovered in 2007 — confirmed the extent of his and Taro's work. His memoir Slightly Out of Focus (1947) remains a classic of war reporting.
