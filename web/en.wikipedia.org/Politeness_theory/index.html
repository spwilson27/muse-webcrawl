Politeness theory

FieldPragmatics, sociolinguistics
Key workBrown & Levinson, Politeness (1978/1987)
Key conceptFace; face-threatening acts

Politeness theory is a framework in linguistics and communication studies explaining how speakers manage social relations through language. Its best-known version, developed by Penelope Brown and Stephen Levinson, builds on Erving Goffman's concept of "face" — the public self-image every person wants to claim.

Positive and negative face

Brown and Levinson distinguish positive face, the desire to be approved of and appreciated, from negative face, the desire for autonomy and freedom from imposition. Many speech acts — requests, orders, criticisms, offers — intrinsically threaten one or both kinds of face and are called face-threatening acts (FTAs).

Strategies

Speakers choose among five superstrategies according to the seriousness of the act, estimated from social distance, power difference, and the size of the imposition: bald on record; positive politeness (friendliness, compliments, in-group markers); negative politeness (hedges, apologies, indirectness); off record (hints); or not performing the act at all.

Criticism

The theory has been criticized for claiming universality from English, Malay, and Tzeltal data while fitting East Asian and other cultures less well, and later work has recast politeness in terms of discursive practice, rapport management, and relational work.
