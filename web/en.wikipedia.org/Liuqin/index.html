The liuqin (Chinese: 柳琴; pinyin: liǔqín) is a four-stringed Chinese mandolin-like lute with a pear- or willow-leaf-shaped body and a fretted neck. Its name means "willow lute," after the shape of its soundboard. It has a bright, penetrating tone and is used in Chinese folk music, opera accompaniment, and the modern Chinese orchestra, where it fills the high plucked-string register alongside the pipa and ruan.

History

The liuqin developed in the Shandong and northern Jiangsu region during the Qing dynasty, originally as a simple two-string instrument used to accompany local opera and narrative singing, where it was sometimes called the liuhua qin or "earth pipa" (tǔ pípá). Through the 20th century it was progressively redesigned: strings were added, the number of frets increased to as many as 29, and the body was refined to give the instrument a range of over three octaves and a richer, more even sound suitable for solo performance.

Construction and technique

The modern liuqin is typically made with a soundboard of paulownia or other softwood and a back of rosewood or sandalwood, often with inlaid decoration. It is held like a small pipa and played with a plectrum worn on the fingers or held in the right hand, producing rapid tremolo, arpeggios, and chordal figures. Its tuning and size make it comparable to the Western mandolin, and contemporary repertoire includes transcriptions as well as original concertos.

Use

In the modern Chinese orchestra the liuqin often doubles melodic lines at a high pitch and supplies rhythmic sparkle. Notable virtuosos, such as Wang Huiran—who was instrumental in the instrument's modernization—helped establish a solo repertoire for it in the later 20th century.
