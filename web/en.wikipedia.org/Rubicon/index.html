The Rubicon (Italian: Rubicone) is a shallow river in northeastern Italy, about 80 kilometres (50 miles) long, running from the Apennine Mountains to the Adriatic Sea through the southern Emilia-Romagna region, between the towns of Rimini and Cesena. The Latin word rubico comes from the adjective rubeus, meaning "red", a reference to the colour the river takes from mud deposits.

History

During the Roman Republic, the Rubicon marked the boundary between the Roman province of Cisalpine Gaul to the north-east and Italy proper, controlled directly by Rome and its allies, to the south-west. Governors of Roman provinces were appointed promagistrates with imperium (roughly, "right to command") only within their province; any such promagistrate who led troops into Italy — that is, south of the Rubicon — forfeited that imperium and was, in effect, declaring war on the Roman state.

The river is famous as the place where Julius Caesar crossed it on 10 or 11 January 49 BC at the head of the Legio XIII, in defiance of the Senate's orders, uttering the phrase attributed to him by Suetonius, "alea iacta est" ("the die is cast"). The crossing began Caesar's civil war against Pompey and the optimates, which ultimately led to the end of the Roman Republic and the rise of the Roman Empire.

Legacy

The phrase "crossing the Rubicon" has entered English usage as an idiom meaning "passing a point of no return". The identification of the modern river with the ancient Rubicon was long disputed; Benito Mussolini decreed in 1933 that the river formerly called the Fiumicino was the historical Rubicon, and the town at its source was renamed Sogliano al Rubicone accordingly.
