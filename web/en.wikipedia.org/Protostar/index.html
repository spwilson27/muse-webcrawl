A protostar is a very young star that is still gathering mass from its parent molecular cloud. The protostellar phase is the earliest one in the process of stellar evolution. For a low-mass star (such as the Sun), it lasts about 500,000 years. The phase begins when a molecular cloud fragment first collapses under the force of self-gravity and an opaque, pressure-supported core forms inside the collapsing fragment. It ends when the infalling gas is depleted, leaving a pre-main-sequence star, which contracts and later ignites hydrogen fusion to become a main-sequence star.

Gravitational collapse

Star formation begins within relatively dense concentrations of interstellar gas and dust known as molecular clouds. These regions are extremely cold (10 to 20 K, just above absolute zero). At these temperatures gases become molecular, and the clouds are unstable to gravitational collapse. When a cloud fragment's mass exceeds the Jeans mass, gravity overcomes internal pressure and the fragment collapses inward, fragmenting as it does so. One fragment, containing a mass of about the mass of the Sun, can collapse to form a dense core that heats as it shrinks, eventually becoming a protostar.

Evolution

As collapse proceeds, the density at the centre rises until the core becomes opaque to its own radiation. The trapped heat raises the central temperature; once it reaches roughly 2,000 K, molecular hydrogen dissociates, absorbing energy and allowing a second collapse that produces a small hydrostatic core — the protostar itself. Material continues to rain onto the protostar through an accretion disk, and powerful bipolar outflows and jets carry away angular momentum. Protostars are often classified observationally as Class 0, Class I, Class II and Class III sources according to the shape of their spectral energy distributions, reflecting the gradual dispersal of the surrounding envelope and disk.

Visibility

Because a protostar is still embedded in the dense cloud of gas and dust from which it formed, it is heavily obscured at visible wavelengths and is best studied in the infrared and radio. Instruments such as the Hubble Space Telescope, the Spitzer Space Telescope and the James Webb Space Telescope have imaged many protostars, revealing disks, jets and Herbig–Haro objects where outflows shock the surrounding gas.
