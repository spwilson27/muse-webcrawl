Adena culture

The Adena culture was a Pre-Columbian Native American culture that existed from 1000 to 200 BC, in a time known as the early Woodland period. The Adena culture refers to what were probably a number of related Native American societies sharing a burial complex and ceremonial system, centred in the Ohio River valley and spreading into present-day Indiana, Kentucky, West Virginia, and Pennsylvania.

Burial mounds

The Adena are best known for their large conical burial mounds and earthworks, such as the Grave Creek Mound in West Virginia, the largest Adena mound at roughly 62 feet high. The dead were buried with grave goods including copper bracelets, beads, mica ornaments, and carved stone tablets — the so-called Adena tablets, inscribed with curvilinear designs.

Society and subsistence

Adena people were semi-sedentary hunter-gatherers who also practiced early horticulture, cultivating plants of the Eastern Agricultural Complex such as sunflower, squash, and chenopod. Their distinctive pottery, among the earliest in the region, and their ceremonial pipes attest to wide exchange networks. The culture was followed in the region by the Hopewell culture, which expanded on many Adena traditions.

The name "Adena" comes from the Adena estate of Ohio governor Thomas Worthington, on whose land a major mound was excavated in 1901.
