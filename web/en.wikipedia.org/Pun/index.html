Pun

A pun, also known as a paronomasia, is a form of word play that exploits multiple meanings of a term, or of similar-sounding words, for an intended humorous or rhetorical effect. These ambiguities can arise from the intentional use of homophonic, homographic, metonymic, or figurative language.

Types

Puns are commonly classified by the linguistic mechanism they exploit. The homophonic pun relies on words that sound alike but differ in meaning, such as "sole" and "soul". The homographic pun uses words that are spelled the same but have different meanings and possibly different pronunciations. A compound pun contains two or more puns in one statement, while a recursive pun requires understanding the first pun in order to decode the second.

History

Wordplay of this kind is ancient: puns appear in Egyptian hieroglyphs, Sumerian cuneiform texts, Chinese poetry, and the works of classical authors such as Plautus. William Shakespeare is renowned for his prolific use of puns, which appear throughout his plays and sonnets. In the 18th and 19th centuries punning was a fashionable salon amusement, though it later acquired a reputation in some circles as a "low" form of wit, famously described by John Dennis as the lowest form of humour.

In culture

Puns are ubiquitous in jokes, advertising slogans, newspaper headlines, and the names of businesses. They are a staple of stand-up comedy, children's humour, and the knock-knock joke format. Because puns depend on the phonetic and semantic structure of a specific language, they are often considered difficult or impossible to translate directly.
