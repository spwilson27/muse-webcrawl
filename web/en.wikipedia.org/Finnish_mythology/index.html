Finnish mythology

Väinämöinen, the wise old hero of the Kalevala, with his kantele.

Finnish mythology is a commonly applied description of the folklore of Finnish paganism, detailing the traditions and beliefs of the Finns in the pre-Christian era. It shares many features with other Finnic mythologies, as well as with Norse mythology and the beliefs of neighbouring peoples. Much of it survives through oral poetry, collected in the nineteenth century and woven into the national epic, the Kalevala, compiled by Elias Lönnrot.

Cosmology and deities

The world was believed to have been formed from the eggs of a bird laid on the knee of Ilmatar, the goddess of air, as recounted in the creation myth of the Kalevala. The chief god was Ukko, the god of the sky, thunder, and weather, whose name became the Finnish word for thunder. Other deities included Tapio, god of the forest, and Ahti, god of the waters. Nature was inhabited by numerous spirits and guardians (haltija) of places, animals, and natural features.

The underworld, Tuonela, was a dark land of the dead across a black river, ruled by Tuoni and his wife Tuonetar; the hero Väinämöinen journeyed there in search of knowledge. Shamans and wise men known as tietäjät ("knowers") used sung incantations, based on knowledge of the mythical origins (synty) of things, for healing and protection.

Heroes

The central heroes of the Kalevala tradition are Väinämöinen, the eternal sage and singer; Ilmarinen, the smith who forged the sky and the magical Sampo; and Lemminkäinen, the reckless adventurer. Their quests for the Sampo, a miraculous wealth-producing artefact fought over with Louhi, the mistress of the northern land of Pohjola, form the core of the epic.

Legacy

Finnish mythology was largely recorded from oral tradition by collectors such as Lönnrot in the nineteenth century and became a cornerstone of Finnish national identity. It has inspired artists including Akseli Gallen-Kallela and composer Jean Sibelius, and has influenced modern fantasy literature.
