Sarthe (French pronunciation: [saʁt]) is a department in the Pays de la Loire region of Western France, named after the river Sarthe, which flows through it on its way to join the Loire. Created in 1790 during the French Revolution from parts of the former provinces of Maine and Anjou, it is numbered 72 in the French departmental system. Its prefecture is Le Mans, famous worldwide for the 24 Hours of Le Mans endurance race.

Geography

The department lies at the eastern edge of the Armorican Massif, a largely rural landscape of farmland, hedgerows and forests such as the Forêt de Bercé. The Sarthe river crosses it from north to south past Alençon, Le Mans and La Flèche before joining the Mayenne to form the Maine. The Loir (not to be confused with the Loire) waters the southern part of the department.

Le Mans and the 24 Hours

Le Mans, the capital, has a Roman-walled old town (Cité Plantagenêt) and the great Gothic Cathedral of Saint-Julien. Since 1923 it has hosted the 24 Hours of Le Mans, the world's oldest active endurance sports-car race, run on the Circuit de la Sarthe, which combines public roads with a permanent track. Sarthe is also known gastronomically for its rillettes and for the wines of Jasnières and Coteaux du Loir.

History

The area was the county of Maine, contested for centuries between Normandy, Anjou and the French crown, and the birthplace of Henry II of England's Plantagenet line. During the Revolution the west saw the royalist Chouannerie revolt, and the department took part in the industrialization of the 19th century with railways and the founding of the Bollée family's automobile works in Le Mans.
