Conjugation

This article is about the grammatical concept. For other uses, see Conjugation (disambiguation).

In linguistics, conjugation (abbreviated conj or cnj) is the creation of derived forms of a verb from its principal parts by inflection (alteration of form according to rules of grammar). The term, from Latin coniugātiō "joining together", refers to the set of forms a verb takes across categories such as person, number, tense, aspect, mood and voice. For example, the verb break has the forms break, breaks, broke, broken and breaking.

Verbal categories

Conjugation encodes some or all of the following, depending on the language:

  • Person — first, second, third
  • Number — singular, plural, sometimes dual
  • Tense — past, present, future
  • Aspect — perfective, imperfective, progressive
  • Mood — indicative, subjunctive, imperative, conditional
  • Voice — active, passive, middle

Conjugation classes

Languages with rich morphology group verbs into conjugation classes with shared patterns: Romance languages have two or three classes distinguished by the infinitive ending (e.g. Spanish -ar, -er, -ir), while Latin had four conjugations. Verbs that depart from the pattern are called irregular verbs; highly irregular verbs such as English be (am, is, are, was, were) are common cross-linguistically.

Agreement

In many languages the conjugated verb agrees with its subject in person and number, and sometimes in gender. Some languages also mark agreement with objects on the verb, making conjugation a central part of sentence structure.
