Mushroom poisoning

SpecialtyToxicology, emergency medicine
SymptomsNausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, liver failure, hallucinations (varies by species)
CausesIngestion of toxic fungi, often mistaken for edible species
TreatmentSupportive care, activated charcoal, silibinin, liver transplant in severe cases

Mushroom poisoning is poisoning resulting from the ingestion of mushrooms that contain toxic substances. Symptoms can vary from slight gastrointestinal discomfort to death in about 10 days. The toxins present are secondary metabolites produced by the fungus. Mushroom poisoning is usually the result of ingestion of wild mushrooms after misidentification of a toxic mushroom as an edible species.

Toxic groups

The most dangerous toxins are the amatoxins found in the death cap (Amanita phalloides) and related species, which cause potentially fatal liver and kidney damage after a deceptive latent period. Other toxin groups include orellanine, which damages the kidneys; muscarine; ibotenic acid and muscimol, found in the fly agaric; psilocybin, which produces hallucinogenic effects; and coprine, which causes illness when combined with alcohol.

Signs and symptoms

Many poisonous mushrooms first cause gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, stomach cramps, and diarrhea. Amatoxin poisoning characteristically begins with a violent gastroenteritis phase 6–24 hours after ingestion, followed by an apparent recovery, and then progressive liver failure. Early medical treatment greatly improves prognosis.

Prevention and identification

There is no simple test to distinguish edible from poisonous mushrooms in the field. Folklore tests — such as whether a mushroom tarnishes a silver spoon or peels easily — are unreliable. Poison control centers advise that wild mushrooms should only be eaten after identification by an experienced mycologist.

Notable cases

Historical figures sometimes said to have died of mushroom poisoning include the Roman emperor Claudius and the Holy Roman Emperor Charles VI, whose death in 1740 prompted Voltaire's remark that "this dish of mushrooms changed the destiny of Europe."
