Hepatitis C

SpecialtyInfectious disease, hepatology
CauseHepatitis C virus (HCV)
TransmissionBlood-to-blood contact
TreatmentDirect-acting antivirals

Hepatitis C is an infectious disease caused by the hepatitis C virus (HCV) that primarily affects the liver. The virus is spread mainly through blood-to-blood contact, such as unsafe injection drug use, poorly sterilized medical equipment, and transfusions of unscreened blood. Globally, tens of millions of people live with chronic infection.

Signs and course

Initial infection is usually asymptomatic or causes only mild, nonspecific symptoms such as fatigue, nausea, and muscle aches. About 70–85 percent of infected people develop chronic hepatitis C, which may silently progress over decades to liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, liver failure, and hepatocellular carcinoma. Chronic infection is often not discovered until serious liver damage has occurred.

Diagnosis and treatment

Diagnosis is by blood tests for anti-HCV antibodies followed by confirmatory HCV RNA testing. Since the 2010s, direct-acting antiviral medicines such as sofosbuvir-based regimens have cured more than 95 percent of infections with 8–12 weeks of oral therapy. There is no effective vaccine, though research continues. Discovery of the virus earned Harvey Alter, Michael Houghton, and Charles Rice the 2020 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine.

Prevention

Prevention relies on screening of donated blood, needle-exchange and harm-reduction programs, safe injection practices, and treatment of infected individuals, which also reduces onward transmission.
