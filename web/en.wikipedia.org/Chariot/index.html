Chariot

A chariot is a type of carriage driven by a charioteer, usually using horses to provide rapid motive power. The earliest known chariots date to about 2000 BCE in the Eurasian steppes (the Sintashta culture), and the use of horses in chariot warfare spread throughout the Near East, Egypt, Greece, India, and China. Chariots were used for war, hunting, racing, and ceremonial purposes; for over a millennium they were the decisive weapons system of Bronze Age and early Iron Age armies before cavalry displaced them.

Design

The war chariot was a light vehicle: a wooden frame, often covered with leather or wickerwork, mounted on two spoked wheels, with a floor of interlaced straps that gave the crew a springy footing. The axle was placed at the rear for manoeuvrability. A team of two horses, yoked to a central pole, was standard; the Egyptian chariot carried a driver and an archer, while Hittite chariots carried three men. Composite bows, javelins, and later spears were the principal weapons.

Warfare

Hyksos invaders introduced the chariot to Egypt around 1650 BCE, and New Kingdom pharaohs built elite chariot corps. The Battle of Kadesh (1274 BCE), between Ramesses II and the Hittite king Muwatalli II, involved thousands of chariots and is among the earliest battles recorded in tactical detail. Chariotry was expensive — vehicles, horses, and trained crews required great resources — and formed an aristocratic arm in many societies, celebrated in the epics of Homer and in the Indian Mahabharata.

Decline and later uses

As horse breeding produced animals strong enough to carry an armoured rider, and infantry tactics adapted, mounted cavalry replaced chariots in most armies by the early centuries BCE; scythed chariots made a brief late appearance in Persian and Hellenistic warfare. Chariot racing, by contrast, flourished: the four-horse quadriga race was a highlight of the Olympic Games and, in Rome, the circus factions drew enormous crowds in the Circus Maximus. In Britain and Ireland, chariots persisted in warfare into the Roman period.
