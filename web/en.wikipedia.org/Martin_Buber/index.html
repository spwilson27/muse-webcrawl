Martin Buber

Martin Buber (1878–1965) was an Austrian-born Israeli Jewish philosopher best known for his philosophy of dialogue, centered on the distinction between the I–Thou (Ich-Du) and I–It (Ich-Es) relationships, set out in his 1923 book I and Thou.

Life

Born in Vienna and raised in Lemberg (Lviv) by his grandfather, the Midrash scholar Solomon Buber, he studied in Vienna, Leipzig, Berlin, and Zurich. He edited the journal Der Jude, retold Hasidic tales for Western readers, and with Franz Rosenzweig produced a celebrated German translation of the Hebrew Bible. After the Nazi rise to power he emigrated to Jerusalem in 1938, teaching at the Hebrew University until 1951. He died in Jerusalem in 1965.

Thought

Buber held that human life finds meaning in genuine encounter: in the I–Thou relation the other is met as an end in itself, whereas in I–It the other is experienced and used as an object. His work influenced theology, psychotherapy, and education, and he advocated a binational solution in Palestine, working with the Brit Shalom and Ihud movements — a stance that made him controversial in both Jewish and Arab politics.
