Ozone layer

The ozone layer is a region of Earth's stratosphere, roughly 15 to 35 kilometres above the surface, where ozone (O3) concentrations are far higher than elsewhere in the atmosphere. It absorbs most of the Sun's ultraviolet radiation in the 200–315 nm range, shielding life on the surface from its harmful effects.

Formation and measurement

Stratospheric ozone forms when ultraviolet light splits oxygen molecules, whose atoms recombine with O2 — the Chapman cycle. Total column ozone is measured in Dobson units; typical values are near 300 DU. The layer was characterised in the 1920s by G. M. B. Dobson's spectrophotometers and explained in 1930 by Sydney Chapman.

The ozone hole and the Montreal Protocol

In 1985 scientists of the British Antarctic Survey reported severe springtime thinning over Antarctica — the "ozone hole" — caused chiefly by chlorine and bromine released from chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) and halons on polar stratospheric clouds. The 1987 Montreal Protocol phased out these substances worldwide and is regarded as the most successful environmental treaty; the ozone layer is projected to recover to 1980 levels around the 2060s.
