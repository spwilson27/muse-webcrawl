Substantive due process

Substantive due process
Legal fieldUnited States constitutional law
SourceDue Process Clauses of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments
Landmark casesLochner v. New York; Griswold v. Connecticut; Roe v. Wade; Dobbs v. Jackson

Substantive due process is a principle in United States constitutional law that allows courts to establish and protect certain fundamental rights from government interference, even if procedural protections are present or the rights are unenumerated elsewhere in the Constitution. It rests on the interpretation that the Due Process Clauses of the Fifth and Fourteenth Amendments protect not only fair procedures (procedural due process) but also substantive liberty interests.

History

The doctrine rose to prominence in the "Lochner era" (roughly 1897–1937), when the Supreme Court struck down economic regulations on the basis of an unenumerated "liberty of contract", most famously in Lochner v. New York (1905). The Court repudiated that approach in West Coast Hotel Co. v. Parrish (1937), and economic substantive due process has since received only rational-basis review.

From the 1960s, the Court again used substantive due process to protect personal and privacy rights: Griswold v. Connecticut (1965, contraception), Roe v. Wade (1973, abortion), Lawrence v. Texas (2003, intimate conduct), and Obergefell v. Hodges (2015, same-sex marriage). In Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022), the Court overturned Roe, holding that rights must be "deeply rooted in this Nation's history and tradition" and "implicit in the concept of ordered liberty" to qualify as fundamental.

Criticism

The doctrine has long been controversial. Critics charge that it lets unelected judges read their own values into the Constitution — Justice Antonin Scalia and others called it an oxymoron — while defenders argue it is essential to prevent government infringement of basic liberties not expressly listed in the Bill of Rights.
