Sixth nerve palsy

The abducens nerve (cranial nerve VI) innervates the lateral rectus muscle

Sixth nerve palsy, or abducens nerve palsy, is a disorder associated with dysfunction of cranial nerve VI (the abducens nerve), which is responsible for causing contraction of the lateral rectus muscle to abduct (turn out) the eye. When the nerve is damaged, the affected eye cannot move fully outward, and patients typically experience horizontal double vision (diplopia) that worsens when looking toward the affected side.

Signs and symptoms

The characteristic finding is an esotropia — an inward deviation of the eye — with limited abduction. Patients often turn their head toward the side of the lesion to maintain binocular vision and minimize diplopia. Children may present with a head turn or closure of one eye.

Causes

In adults, the most common cause is microvascular ischemia, often associated with diabetes and hypertension. Other causes include raised intracranial pressure, trauma, tumors, stroke, multiple sclerosis and inflammation. Because the sixth nerve has the longest intracranial course of the cranial nerves, it is particularly vulnerable to increased intracranial pressure, making sixth nerve palsy a potentially "false localizing sign."

Treatment

Management focuses on treating the underlying cause. Many microvascular palsies resolve spontaneously within a few months. Temporary measures include prisms, occlusion of one eye, and botulinum toxin injection; persistent cases may require strabismus surgery.
