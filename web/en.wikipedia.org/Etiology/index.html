Etiology

Etiology (also spelled aetiology) is the study of causation or origination. The word is derived from the Greek aitiologia, "giving a reason for" (αἰτία, aitia, "cause"; and -λογία, -logia). More completely, etiology is the study of the causes, origins, or reasons behind the way things are, or the way they function. The word is most commonly used in medical and philosophical contexts, discussed in relation to a certain condition or disease.

In medicine

In medicine, etiology refers to the cause or origin of a disease or condition. Identifying an etiology is fundamental to diagnosis and treatment: diseases may be caused by pathogens (bacteria, viruses, fungi, parasites), genetic mutations, environmental exposures, nutritional deficiencies, or combinations of factors. When no cause can be determined, a disease is described as idiopathic.

Historically, etiological investigation was transformed by the germ theory of disease in the 19th century. Robert Koch formulated his famous postulates for establishing that a microorganism causes a disease. In modern epidemiology, causal inference is guided by frameworks such as the Bradford Hill criteria, which consider strength of association, consistency, temporality, biological gradient, and plausibility.

In philosophy and mythology

In philosophy, etiology concerns the study of causes in general. In mythology, an etiological myth (or origin myth) is a story intended to explain how some feature of the world came into being — for example, why a place has its name, or how a species acquired its characteristics. Such myths appear in traditions worldwide, from Hesiod's Theogony to the Just So Stories of Rudyard Kipling.
