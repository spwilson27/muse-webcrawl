The Rights of Way Act 1932 was an Act of the Parliament of the United Kingdom that made important changes to the law of public rights of way in England and Wales. Its most significant provision established a presumption that twenty years' uninterrupted use of a way by the public could give rise to a deemed dedication of the way as a public highway.

Background

Before the Act, landowners faced prolonged uncertainty about whether long use of paths across their land could mature into public rights. The Act provided a statutory framework balancing the interests of landowners and the public, allowing a landowner to negate the presumption of dedication by erecting notices or by depositing a map and statement with the local authority.

Provisions

The Act provided that where a way had been enjoyed by the public as of right and without interruption for a full period of twenty years, the way was deemed to have been dedicated as a highway unless there was sufficient evidence that there was no intention during that period to dedicate it. It also allowed landowners to deposit maps and statements indicating which ways they admitted to be dedicated, a mechanism later retained in the Highways Act 1980.

Legacy

Much of the Act was repealed, but its core presumption survived in later highways legislation and remains central to claims for public rights of way based on long user.
