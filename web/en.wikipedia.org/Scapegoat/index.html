Scapegoat

The scapegoat bearing the sins of the people into the wilderness.

In the Bible, a scapegoat is one of a pair of kid goats that is released into the wilderness, taking with it all sins and impurities, while the other is sacrificed. The concept first appears in the Book of Leviticus, in which a goat is designated to be cast into the desert to carry away the sins of the community on Yom Kippur, the Day of Atonement.

In modern usage, a scapegoat is a person or group blamed for the wrongdoings, mistakes, or faults of others, often as a convenient target for frustration or as a way of deflecting responsibility.

Origins

The English word "scapegoat" was coined by William Tyndale in his 1530 translation of the Bible, rendering the Hebrew Azazel as "the goat that escapes". In the Leviticus ritual, the high priest laid both hands on the head of the live goat, confessed over it all the iniquities of the Israelites, and sent it away into the wilderness.

Scapegoating

Scapegoating is the practice of singling out a person or group for unmerited blame and consequent negative treatment. Psychologists and sociologists have studied it as a mechanism by which groups displace aggression and anxiety onto a weaker or marginalised target. The philosopher René Girard placed the scapegoat mechanism at the centre of his theory of human culture and ritual sacrifice.

In politics and society

Throughout history, minorities and outsiders have frequently been scapegoated during times of plague, economic hardship, or social unrest. Scapegoating remains a recognised pattern in propaganda and populist rhetoric.
