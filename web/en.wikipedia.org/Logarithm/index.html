Logarithm

Logarithm
Inverse operation: exponentiation
Notation: logb x
Introduced by John Napier, 1614

In mathematics, the logarithm is the inverse function to exponentiation. The logarithm of a number x to the base b is the exponent to which b must be raised to produce x: if by = x, then y = logb x. For example, log10 1000 = 3, because 103 = 1000.

History

John Napier published the first tables of logarithms in 1614 (Mirifici Logarithmorum Canonis Descriptio), transforming calculation in astronomy and navigation; Henry Briggs developed common (base-10) logarithms. The natural logarithm, base e, arises naturally in calculus: the derivative of ln x is 1/x, and ln is the integral of 1/t from 1 to x.

Properties and uses

Key identities include log(xy) = log x + log y and log(xr) = r log x — the property that made logarithms a computational aid before electronic calculators. Logarithms are used in measuring sound intensity (decibels), acidity (pH), earthquake magnitude (Richter scale), information (bits, with base 2), and in analysing exponential growth and algorithmic complexity.
