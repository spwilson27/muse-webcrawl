Hogmanay

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

Fireworks over Edinburgh Castle during the Hogmanay celebrations.

Hogmanay (Scots: [ˌhɔɡməˈneː]) is the Scots word for the last day of the old year and is synonymous with the celebration of the New Year (Gregorian calendar) in the Scottish style. It is normally followed by further celebration on the morning of New Year's Day (1 January) and in some cases 2 January, both of which are Scottish public holidays.

Origins and history

The origins of the word are unclear; proposed derivations include Gaelic and Old French terms for a new year gift. The roots of Hogmanay perhaps reach back to the celebration of the winter solstice among Norsemen, incorporating elements of the Gaelic festival of Samhain. The Vikings celebrated Yule, which later contributed to the Twelve Days of Christmas.

New Year became especially important in Scotland because Christmas was effectively banned by the Kirk following the Reformation; for about 400 years, until the mid-twentieth century, many Scots worked over Christmas and celebrated instead at New Year. Christmas Day only became a public holiday in Scotland in 1958, and Boxing Day in 1974.

Customs

Customs vary across Scotland. The best known is first-footing: the first person to cross a home's threshold after midnight brings luck for the year — ideally a tall, dark-haired man bearing symbolic gifts such as coal, shortbread, salt, a coin and whisky. House-cleaning ("redding the house"), paying off debts and opening doors at midnight to let the old year out and the new year in are also customary. At midnight people sing Robert Burns's "Auld Lang Syne" and toast the New Year.

Local customs include the fireballs of Stonehaven, the burning of the clavie at Burghead and the tar barrels of Allendale and Comrie. Edinburgh's Hogmanay, with its torchlight procession, street party and fireworks over Edinburgh Castle, is among the world's most famous New Year celebrations, drawing tens of thousands of visitors.
