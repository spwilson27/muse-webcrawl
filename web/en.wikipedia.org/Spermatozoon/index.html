Spermatozoon

From Wikipedia, the free encyclopedia

A spermatozoon (plural spermatozoa) is a motile sperm cell, or moving form of the haploid cell that is the male gamete. A spermatozoon joins an ovum to form a zygote in the process of fertilization. Sperm cells contribute approximately half of the nuclear genetic information of the resulting offspring, and in mammals they also determine the sex of the offspring, since the sperm may carry either an X or a Y chromosome.

Structure

The mammalian spermatozoon consists of a head, midpiece, and tail (flagellum). The head contains the highly condensed haploid nucleus and is capped by the acrosome, a vesicle of enzymes that help penetrate the egg's outer layers. The midpiece is packed with mitochondria that supply energy, and the tail is a flagellum whose beating propels the cell. A human spermatozoon is about 50–60 micrometres long in total.

Production

Spermatozoa are produced in the seminiferous tubules of the testes through spermatogenesis, a process taking roughly two months in humans, followed by maturation in the epididymis. Healthy men produce on the order of a hundred million sperm per day. Sperm were first observed under the microscope by Antonie van Leeuwenhoek and his student Johan Ham in 1677.

Function and journey

After ejaculation, sperm swim through the female reproductive tract; only a tiny fraction reach the fallopian tubes where fertilization may occur. Capacitation — biochemical changes in the female tract — enables the acrosome reaction and hyperactivated motility needed to penetrate the egg. A single spermatozoon normally fertilizes the egg, after which the egg blocks entry of others.
