Optical disc

A compact disc. Optical discs store data in microscopic pits read by a laser.

An optical disc is a flat, usually circular disc that encodes binary data in the form of pits and lands on a special material layered on one of its flat surfaces. Data are read by shining a laser beam onto the disc and detecting changes in reflected light. Optical discs are a major medium for the distribution of software, music, and video.

History

The technology was developed in the 1960s and 1970s, with early systems such as LaserDisc (1978) used for video. The Compact Disc (CD), launched in 1982 by Philips and Sony, became a mass-market format for audio and later for data (CD-ROM). Recordable (CD-R) and rewritable (CD-RW) variants followed.

Major formats

  • CD — about 700 MB; infrared laser.
  • DVD — 4.7 GB per layer; red laser; widely used for films and software.
  • Blu-ray Disc — 25 GB per layer; blue-violet laser; high-definition video.
  • HD DVD — a rival high-definition format abandoned in 2008.

Decline

From the 2010s, internet streaming, downloads, and flash storage displaced optical media for most consumer uses, although discs remain in use for archival storage, game consoles, and film distribution.
