Hyaluronic acid
A glycosaminoglycan distributed widely throughout connective, epithelial and neural tissues; a major component of the extracellular matrix.

Hyaluronic acid (also called hyaluronan) is an anionic, nonsulfated glycosaminoglycan distributed widely throughout connective, epithelial, and neural tissues. It is unique among glycosaminoglycans in that it is not sulfated, forms in the plasma membrane instead of the Golgi apparatus, and can be very large — a single molecule in a human knee joint can reach millions of daltons.

Function

Hyaluronic acid is a chief component of the extracellular matrix and contributes significantly to cell proliferation and migration. Its remarkable water-binding capacity — up to 1,000 times its weight in water — makes it a natural lubricant and shock absorber in synovial fluid, and it maintains hydration and turgor in skin. Concentrations are high in the vitreous body of the eye and in umbilical cord tissue, from which it was first isolated by Karl Meyer in 1934.

Medical and cosmetic uses

Injectable preparations are used in viscosupplementation for osteoarthritis of the knee, in ophthalmic surgery, and as dermal fillers in cosmetic procedures. Hyaluronic acid is also a ubiquitous ingredient in skin-care products, where it acts as a humectant. Degradation is carried out by enzymes called hyaluronidases; fragments of hyaluronan participate in wound healing and inflammation signalling.
